Perinatal elimination of genetically aberrant human cortical neurons

Human neurons are postmitotic and long-lived, requiring precise genomic regulation. Normal neuronal function is highly dosage-sensitive, with copy number variants (CNVs) and heterozygous point mutations associated with a host of neurodevelopmental and neuropsychiatric conditions1,2,3,4. Here we investigated somatic CNVs arising in single neurons to understand the dynamic processes that shape the normal neuronal genetic landscape. Altogether, we sequenced 2,357 single neurons from human postmortem brain from fetal to postnatal life. Two single-cell whole-genome sequencing approaches—Tn5-transposase-based amplification (1,526 neurons, 16 individuals) and primary template amplification5 (831 neurons, 12 individuals)—showed that an average of 25.9% [12.7–46%] of prenatal cortical neurons have highly aberrant genomes, that is, widespread CNVs involving multiple chromosomes. This proportion reduced by about 4.5-fold after birth across the two single-cell whole-genome sequencing approaches (Fisher meta-analysis P = 3.5 × 10−6). Neurons with widespread CNVs were mainly eliminated perinatally, timed roughly parallel to periods of programmed cell death6, whereas neurons with smaller CNV burdens declined slowly in frequency with postnatal age. CNVs in surviving neurons were depleted for dosage-sensitive genes and genes involved in neurodevelopmental disorders (hypergeometric test P < 0.05), suggesting selective elimination of neurons with these CNVs. Analysis of 44,861 nuclei with single-nucleus assay for transposase-accessible chromatin (snATAC) and single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) showed that neurons with high CNV burdens exhibited abnormal synaptic gene expression, suggesting that abnormal synaptic gene regulation contributes to neuronal elimination. Elimination of defective neuronal genomes might represent an important developmental genome quality-control process that may be vulnerable in neurodevelopmental disease, and suggests purifying selection during human neurodevelopment.

Chromosome-scale CNVs are common in early post-zygotic development, with up to 73% of embryos exhibiting large chromosomal aneuploidy in some cells 7,8,9. Similarly, human neural progenitor cells (NPCs) exhibit extensive mosaic aneuploidy, involving up to 30% of progenitors10. Rates of aneuploidy in adult neurons have been controversial, with some reports of widespread aneuploidy11, whereas other reports suggest rare aneuploidy12,13,14,15,16 or frequent, predominantly sub-chromosomal CNVs14,17,18. These discrepancies arise from differences in methodology and the sensitivity of techniques, making it unclear whether the high aneuploidy rates observed early in development are maintained through to adulthood in the brain.

The control of copy number is especially crucial in the brain, because genetic variation in neurons can have profound functional impact. Autism, schizophrenia, epilepsy, intellectual disability and other neuropsychiatric conditions are all commonly caused by chromosomal or sub-chromosomal CNVs, as well as by heterozygous loss-of-function point mutations in more than 1,000 genes that are essential for brain functions1,2. Many of these genes encode structural proteins of the synapse or regulators of synaptic plasticity, suggesting that key aspects of neuronal plasticity underlying these conditions are intensely sensitive to gene dosage19,20,21.

Although a variety of cell-cycle checkpoints regulate progenitors to prevent genomic abnormalities or eliminate cells harbouring them6,11,22, it is not clear whether any such pathways exist to control gene dosage in noncycling cells such as neurons. One hypothesis suggests that genetically aberrant neurons are eliminated through programmed cell death6,23, a form of widespread apoptosis, which, in human cerebral cortex, is estimated to remove anywhere from 20% to 50% of neurons during development24. In all examined mammalian species, the most pronounced wave of apoptosis eliminates up to 30% of neurons in the perinatal period25, followed by ongoing elimination of postnatal neurons6,26 coincident with the establishment of neuronal circuits and connectivity mediated by synapse formation and pruning27. Although the roles of synapse formation in the regulation of neuronal survival have been described for more than a century28,29, the mechanisms and importance of widespread neuronal loss have not been clear, because dying neurons tend to be dispersed across the cortex, without clear relationships to neuronal cell type, laminar location or topographic patterns of connectivity6,26,30.

Using single-cell whole genome sequencing (scWGS), we compared CNVs in postmitotic neurons during neurodevelopment and ageing. Our analysis shows widespread CNVs across chromosomes in fetal neurons, followed by removal of most neurons with highly defective genomes around the time of birth. We also confirmed previous findings of slower removal of defective cells occurring postnatally into ageing17. Our data also show that this genome quality control mechanism is linked to gene pathways that are essential for synapse development, including disease-relevant genes. These findings indicate that synaptogenesis can play a key part in controlling genome quality in neurons, acting most strongly near birth but also extending into later life. This process is potentially vulnerable to environmental or genetic defects that disrupt normal brain development.

To profile CNVs in the human prenatal brain, we first generated low-coverage scWGS data from 1,526 neurons, collected from non-diseased postmortem brains from (1) nine prenatal donors ranging from gestational weeks 14 to 25 and (2) seven postnatal brains from infancy to 90 years or older (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table 1). To obtain a pure population of postmitotic prenatal cerebral cortical neurons, we cryo-dissected the human prenatal cortical plate (Fig. 1b) followed by flow cytometry to isolate single neuronal nuclei (Extended Data Fig. 1a). Dissection of cortical plate neurons provides an exclusively postmitotic, noncycling fetal neuronal population, because NPCs and glial nuclei are located in undissected deeper cortical regions. Neurons migrate into the cortical plate after withdrawing from the cell cycle and becoming permanently postmitotic. Single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) confirmed that our cortical plate dissection yielded 98% purity for neurons (Fig. 1b,c). Postnatal nucleus suspensions were stained with anti-NeuN antibody (neuronal marker) and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) before flow cytometry to isolate neuronal nuclei as previously described31 (Extended Data Fig. 1a). scWGS libraries were generated using Tn5-based amplification (TbA) after removal of chromatin-associated proteins with proteinase K and sequenced at about 0.05× depth (Methods and Extended Data Fig. 1b). Tn5-based single-cell whole genome amplification (scWGA)32,33 for scWGS results in even coverage of the genome, that is, low median absolute pairwise difference (MAPD) scores, and is cost-effective for scaling. In our case, this scWGA method was chosen to enable a bioinformatic validation pipeline to overcome typical challenges in validation of CNV, as the original single-cell DNA substrate is destroyed in the process of scWGA. To establish a reference for high-quality genomic profiles, we characterized cultured NPCs with and without disrupted nuclear integrity after inducing cell death with staurosporine (Extended Data Fig. 1c) and applied these data as the basis for transfer learning to distinguish high-quality from poor-quality sequencing profiles from our postmortem tissues (Methods and Extended Data Fig. 1d–i). These stringent quality control steps, together with restricting MAPD to less than 0.5 (Extended Data Fig. 1j), resulted in 1,248 high-quality, single-cell neuronal genomes.

To identify CNVs, we used HiScanner34, an algorithm we previously developed specifically for CNV detection in scWGS data. For robustness against amplification noise and sparse sequencing coverage, HiScanner models technology-specific allelic dropout patterns from scWGS data and leverages probabilistic segmentation to maintain high detection precision. Moreover, we designed another informatics validation approach: fragment overlap density (FOD) metric. Tn5-transposase-based libraries result in a specific library property in that we expect all amplified fragments to retain unique start and end positions of the original tagmented molecule; thus, in a copy-loss region, in which only one allele is present, no fragments should overlap, unlike in copy-neutral or copy-gain regions (Fig. 1d). This heuristic is represented by the FOD metric (Methods), which we use to further increase our CNV detection accuracy (Extended Data Fig. 2a–g and Supplementary Results).

We found that neurons with aberrant genomes were present in both the prenatal and postnatal human cerebral cortex (Fig. 1e,f and Supplementary Table 2). Many genomes with alterations had multiple affected regions across more than one chromosome, often involving large copy number changes. In prenatal samples, copy losses were nominally more frequent than copy gains, with 53% (3,090/5,844) of CNVs being copy losses and 47% (2,754/5,844) copy gains. In postnatal neurons, copy losses (59%, 1,722/2,921) were again more frequent than copy gains (41%, 1,199/2,921).

We observed that prenatal neurons more frequently carried at least one CNV of any size than postnatal neurons (mean 62.9% compared with 29.2%; P = 0.002), a difference driven by both copy gains (mean 40.6% compared with 10.0%; P = 0.0002) and copy losses (43.9% compared with 23.4%; P = 0.04) (two-sided Student’s t-test throughout; Extended Data Fig. 2h).

Because arbitrary CNV size- or count-based thresholds fail to capture the true biological complexity of each neuron—which can carry small and chromosome-scale CNVs—we applied mutational signature analysis35,36 to classify cells with aberrant genomes. Using the COSMIC (Catalogue of Somatic Mutations in Cancer) copy number signature catalogue36, we classified neurons into four distinct signature groups (Supplementary Table 3): (1) diploid-predominant CNV neurons (COSMIC SigCN1), which represents neurons with primarily diploid genomes and those with minimal CNVs (Fig. 2a); (2) gain-predominant CNVs (combination of SigCN1 and SigCN2); (3) focal-loss-predominant CNVs (SigCN1 and SigCN9); and (4) broad-loss-predominant CNVs (SigCN1 and SigCN13). The last three groups represent neurons with widespread CNV (Fig. 2a and Extended Data Fig. 3). Compared with diploid-predominant CNV neurons, widespread CNV neurons harbour genomic alterations that encompass larger regions of the genome, larger size distributions of CNV and have more aneuploidy (here, defined as gain or loss of >90% of a chromosome) (Extended Data Fig. 4). These neuronal copy number signatures closely resemble those defined in COSMIC from cancers (mean cosine similarity 0.9716; Extended Data Fig. 3d), suggesting that neuronal CNVs may have arisen in dividing cells, presumably NPCs, in the developing human brain.

The proportion of neurons with widespread CNVs (last three of the four groups defined above) in prenatal samples was on average 25.9% (range 12.7–46.0%), but decreased 4.2-fold to about 6.2% postnatally (P = 0.002; two-sided Student’s t-test) (Fig. 2b). The mean during infancy (0–2 years) was 7.1% and was 4.8% for older samples (>60 years) (Fig. 2b,c), suggesting relative stability of this population during postnatal ageing.

To validate our results from TbA, we performed primary template amplification (PTA)5 in single neurons, as an orthogonal scWGA method, on an additional 831 neuronal nuclei from 5 prenatal and 7 postnatal donors, yielding 659 high-quality neurons that passed stringent QC filters (Extended Data Fig. 5 and Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). Neurons amplified with PTA were sequenced at higher depth (about 0.5×), resulting in generally even genome amplification as determined by MAPD (Extended Data Fig. 5a,b). Copy number signatures derived from PTA-amplified neurons also segregated into distinct groups highly concordant with the diploid predominant and widespread CNV categorizations from TbA (Extended Data Fig. 5c–f). The mean widespread CNV neuron proportion decreased 4.6-fold between the prenatal and postnatal periods, from 33.1% to 7.2% of neurons, respectively (P = 0.0001, Student’s t-test, two-sided; Fig. 2b,c), and for each age category was not significantly different from the widespread CNV proportion from TbA. The combined Fisher meta-analysis P value across both scWGA methods for the prenatal-postnatal changes was P = 3.5 × 10−6. To ensure that the observed reduction in the proportions of neurons with aberrant genomes is not affected by poor-quality cells, we checked that there is no significant association of widespread CNV proportion with the percentage of neurons passing quality control (QC) filters and sample size (Extended Data Fig. 6a,b). Prenatal samples as a group had a lower postmortem interval (PMI) (Extended Data Fig. 6c), but PMI-driven artefact would predict higher widespread CNV proportion at longer PMI, and no such positive association was observed (postnatal r2 = 0.05, P = 0.44).

Previous reports indicate that the proportion of neurons harbouring at least one CNV declines with age14,17. In our combined TbA and PTA data, this proportion dropped from 63.9% prenatally to 30.9% in early postnatal periods (age 0–5 years) (P = 0.0007). From early postnatal to later ages (6+ years), we found a nominal trend towards a decrease in mean proportion from 30.9% to 22.5% (P = 0.27, both two-sided Student’s t-tests with Bonferroni correction; Extended Data Fig. 7a). The primary decline thus again occurs perinatally, with the later change not reaching significance in our data alone. Therefore, we performed a meta-analysis, combining data from TbA and PTA in our study and six additional scWGA studies in postnatal neurons (Supplementary Table 4) using a linear mixed-regression model, thus accounting statistically for differences in sequencing platform and CNV-calling methods from independent studies. At birth, the model estimated that approximately 32.8% of cells carry a CNV (95% confidence interval (CI) 25.8–39.9%), and age was significantly negatively associated with the proportion of neurons carrying any CNV (−0.27 percentage points per year, P = 0.0001; Extended Data Fig. 7b), supporting ongoing elimination of CNV-carrying neurons during postnatal life.

To determine whether CNVs arise in replicating progenitor cells or postmitotic neurons, we analysed their associations with DNA replication timing and physiological double-strand breaks (DSBs)37. We found that CNV breakpoint density was highly correlated with replication timing (Pearson r = −0.95, P < 0.0001), such that CNV breakpoints were more frequent in early-replicating regions (Fig. 2d and Extended Data Fig. 8a). This result indicates that CNVs may be generated in mitotic progenitor cells during DNA replication.

We next evaluated hotspots of DSBs in NPCs determined previously by in-suspension breaks labelling in situ and sequencing (BLISS), as DSBs have been associated with CNV generation in neurological disease37. BLISS hotspots (top 1% of BLISS regions) were associated with copy loss at recurrent CNV breakpoints between donors and showed a nominal trend towards enrichment at BLISS hotspots associated with copy gain at recurrent CNV breakpoints (P = 0.06; Extended Data Fig. 8b). By contrast, CNV breakpoints unique to individual neurons (gain or loss) were not significantly associated with BLISS hotspots (Extended Data Fig. 8c). Analysis of within-donor recurrent breakpoints was insufficiently powered. Together, these findings suggest that DSB-prone loci contribute to recurrent CNV breakpoints, with the strongest evidence for copy losses.

We quantified recurrent CNV breakpoints across our dataset and found that 26.9% of CNV breakpoints were precisely shared by more than one individual, consistent with genomically susceptible regions prone to recurrent somatic CNV formation. Within individual donors, 0–5.1% of CNVs were shared (both breakpoints match) between individual neurons, indicating that a subset of somatic CNVs may be clonally propagated.

DNA replication stress has been associated with the formation of micronuclei, which encapsulate missegregated chromosomes or fragments that lie outside the nucleus, as observed in both early post-zygotic cell divisions and cancer7,38. Thus, we proposed that micronuclei would be visible in some human prenatal neurons. High-resolution microscopy on fixed slices of human fetal brain at mid-gestation, stained with DAPI, revealed co-occurrence of micronuclei in cells with neuronal identity as assayed by immunostaining with the prenatal neuronal marker, SATB2 (Extended Data Fig. 8d). Three-dimensional (3D) reconstructed images demonstrate localization of micronuclei in the fetal cortical plate (Fig. 2e). We observed that micronuclei were associated with around 6% of primary nuclei in the cortical plate (Supplementary Table 5), providing direct, in situ demonstration of relatively common, missegregated chromosomal material at mid-gestation.

We sought to orthogonally confirm the elimination of cells with widespread CNVs over time by evaluating whether micronuclei are also cleared over time. We isolated micronuclear and nuclear fractions from human brain tissue from fetal, infant and adult individuals by serial gradient sucrose fractionation (Supplementary Methods) with visualization to identify micronuclei-enriched genetic content (Fig. 2f and Extended Data Fig. 8e). By comparing the genetic content of micronuclear and nuclear fractions using genome sequencing, we found that micronuclei in the prenatal samples harboured aberrant genetic material, observed as elevated read depth relative to the nuclear fraction (Extended Data Fig. 9 and Supplementary Table 6). The magnitude of this deviation decreased in infancy/childhood and adulthood, indicating clearance of micronuclei genetic material over time (Extended Data Fig. 9). The significant decrease in micronuclear content with age persisted after regression analysis adjusting for chromosome size and GC content (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Methods). The age-related decline in micronuclear content occurs temporally in parallel with the decline in CNVs in primary nuclei (as measured by TbA and PTA), providing further evidence for the selective depletion of genomically aberrant neurons over time.

To identify pathways regulating the elimination process by associating CNV changes in single neurons with gene expression, we performed 10× multiome snATAC- and snRNA-seq co-assay of 44,861 single nuclei (Fig. 3a and Extended Data Fig. 10). We generated single-nucleus libraries from three prenatal and five postnatal brains (Supplementary Table 7), followed by dimensionality reduction, cell-type annotation and gene module scoring for each gene set from snRNA-seq, and CNV calling from snATAC-seq (Methods). Unlike CNV calling from scWGS, CNV calling from snATAC-seq lacks sufficient resolution for signature decomposition of CNVs, so we segregated nuclei broadly into CNV neurons and non-CNV neurons. To identify CNV neurons, we inspected the distribution of the proportion of genomic regions affected by CNVs and, using Tukey’s rule, classified outlier cells as CNV neurons (Methods and Extended Data Fig. 10a,b). The correlation of CNV neuron proportion from snATAC to widespread CNV neurons determined by scWGS was high (r = 0.95) between three donors for which both technologies were applied (Extended Data Fig. 10c).

Our findings from snATAC-seq confirmed a significant reduction in CNV neurons between prenatal and postnatal ages (Fig. 3b; P = 0.036), suggesting that these CNV neurons identified by snATAC are most similar to the widespread CNV neurons observed in scWGS. We confirmed that CNV neurons have high cell-quality metrics, consistent with the rest of the dataset (Extended Data Fig. 10d). We found CNV neurons in excitatory, inhibitory and maturing neuron subsets, as well as CNV progenitor cells, as expected (Extended Data Fig. 10e). CNV analysis among glial cells did not detect differences in CNV rate prenatally and postnatally (Extended Data Fig. 10f; P = 0.57, Wilcoxon rank sum), although we cannot draw firm conclusions because of the limited prenatal glial sample size (approximately 1–2% of nuclei).

To identify pathways that are specifically dysregulated in CNV neurons and that might drive elimination, we inferred gene modules that were differentially expressed (differential gene modules (DGMs)) between CNV neurons and non-CNV neurons. The significant DGMs associated with postnatal CNV neurons (Supplementary Table 8) frequently represented components of synaptic function and connectivity (Fig. 3c). To ensure that this enrichment did not simply reflect that our analysis focused on neurons, we developed a null background by permuting CNV status within cell types. We determined that these synaptic DGMs distinguish a subset of excitatory neurons across all layers of the cortex, and a representative DGM is shown (Fig. 3d). In progenitor cells, CNV progenitor cells are significantly enriched for known gene modules related to cell cycle checkpoints, for example, CDKN1A- and TP53-related pathways (Extended Data Fig. 10g). These results highlight differential regulation of dividing CNV progenitor cells and postmitotic CNV neurons, with CNV neurons demonstrating significant differences in synapse function gene expression compared with non-CNV neurons.

Differential gene module analysis showed that synaptic gene modules are significantly downregulated in CNV neurons compared with non-CNV neurons during the postnatal period, coinciding with the established timelines for neuronal elimination by programmed cell death. This process is characterized by extensive neuronal elimination around birth, followed by ongoing cell death postnatally through processes thought to foster appropriate synaptic connectivity during brain development6,25,39. Hierarchical clustering of CNV neurons based on synaptic and cell death gene modules revealed two distinct subpopulations: one subset (hereafter, cluster 1) exhibited reduced synaptic module expression and upregulated cell death pathways (apoptosis and autophagy) (Fig. 3d,e), whereas the remaining neurons (cluster 2) maintained normal to high synaptic module expression with low cell death pathway activation. The proportion of CNV neurons in cluster 1 was highest in infant and youth samples and declined with age (Fig. 3f), consistent with the timeline of synaptic connectivity and pruning during early life6,26,27. Notably, cluster 1 CNV neurons were absent in our mid-gestation samples, and limitations in third-trimester sample availability prevented assessment of late gestation. These findings indicate that cluster 1 gene expression patterns emerge sometime during the perinatal period and that the processes of synaptic function and cell death can contribute to the decline in CNVs observed in the postnatal period.

As the elimination of neurons across perinatal and postnatal ages favours survival of euploid neurons, we sought to identify genetic loci that are preferentially preserved or disrupted by neuronal CNVs, to identify signals of genetic selection. Using our scWGS data, we identified regions of the genome that were coldspots, regions rarely or never disrupted in surviving neurons—indicating purifying selection, as disruption of these regions is poorly tolerated—or hotspots, regions recurrently disrupted in surviving neurons that imply CNVs at these loci are tolerated and thus not selected against (Fig. 4a). To maximize analytical power, we included breakpoints from CNVs of all sizes. Coldspots frequently overlapped regions with higher gene density (Fig. 4b), a pattern not observed for hotspots.

Given this observation, we proposed that coldspots may harbour key neurodevelopmental genes that neurons or progenitors may not tolerate losing. We assessed coldspots for enrichment of genes associated with autism, loss-of-function intolerance, epilepsy, neurodevelopmental disorders, schizophrenia, cancer and housekeeping gene sets (Fig. 4c and Methods), and found significant enrichment for autism genes, which frequently regulate synapse formation, maintenance and synaptic pruning20. Furthermore, postsynaptic genes were also significantly enriched within coldspots (Fig. 4c), reinforcing the important role of synaptic genes in neuronal genome selection. Collectively, these findings indicate that negative selection against CNV in autism-related and synapse-associated genes shapes the neuronal genetic landscape. Coupled with our DGM analyses, these data imply that regulation or disruption of synaptic gene modules—whether through large-scale genomic alterations or through gene-specific loss of synaptic proteins—influences neuronal survival during neurodevelopment.

Our data suggest that widespread CNVs are a prominent feature of neurons during prenatal human neurodevelopment and are dynamic over time. These genetic abnormalities share characteristics with those seen in early post-fertilization zygotes, including signatures of genetic stress and the formation of micronuclei7. We describe two distinct phases in the elimination of neurons with CNVs: a marked reduction in widespread CNV neurons during the perinatal period and a slower ongoing removal of cells that harbour CNVs over the lifespan (Fig. 5). The earlier, widespread elimination of aberrant neurons parallels the timing of neuronal cell death described during cerebral cortex development6,40, whereas our data also confirm earlier reports of slow, ongoing preferential elimination of neurons carrying sub-chromosomal CNVs through postnatal life and into adulthood14,17,18,40 (Fig. 5).

Neurogenesis in the cerebral cortex begins at roughly 10–12 gestational weeks, continuing to about 24 gestational weeks for excitatory neurons of the cerebral cortex and potentially until after birth for inhibitory neurons of the neocortex, and produces neurons with aberrant genomes. Estimated trends of programmed cell death6 establish a large wave of cell death (about 30%) near birth, a time of extensive neuronal connectivity and maturation, followed by ongoing elimination of neurons that do not form appropriate synaptic connections. Our data indicate that previously described neurobiological processes of cellular apoptosis and programmed cell death6 may represent a core mechanism of genetic quality control for postmitotic neurons. We show that the dysregulation of synaptic gene modules in a subset of neurons with high CNV burden correlates with cellular elimination, along with selective pressure against dosage changes in crucial neurodevelopmental genes related to synaptogenesis. Correct synaptic connectivity is important in the human cerebral cortex for cell survival41, and competition among neurons for synaptic space is known to strongly modulate neuronal cell death42,43. We show that neurons with high CNV burden show dysregulated expression of synaptic pathway genes and negative selection against neurons with disruption of autism-related genes and postsynaptic genes. The force of selection is probably related to the extent of CNVs and their impact on synaptic function, with widespread CNVs predicted to impact cellular fitness more severely than smaller CNVs. The perinatal timing of the reduction of prenatal neurons with widespread CNVs proportionally mirrors the time course of a large wave of neuronal loss of about 30% occurring during late gestation42,44,45,46, a period of crucial neuronal circuit formation, although patterns of cell death in humans have not been characterized as thoroughly as in model mammalian species because of the limited availability of appropriate tissue. Our meta-analysis of the slow postnatal decline in neurons with CNV estimates 13% neuronal loss over the course of 60 years (Extended Data Fig. 7b), on par with the estimated approximately 10% decline in ageing neuron number as estimated from stereological methods47.

Our findings of widespread CNV prenatally suggest that neurons of the human brain are competitively selected from extensive genomic diversity generated in developing neural cells. At least two distinct processes seem to shape the CNV landscape. Replication stress during neuronal progenitor divisions leaves a molecular signature: CNV breakpoints cluster at early-replicating loci, and micronuclei, the cellular by-product of replication-induced chromosomal damage, are abundant in the fetal cortex. In parallel, recurrent copy losses at BLISS-defined double-strand break hotspots implicate repair mediated by non-homologous end-joining as a second source of genomic rearrangement, operating preferentially at constitutively fragile loci. The age-related clearance of micronuclear content mirrors the elimination of CNV-bearing primary nuclei, consistent with a shared fate for cells carrying replication-derived genomic damage, although precise mechanisms linking micronuclei to neuronal elimination remain to be established. Together, these observations indicate that the somatic CNV landscape of mature neurons is shaped by at least two mechanistically distinct processes operating during development.

Because persistence of aneuploid neurons would disrupt neuronal circuit function, the perinatal period emerges as an critical developmental window ensuring that neurons surviving into adulthood are predominantly euploid. The relative absence of CNVs that encompass dosage-sensitive and synapse-related genes in surviving neurons suggests intrinsic negative selective mechanisms during pre- and postnatal central nervous system development. Disruptions to the processes of perinatal neuronal genomic selection would permit genomically aberrant neurons to persist, with potentially profound implications for brain function, so it will be important to determine whether genetic factors that cause developmental disabilities show abnormalities of these selective processes. Finally, ageing and neurodegenerative conditions create large burdens of other types of somatic mutations (for example, SNVs and insertion–deletion events) with potential impacts on neuronal functions31,48,49, so the persistence of selective mechanisms that target neurons with damaged genomes for death may contribute to neurodegeneration later in life.

Human prenatal brain tissue was obtained from the NIH NeuroBioBank. Tissue from the frontoparietal lobes was sectioned into 40 μm sections with a cryostat. The cortical plate was dissected using a microscalpel under a light microscope and dissociated into nuclei using gentle dounce homogenization in 1.5 ml lysis buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 250 mM sucrose, 25 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 0.1% Triton-X, 1× cOmplete Mini Protease, 1 mM DTT). Nuclei were pelleted by centrifugation for 10 min at 900g. The supernatant was removed, and the nuclei were resuspended in sorting buffer (1× PBS pH 7.4, 0.8% BSA, 0.1 μg ml−1 DAPI). Flow cytometry was performed, gating for FSC, SSC and DAPI content to isolate single nuclei. For postnatal brain nuclei, staining was performed with both DAPI and Alexa Fluor 488 conjugated anti-NeuN at 1:1,000 for 1 h at 4 °C (Sigma Aldrich MAB377X) as previously described50.

TbA has been used in multiple contexts for scWGA under different names, with a core workflow comprising (1) nucleosome depletion; (2) Tn5 tagmentation and direct adapter addition; and (3) library amplification. Single nuclei were lysed in microwells in 2 μl lysis buffer (20 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 0.15% Triton-X, 1 mM EDTA, 250 mM DTT, 0.05 μl Thermolabile Proteinase K (NEB P8111S)) at 30 °C for 1 h, followed by proteinase inactivation at 55 °C for 10 min. Proteinase K digests histones within nucleosomes and improves accessibility of the genome by eliminating chromatin structure. Tn5 was loaded according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Diagenode C01070010-20), and 1 μl of Tn5 diluted 1,000-fold was used per single nucleus. Tagmentation was performed using manufacturer’s buffers and instructions (Diagenode). Single-cell genome libraries were generated by 16 cycles of PCR using NexteraXT v.2 indexes. Rows of 12 single cells were pooled for cleanup using the Zymo Clean and Concentrator Kit. Each row of single cells was eluted in 42 μl Zymo elution buffer for subsequent AMPure XP size selection at 0.8×. Four rows (48 cells) were pooled for sequencing in equimolar concentration, and sequencing was performed on NovaSeq 6000 (Novogene) for a target library size of 0.5 Gb per single-cell library. Illumina reads were aligned to the human reference with decoy sequence GRCh37d5 (hs37d5) using bwa-mem51, followed by duplicate marking and base quality score recalibration according to GATK best practices52.

To ensure analysis of only high-quality single-cell genomes, we first removed cells with fewer than 10 million total covered bases (the ‘total’ column from GATK DepthOfCoverage, that is, summed per-base depth across all positions), and then used an NPC line dataset as the basis for transfer learning to distinguish high-quality from poor-quality sequencing profiles. The TbA libraries from single cells of the NPC line allowed us to establish a benchmark of sample quality, that is, on staurosporine treatment 24 h to induce apoptosis, we created a labelled TbA dataset comprising (1) high-quality, recently viable cells; (2) cells marked for apoptosis by Annexin V staining with nuclear envelope still intact; and (3) cells with compromised nuclear integrity marked by propidium iodide (Extended Data Fig. 1c). The NPC line was differentiated as previously described53 using an induced pluripotent cell line (iPSC) derived from well-characterized human fibroblasts54. NPC identity was confirmed (21 March 2021) by immunohistochemistry using antibodies against PAX6 (BD Pharmingen 561462; diluted 1:300) and Nestin (Millipore Sigma HPA026111; diluted 1:500).

To categorize cells computationally, we first normalized read counts for each cell in the NPC dataset using the read-depth normalization function of HiScanner34 with bin size set to 500 kb, resulting in a cell-by-bin matrix. Next, we used Scanpy55 to scale the read counts, standardizing variance across cells, followed by principal component analysis for dimensionality reduction. Cell-to-cell similarity was quantified by constructing a neighbourhood graph with scanpy.pp.neighbors. Clustering was performed using the Leiden algorithm with a resolution parameter of 0.2. After visual inspection and correlating cluster-wise read count profiles with existing replication timing tracks, library sizes and MAPD scores, we determined that the three Leiden clusters (QC Leiden 0, 1 and 2) in NPC data are high quality, atypical and poor quality, respectively. We subsequently applied scanpy.tl.ingest to project these labels and embeddings from NPC to human brain single-cell TbA data with default parameters. Only cells labelled as ‘high quality’ (Leiden 0) and MAPD < 0.5 were retained. We excluded samples in which the proportion of cells in Leiden 0 (within each sample batch) fell below 65%, because of concerns about poor sample or batch quality. Samples in batches that yielded fewer than 25 cells after all cell-level filters were also excluded from downstream analyses.

After filtering at the cellular and sample level as described above, we called CNVs on single-cell genomes. For TbA data, CNV calling was based on read depth followed by validation using FOD (Supplementary Methods). For PTA data, CNV calling jointly leveraged read depth and B-allele frequency (BAF).

HiScanner34 was applied in rdr_only mode with specific parameters optimized for our analysis. The bin size was set to 500 kb mappable positions. The hg19/GRCh37 genome was used as the reference. λ was set to 64, and ploidy restriction was enabled (max_wgd=1 and restrict_gamma=true). The multisample option was disabled (multisample=false) to process each cell individually. HiScanner v.1.6.1 was installed through pip. To ensure the reliability of detected CNVs, we implemented a stringent filtering approach: CNV candidates with P ≥ 0.05 or those spanning less than 5 Mb were excluded. We also removed candidates within the pseudoautosomal regions (PARs) of chromosomes X and Y, as defined in the hg19/GRCh37 genome assembly.

To refine CNV calls, we first defined a cell-specific diploid FOD distribution using the five longest segments with predicted copy number 2 (assuming no whole-genome doubling), from which the mean and standard deviation were derived. FOD z-scores for each CNV candidate were then computed against this baseline (Supplementary Information). Calls were retained if the FOD z-score exceeded 1.96 for gains (CN > 2) or fell below −1.96 for losses (CN < 2); cells with a diploid mean FOD of zero were excluded. Calls that failed validation were reset to the diploid background value of 2.

Matched bulk whole-genome sequencing data were processed using the same alignment and preprocessing pipeline. For PTA data, HiScanner34 was applied in BAF+RDR mode (rdr_only=false) with the matched bulk as reference, with a bin size of 500 kb mappable positions, λ set to 64, max_wgd=1, ADO threshold of 0.2, and restrict_gamma=true. CNVs were reported as allelic copy numbers (CNA|CNB), from which total copy number was derived as the sum. Post-processing filters were applied sequentially: (1) bins called as non-diploid across cells from three or more donors were treated as recurrent artefacts and reset to CN = 2; (2) CNV segments spanning less than 5 Mb were excluded; (3) putative homozygous deletion calls (allelic pattern X∣0) inconsistent with the observed VAF (VAF > 0.1) were removed; (4) extreme copy number states (CN > 4) were excluded; and (5) gains (CN > 2) with VAFestimate < 0.2 were removed as likely artefacts. Filtered regions retained the diploid background value of 2.

To identify copy number signature exposures at the single-cell level, we used SigProfilerAssignment (v.0.1.4) in conjunction with SigProfilerMatrixGenerator (v.1.2.26; refs. 56,57). First, we generated a 48-channel copy number summary using the channels defined by COSMIC. Given the lack of allele specificity in our single-cell copy number calls, we developed a conversion scheme to transform our integer copy number states into pseudo-allele-specific states. Specifically, we mapped copy numbers into allelic states as follows: 0 to 0|0, 1 to 1|0, 2 to 1|1, 3 to 2|1, 4 to 3|1, and so on, up to a maximum copy number of 11 (10|1). This approach enabled construction of 48-channel copy-number summary vectors compatible with existing signature-analysis frameworks. Next, we linearly decomposed the copy number summary into copy number signature exposures. SigProfilerAssignment was run using default parameters, using the COSMIC v.3.4 pan-cancer signature panel (CN1–22, also referred to in this study as SigCN1–22; ref. 36) as the reference. Before signature clustering, we excluded three cells for which CNV calling produced no callable output and cells for which SigProfilerAssignment could not produce a valid signature decomposition.

To cluster cells by signature exposure profile, we first normalized the signature exposure vector of each cell to sum to 1, then added a small amount of Gaussian noise (σ = 10−4) to break degeneracies in the sparse exposure space. For TbA cells passing quality filters (MAPD < 0.5), we constructed a k-nearest-neighbour graph (k = 30, cosine distance) on these perturbed exposures using Scanpy (v.1.11.0; ref. 55), computed a two-dimensional UMAP embedding, and applied the Leiden community detection algorithm (resolution = 0.1) to identify four discrete groups of cells: cluster 0 (diploid predominant), cluster 1 (gain predominant), cluster 2 (focal loss predominant) and cluster 3 (broad loss predominant). PTA cells (after the same noise perturbation) were subsequently assigned to clusters by mapping onto the TbA reference embedding using the ingest function of Scanpy, which propagates cluster labels through k-nearest-neighbour label transfer in the reference UMAP space. To assess the robustness of the signature decomposition, we computed for each cell the cosine similarity between its observed copy number profile and the profile reconstructed from its assigned signature weights. A cosine similarity close to 1 indicates that the assigned signatures faithfully recapitulate the copy number landscape of the cell, whereas lower values flag cells whose profiles are poorly explained by the reference panel. Cosine similarities were aggregated and compared across clusters, serving as a per-cluster measure of decomposition confidence.

To investigate the relationship between CNV breakpoints and genomic features, we analysed replication timing and DNA double-strand break (DSB) distributions. For replication timing analysis, we used the replication timing track for the BG02ES (human embryonic stem cell) line generated by the ENCODE project58,59 downloaded from the UCSC Genome Browser60. The average replication timing signal was calculated for each 500 kb mappable bin and normalized between 0 and 1. For DSB analysis, BLISS coordinates were downloaded from FigShare (https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.18530531.v2)61, and DSB BED files for each replicate were intersected with the 500 kb genomic bins to quantify DSB signals, which were then normalized to the per-track maximum.

To establish a background distribution for CNV enrichment analysis, 10,000 random CNVs were generated for each original CNV, matching their length distribution within autosomes (chr1–22). Peri-centromeric and telomeric regions were excluded to minimize biases, with centromeric regions defined by extending UCSC cytoband coordinates by ±5 Mb and telomeric regions defined as the first and last 5 Mb of each chromosome. Replication timing data were divided into deciles representing quantiles across the genome. For each decile, the observed-to-expected (O/E) ratio was calculated by normalizing observed breakpoints (that is, start and end sites of a CNV) to the expected number based on random simulations. The correlation between replication timing O/E ratios for CNVs from different categories (age group, signature group) was calculated, and statistical significance was evaluated using Pearson correlation tests.

To analyse the relationship between CNV breakpoints and DSBs, we compared CNV breakpoint distributions to BLISS data from six neural cell preparations: two biological replicates each from human neuroepithelial stem cells, NPCs and postmitotic neurons (NEUs). BLISS read counts were summed into 500 kb genomic bins and normalized to the per-track maximum. DSB hotspots were defined independently for each track as bins exceeding the 99th percentile of normalized signal; a bin was classified as a hotspot if it met this threshold in any of the six tracks. For each CNV, 10 length-matched random CNVs were generated by placing segments of identical length at uniformly sampled positions across autosomes, and breakpoint positions (start and end of each CNV) were extracted for both observed and simulated sets. O/E ratios were calculated per individual as the count of breakpoints overlapping hotspot or non-hotspot bins divided by the mean count across the 10 simulated replicates. Neuroepithelial stem cell replicates were excluded from the primary analysis because of their strong correlation with replication timing (Pearson r = 0.50–0.53, FDR-corrected P < 0.05), which is substantially higher than the correlations observed for NPC (r = 0.17–0.23) and NEU (r = 0.15–0.24); NPC and NEU tracks were therefore used for the aggregated hotspot analysis. O/E ratios were capped at 4 for visualization. Statistical significance was assessed using paired t-tests comparing the hotspot O/E ratio of each individual with their non-hotspot O/E ratio (n = 15 pairs each for gains and losses), with P values adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR procedure.

CNV breakpoints were further stratified by recurrence. A breakpoint was classified as recurrent if the same genomic position was observed in more than one donor; breakpoints detected in more than two donors were used for full-CNV recurrence analysis. A breakpoint was classified as private if it was detected in exactly one cell from a single donor. Within-donor clonal breakpoints—shared by two or more cells from the same individual—were analysed separately. BLISS hotspot enrichment analysis was performed independently for each recurrence class.

To identify hot and cold spots in diploid-predominant neurons, we adapted an approach similar to that in ref. 18. We defined genomic bins of 5 Mb by aggregating the existing 500 kb bin coordinate set that was used in CNV calling, with any remaining small segments at the end of the chromosome discarded. Each 5 Mb bin was assigned an empirical overlap count, representing the number of overlaps with observed CNVs. We then performed 10,000 permutations, as outlined in the previous section, preserving the CNV sizes. For each permutation, the number of overlaps with random CNVs was also calculated, allowing us to generate a null distribution for each bin. Bins were classified as hotspots or coldspots using unadjusted one-sided empirical P values, with upper-tail P < 0.05 defining hotspots and lower-tail P < 0.05 defining coldspots, with the remaining bins classified as neutral. Each 5 Mb bin was further evaluated for overlaps with gene sets, including housekeeping genes (Gene Ontology), loss-of-function intolerant genes62 (pLI > 0.9), SFARI63 autism genes, neurodevelopmental disorder genes64, schizophrenia64 genes, postsynaptic genes65 and cancer driver genes from COSMIC66, and epilepsy genes (OMIM67 symptom search ‘epilepsy’). To assess gene set enrichment within hotspots and coldspots, hypergeometric tests were performed to evaluate over-enrichment of each gene set within these regions compared with all genomic bins.

Nuclei were isolated without dissection as described in section ‘Isolation of neuronal nuclei’ and permeabilized after flow cytometry according to the Nuclei Isolation from Complex Tissues for Single Cell Multiome ATAC + Gene Expression Sequencing protocol from 10× Genomics (Demonstrated Protocol, CG000375). Chromium Next GEM Single Cell Multiome ATAC + Gene Expression libraries were prepared according to the manual with adaptations to also enable detection of complementary DNA strands (duplexes)68. Duplex information was not used for this study.

The multiomic dataset consists of snRNA-seq and snATAC-seq data from prenatal and postnatal brain samples, produced following the 10× Genomics protocol. Data were processed using the 10× Genomics Cell Ranger ARC pipeline (v.2.0.1). Subsequently, the dataset was analysed with Seurat (v.5.0.1; ref. 69) and Signac (v.1.12.0; ref. 70) R packages.

For both prenatal and postnatal brain samples, nuclei were filtered on several metrics. First, multiplet detection was performed on snATAC-seq data using AMULET, after which cells classified as multiplets were filtered out. Next, filtering thresholds for several metrics were determined by visual inspection of rank plots of each metric for all samples. For total RNA molecule count, RNA feature count (number of genes), and total ATAC fragment count, a lower threshold of 1,000 was used to remove empty background nuclei, and no upper threshold was used given multiplet removal had already been performed. Finally, for both nucleosome signal and transcription start site (TSS) enrichment values from snATAC-seq, nuclei were removed with lower (0.32 for nucleosome signal and 1.75 for TSS enrichment) and upper (1.59 for nucleosome signal and 6.80 for TSS enrichment) thresholds determined by the rank plots. Moreover, a lower threshold of 0.5 was determined from the rank plots for the fraction of reads in peaks.

Cell-type annotation was performed by mapping each sample to a reference sample. We chose separate references for prenatal and postnatal because of the differences in cell-type composition between prenatal and postnatal cells. For the prenatal samples, H29180_F4 was chosen as reference, and for the postnatal samples, the downsampled reference was used from a previously published dataset71. The prenatal reference was manually annotated by first performing unsupervised clustering on normalized, principal component analysis-transformed RNA count data. The resulting clusters were then manually annotated using known marker genes for different cell types. A UMAP (uniform manifold approximation and projection) representation was generated for the prenatal reference. For the postnatal reference, the pre-existing annotations from ref. 71, based on clustering performed in the paper, were used. The remaining non-reference ‘query’ samples were then projected onto the reference UMAPs using the MapQuery function of Seurat. The MapQuery function transferred the manually annotated cell-type labels from reference to query samples, resulting in complete cell-type annotation for all samples in the multiome dataset.

CNV calling was performed using the R package epiAneufinder (v.1.1.3; ref. 72), with a window size of 100 kb and a minimum fragment number of 20,000 per sample. The parameter minCNVsize was set to 0, meaning that the minimum callable CNV size was one bin, that is, 100 kb. After CNV calling was complete, the CNV calls were combined across all samples by taking the intersection of bins in all samples. For the resulting bins, for each cell across all samples, the percentage of bins with a CNV (either gain or loss) was calculated. The resulting distribution of the percentage of bins affected was used to determine a cutoff for our definition of CNV Neurons or CNV Cells. Tukey’s rule was applied for identifying outliers, such that any cell with a percentage of bins greater than the third quartile plus 1.5 times the interquartile range (Q3 + 1.5IQR) was classified as an outlier. These outliers were then classified as CNV neurons, and non-outliers as non-CNV neurons.

We analysed the snRNA-seq part of single-cell multiome data separately for prenatal samples (n = 3; UMB4827, UMB1932 and 29180_F4) and postnatal samples (n = 5; APGEQ, UMB1790, UMB4643, UMB1278 and UMB1864) because of the differences in cell-type composition. For each age group, sample-specific Seurat objects were merged into a combined dataset while preserving sample identifiers. Gene expression values were log-normalized with a scale factor of 10,000. To assess pathway activation, we calculated module scores using gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database73, including Gene Ontology Cellular Component terms and curated gene sets (C2). For C2, we only included gene sets that matched the keywords ‘senescence’, ‘apoptosis’, ‘autophagy’, and ‘caspase’—hereafter referred to as ‘cell elimination C2 pathways’. Module scores were computed using the AddModuleScore function of Seurat with default parameters, which calculates the average expression of each gene set in a given cell after controlling for gene expression program complexity by subtracting the aggregated expression of randomly selected control gene sets with similar average expression levels.

To test for associations between gene module scores and CNV neuron status, we implemented a stratified permutation testing approach. For each gene module, we calculated the observed difference in mean module scores between CNV and non-CNV cells (hereafter referred to as ‘absolute observed difference’). Statistical significance was assessed through 10,000 permutations, in which CNV neuron labels were randomly shuffled within each stratum defined by sample and cell type, preserving the underlying data structure. P values were calculated as the proportion of permuted differences that exceeded the observed difference in absolute value and were adjusted for multiple testing using Bonferroni correction. To avoid confounding by cell-type-specific gene expression differences, this analysis was performed separately for each major cell type (excitatory neurons, inhibitory neurons, progenitors and other cell types) and developmental stage (prenatal and postnatal).

To identify distinct subpopulations of CNV neurons based on their pathway activation patterns, we performed hierarchical clustering on the normalized gene module scores from a selected set of pathways. As clustering on all available pathways could introduce noise from non-informative or weakly differential pathways, we focused on the top 10 most differentially activated gene modules from Gene Ontology Cellular Component and cell elimination C2 pathways, ranked by the absolute observed difference (see the previous section for definition of ‘absolute observed difference’ and the selection of cell elimination C2 pathways). The module score matrix was normalized by subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation across cells. Hierarchical clustering was performed using average linkage with the cosine distance metric. We determined the optimal cluster number (n = 2) through visual inspection of the dendrogram and cluster stability.

To visualize pathway activation patterns, we generated heatmaps using the seaborn clustermap function with row clustering enabled for gene sets and column clustering for cells. The resulting visualization showed two main clusters of cells with distinct pathway activation patterns. Cell cluster assignments were validated through multiple approaches, including assessment of cluster sizes (cluster 1: 1,667 cells; cluster 2: 2,487 cells) and evaluation of enrichment for CNV neurons using Fisher’s exact test. The association between cluster membership, and CNV neuron status was quantified using odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals. Multiple testing correction was performed using the Bonferroni procedure.
