There's little doubt that elephants and many other animals experience grief. Elephants often are the "poster animals" for their profound response to the death of other elephants, something I observed after the death of an elephant matriarch in the Samburu National Reserve along with elephant expert Dr. Iain Douglas-Hamilton. I could literally feel their grief and the depth of their loss. Psychologists Drs. Dustin Kieschnick and Katie Lawlor's research on this important topic speaks to many current questions of interest about this important and hot topic.

Marc Bekoff: Why did you decide to study grief in elephants?

Dustin Kieschnick and Katie Lawlor: Our interest in elephant grief grew from our work as psychologists and our longstanding curiosity about the emotional lives of animals. Much of our professional work has centered on grief, attachment, and the ways people navigate loss, including the loss of beloved companion animals. Through this work, we've come to appreciate that grief is, in many ways, a reflection of love and attachment. It speaks to the significance of relationships.

That led us to ask: If grief emerges from attachment, memory, and the disruption of social bonds, why assume it is exclusively human?

Elephants are especially compelling because of their complex, multigenerational societies, enduring relationships, and remarkable communication. Our interest deepened through our connection with Dr. Caitlin O'Connell-Rodwell and a visit to Mushara, a research waterhole in Namibia's Etosha National Park. Witnessing elephants interact in their natural environment was profoundly moving.

Our work draws on decades of field research to explore what elephant responses to death might reveal about attachment, memory, and loss. That said, scientific humility is essential: each species must be understood within its own biological, social, and ecological context.

MB: Who do you hope to reach with your research?

DK/KL: People who have never considered that wildlife might respond to loss in ways resembling grief. Many of us readily recognize the attachments of dogs and cats, yet don't always extend that understanding to wild animals.

We also hope to reach psychologists, educators, conservationists, and policymakers. For conservation, protecting elephants means more than maintaining population numbers. It also means considering family structures, relationships, and knowledge passed between generations.

Most importantly, we hope readers will see animals through a more compassionate and thoughtful lens. The more we understand their social lives, the harder it becomes to regard them solely as populations to manage. Greater understanding can inspire not only empathy, but also a deeper sense of responsibility for how we coexist with other species.

MB: What are some of your major findings and major messages?

DK/KL: One striking finding is elephants' sustained interest in the dead. Researchers have observed them touching bodies with their trunks, investigating bones, remaining near remains, and sometimes revisiting death sites. These behaviors suggest that death has social and behavioral significance beyond the immediate moment.

Equally important is the complexity of elephant relationships. Families depend on communication, memory, and intergenerational learning. The death of a matriarch can mean not only the loss of an individual, but also the loss of ecological knowledge that helps others survive.

Elephant brain structures associated with memory and social-emotional processing offer relevant biological context, although anatomy alone cannot establish what an elephant feels. And elephants are not alone: researchers have observed potentially grief-related responses to death in chimpanzees, orcas, and other species. Such observations are studied within comparative thanatology, the interdisciplinary study of how animals respond to death.

Our central message is that an elephant's death can affect an entire social world: relationships may be disrupted, knowledge lost, and surviving animals profoundly affected.

MB: How does your work differ from others concerned with these topics?

DK/KL: We see our contribution as complementary to the work of field biologists, ethologists, and neuroscientists. Their observations provide the essential evidence. We bring those findings into conversation with psychology, grief research, and animal ethics.

In particular, we ask what our understanding of attachment and bereavement might help us recognize in other species, while remaining cautious about direct comparisons. We also ask what ethical responsibilities follow when research shows that animals maintain lasting relationships and experience social disruption following death.

What should that mean for conservation decisions, human-wildlife conflict, and the care of elephants in captivity? We hope to honor the contributions of field researchers by making these questions accessible and relevant to how we understand and treat other animals.

MB: Are you hopeful that, as people learn about grief in elephants and other animals, they will use this information on behalf of the animals?

DK/KL: Absolutely. How we understand animals influences how we treat them.

Conservation has rightly emphasized habitat protection and population survival. But with highly social species, numbers tell only part of the story. An elephant killed by poachers may also be a mother, a companion, or a matriarch carrying decades of ecological knowledge. Her death may have consequences throughout the group. Likewise, caring for elephants in captivity requires attention not only to physical health, but also to social relationships and opportunities for natural behavior.

We hope this research encourages greater humility, curiosity, and compassion. Rather than judging animals by how closely they resemble humans, we can ask how they experience their own worlds.

Recognizing the possibility of grief in elephants does not require us to say their experience is identical to ours. It asks us to take their relationships seriously. If our work helps even a few people see an elephant as an individual with relationships, memories, and a life that matters, then it has accomplished something meaningful. Ultimately, we hope understanding animals' emotional lives will inspire us to treat them with the dignity and respect they deserve.
