Best Wireless Chargers (2026): My Picks After Testing 100+

Wireless charging has been around for more than a decade, yet many people still plug in the old-fashioned way. But trust me: Once you free yourself from fishing for and fumbling with cables in the dark, you’ll never want to go back. Dropping your phone on a pad or stand at the end of the day and waking up to a fully charged battery in the morning is easy and convenient. Yes, it’s slower than wired charging—but at night that hardly matters anyways.

After testing more than 100 wireless chargers over the past decade, sorting the good from the bad (there are certainly bad ones), I have identified the best wireless chargers. You will find many pads and stands in different styles, shapes, and build materials with spots for other devices, alongside your phone.

Not all phones support wireless charging, but most brands have models that do, so look up your phone model first. You’ll usually see “Qi wireless charging” (the default standard) or simply “wireless charging” if it does on the product page or specs list.

Phones that support the latest Qi2 standard bear the Qi2 logo and have a ring of magnets for easy alignment (just like Apple’s MagSafe). Qi2 is backward compatible, so Qi phones can charge on Qi2 chargers, though they may charge more slowly. Sadly, this does not always apply the other way round, as we discovered with the HMD Skyline (the first Qi2 Android phone), which cannot be charged with all Qi chargers.

Some phones are also Qi2 Ready, like Samsung’s Galaxy S25 series. This means they have the necessary internal components to enable Qi2 functionality—faster charging and more efficient energy transfer—but they don’t have built-in magnets. To enable full Qi2 magnetic wireless charging, you need to pair the phone with a Qi2 Ready case with magnets inside. The latest update, Qi2 25W, allows for a faster maximum charging rate of 25 watts, up from 15 watts. Supported phones include the iPhone 17 range and Google’s Pixel 10 Pro XL.

Do Wireless Chargers Work With Phone Cases?

Yes, most wireless chargers can charge through cases, unless the case is particularly thick. Check the product listing—there’s usually a case thickness limit in millimeters. Phones can get hot when charging wirelessly, so don’t worry if your phone is really warm when you pick it up. Most smartphones have limits that stop charging if they get too hot.

If you have a MagSafe charger or a Qi2 charger with magnets, you can use a magnetic case even if your phone doesn’t support those standards. You may not be able to take advantage of all the features, but it should deliver basic wireless charging that’s more precise thanks to the magnets.

Some manufacturers like Apple make wireless chargers that recharge their respective phones faster than others, but if you want the fastest phone charger, you're better off sticking with a cord. Wireless charging works best on desks or nightstands when you're not using your phone or in a rush to recharge it. Check out our Best USB-C Cables guide if you need one, though the cable that comes with your phone should be sufficient.

Most MagSafe and Qi2 chargers can go up to 15 watts, and a handful of proprietary wireless chargers for specific phone models can charge even faster. The latest Qi2 25W standard allows for charging up to 25 watts. It's worth noting that, even where phones support them, the maximum charging rates are only reached some of the time (the charging rate is automatically adjusted to preserve battery health). Many older iPhones are capped at 7.5 watts for wireless charging and many older Android phones are capped at 10 watts. We have included the maximum wireless charging rate in the specs table for each entry. Remember that the charger also requires the correct cable and power adapter to charge at its maximum rate.

There’s no definitive evidence that it’s harmful. You may worry the phone’s battery might degrade faster with wireless charging, but manufacturers set safe limits for phone batteries, stipulating how much a battery can be charged and how far it can discharge. Regardless of the charging source you use, whether you plug into a wall adapter or use a wireless charging pad, these limits can’t be overridden. You won’t overcharge your phone by leaving it on a wireless charger all night.

How Do I Keep My Phone’s Battery Healthy?

Try to keep your battery between 50 and 80 percent for optimum battery health. Keeping your phone fully charged or fully discharged will degrade it slightly faster, and regularly swinging between full and empty will shorten its life. Battery technology has improved in recent years, and phone batteries are more reliable than ever. If you switch phones every two to three years or don’t mind paying a modest fee for a battery replacement on that timescale, it’s not worth worrying too much about how often or when you charge your phone.

What Is the Downside of Wireless Charging?

The main downside is that wireless charging is slower than wired charging. Wireless charging is less efficient because some of the power is lost as heat. Original Qi chargers are only around 60 to 75 percent efficient. Qi2 chargers manage 75 to 80 percent efficiency, but wired charging is typically 85 to 95 percent efficient.

I test every wireless charger for at least a week, using it to charge both my Pixel 11 Pro XL and iPhone 16. I also test with an older iPhone 12 and Pixel 9. I live with most chargers on my nightstand or work desk. When possible, I check the charging rate and note how long it takes to charge my phone. I assess the design’s usability and the magnets' strength, test any additional charging ports or pads, and try out any special features.
