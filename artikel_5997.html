Riyadh’s fourth quarter runs on international corporate and event demand that flies in, and the missile strikes hit that badly. Domestic demand can’t fill the gap, so hotels will trade rate for occupancy in the quarter that in normal times supports their strongest pricing.

Riyadh’s most important hotel season is turning into one of its weakest.

The year’s final quarter is normally the capital’s strongest, built on corporate travel, conferences, exhibitions and entertainment. This year, Iran-backed Houthi strikes on King Khalid International Airport in October cut off inbound flights just as peak season began. A run of attacks since then, the latest on Nov. 24 in King Abdullah Financial District, has kept foreign airlines, event organizers and international visitors away.

The repeated strikes on the financial district point to a Houthi effort to impose economic costs beyond disrupting oil production and exports, said Ameneh Mehvar, Middle East senior analyst at nonprofit ACLED (Armed Conflict Location and Event Data).

Riyadh's strongest hotel quarter has collapsed. Houthi strikes on King Khalid International Airport in October killed at least 17 people and forced the cancellation of 86% of scheduled flights between Oct. 8 and 25, according to Cirium, and a run of attacks since then — the latest on Nov. 24 in the King Abdullah Financial District — has kept foreign airlines, event organizers, and international visitors away. Riyadh hotel occupancy fell 34.7 percentage points year over year in October to 34.3%, with average daily rate down 29.9% to $190 and RevPAR down 65.1% to $65, according to CoStar's STR. Dammam and Jeddah largely held rate but lost roughly a quarter of their RevPAR. No major Western or Asian carrier plans to resume Saudi flights before the new year: Air France is out until Jan. 4, British Airways until Jan. 11, Cathay Pacific until Jan. 31, with Delta's Atlanta-Riyadh launch pushed to March and Singapore Airlines to Mar. 28. Ten major events have been postponed, canceled, or moved since October, including the Future Investment Initiative, which has been pushed a full year to October 2027. Finance minister Mohammed al-Jadaan said the economy will shrink 2.1% this year, that inbound tourism is down 7% since the start of the year, partially offset by domestic growth, and that Saudi Arabia is prepared for a prolonged conflict with the Houthis.
