Hakeem Jeffries wins overwhelming Speaker-designate vote as progressives' revolt falls flat

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

House Democrats nominated Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries, D-N.Y., for speaker after he ran unopposed. 11 Democrats voted against his nomination, including two from his home state of New York, Rep.-elects Claire Valdez and Darializa Avila Chevalier.

Democrats are fretting that the "Bloc" of progressives will lug House Democrats to the left.

Yours truly wanted to understand just how much sway progressives might have over the House Democratic Caucus. Look no further than Wednesday morning’s vote for speaker.

No one challenged House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries (D-N.Y.) to become speaker-designate in the internal caucus vote.

House Democratic Leader Hakeem Jeffries, House Minority Whip Katherine Clark and Democratic candidate Suzan DelBene celebrate at a Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee midterm election night watch party in Washington, D.C., on Nov. 3, 2026. (REUTERS)

Jeffries marshaled 251 of the 262 secret ballots cast. The recorded vote revealed that despite all of their noise and clamor, extreme progressives really don’t have much juice compared to the rest of the Democratic Caucus. In fact, rank-and-file Democrats crushed their progressive colleagues.

Democrats did not vote by name. But numbers say a lot. While the recorded vote could have revealed potential craters of dissent among Democrats and questions about Jeffries, the tally registered the opposite. It showed just how diminutive the hard left is in the Democratic Caucus.

Do they exude an outsized political voice? Yes. But success in Congress is measured in numbers. And at least on Wednesday, the numbers signaled few results for the Bloc and a show of unity among Democrats.

"With the majority as big as it is, it's going to be very hard for anybody to try and hold our group of hostage," forecast Rep. Tom Suozzi (D-N.Y.).

"We’re going to work together to find the highest common denominator in order to make life better for the American people," said Jeffries.

With more than 260 likely members in the Democratic Caucus, the Bloc wasn’t a factor on the first vote which mattered.

In January, Jeffries becomes the 57th person and the first Black speaker to grip the gavel. Members of the Congressional Black Caucus celebrated the ascendency of their colleague.

"I've been thinking about (the former Rep.) John Lewis (D-Ga.) a lot this week," said former House Majority Whip and Rep. Jim Clyburn (D-S.C.), invoking the civil rights figure. "This is a very surreal moment for me."

Clyburn spoke on behalf of Jeffries’ candidacy in the caucus, declaring that the path for the New York Democrat to the speakership makes him "feel vindicated."

"As a Black woman from the South, having the next speaker of the House to be a Black man means a lot to me," said Rep. Nikema Williams (D-Ga.).

Rep. Jonathan Jackson (D-Ill.) is the son of late Rev. Jesse Jackson. I asked what Jackson’s father would think of Jeffries rising to the speakership.

"He’d be more than happy to see America making progress. This is what the world looks like. This is what America looks like," said Jackson.

But let’s filet the caucus vote for speaker to understand what unfolded with 11 Democrats who withheld their support from Jeffries.

House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries, D-N.Y., speaks during a news conference on Capitol Hill in Washington. (J. Scott Applewhite/AP)

Representative-elect Don Leonard (D-Ohio) is a populist in a district which President Trump won by 10 points in 2024. Leonard defeated Rep. Mike Carey (R-Ohio). The district Leonard flipped in central Ohio is a long way politically from left-leaning districts in New York City and New Jersey where renegade members of the Bloc refused to cast their ballots for Jeffries.

"What we need is a commitment from the speaker to get a 28th Amendment passed in 2028, to end Citizens United, end the corruption and call a vote on U.S. military aid to Israel," said Leonard. "If he does those things, he's got my vote."

Seven of the progressives published a joint statement, demanding commitments from Jeffries to stop ICE and end "genocide in Gaza."

The seven declared they saw "meaningful progress" in their talks with Jeffries. But since those negotiations continue, "our vote in caucus today was no" against Jeffries.

Despite repeated efforts, none of the seven spoke to Fox.

But what’s remarkable is that two of the seven withholding their support hail from Jeffries’ hometown, New York City. One is Representative-elect Claire Valdez (D-N.Y.) — who will represent a wide swath of Brooklyn and Queens. The other is Representative-elect Darializa Avila Chevalier (D-N.Y.). That district comprises parts of Upper Manhattan and The Bronx.

Your trusty reporter tried to understand what the dissent meant.

"How are their districts different than yours, being next door?" I asked Jeffries.

"The numbers speak for themselves," said Jeffries.

"But those districts — are they that different from what you represent? And yet they weren't willing to vote for you, Mr. speaker-designate?"

The lack of support for Jeffries from the dissidents annoyed Rep. Marilyn Strickland (D-Wash.).

"This idea that Hakeem Jeffries has not earned this is completely absurd. He has worked hard. He's done everything he needed to do," said Strickland.

Rep. Nydia Velázquez (D-N.Y.) is retiring at the end of this Congress. She opposed Valdez as her successor. She was bewildered that Valdez and Avila Chevalier wouldn’t support their hometown, Big Apple Congressman for speaker.

"My advice to them is take the time to know how this institution runs and what it takes to be successful here," said an agitated Velázquez.

Rep. Joe Morelle (D-N.Y.) served with Jeffries in the state legislature. He argued that Valdez and Avila Chevalier made a mistake.

"They’re going to have to answer to their constituents," said Morelle. "I think they should have been with Hakeem Jeffries who’s led us to this historic majority."

And if you aren’t on the team in politics, the team also isn’t on you.

"It is a small group that marginalized themselves," said Rep. Henry Cuellar (D-Texas) about the holdouts.

However, Representative-elect Dan Koh (D-Mass.) saw it differently.

"They all got elected with very different constituencies. A lot of them have different priorities," said Koh. "There are things that their constituencies have demanded. And they want to make sure have the audience of the leader."

Rep. Greg Landsman (D-Ohio) believed there was strategy in the dissent.

"They probably chose to do this because it's a way of lifting up their issues," said Landsman.

And keep in mind, this wasn’t the actual vote for speaker. That comes on the floor in January. It’s a win-win for Jeffries and the holdouts if they forge an accord. He promises them certain votes. And they can then tout what they extracted from the next speaker.

But let’s put the 11 defections into context.

Democrats flipped the House in 2018. In the internal caucus election, former House Speaker Nancy Pelosi (D-Calif.) lost 32 votes. In the vote for speaker in January 2019, 12 Democrats voted for someone else. Three members voted present.

"Nancy Pelosi will be remembered," said Rep. Jared Moskowitz (D-Fla.). "And the 30 people who voted against her will not be remembered."

Jeffries will go down in the history books as the first Black speaker. The holdouts? They’re freshmen. As Moskowitz suggests, we’ll see if anyone recalls them in a few years.
