A trapped drop of water might be the secret to how brain diseases spread from cell to cell

Tanggal: 2026-11-15

Scientists found that a tiny pocket of water acts as a hotspot, causing healthy brain proteins to misfold.

Water fills gaps in the brain between molecules, playing an important role by keeping them wet. Yet in the tau protein tangles of Alzheimer’s disease, water marks the exact spot where one damaged tau grabs the next. Letting go of that water is what pulls the two tau molecules together.

Researchers at Northwestern University and the University of California, Santa Barbara (UCSB) found this in a short, lab-made piece of tau, not in a brain.

In a test tube, the fragment latched onto a growing tau fiber at one spot first. A chemical cap placed on that one spot left the fibers barely growing.

“We might have found a shortcut for the pharmacist or biochemist because instead of searching for everything, we can say, ‘Look for that hotspot. That’s the spot that matters,'” said Songi Han, the study’s senior author, who runs a chemistry lab at Northwestern.

Healthy tau is a floppy protein with no fixed shape that helps keep the tiny support tubes inside a neuron stable. In Alzheimer’s disease and related conditions, tau folds wrongly and stacks into long fibers.

Those fibers pile up inside neurons as the tau tangles that a brain scan may spot before symptoms appear, and a blood test may flag years earlier.

When a neuron dies, the fibers spill out. A fiber’s loose end can then grab healthy tau nearby and bend it into the same wrong shape. That is how the damage spreads from cell to cell, though some neurons resist it.

Copying a shape that precisely was the puzzle. A floppy molecule has no obvious way to land in the same alignment thousands of times over.

Full-length tau is too unruly to watch, piece by piece, so Han’s team used a slice of it. The slice is 19 amino acids – the building blocks of a protein – long, and it comes from the region where tau fibers form their core.

One block, at position 301, is changed in a way known to speed up clumping, and in earlier work fibers made from the slice recruited full-length tau.

The team mixed fresh fragments with fibers made in advance. Clumping began the moment the two met and leveled off after about two hours. Fragments with no fibers to join didn’t clump at all in the same period.

To see which part of the fragments grabbed the fiber first, the team tagged seven of its 19 positions with heavy forms of carbon and nitrogen.

A magnetic resonance machine, a cousin of a hospital MRI scanner, then took a picture of those seven positions about every 15 minutes for 12 hours. A free position shows up in the picture, and one locked into a fiber goes dark.

Position 300 went dark first, and fastest. Positions 301 and 306, its neighbors at the bottom of the fragment’s U-shaped fold, followed. The three positions at the open ends of the U, 308, 309 and 313, lagged well behind.

So the fragment doesn’t fall onto the fiber all at once. It attaches at one spot, and the rest of the chain folds into place afterward.

The team got the same answer with a tiny magnetic tag attached to position 300, 306 or 314 on separate batches of the fragment. As each batch joined fibers, the tag at position 300 slowed down soonest.

Parts of a protein’s surface mix with water easily. Other patches repel it, the way oil does. The water next to those patches arranges itself into a loose, ice-like cap that still keeps moving.

In the team’s computer simulations of a fiber’s end, position 300 showed the largest change in the water around it between the free fragment and the tau fiber.

A second measurement reads how fast water moves next to a magnetic tag. It showed the most slow-moving, ordered water at position 300 as well, with less at positions 306 and 314.

“You have a certain site, and water cannot quite coat it because it doesn’t like water. But that part still needs to be protected, because if you don’t, then the protein crashes out and is no longer available,” Han explained.

“What water tends to do is build a stabilizing cap around it and basically these water caps attract each other.”

When two capped spots meet, the ordered water is set free. Freeing that water releases energy, and that energy is what holds the two tau molecules together, lined up the same way every time.

If position 300 is where joining begins, blocking it should hurt the fiber most.

The team’s magnetic tags doubled as caps, because a tagged fragment that joins a fiber leaves its tag on the fiber’s new end.

After 12 hours, fibers grown from fragments tagged at position 300 looked almost like the starting fibers, in both length and number, under an electron microscope. Fibers tagged at positions 306 or 314 had stretched out well beyond them.

A tag at position 300 still let the next fragment dock, but it stalled the locking step that extends the fiber.

Everything here happened in a tube, to a 19-piece fragment carrying one mutation. Full-length tau in a living neuron can carry chemical add-ons, including phosphate groups, that the fragment lacked.

The team expects the first-contact spot to move as those add-ons and other mutations change the protein’s surface. The point, they wrote, is not position 300 itself but that a growing tau fiber has a dominant first-contact spot at all.

Whether the matching spot on full-length tau can be blocked inside a living neuron, and whether that slows the spread of tangles, are open questions.

The team hopes others can now design molecules that cover the hotspot and stop fibers from growing.

Han also said a disease has to be recreated before it can be cured, and lab-made tau fibers that copy each disease are still hard to make.

The full study was published in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.
