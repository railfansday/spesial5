Why do some images stick in your memory while others fade?

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Recordings inside the amygdala show which pictures a person will remember, flagged within a third of a second of first seeing them.

You can look at two pictures for the same length of time and, minutes later, recognize only one. In a new experiment, that difference showed up in the brain about a third of a second after each picture appeared.

Some cells deep in the brain fired more strongly for pictures people later recognized. Those cells sit in the amygdala, a structure better known for fear.

Jiayang Xiao and Ueli Rutishauser of Cedars-Sinai in Los Angeles led the work.

They collaborated with colleagues at Johns Hopkins in Baltimore, Toronto Western Hospital, and the University of Toronto.

Everyone in the study had drug-resistant epilepsy and was already wired with electrodes, so surgeons could find where the seizures start. The memory test was a run of photographs on a screen, not anything from daily life.

“Our results demonstrate that the activity of specific kinds of neurons in the amygdala predicts, while the person sees an image for the first time, whether that image will later be remembered,” said Rutishauser, who directs the Center for Neural Science and Medicine at Cedars-Sinai.

“Such episodic autobiographical memories, such as where we parked our car or how we spent a recent family vacation, are critical to navigating everyday life and a sense of who we are.”

The amygdala’s reputation comes from fear and feeling. People with damage on both sides of it remember emotional material poorly.

Brain scans going back to the 1990s found that the harder the amygdala worked while someone studied an emotional picture, the better that person held onto it.

So the working explanation was feeling. A strong reaction marks a moment as worth keeping, and the amygdala supplies the reaction.

Xiao’s team asked something narrower. Instead of the amygdala’s overall activity, they tracked single neurons that each answer to one kind of thing.

One cell fires for faces and ignores cars. Another fires for landscapes and ignores food. These are called category cells, and 40% of the amygdala neurons here were one.

The two explanations predict different things. If feeling is the mark, a jolt should lift every cell at once. If the content of the moment is the mark, only the cells tuned to that content should move.

The electrodes used for seizure monitoring carry eight hair-thin wires at the tip. That is fine enough to hear one neuron at a time.

Across 49 sessions with 42 patients, the team logged 1,871 well-isolated neurons. Of those, 575 were in the amygdala and 405 in the hippocampus, the region usually given credit for building memories.

The task was plain. Photographs from five categories – people, food, animals, cars and landscapes – came up for two seconds each, and all of them were new to the patient.

Ten to 30 minutes later came a test of 400 images. Half had been in the earlier run and half had never been seen.

Six buttons were available for each one. Three said new and three said old, and the button within each set said how sure the patient was. Patients got 70.3% right, against 50% for guessing.

When patients first saw the pictures, category cells in the amygdala fired more strongly for images the patients later recognized than for those they didn’t.

The effect held only when the picture belonged to the cell’s own category.

A cell tuned to faces fired more strongly for faces the patient later recognized. When the patient saw a car, that same cell’s activity gave no clue about whether the patient would recognize it later.

The difference in firing appeared about 350 milliseconds after the picture came into view and lasted until about 1,850 milliseconds.

The cells started responding to their preferred category at around 300 milliseconds, just before the first signs of which pictures patients would remember.

The effect held after researchers accounted for an online panel’s ratings of how pleasant and exciting the images were.

The ratings alone did not predict which pictures patients later recognized.

The first dataset also showed a weaker hippocampal signal that emerged around 1,300 milliseconds. In sessions that recorded both regions, the amygdala’s signal was stronger.

Rutishauser told Earth.com that the amygdala finding surprised him because the region is best known for responding to emotionally charged experiences. Here, its activity predicted which neutral images patients would later recognize.

The fusiform gyrus is a patch of visual cortex where two-thirds of the neurons were category cells, the highest share anywhere in the study.

It showed no such split between the pictures that were remembered and the ones that were not. Strong tuning by itself does not do this.

For the kind of memory tested here, Rutishauser said, the memory itself is stored in the hippocampus, and the amygdala works more like a controller.

“The system that produces the hot air (the heater) is separate and distinct from the controller (the thermostat),” he noted to Earth.com. “In analogy, here the amygdala would be the thermostat that controls the memory system.”

The two regions were also in step. Slow electrical waves run through the hippocampus at around 10 cycles a second.

Amygdala cells fired more tightly in time with those waves when a picture was going to be remembered than when it was going to be forgotten.

A second set of recordings, from 95 other patients and 5,744 neurons, carried the amygdala result again. The hippocampal effect did not come back in that one.

Among the pictures patients did recognize, the ones they felt sure about had drawn more firing at first sight than the ones they guessed at.

The team then split each patient’s hits into two kinds of remembering. There is the vivid sort, where a person gets the moment itself back, and the vague sort, where a picture only feels familiar.

The amygdala signal tracked the vivid kind, with a session-by-session correlation of 0.50. It showed no reliable link to familiarity.

The effect was also stronger in the right amygdala than in the left. The authors call that part provisional and say it needs replication.

It may follow from their choice of material, since the right side of the brain is tied to remembering pictures and the left to remembering words.

Nothing here was switched on or off. Every number is a prediction read off a recording, so what the amygdala does for a memory, as opposed to what it says about one, is still open.

The hippocampal effect that showed up in one dataset and not the other is unsettled too.

The patients were also people with epilepsy severe enough to need brain surgery, and the electrodes went where their care required.

Amygdala tissue shrinks early in Alzheimer’s disease, and memory trouble is common in temporal lobe epilepsy.

Rutishauser said the findings are still too preliminary to guide treatment for people with memory problems.

However, he also noted that the research raises the possibility that a device designed to aid memory could target the amygdala, particularly on the right side of the brain.

One possibility is that deep brain stimulation could boost the activity of cells already responding to what a person sees.

The idea is untested. Earlier teams that stimulated the whole amygdala electrically improved recognition of neutral pictures, but none aimed at the category cells.

Rutishauser’s team is now studying how the amygdala coordinates with other brain regions and whether stimulating it can improve memory.

The full study was published in the journal Nature Neuroscience.
