Scientists reach record-low temperature aboard China’s space station

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Physicists on China's Tiangong station cooled 20,000 atoms to the coldest temperature in space: 9.7 trillionths of a degree above zero.

For 30 years, physicists have pushed clouds of atoms toward ever lower temperatures, with Earth’s gravity limiting how far they could go.

Now, a speck of gas aboard China’s Tiangong space station has reached what its creators report is a record-low temperature.

There, physicists chilled about 20,000 rubidium atoms to an equivalent temperature of 9.7 trillionths of a degree above absolute zero, which is minus 459.67°F (minus 273.15°C).

The previous record for a cloud like this was 38 trillionths, set in a drop tower on Earth.

The temperature here is a measure of how fast the cloud spreads once released, not a reading from a thermometer. The authors wrote that the result is the lowest they know of.

Xiaoji Zhou and Xuzong Chen of Peking University led the work with Weibiao Chen of the Shanghai Institute of Optics and Fine Mechanics and colleagues at Fudan University.

Their apparatus has been running on the station since October 2022. When they released the atoms, they could still see the cloud seven seconds later.

The usual way to make atoms this cold is to hold them in a trap and let the hottest ones escape, the way steam leaving a cup cools the coffee.

The looser the trap, the colder the atoms that remain. On Earth, though, a trap that is too loose simply drops its atoms. Gravity sets a floor.

That is why laboratories on the ground have stalled around 350 trillionths of a degree in three dimensions.

Drop towers and sounding rockets buy a few seconds of free fall.

NASA’s Cold Atom Laboratory on the International Space Station, which holds atoms with magnetic fields, has reached 52 trillionths and watched clouds for about a second.

The Chinese team used light instead of magnets. Two crossed laser beams make a trap that can hold atoms whatever their magnetic state, which opens up experiments a magnetic trap can’t run.

No one had made a quantum gas this way in orbit before.

The experiment lives in the Cold Atom Physics Rack aboard the space station‘s Mengtian module.

About two million rubidium atoms were loaded into the first trap and the laser power was eased down over about 20 seconds.

As the trap weakened, the gas formed a Bose-Einstein condensate, a state in which many atoms share a single quantum state and behave collectively.

Weightlessness turned out to have a cost. On Earth, gravity tilts the trap and helps the hottest atoms spill out.

Without that tilt, evaporation in orbit was slower and less complete, and only about 65% of the atoms joined the condensate, against 89% in an identical setup on the ground.

The team’s fix was a stand-in for gravity. A magnetic field gradient pulled on the atoms whose spins made them magnetic, speeding their escape and pushing the rest into a single, magnetically neutral state.

The fraction in the condensate climbed to 89%, about 23,000 atoms.

Even a condensate isn’t finished cooling. The atoms still push on one another, and when the trap opens that pressure becomes motion.

So the team handed the atoms to a second, wider pair of beams, let the trap relax for just over a second, and then switched it off.

Released, the cloud drifted outward at about 70 to 88 micrometers a second, a few thousandths of an inch per second.

Translated into temperature, that is 9.7 trillionths of a degree, with an uncertainty of roughly three trillionths either way. The atoms were, for practical purposes, barely moving.

The team’s computer simulation of the same steps predicted 7.4 trillionths of a degree, and the authors attributed the difference to jitter in the laser beams.

A cloud that expands this slowly could improve atom interferometers, whose sensitivity can increase with the square of the atoms’ time in free flight.

Seven seconds of free fall in orbit is a very long time by that standard.

The temperature estimate rests on the first two seconds of expansion. Although the cloud remained visible afterward, the later images were too faint to use in the calculation.

The condensate is also small. The authors wrote that a trap whose volume can be tuned should let them hold more atoms.

Their stated goals are to study exotic quantum phases that only appear at these temperatures, and to build an atom interferometer in orbit.

That is a device that could test whether all objects really fall at the same rate.

Both goals would benefit from more atoms and longer free flight. The experiment marks a step toward creating those conditions in orbit.

The full study was published in the journal Science.
