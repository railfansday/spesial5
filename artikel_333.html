Robo-soldier startup backed by Eric Trump misses payroll, may collapse

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Robotics startup misses payroll a year after CEO used funds for luxury home.

A humanoid robotics company backed and advised by President Donald Trump’s son, Eric Trump, has reportedly missed payroll and may be on the brink of collapse after a funding shortfall. The Silicon Valley startup, Foundation Future Industries, claims it is developing and testing robots for military roles on battlefields from Ukraine to Gaza.

Foundation failed to pay employees for the payroll period ending on October 30, anonymous sources told CNBC. The startup’s staff is especially angry because Foundation’s co-founder and CEO, Sankaet Pathak, supposedly borrowed $4.6 million in company funds to buy a luxury home overlooking San Francisco Bay about a year ago.

This comes as Pathak and Foundation’s leadership are still looking to raise funding to support a $3 billion valuation, based on their promise to develop a humanoid robot ready for combat duty and capable of wielding various weapons.

The company has demonstrated a humanoid robot named the Phantom MK-1 and is touting an upcoming robot called the Phantom MK-2. Foundation has touted its humanoid robots for roles in warehouses and other workplaces—but has courted media coverage by claiming it is developing humanoid robots for battlefield use.

Now CNBC’s reporting based on “people with direct knowledge of the matter” indicates that insiders worry the startup could soon collapse without additional funding.

Eric Trump holds an equity stake in Foundation and was announced as its chief strategy advisor in March 2026, according to the Financial Times. Investment funds linked to both Eric Trump and Donald Trump Jr. are involved with multiple military technology companies that have received Pentagon loans and contracts worth more than $3.2 billion as of July 2026, according to The Washington Post.

After Trump claimed that Foundation had “just secured a $24 million contract with the Pentagon” during the Fox Business interview in April, Sen. Elizabeth Warren (D-Mass.) responded by questioning whether the Pentagon is “just a cash machine for Trump’s kids now” in a social media post. “This looks like corruption in plain sight,” Warren wrote.

But publicly available evidence does not back up Trump’s claim about Foundation directly receiving US government funding. Federal spending records show that Foundation holds no publicly listed contracts, according to The San Francisco Standard.

Instead, the company Boardwalk Robotics received four contracts worth $3.22 million prior to being acquired by Foundation in late 2024.

Meanwhile, the Institute for Human & Machine Cognition—a Florida-based nonprofit research institute and research partner to Boardwalk Robotics—secured a $24.8 million Navy contract in 2025, The San Francisco Standard reported.

This was not the first time that Foundation’s leadership and investors have made unsupported statements. In 2024, General Motors pushed back on Foundation’s claims that it was about to get an investment from the Detroit automaker, according to CNBC.

On November 11, 2026, Mike LeBlanc, co-founder and president of Foundation Future Industries, told the Financial Times that the company was in talks with the Israeli military to provide humanoid robots for patrol and combat duties in Hamas tunnels beneath Gaza. He also described taking the company’s Phantom MK1 humanoid robot along to tour some of the tunnels.

However, the Israeli defense ministry declined to comment when the Financial Times reached out.

LeBlanc also previously claimed that two of the company’s Phantom MK-1 units went to Ukraine for testing in frontline reconnaissance and logistics roles in February 2026.

But Craig Smith, an independent journalist and host of the podcast Eye on AI, described tagging along during the delivery of the Phantom robots to Ukraine. He said that the robots never made it to Ukraine because they were stopped at the border with Poland.

When questioned by Smith, LeBlanc only said that Foundation had conducted a pilot demonstration at an undisclosed location, without clarifying whether the robots ever reached Ukraine. LeBlanc also walked back a claim that Foundation had demonstrated its humanoid robots to the Ukrainian government organization Brave1.

Promotional videos showing Foundation’s humanoids have not provided evidence that the robots are ready for battlefield deployments. For example, a “mortar demo” video on the company website dated to May 2026 shows a person handing a mortar shell to one of the humanoid robots, which slowly lowers the shell over the muzzle of a mortar weapon before finally dropping it in.

Whatever happens to Foundation, humanoid robots remain largely unproven technology and have yet to fulfill company promises that they can become general-purpose robots handling many different tasks. Companies have just begun testing and deploying such robots in greater numbers within relatively controlled environments such as factories and warehouses.

Similarly, humanoid robots have not yet had any impact on the battlefield. Instead, pioneers in robotics warfare, like the Ukrainian military, have focused on developing and deploying unmanned ground vehicles (UGVs)—robotic vehicles that roll along on wheels or tank-style caterpillar tracks.

While defending against the Russian invasion of Ukraine, the Ukrainian military has increasingly deployed more ground robots along with swarms of flying drones to make up for a shortfall in human soldiers compared to Russia. Such ground robots have typically been tasked with performing risky supply runs or evacuating wounded soldiers from frontline positions, but have also entered combat while wielding projectile weapons or even explosives when equipped as roving bombs.

Most recently, Ukraine has deployed armed ground robots directly into combat through both amphibious landings and through airdrops. In 2026, part of Ukraine’s counteroffensive success in rolling back Russian territorial gains has included dropping military robots from drones in airborne assaults behind Russian frontline positions.
