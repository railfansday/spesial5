Why homebuilders need business plans that test scenarios

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Scott Cox explains why forcing long-range plans to match annual budgets shifts focus to deadlines over analysis. He recommends a 1-page output and a scenario-ready model leaders can adjust without analyst help.  AI Summary

[In the fourth of seven excerpts from ‘Less Wrong, More Right,’ Scott Cox argues argues a 5-year business plan should be a thinking tool, not a control document. He shares two tests: keep key output to one page and use a single assumptions page for scenario changes.]

Sometimes I think the most misunderstood part of our business is the business plan.

The real purpose is to learn about your business and understand the consequences of the choices available to you. If we go in this direction, what does it mean for our capitalization and our risk? How many people will we need, and when? Is our subcontractor base capable of that volume? For this to pay off, how long does the market have to hold? A plan exists to force that thinking. Everything else it does — satisfying the bank, setting the bonus targets, giving the board something to approve — is a byproduct, and the byproducts have a way of eating the main course.

I sat in a business plan meeting for the division of a multi-region builder. A number of organizational things were in flux, the market was slipping, and to be fair to the local team, they were being asked to work on too many initiatives at once. While everyone debated next year’s goals, I was looking at year five. I did a simple return-on-assets calculation on the page in front of me. It was terrible. I asked whether that was really what they expected, because if that was the plan, I could not support doing any new deals at all. The return on capital did not justify the investment.

After what I will politely describe as an agitated discussion, we got down to the why — the size of the deals, the type of deals, the organizational structure, the capital those choices required. And that should have become the start of a worthwhile conversation. Next year’s results were, within limits, going to be what they were going to be. The bigger issues were the ones worth the room’s time.

Then the deadline arrived. Divisional plans had to be consolidated. The banks and others were waiting. Some minor tweaks were made and the plan went out substantially as drafted. I doubt anyone looked past year one in that document ever again.

That is the failure I want to dwell on, because it was not a failure of intelligence or effort. It was a process that answered to form and deadline instead of thought — a date to hit, a box to check — and once those became the real assignment, the thinking we’d just started in that room stopped mattering.

What we should have been able to do, right there, was stop the meeting and start putting different approaches into the model. Bigger deals, fewer of them. Entitled land versus unentitled. Different timing. Then look at what came out — capital required, when it came back, what the returns actually were. We could not, so we did not. The model was capable of producing one future in enormous detail and incapable of producing a second one at all.

Underneath all of this is an instinct I want to focus on, because it is the one doing the damage. People believe everything should foot. The five-year plan should tie to the annual budget, which should tie to the phase budgets, which should tie to the cash flow. It feels like discipline. It is not.

Budgets are control instruments. Plans are thinking instruments. A control instrument has to foot — that is its job. A six-month cash flow projection cannot be materially wrong without serious consequences. An annual budget is an accountability document and people are going to be held to it. A phase budget for a project you are about to start is a commitment on money you are about to spend. All of those demand precision.

A five-year company plan does not. It is for asking what happens if we run this business differently? When you force a thinking instrument to reconcile with a control instrument, the thinking instrument always loses — because the deadline always belongs to the control instrument. That is not a hypothetical. That is exactly what happened in the meeting I just described.

The first is the one-page test, and it is a test of the output. If what you circulate, at least the important parts, does not fit on an 11×17, you have included things that do not matter. That is partly a communication discipline — that is how a lot of people are actually going to read it — but it is mostly a forcing function. Something has to be left off, and deciding what gets left off is the same as deciding what matters.

The second is a test of the design of the model, and it is the one I care about more. Can the CEO change the assumptions herself, in the room, in real time, to test an idea — without the analyst who built the model sitting next to her? Can I take it home, pour a bourbon after dinner, and try three scenarios on my own? If the answer is yes, the model is simple enough. If the answer is no, you do not have a tool. You have a document.

Back-up tabs are fine. Detail underneath is fine. The requirement is that a single, clearly marked assumptions page drives all of it with comprehensible results, and that changing one thing changes everything downstream without anyone opening the hood.

The principle underneath is that granularity should be set by what you need to move, not by what the budget happens to contain.

The 11×17 should just be a bigger, prettier version of the yellow pad. If it is anything more than that, ask yourself what all the rest of it is actually for.
