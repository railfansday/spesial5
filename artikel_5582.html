Secure borders. A stronger economy. Washington must deliver both

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

The editorial calls for immigration enforcement that protects due process and lawful work, plus modernized pathways aligned to construction labor needs. It points to Harvard and NAHB data and urges homebuilding leaders to press for measurable policy outcomes on affordability and productivity.  AI Summary

In the first 100 days of the Trump administration, business leaders at residential construction and development companies were, in effect, summoned to the nation’s capital for an all-hands meeting with administration officials on housing affordability. The summons came on short notice, on a Sunday afternoon. The invitation carried an undertone. Attend or else.

The Washington, D.C., meeting never took place. But the episode captured the breakneck pace and take-no-prisoners approach of those early, flood-the-zone months. It also dialed in a distinctly unsettling tone for the rules of engagement between the new administration and the nation’s private-sector business leaders in one of the economy’s critical engines: homebuilding.

Now, following the 2026 midterm elections, those rules of engagement stand an opportunity to change.

Business leaders, including corporate strategists and their representatives on Capitol Hill, have a window to act quickly, elevate their priorities, and press for policy changes that have damaged or threaten to undermine some of the nation’s most important economic capabilities.

For homebuilding, residential development, and residential investment, immigration enforcement belongs near the top of the agenda.

The United States needs secure borders and enforceable immigration laws. It also needs the workforce capacity to build homes, grow food, manufacture products, and sustain economic growth. These are not competing national interests, and treating them as such has real economic consequences.

The question for President Trump, the incoming Congress, and the business leaders with a stake in the outcome is whether the next two years can achieve a more rational, workable balance among those objectives.

For the housing industry, evidence of what’s at risk is already accumulating.

Over recent months, HousingWire TBD has documented the increasingly disruptive effects of immigration enforcement on residential construction businesses and their trade partners.

Builders and contractors report job site interruptions, workers reluctant to report for work, and crews disappearing from construction schedules. In some affected markets, executives have reported reductions of 60% to 80% in available trade crews. These are accounts from specific businesses and markets, not nationwide estimates.

The disruption also extends beyond workers who lack legal authorization. Reports that U.S. citizens and legally authorized workers are apprehensive about reporting to job sites suggest that enforcement-related fear can diminish workforce availability and productivity.

Residential construction depends on the coordinated sequencing of specialized trades. When a framing crew is unavailable, subsequent work may be delayed, schedules are extended, financing costs accumulate, and closings slip.

For builders and their trade partners operating with already compressed margins and limited capital flexibility, repeated disruptions can pose a serious business risk.

These accounts do not establish that immigration enforcement has caused a nationwide construction crisis. They do, however, raise a question policymakers cannot responsibly ignore: Are enforcement practices achieving legitimate security objectives while imposing avoidable economic costs on lawful workers, responsible employers, and American households?

A study published on October 8 by Harvard University’s Joint Center for Housing Studies found that foreign-born workers accounted for 35% of the nation’s construction trades workforce in 2024, compared with 18% of the overall U.S. labor force.

Their representation is especially high in trades critical to home completion: 57% of drywall and ceiling-tile installers, 53% of roofers and painters, and 51% of flooring installers were foreign-born.

Harvard’s research found that foreign-born workers accounted for all net growth in the construction trades workforce from 2013 to 2024.

Separate NAHB research found that Hispanic workers represented 32.8% of the construction workforce in 2024, up from 23.6% in 2010. In Texas and California, approximately six in 10 construction workers are Hispanic.

Foreign-born status, Hispanic ethnicity, citizenship, and legal employment authorization are distinct characteristics. These statistics cannot be used interchangeably to describe immigration status. Nor do they, nor can they, truly reflect the full impact of foreign-born workers on job site capability and productivity because many undocumented frontline crew members are not counted at all.

The statistics, however, illustrate the industry’s substantial dependence on a diverse workforce, whose lawful participation must be protected.

Meanwhile, labor shortages are worsening despite reduced housing production. NAHB’s September 2026 survey found that, averaged across nine consistently tracked trades, 47% of builders reported labor shortages, up from 42% in April 2025. Framing shortages reached 57%, while finished carpentry shortages reached 58%.

NAHB also identified immigration-enforcement-related worker apprehensions as a contributor to worsening shortages, along with competition from other construction sectors.

Immigration enforcement did not create the industry’s longstanding workforce problems. Those problems reflect decades of insufficient domestic workforce development, shifting demographics, competition for skilled labor, and limited productivity gains. But making an already constrained workforce even less available cannot be a rational part of a national housing affordability strategy.

Washington does not need to start from scratch.

Bipartisan immigration reform initiatives have repeatedly recognized the need to combine effective border security, lawful employment pathways, immigration-system modernization, and solutions for established workers and employers.

NAHB’s comprehensive immigration reform policy, updated in November 2025, provides a practical foundation for the industry. It supports border security and effective enforcement, along with construction-specific visa reforms, legal workforce pathways, and domestic workforce development.

The association has also endorsed the bipartisan DIGNITY Act, which would strengthen border security while establishing conditional pathways to lawful employment for certain undocumented workers who meet defined requirements.

These proposals involve difficult choices and legitimate disagreements. They show that practical approaches exist beyond the familiar binary choice between unrestricted enforcement and abandoning enforcement altogether.

The remaining weeks of the current Congress and the next two years should be evaluated against three governing objectives.

First, the administration must enforce immigration laws while protecting lawful employment and upholding due process. Enforcement priorities, worker-identification procedures, and accountability mechanisms should minimize unnecessary disruption to legitimate businesses and protect citizens and authorized workers from improper detention or interference.

Second, Congress and the administration must develop lawful workforce pathways that reflect documented economic needs. That means considering construction-specific employment programs, modernizing immigration processing, and addressing established workers’ circumstances through clear eligibility, screening, compliance, and worker-protection requirements.

Third, immigration policymaking must elevate economic productivity and affordability as measurable priorities. Congress should assess workforce availability, construction delays, business costs and housing production alongside border-security and enforcement outcomes.

None of these objectives diminishes employers’ obligation to comply with the law, protect workers, invest in apprenticeships and technical education, or to improve productivity through innovation.

America needs stronger domestic workforce development and a workable immigration policy, not one in place of the other.

The industry’s responsibility cannot end with documenting the disruption and asking Washington for relief. Homebuilders, residential developers, investors, building-products manufacturers, trade contractors, and their associations have the evidence, economic standing, and organizational capacity to demand a coherent policy response.

NAHB has already established a reform framework and identified legislative priorities. Industry executives have documented the operational consequences, and research institutions have quantified the workforce’s dependence on immigration.

What, then, are business leaders waiting for?

The next step should be a coordinated, industry-wide agenda presented to the administration and congressional leadership, including specific legislative priorities, credible evidence of business impacts, and commitments to lawful employment, investment in the domestic workforce, and improved productivity.

Not another statement of concern. Not another round of partisan accusations. A sustained effort to translate widely acknowledged economic needs into workable policy.

The new political balance in Washington creates an opening, but divided government does not automatically lead to better governance. Progress will require leaders willing to negotiate, make difficult choices, and accept accountability for results.

The homebuilding industry should not passively wait to see whether that happens. Its leaders have both a direct business interest and a broader responsibility to help make it possible.

America should not have to choose between secure borders and a productive economy. President Trump and Congress should be held accountable for delivering on both fronts.

Not only should the homebuilding industry insist that it do so, but the industry’s leaders should strike with the same speed and ferocity the Administration used to kick-start its agenda almost two years ago.
