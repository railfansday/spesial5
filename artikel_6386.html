AI can copy how people write online, but one thing still gives it away

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Nine AI models learned the shape of online replies in a new test, but AI-generated text still gave itself away through emotion and tone.

Ask a chatbot to write a reply to a post the way a regular person would, and the result usually reads fine. That is the problem. A reply can sound natural to a human reader and still be nothing like what a real person would have written.

A computer program, however, told the two apart 70 to 80 percent of the time.

Nicolò Pagan of the University of Zurich and Petter Törnberg of the University of Amsterdam ran that test on nine AI language models. The raw material was real conversations from X, Bluesky, and Reddit.

The models learned the shape of online talk well enough. What they couldn’t fake was the feeling behind it, and the harder the team pushed them to sound human, the less they said what the human had actually said.

The stakes go beyond spotting bots. Social scientists have started using these models as stand-ins for people, simulating how crowds might react to a news feed or a policy.

The study asks whether the stand-ins behave like the people they replace.

The team collected replies that real users had posted to real messages. The sample held 275 users on X, 492 on Reddit’s politics forum, and 59 groups of similar accounts on Bluesky, with 20 replies each.

Then they asked each AI model to write its own reply to the same original message, in the style of the same user.

A separate program was trained to look at a reply and guess whether a person or a model wrote it. If the AI replies were indistinguishable from the human ones, that guess would be right about half the time, no better than a coin toss.

The nine models came from six families, including Llama, Mistral, Gemma, and DeepSeek. Most were small by today’s standards, and one, Llama 3.1 70B, was far larger.

All were open-weight models that researchers can run on their own computers, so the biggest commercial chatbots, such as ChatGPT, were not tested.

Christopher Bail of Duke University and Christopher Barrie of New York University and the University of Oxford were co-authors on the research publication.

The standard setup feeds the model a written description of the user’s personality and interests. With that alone, the detector was right at least 87 percent of the time for every model on every platform.

The clearest tells were how long the replies ran, how long the words were, and how toxic the language was. The models wrote longer replies than people did, and the versions tuned to be helpful assistants produced less hostile language than real users.

Several models also stuffed replies with hashtags and mentions that real users almost never used.

On the emotional side, the replies differed from human ones in categories such as positive emotion, negative emotion, and the language of speaking and communicating.

In an interview with Earth.com, Törnberg said the most surprising part was how stubborn those gaps were. “What surprised me most was how persistent the differences were,” he said.

“So in a sense, the models can become quite good at reproducing the form of online conversation, but less so with its feeling.”

The team tried five ways of coaching the models, each one adding to the last.

These were a bare prompt, a prompt with a persona description, ten examples of the user’s past replies, relevant snippets pulled from the user’s history and, finally, retraining the model itself on the user’s writing.

The persona description, which is how most researchers currently do this, made little difference and sometimes made the replies easier to spot. Retraining was unpredictable, and for several models it made detection easier.

The two simplest moves worked best. Showing the model ten real examples cut the detector’s accuracy between about 2 and 10 percent. Adding the user’s past context cut another 2 to 3 percent.

Even the best combinations left the detector right 70 to 80 percent of the time. The bigger model was no better at passing than the small ones, and models tuned to be helpful assistants were generally worse than their raw counterparts.

“I would not assume that newer models will necessarily be better models of human behavior,” Törnberg told Earth.com. He said the training that makes a model helpful and well behaved may be the same training that makes its language less like real human communication.

The study’s second measure asked whether the AI-generated text meant the same thing as the reply the human actually wrote. The team scored that by converting both replies into numbers that capture meaning and comparing them.

The coaching that made replies harder to detect also moved them slightly further from the human reply’s meaning on every platform. The models were learning to write like a person in general, not to say what that person would say.

Next, each model wrote 20 candidate replies, and the team picked one in two ways. Choosing the reply that best matched the human’s meaning roughly doubled the similarity score but did not reliably make the reply harder to detect.

Choosing the reply most likely to fool the detector trimmed its accuracy by 5 to 10 points but left the meaning no closer. The two methods picked the same reply only 5 to 10 percent of the time, about what chance would give.

Asked by Earth.com what else stood out, Törnberg pointed to that trade-off. The team could make replies harder to tell from human writing, he said, but doing so often made them less similar in meaning to what the real person wrote.

Every message in the dataset had only one real human reply, so the meaning score measured closeness to that one reply, not whether the AI’s reply was a reasonable one.

On X, the comparison between two humans answering the same post rested on only 18 pairs, which the authors treated as exploratory.

The models also saw less of each conversation than the human replying had seen. And the detector was more likely to flag a real person as a bot than to miss a bot.

Whether future models close the gap is an open question, the authors wrote, and their test is built to keep asking it. Even a model that passed every statistical check, they added, would not necessarily hold the attitudes behind a human reply.

Törnberg said the fix for researchers is to validate a simulation against real human behavior in the specific setting being studied, rather than trust that it sounds right.

And getting the feeling right, the authors wrote, may take new training goals that target emotional expression directly instead of copying style.

The full study was published in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.
