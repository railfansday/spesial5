Spain thought it had the 2030 World Cup final. Morocco and Infantino have other plans

When Spain secured a joint bid for the 2030 World Cup, many automatically assumed as a certainty that the final match would take place on home turf.

As former world champions – a title since won again – and the second-most visited country globally, Spain held the sporting pedigree and tourism capacity for huge crowds, creating a widespread expectation the final was guaranteed.

Co-host Portugal had no desire for the fixture, and Spanish representatives assumed Morocco, having joined the bid late, would not fight to secure it.

In recent months, however, alarm has spread across Spain that the North African nation, a rising power in world football, might steal the tournament's biggest showpiece event.

The rivalry to host the game has evolved into a fierce clash involving Real Madrid's Bernabeu, Barcelona's Camp Nou and the $1 billion Hassan II stadium now being built near legendary Casablanca.

The contest is now tied to FIFA President Gianni Infantino's survival battle and political tensions between Spain and Morocco regarding migration.

Internal politics in Spain have also been impacted, with Alberto Núñez Feijóo, frontrunner to become prime minister following Sunday's vote, insisting Spain should drop out of hosting the tournament if denied the final.

Top-flight LaLiga chief Javier Tebas and conservative mayor of Madrid José Luis Martínez-Almeida have voiced similar concerns, with the latter admitting he "wasn't optimistic" given he saw no sign that the Spanish government was matching Morocco's ambition.

Inspectors from world governing body FIFA are touring host cities during this month and next, but no date has been set for choosing the location. For the 2026 World Cup final, the venue near New York was named two years beforehand.

With its team bankrolled by ⁠the state fertilizer behemoth, Morocco has united behind bringing the final to its new stadium, named after its former king, which with a 115,000-seat capacity will ​be the world's ⁠largest soccer arena.

By contrast, Spain's campaign is divided along familiar lines between Madrid and Barcelona and their two biggest clubs.

Morocco deserves the final as much as Spain, fan Bilal Jouhari, 39, said in Rabat, pointing out that Morocco is fifth in FIFA's world rankings.

"Those who threaten to walk out if FIFA chooses Morocco as the final's host are just like those who abandon the game because they don't like the referee's decisions," said Jouhari, who runs a platform promoting cultural events.

Ricardo Trade, former CEO of the ⁠FIFA World Cup Organising Committee for Brazil 2014, said Spain should have settled which city got the final as part of the original bid.

"Spain is terrified of losing the final, a final they were absolutely certain they’d be hosting," said Trade, also CEO ​for the 2016 Olympics and deputy ⁠CEO for operations at the 2022 World Cup.

"You have Barcelona on one side saying what they want, ‌and Real Madrid on the other also saying what they want, whilst Morocco seems to be very united on this World Cup project and has a stadium for 115,000 people."

After Barcelona's president, Joan Laporta, lobbied in September for the final to go to the Camp Nou, arguing that the Bernabeu had already hosted the 1982 final, some sources in the Spanish football federation (RFEF) expressed frustration at him for undermining the unity needed to counter Morocco.

A senior Portuguese official involved in the bidding process said Spain was caught off guard, wrongly assuming that since Morocco was ‌not part of the original bid then the final was guaranteed for Spain. "They should have seen it coming," the person said.

Alvaro de Miguel, the RFEF secretary general, denied ‌there were divisions in the Spanish camp and said presenting two options against Morocco's one gave Spain better odds of success.

He noted that the bid to host the tournament was put together during a moment of instability at the Spanish FA when former president Luis Rubiales was embroiled in scandal over kissing player Jenni Hermoso on the lips at the 2023 Women's World Cup.

The atmosphere at the RFEF at the time "was not the most conducive to a project of this scale, involving a range of complex interests," he said.

It also coincided with a testy time between Spain and Morocco over migration, including a 2021 attempt by thousands of people to enter Spain's African enclave of Ceuta.

Prime Minister Pedro Sanchez overruled the RFEF's plans to announce ⁠the invitation to Morocco to join the bid, insisting on delivering the message himself during a bilateral visit to Rabat in 2018, according to an RFEF source.

His government was heavily involved in the creation of the bid and the RFEF ended up agreeing to a request from the government and FIFA to include Casablanca as an option for the final, according to three RFEF sources.

FIFA's Infantino has been locked in a power struggle with UEFA since Europe's governing football body demanded he step down over his plan to sell a 20% stake in the World Cup.

Tebas said Infantino would favour Morocco because it was backing his reelection bid. "If Infantino stays on, Spain’s chances of getting that final are negligible or non-existent," Tebas said in September.

Morocco's football association has been one of his staunchest supporters and Infantino has spent long periods at FIFA's newly opened regional headquarters in Rabat since the selloff plan was revealed. Infantino is the favourite to win reelection at FIFA's March congress – in Morocco.

However, Real Madrid President Florentino Perez, who has a close relationship with Infantino, could help persuade him to choose Spain for the final, a LaLiga source said.

De Miguel said the fact that FIFA's governance was under such scrutiny played in Spain's favour, since he expected it to be awarded to the "natural" candidate.

FIFA said in a statement it has no standard procedure for selecting the venue and that the decision would follow consultations with ‌host cities, countries and governments.

Spanish officials say Madrid has experience in handling high-octane matches such as the 2018 final of South America's Copa Libertadores between River Plate and Boca Juniors, switched there due to fan violence.

They also point to the debacle in ​Rabat during the 2025 Africa Cup of Nations final, when Senegal's coach ordered his players off the pitch over a penalty awarded to Morocco, footballers fought each other, and the ball boys stole the Senegal goalkeeper's equipment.

De Miguel said that if size ‌is a factor, then the Camp Nou will hold 105,000 by the 2028-2029 season after a $2 billion refurbishment, compared to the ⁠Bernabeu's 84,000 after its $1.5 billion makeover.

Moroccan FA President Fouzi Lekjaa said construction of the Hassan II will be completed in early 2028. According to the IMF, Morocco plans to spend about $19 billion between 2024 and 2030 building airports, stadiums, hotels and a high-speed ⁠rail network, as well as a train station and highway to connect the stadium with Casablanca 45 km (28 miles) away.

Casablanca will need to work on its hotels to compete with Madrid and Barcelona after a FIFA technical report rated its accommodation offering for the general public at 3.1 compared to 5 for Spain.

But a successful bid is not just about who has ‌the best stadium, hotels and transport. Often bids go to the country that would ​receive the greater benefit, Trade said.

In Morocco, the World Cup was an opportunity to expand railways and develop the whole Casablanca area, Lekjaa told state ‌broadcaster 2M. A Moroccan source familiar with the discussions said the final should go to the stadium with the largest ​capacity — and to an African country.

"It would be awkward – embarrassing, even – and would send the wrong message to Africa, one of the world's biggest producers of raw football talent, if the World Cup final were ultimately awarded to a non-African country with a smaller stadium," the person said.
