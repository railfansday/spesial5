Standard fertility tests may be missing an important clue about sperm health

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Retained mitochondrial DNA in mature sperm has been linked to longer times to pregnancy, even among men with normal semen test results.

Men in couples who struggle to conceive are usually sent for a semen analysis. The test measures sperm count, concentration, shape and movement. Normal results do not guarantee a quick pregnancy.

A study of 327 men with normal semen results has now pointed to one extra factor. Some of their mature sperm still carried mitochondrial DNA.

This DNA belongs to the mitochondria, the parts of a cell that make energy. Couples needed more time to conceive when the man’s sperm held more of it.

The study was carried out at the Wayne State University School of Medicine in Detroit. It was observational, so a link was found but a cause was not established.

The senior author was J. Richard Pilsner, a professor at Wayne State’s C.S. Mott Center for Human Growth and Development.

“These findings provide an important new window into the male contribution to reproductive success,” Pilsner said.

Almost every cell in the body contains mitochondria. Energy for the cell is generated within these small compartments.

Mitochondria contain their own circular DNA, which carries 37 genes. In humans, it is passed on through the mother.

R. E. Giles and colleagues reported this maternal pattern in PNAS in 1980. Defects in these genes underlie several inherited diseases, and mitochondrial donation has been used to prevent their transmission.

Mitochondria are retained by the egg. According to a separate study, women’s egg cells keep this DNA largely free of age-related damage.

In sperm, a different process is assumed. Most paternal mitochondrial DNA is thought to be eliminated during sperm production.

That assumption sat awkwardly beside earlier data. Higher sperm mitochondrial DNA had been associated with lower semen quality and with longer times to pregnancy.

Two sources were proposed for the excess DNA. Ordinary body cells may have contaminated the samples, or immature sperm cells may have been present.

Purified, frozen sperm from the LIFE Study were analyzed by the Wayne State team. Couples in that cohort had been enrolled before conception and followed while they attempted pregnancy.

Female partners kept daily journals. Time to pregnancy was then calculated in menstrual cycles.

Inclusion was restricted to men who met the 2010 WHO criteria for sperm count, concentration and shape. Of these, 327 were analyzed.

Mitochondrial DNA was quantified by digital PCR, a method that detects and counts individual copies of a target sequence.

Values ranged from 0.5 copies at the lower end to 49.0 copies at the upper end.

Bulk measurements cannot indicate which cells contain the DNA. Imaging of individual sperm was therefore required.

Samples from 18 men were selected at random, six from each of the low, moderate and high groups. Fluorescence in situ hybridization, which labels a chosen DNA sequence with a glowing tag, was adapted for sperm.

Image scoring was performed blind to group. A total of 50 sperm per participant were assessed.

Signal was located in the midpiece, a segment between the head and the tail. Mitochondria are arranged in a sheath around this segment.

Distinct spots were resolved around the midpiece in three-dimensional microscope images. Signal was not localized to the head or tail.

Jeffrey Mann, the study’s first author, is a postdoctoral fellow at Wayne State. He described the meaning of the images.

“Directly visualizing mitochondrial DNA within the sperm midpiece provides compelling evidence that the mitochondrial DNA detected by polymerase chain reaction reflects DNA retained within mature sperm,” Mann said.

Men with higher DNA levels had a larger share of glowing sperm. The correlation between the two measures was 0.87, where 1 would be a perfect match.

In men with moderate or high mitochondrial DNA levels, the glowing signals covered a larger area of the sperm midpiece than in men with low levels.

Sperm within a single sample differed from one another. In high-group samples, glowing sperm were found beside sperm with no signal.

A high reading appears to reflect a mixed population. Some sperm kept their mitochondrial DNA, while others removed it as expected.

DNA levels were then compared with the time each couple took to conceive. Higher levels were linked to a lower chance of pregnancy per menstrual cycle.

The team reported a fecundability odds ratio of 0.79. Values below 1 indicate lower odds of pregnancy per cycle as levels rise.

Differences between the low, moderate and high groups were unlikely to be due to chance. The link remained after adjustment for both partners’ age, body mass index and smoking.

Pilsner linked the imaging and pregnancy results.

“For the first time, we directly visualized mitochondrial DNA retained within mature sperm and showed that this feature is associated with lower likelihood of achieving pregnancy, even among men with normal semen parameters,” Pilsner said.

The team views the measure as a possible addition to routine semen analysis. A separate study found that body fat can lower sperm counts in men of normal weight.

“These findings suggest that conventional semen analysis may miss molecular signatures within sperm that provide important information about couples’ ability to conceive,” Pilsner said.

The study did not prove that retained DNA slows pregnancy. Only an association was shown.

Men with abnormal semen results were excluded. This limited the influence of other sperm problems on the findings.

The results cannot yet be extended to men with abnormal semen or diagnosed infertility.

Some factors were not measured. These included how often couples had sex, which also affects time to pregnancy.

The probe detects specific DNA sequences. Whether each spot represents a complete mitochondrial genome or a fragment is still unknown.

Imaging was performed on only 18 men. The microscope images have been posted online for other researchers.

Future studies could test a link with TFAM, a protein that packages mitochondrial DNA. Retention in men with abnormal semen results could also be examined.

“This finding opens new questions about the molecular mechanisms that allow some sperm to retain mitochondrial DNA,” Mann said.

Normal semen results may not give a complete picture of male fertility. Leftover mitochondrial DNA in sperm could be worth measuring alongside the standard tests.

The full study was published in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.
