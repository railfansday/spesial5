Dallas Wants More Gates At Love Field—And To Give Southwest Airlines A Bigger Monopoly

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

Dallas is opening the door to more gates at Love Field airport. Two things stand in the way. Federal law caps the airport at 20 gates, and Dallas has already negotiated a lease that would offer most of the additional gates to Southwest Airlines if the law were to change. Southwest already controls nearly all of the gates there. More room for adding Southwest flights is different than more competition for Southwest. Dallas needs a larger airport and to open itself to competition. The Dallas City Manager raised the prospect of additional gates at an aviation event last week. The airport is undergoing a $2.5 billion redevelopment that doesn’t include more gates. Former American Airlines Chairman and CEO Doug Parker defended the existing arrangement on this week’s Airlines Confidential podcast. His argument rests on the existing five-party agreement, federal law and investments made in reliance on both: “agreements are agreements.” That’s only why changing the rules will be difficult, not a reason to keep limits on competition forever. Why Dallas Can’t Just Build More Love Field Gates Southwest Airlines had to fight to operate from Dallas Love Field when it first launched. It flew its first flight in 1971 after being tied up in the courts. Existing airlines agreed to leave Love Field for DFW airport. Southwest wasn’t a party to that agreement. In 1979, the Wright Amendment was passed to limit competition from Southwest Airlines. Deregulation meant that Southwest could fy beyond the state of Texas, and other carriers feared that the close-in airport they operated from would be more desirable than DFW. The upstart Southwest that fought for the right to fly was mature and pivoted to using government to its own advantage by the time they succeeded in securing the Wright Amendment Reform Act of 2006. It was a compromise among Dallas, Fort Worth, DFW Airport, American Airlines and Southwest Airlines. Southwest gained the ability to sell connecting service beyond the old geographic limits, followed by nationwide domestic nonstop service in October 2014. Love Field’s gate count had to be reduced from 32 to 20. Southwest kept most of the gates. The airport couldn’t offer nonstop international passenger service. Basically Southwest got more flying from Love Field and a monopoly there, while American Airlines got a monopoly on international service from Dallas and a cap on how much service could compete from Love Field. Initially, Southwest effectively couldn’t even fly out of Dallas – Fort Worth offering direct competition with American. The five party agreement specified that any gate Southwest operates at DFW (or another airport within 80 miles) would require giving up a Love Field gate, up to 8 total gates. That restriction ended in 2025. The provision even covered affiliates and flights carrying Southwest’s code, with exceptions for existing DFW–Midway codeshare service. Southwest couldn’t even codeshare with partners who flew to DFW, and could set up a regional affiliate to operate there. One gate is under Delta’s control and the final gate is under city control and can (theoretically) be used for new service. Southwest receives an option on three of four, five of eight, or eight of twelve new gates. Even after changing a law that protects incumbents, Dallas would offer most new gates to its dominant airline. The Federal Circuit reversed in 2018 arguing that the fact the gates couldn’t legally be used meant it had no value. It might soon have had value. The terminal was turned into a Lincoln dealership. North Texas Has Outgrown This Bargain DFW is growing. Its new Terminal F is planned for 31 gates, all serving American, with the first 15 expected to open in 2027. Meanwhile, McKinney is preparing to launch scheduled commercial service on November 11. Different parts of the Metroplex support different airport choices. DFW, Love Field and McKinney aren’t interchangeable for a passenger near downtown Dallas. Noise and road access are real though anyone living near the airport knew there was an airport there. Additional gate construction would necessarily involve an operational, environmental and access plan. American Airlines certainly didn’t walk away from the Naples, Florida airport just because residents there don’t like commercial air service! Love Field should be allowed to grow. The additional preferential gates should be open to airlines that bring new competition, rather than committing ex ante to enlarging Southwest’s existing control. These lease commitment is an impediment to that, and actually gives American Airlines its strongest argument against expansion. Southwest built its business by fighting attempts to keep it out. American chose to leave Love Field. Neither of those things justifies keeping other competitors out of the Dallas market, or serving each of the local markets in North Texas separately.
