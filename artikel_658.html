Ancient ape fossil in Egypt could rewrite the story of human origins

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

An ancient ape fossil discovered in Egypt is challenging a long-standing theory about where the ancestors of modern apes, including humans, first evolved. The remarkable find suggests that a crucial chapter of our evolutionary history may have unfolded much farther north than scientists previously believed.

The newly identified species, named Masripithecus moghraensis, lived approximately 17 to 18 million years ago during the Early Miocene. Its fossilized remains were recovered from Wadi Moghra in northern Egypt, making it the first definitive fossil ape ever discovered in North Africa.

The discovery, described in a study published March 26, 2026, in Science, comes from an international team at the Mansoura University Vertebrate Paleontology Center (Egypt) and the University of Southern California (USA). Their findings suggest that northern Africa and the Middle East may have played a central role in the emergence of the evolutionary lineage that eventually gave rise to all living apes.

A Missing Piece of the Ape Evolutionary Puzzle

For decades, scientists believed that early apes and their close relatives were concentrated primarily in regions farther south in Africa. Fossil sites in North Africa had produced remains of ancient monkeys from the Early Miocene, but researchers had never found definitive evidence of apes from the same period.

That gap helped shape prevailing theories about ape evolution. Although younger ape fossils have been discovered across Africa, Europe, and Asia, their evolutionary relationships and geographic origins remain subjects of scientific debate.

The newly discovered Egyptian fossil may help explain what was missing from this picture.

"We spent five years searching for this kind of fossil because, when we look closely at the early ape family tree, it becomes clear that something is missing -- and North Africa holds that missing piece," said Hesham Sallam, a paleontologist at Mansoura University and senior author of the study.

The discovery is especially important for understanding the origins of crown Hominoidea, the evolutionary group that includes the last common ancestor of all living apes and its descendants. Today, that group encompasses gibbons, orangutans, gorillas, chimpanzees, and humans.

Scientists suspect that the incomplete geographic distribution of ancient fossils has obscured important details about when and where this group first emerged.

An 18-Million-Year-Old Ape With Unusual Teeth

The fossils were uncovered during expeditions in 2023 and 2024 by researchers from the Sallam Lab at Wadi Moghra, a well-known fossil site in northern Egypt.

The team named the species Masripithecus moghraensis. Its genus name combines Masr (مصر), the Arabic name for Egypt, with píthēkos, the Greek word for ape. The species name honors Wadi Moghra, where the fossils were found.

Although the remains consist only of lower jaw material, they contain an unusual combination of anatomical features that distinguishes the animal from other apes of similar age.

The jaw is exceptionally sturdy, with remarkably large canine and premolar teeth. Its molars also have rounded chewing surfaces covered in pronounced textures. These characteristics offer valuable clues about the foods the animal could eat and how it survived in its environment.

Researchers believe Masripithecus primarily consumed fruit but had teeth powerful enough to process tougher foods when necessary. This dietary flexibility may have been an important survival advantage.

"Together, they suggest that Masripithecus was adapted for versatility. The study interprets its chewing anatomy as evidence of a flexible, mainly fruit-based diet, with the ability to process harder foods such as nuts or seeds when needed. This flexibility would have helped Masripithecus to thrive at a time when climatic changes were leading to more pronounced seasonality in northern Africa and Arabia," said Shorouq Al-Ashqar, a researcher at the Mansoura University Vertebrate Paleontology Center and a first author of the study.

As seasonal differences became more pronounced, the availability of certain foods likely changed throughout the year. An ape capable of eating a wider range of foods would have been better equipped to cope with those fluctuations.

A Discovery That Challenges the Ape Family Tree

The importance of Masripithecus extends well beyond its unusual anatomy. When researchers examined where the species belonged on the evolutionary family tree, they uncovered a surprising result.

Their analysis suggests that Masripithecus was more closely related to living apes than any known ape species from the Early Miocene of East Africa.

To investigate these relationships, the team used advanced statistical techniques known as Bayesian methods. These approaches allow scientists to compare different evolutionary possibilities and estimate how strongly the available evidence supports each one.

The researchers combined anatomical information from living and extinct apes with genetic evidence from living species. They also incorporated the geological ages of fossil specimens, helping them estimate when different evolutionary branches separated.

By bringing these sources of evidence together, the scientists reconstructed relationships among ancient and modern apes and explored where their shared ancestors may have lived.

The results challenge the long-standing assumption that the evolutionary roots of all living apes were centered in or around East Africa.

Could North Africa Be the Birthplace of Modern Apes?

Additional analyses of ancient animal distributions pointed toward northern Africa and the Middle East as the most likely geographic home of the common ancestor of all living apes.

That ancestor is estimated to have lived during the Early Miocene, an interval spanning roughly 23 million to 16 million years ago. This was an important period in primate evolution, when early ape lineages were diversifying, and environments were undergoing substantial changes.

Geography may help explain why this region was so important.

Millions of years ago, Africa, Arabia, and Asia were moving toward a new geological arrangement. As the African and Arabian tectonic plates traveled northward and approached the final stages of their collision with Asia, the connections between these landmasses began to change.

Sea levels also rose and fell, periodically reducing the stretches of water that separated different regions. At certain times, these changes made it easier for animals to move between Africa and Eurasia.

Northern Africa and the Middle East therefore occupied a potentially crucial position in the movement and evolution of ancient mammals, including apes.

The discovery of Masripithecus helps connect previously separated fossil records from Africa and Eurasia. Its presence suggests that apes were already evolving into distinct forms in this region before new land connections made it possible for them to expand into Europe and Asia.

Rather than being a peripheral location in ape evolution, the region may have been an important center of evolutionary diversification.

Scientists Rethink Where Our Ancient Ape Ancestors Lived

The findings have even prompted some of the researchers to reconsider ideas they held throughout their careers.

Erik Seiffert, a paleontologist at the University of Southern California and co-author of the study, explained how the evidence has changed his understanding of ape origins.

"For my entire career, I considered it probable that the common ancestor of all living apes lived in or around East Africa. But this new discovery, and our new and novel analyses of hominoid phylogeny and biogeography, now strongly challenge that idea. And, importantly, the likelihood of this scenario doesn't depend on Masripithecus -- but it is very much consistent with it."

Importantly, the researchers are not claiming that Masripithecus was a direct ancestor of humans or even necessarily the direct ancestor of living apes. Instead, its position on the evolutionary family tree provides evidence about where the broader ancestral lineage may have originated.

The distinction matters because humans emerged much later in evolutionary history. The Egyptian fossil offers clues to an earlier stage, when the distant ancestors shared by humans and other living apes were evolving.

The team's findings also suggest that the traditional emphasis on East African fossils may have provided an incomplete view of this much older evolutionary story.

More Evolutionary Secrets May Be Hidden in Egypt

Although the discovery offers compelling new evidence, scientists still have much to learn about Masripithecus and the early history of modern apes.

Only part of the animal's jaw has been recovered, leaving important questions about its overall appearance, body size, movement, and behavior unanswered. Additional fossils could provide a more complete picture of how this ancient species lived and how it relates to other early apes.

The researchers believe that further exploration of North Africa and the Middle East could reveal additional species and help clarify when and where the major branches of the modern ape family tree began to emerge.

For now, Masripithecus moghraensis offers a striking reminder that even fundamental ideas about our distant evolutionary past can change when scientists uncover evidence in places that have received relatively little attention.

A fossil jaw preserved for nearly 18 million years may be helping scientists piece together a chapter of evolutionary history that was missing all along.
