This New Book Strives to Help Climbers Rewire Their Mental Game

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

(Photo: Left: Sam Douglas Jones / Right: Vertebrate Publishing)

We all do it: Eyeing other climbers and comparing ourselves to them. Wishing we were better at dynos, heel hooks, or some other elusive climbing move. Loading ourselves with shoulds when it comes to training or grades.

Even non-performance-oriented climbers like me tend to be guilty of at least some negative thoughts when we’re up on the wall. It’s hard not to think that way when so many highly specific levels dominate the sport. This makes the idea of progression almost impossible to resist, even if you claim to do it only for fun.

There is something about the progress ladder of the Yosemite Decimal System. Its slash grades, letter addendums, and pluses and minuses tempt you to try and tick routes with higher grades. The same goes for the V-scale, as well as many other grading scales. We often ask “What if?” questions about breaking into a harder grade. And we often define ourselves by the grades we climb. “I’m a V10 boulderer,” someone might say, or “I’m a 5.10 climber.”

Meanwhile, when I’m skiing or mountain biking, there are essentially only four, color-coded levels, green through double black. Compared to climbing, I find it rare for someone to ask what color run I skied or trail I rode—and did I make it down without falling? But after someone climbs a route, one of the first questions might be what the grade was. And did you send it?

As the sport continues to grow, two British climbers have noticed that the expansion of training content has far outpaced the rise of mindset content when it comes to climbing. And they agree that climbing tends to be uniquely progress-oriented compared to other sports.

“It’s great to see the growth in top-end performance climbing, but for a lot of people, that might come with a lot of pressure to perform the same way,” says Louis Parkinson, the founder of the coaching company Catalyst Climbing, a climber of over 20 years, and co-author of the new book Get a Grip.

To write a self-help book for climbers focused exclusively on mindset, Parkinson teamed up with Joel Robertson, an MA MBACP psychotherapist and climber. Nicknamed “The Climbing Therapist,” Robertson insists that their book isn’t only for elite or performance-oriented athletes. That’s because he believes climbers at any level can experience the pressure and negative thought patterns that the sport can induce. He also sees negative mentality extending beyond the sport.

“If you’re being brutal to yourself at the crag or in the gym in ways that are holding you back in climbing, then you’re almost certainly doing that to yourself at family Christmas or at work or whatever,” Robertson says. “Mental health and climbing mindset are not separate, right? Let’s treat them the same. That’s the whole ethos of the book.”

Both Parkinson and Robertson admit that they are far from perfect when it comes to their own mentality. They emphasize that a healthy climbing mindset requires continuous work, rather than a one-time solution.

A former competitive climber, Parkinson recalls a period where he stopped enjoying climbing. This triggered an identity crisis of sorts, since he had oriented his entire life and career around the sport. “If I stop climbing, then I don’t know who I am,” Parkinson recalls thinking.

Eventually, Parkinson realized that his lack of enjoyment stemmed from the immense pressure he was putting on himself to continuously improve. When he couldn’t perform up to his own standards, he became “really, really upset.” Once he eased off the unrealistic expectations he was holding himself too, the joy came back. Parkinson’s past struggle echoes a similar experience of Chris Sharma. Earlier this year, he talked with Climbing about letting go of grades in order to send Biographie, the world’s first 5.15.

Robertson, primarily a casual indoor boulderer, shares a more recent experience from a couple weeks ago. After three sessions, he flashed his gym project. In the midst of some celebratory fist pumps, however, he watched in mild horror as someone else came and flashed his project as their warm-up. “It’s so classic,” Robertson says of this common climbing predicament. “I genuinely felt way less good about the achievement and not just the achievement, but myself.”

To avoid comparing yourself to others in a negative way, one suggestion Get a Grip offers is to think about your climbing friends. Do you love and value them because of their climbing ability, or because of who they are? Most people care about each of their friends as a person, not in terms of their climbing abilities. When you start to compare yourself to others, or base your self-worth on whether you, for example, sent your project, try to view yourself as you would your friend instead.

Robertson and Parkinson also suggest focusing on comparisons that are accurate and valuable, rather than grounded in envy or self-pity. Instead of thinking, “Damn, I wish I could climb like that,” ask, “What could I learn from watching someone else that I could apply in my own climbing?”

One of the critical ways to work on climbing mindset is to cultivate more awareness around our own thoughts, according to Robertson and Parkinson. Rather than becoming bogged down in the thought, attempt to view the thought impartially. Try reframing the thought, or compartmentalizing it. “Rather than ‘I am anxious,’ it’s ‘a part of me is anxious,’” Robertson explains. This can help you get some distance between yourself and a fleeting notion or emotion.

Throughout the book, reflections from pro climbers bring more color and insight to what a strong mindset looks like. In one of my favorite sections, British boulderer and competitive climber Shauna Coxsey talks about asking questions instead of making statements. An “exploratory mindset,” as Robertson and Parkinson term it, will fuel you with curiosity rather than pressure. “People often think I’m a very motivated person, but I would disagree,” Coxsey writes after sharing her focus on curiosity. “I think I’m a very committed person. For me, motivation and commitment have to go hand in hand because it’s impossible to be motivated all the time.”

Another standout pro mindset cameo comes from another British boulderer and comp climber: Hamish McArthur. Essentially, he reveals that he more or less blacks out when he climbs hard. This allows him to enter the coveted flow state that so many of us seek. “The times when I’ve performed at my best, I don’t really have any memory of what it was like,” he writes.

I took a lot away from the book in terms of how to edit and take control of my internal monologue. But I was left with one nagging question: How can we all climb more like McArthur, and find that elusive flow state?

“Just think less. It’s easy!” Parkinson joked with me. “No, it’s really hard. It’s insanely difficult.” Parkinson clarifies that achieving a mindset like McArthur’s requires a long-term commitment to honing your meditation skills so you can quiet your mind and just climb. But the lessons in Get a Grip can help get you started.

Vertebrate Publishing released Get a Grip on October 8. You can find a copy from Catalyst Climbing, Amazon, and wherever books are sold. Climbers can also listen to Get a Grip as an audiobook narrated by Parkinson.
