“Two Girls Who Speak All the Time About Politics”: Tao Okamoto and Virginie Efira Take On Capitalism in <i><em>All of a Sudden</em></i>

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Okamoto and Efira shared the Best Actress Award at Cannes, where All of a Sudden premiered this summer

We’re all seeking comfort as the world ends. Environmental, economic, and political pressures weigh heavily on our minds as tangible solutions feel farther and farther out of reach. Solace is a rare commodity in a world controlled by unrelenting capitalist forces. In All of a Sudden, filmmaker Ryusuke Hamaguchi suggests that as we face tragedy, we must remember our vital need for human connection.

All of a Sudden tells the story of two women who forge a formidable friendship as they face personal and professional hardship. Marie-Lou Fontaine (Virginie Efira) runs an elder-care facility near Paris, where she introduces a “Humanitude” methodology designed to treat patients with dignity and introduce listening-heavy caretaking practice. She befriends Mari (Tao Okamoto), a terminally ill theater director from Japan. Ryusuke chronicles this relationship through extended, emotional conversations, spoken in both Japanese and French, and covering topics from identity to mortality.

The film is an adaptation of a nonfiction book, When Life Suddenly Takes a Turn: Twenty Letters Between a Philosopher With Terminal Cancer and a Medical Anthropologist, which collects correspondence from two Japanese-speaking academics as they reflect on health care and death during the last few months of philosopher Makiko Miyano’s life (who inspired Okamoto’s character). Instead of keeping the fact that the two pen pals spoke the same language, he introduced French and moved the story to Paris. This small change forced the story to confront language gaps, exploring the power of conversation and listening and asserting that so much can be achieved through this mediation.

Efira and Okamoto understand their characters’ simmering anxieties and outlooks on the world, providing the bedrock for these impressively naturalistic performances. The two actresses sustain long, philosophical conversation with such intensity throughout the entire three hours and 16 minutes. In a rare occurrence, the two shared the Best Actress Award at the Cannes Film Festival, where the movie premiered this summer. In the film, their budding friendship serves as a sounding board for each other, as Marie-Lou grapples with the pressure of capitalism on her medical staff and Mari comes to terms with the end of her life. In typical fashion, Ryusuke lets the camera linger on these two actresses during these prolonged philosophical interactions, forcing the audience to sit with the gravity of their situations.

Ahead of the theatrical release of Ryusuke’s latest feature film, Bazaar spoke to Efira and Okamoto about the first time they read the script, how they built an intimate bond despite speaking two different languages, and the punk, anti-capitalist edge fueling this intimate, conversation-heavy film.

Tao Okamoto: It’s the scene where she talks about capitalism while writing on the whiteboard. That’s one of the strangest scenes in the movie. I felt connected to the character because I am also a climate activist myself. I’ve been spreading the word about climate crises on podcasts, and I talk about it daily. When I read the part, I felt, “I need to play this character.”

Virginie Efira: It’s the same for me, because this scene is so special. When I read it, the movie felt like one of the most profound, special, and radical readings. You have two girls who speak all the time about politics. We don’t see that. I never see that in other movies. Sometimes, when a director wants to put something philosophical, political, or intellectual, they say, “Maybe we’ll do three minutes,” otherwise the audience will be bored. No, this movie spent 15 minutes [discussing capitalism], and I said, “Oh yes, that’s so punk!” The film has its own language. When I read All of a Sudden, I could hear the language of the movie, and I wanted to approach it. When I met Tao, I thought, “Oh, we are so lucky to be an actress and work with [Ryusuke], but also to share this material with him.”

VE: I only knew one word in Japanese before making the movie. Then, to be able to speak Japanese at a high level, because the character studied in Japan, I must first learn to read, then learn to embody the language. Finding the language of the movie was strange. There’s a flat and natural way to play [in one of Ryusuke’s movies]. He spoke to me about Cassavetes and Gena Rowlands, but also about Bresson. Neutral performances. You find it when you open up, and when I opened to Tao, we discovered the right language for the movie. We did a lot of flat readings before shooting. We never rehearsed in motion, so when we finally performed for the first time, we awakened the text.

TO: I’m going to add that because I started my career in America, finding a middle ground by using a language that’s not my mother tongue is normal for me. On the surface, that part wasn’t a challenge for me. Finding the language for this film and respecting Hamaguchi’s way of working were priorities. We were naturally drawn to his talent and wanted to make his dream happen and be part of it.

TO: I joined this project way after Virginie. I luckily had months to prepare with him, and during rehearsals, you drop all emotion and read the text as if you were reading numbers. On top of that, I got to visit some institutes, such as the Cancer Research Center and places where people work with disabled people, to build up my understanding of my role’s point of view. Reading the text over and over was a unique experience for me. [Ryusuke] can really listen to the actor’s voice and tell the difference between someone who is simply reading and someone really concentrating. Even though you’re not supposed to put any emotions, you have to be present; you have to be there. I’ve never experienced such rich, profound preparation for a role.

VE: We shot in a real nursing home. There was a mix between fiction and reality. Sometimes filmmaking becomes standardized, with no room left to breathe, but with Hamaguchi, you’re sure he will leave room for the unexpected and for uncertainty. It allows you to embrace the unpredictability of reality. He’s not looking for an accident, but for the ability to be in the present. A viewer of Hamaguchi’s work will always feel a sensation of documentation, but it’s still always cinema. To be an actor in his movies is to be naturalistic, to be in the present, to capture some details and really embrace this unpredictability. The filming process becomes an adventure he wants you to have. One day, he told me, “I hope that you will find the light.” He said a lot of cryptic things. He said to me, “Don’t use your text like a tool; just go inside the text.”

TO: The script is already amazing. And I could read it over and over again, and that was a joy. But he mentioned that I should read the original book. It’s about two women exchanging letters—or emails. They are real people who the characters are modeled after. I wanted to please the people who know these women, not to make them too different from them. I first put pressure on myself to portray this woman as close to the real person, but it was impossible for me because this person passed away in 2019. I couldn’t do much research, so I interviewed her mother and the co-writer. That provided me with a lot of clues, but reading their exchanged letters already gave me the soul of this movie.

VE: He gives the book to the actresses, but also to the entire team. I never felt something so collective, and you will feel it in the movie, from the sound engineer to someone who only stayed two days on set. I also met the anthropologist. When you read this book, it’s full of life. It gives you a perceptible beauty of things. The book has a kind of energy; it helps you keep a level of optimism even when you know the situation is hopeless. In this book, it speaks about what it’s like to be alive. Maybe there’s beauty in life because we are going to die.

TO: For me, I came on this project at the last minute, only a month before. Virginie was so dedicated to studying Japanese from scratch; it was so impressive how she studied all this writing, reading, and lines. Seeing that, I had a huge admiration, like “Who can do this!?” As you can see, Virginie’s so charming; nobody would hate her. It was so easy for me to be drawn to her as a person, as the characters in the movie were discovering each other.

VE: We got to know each other through the shoot. I was happy not to know much at the beginning and to progressively get deeper into our relationship. We lived all that together. I conflate Marie-Lou’s feelings for Mari with Virginie’s feelings for Tao, because we share that. It will be an end, not a tragic one like in the movie, but it will be an end. But I know Tao will carry this experience in her heart forever, and I will too. He let us feel what we want to feel.

TO: I study environmental issues, and I always thought capitalism is responsible for our world’s issues. Something I didn’t know until I read this script was that capitalism doesn’t only exploit our environment, but also our human bodies—human labor and our time. When I connected those dots, I realized all the problems could be blamed on capitalism. We’re looking for time and looking for happiness—or security—and all these things can be related to a capitalistic way of living. I think it’s great that Hamaguchi connected that fundamental issue to Marie-Lou’s situation in the elderly home. She’s frustrated, as is Marie. She’s battling her cancer, and it can be connected to this economic issue.

VE: Cinema can show bodies, fatigue, and with Marie-Lou, you see a certain fatigue because capitalism creates it. How do we deal with that? We say, “Oh, capitalism’s not good. We don’t have time. And we want to be richer and richer.” But how do we fight that, because everything is already there? I come from France, and you see all the young people outside. The real question is, what can cinema do with that? It’s not a theoretical thing, but Hamaguchi can find a solution. It’s speaking, listening to the other, sharing ideas; it’s movement. If I look for a solution to this problem with money, I’ll remain a prisoner of this way of functioning. Even if you think you’re not in a stage of capitalism, you are.
