Peter Berg on the ‘Two Truths’ of War in ‘The Mosquito Bowl’ and the ‘Incredibly Funny’ ‘Hancock 2’ Script Will Smith Sent Him: ‘I Love Will Smith, and I Always Will’

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

The filmmaker also teases Captain Price probably coming to “Call of Duty” and lays out what a “Friday Night Lights” reboot needs before anyone writes a script.

Peter Berg knows what he wants from the world, and that’s peace.

The 62-year-old filmmaker behind epics like “Lone Survivor” and “Deepwater Horizon” and the sports drama series “Friday Night Lights” may have made his most personal film yet with the World War II drama “The Mosquito Bowl.” He also, it turns out, has a knack for timing.

When we spoke on Nov. 13, a global Microsoft outage had knocked Outlook offline for millions of users the day before. Berg held it up as a warning shot, proof of how little it would take for the world to go dark in a war with China. “Your phones won’t work. Your bank accounts won’t work. Every system that we are depending on will shut down instantly,” he says. “What we saw was just a tiny taste of what that can feel like.”

That same afternoon, the Federal Aviation Administration ordered a nationwide ground stop after a communications failure hit the country’s air traffic control centers, halting every departing flight in the United States. Then I received a text message from Berg.

“Interesting after our talk,” he wrote. “All flights grounded.”

Based on Buzz Bissinger’s 2022 book “The Mosquito Bowl: A Game of Life and Death in World War II,” the film follows a group of college football stars who joined the Marine Corps after Pearl Harbor, played a now-legendary game on Guadalcanal on Christmas Eve 1944 and landed on Okinawa months later. Many of them never came home. Nicholas Galitzine, Bill Skarsgård, Ray Nicholson and Tom Francis lead the ensemble and Berg steps in front of the camera as Lt. Gen. Simon Buckner. After an Oct. 30 theatrical run, the film is now streaming on Netflix.

But the biggest news Berg made has nothing to do with World War II. Flights may have been grounded, but he’s ready to get “Hancock” airborne again. Berg tells Variety that Will Smith has sent him a script for “Hancock 2,” the long-gestating sequel to their 2008 superhero hit and that he would sign on to make it.

“I don’t even know if I’m supposed to say it or not, but Will sent me an incredibly funny, great script for ‘Hancock 2,’” Berg says. “I would do it. The universe has to line up.”

It’s the most concrete sign of life the sequel has shown in years. Talk of a follow-up has circulated since the original grossed more than $600 million worldwide, and in February 2025, Smith teased a “really cool” idea during a Twitch livestream, saying Zendaya was being approached for a role. Sony has never confirmed a sequel is in the works.

Berg also offered something close to a character reference for his star, who was banned from Academy events for 10 years after slapping Chris Rock onstage at the 2022 Oscars. Berg doesn’t mention the incident by name.

“I think the time for me is right for Will to do that role, and for many reasons,” he says. “I think Will Smith is a really good human being and a very, very talented human being and just like me and I would guess you, not a flawless human being. I’m flawed. I’ve made some mistakes, and I’ll probably make some more. But I love Will Smith, and I always will. And I know a lot of other people do too. So if that ever happens, it would be, I think, a really special film.”

Before any return to the “Hancock” universe, Berg has another mission to finish. He’s co-writing and directing Paramount’s “Call of Duty” with Taylor Sheridan, set in the “Modern Warfare” universe and dated for June 30, 2028. When asked about it, he offered the only thing he could at this stage: a tease that the franchise’s most famous face is likely coming along.

“We’ve already said that we’re basing it on the ‘Modern Warfare’ universe,” Berg says. “So you can probably expect to see Captain Price, I would think.”

Casting is “beginning,” Berg says. He credits Paramount chief David Ellison and Activision for being “really smart about how they want to do it,” adding, “Personally, I think they hired the right two guys to help them do it.” He calls Sheridan “the best possible partner I could have in the whole world.” As for more details, he says, “Check in with me, I’d say, in four months.”

Still, the conversation was focused on “The Mosquito Bowl” and on Okinawa, where Berg, whose father served as a Marine, as did Bissinger’s, went to research the script. Marine historians walked him across the battlefields where Tony Butkovich, Dave Schreiner, Bob Bauman and George Murphy were killed and down to the Cornerstone of Peace, the memorial that bears the names of more than 240,000 people who died in the battle.

Below, Berg talks about how “Chicago Hope” and a lunchtime question to James Mangold turned him into a director, why Ray Nicholson deserves respect for owning his last name, what a “Friday Night Lights” reboot needs before anyone writes a script and the “Shocker” drive-in prank he calls the greatest actor moment of his career.

This interview has been edited and condensed for clarity.

You’re one of the rare actors-turned-directors who seems to genuinely love being behind the camera. When did you get the bug?

When I was in college, I was acting and my school didn’t have a film department. I went to this little school called Macalester College in St. Paul, Minnesota. There was another tiny little school called St. Thomas, and they did. So I would go down there and take little film classes and make little 16-millimeter, sometimes Super 8 films. I always just liked the whole world of it. I liked acting. I liked theater. I liked building sets. I liked playing around with cameras.

I moved to L.A. thinking I was going to go to film school, and a series of twists and turns led me to doing a play in like 1990 at the Odyssey Theatre and that started me as an actor. I had a nice little acting career going, and then I got on this show “Chicago Hope.” Each episode was nine days and like 14 hours of shooting. I sat there and started hating acting on a television show. It just became like a treadmill to oblivion in my mind.

But I had this front-row seat to all these different directors, and most of those directors were kind of like failed feature directors back then. These were guys that all thought they were going to be Scorsese, and it didn’t work out for whatever reason. They would come in with all this passion to direct an episode of “Chicago Hope,” and for two days, I’d feel their passion and then the reality of TV scheduling would just turn them into factory workers. But I got to learn from being there and seeing that. It was kind of like my film school.

There was a guy named Bill D’Elia, who was the executive producer of “Chicago Hope,” who let me direct my first episode. David Kelley let me write my first episode, and then he let us produce the episode that I wrote. That was a real game changer for me. And there was another director named Oz Scott, who kind of took me under his wing and let me in on his process.

Then the final moment that really pushed me over the edge: I was acting in a movie called “Cop Land,” and I had a small part. De Niro was in it, Harvey Keitel, Ray Liotta. This was before Harvey Weinstein had been revealed to be what Harvey Weinstein is. He was the biggest superstar producer in town. And there was this young filmmaker in the middle of it all. He had written the script, and he was arguing with Weinstein and with De Niro and I was just sitting there waiting for my one line. I finally walked up to him at lunch. I barely knew him. I’m like, “Excuse me, how do I get your job?” And it was James Mangold. He goes, “You got to write. Do you have an idea?” I said I did. He said, “Write it.”

So every night after we’d wrap “Cop Land,” I would go back to my hotel instead of going out and partying with all the other actors and I would just write, write, write. Mangold, and I’ve told him this, was the final catalyst that made me jump because I saw what he was doing and I wanted that.

Mangold finally earned his first directing nomination for “A Complete Unknown.” You two come from the same school of filmmaking, it seems.

Certainly not an overnight success. This has been earned for a long, long time. James Mangold has definitely earned it.

War movies can be a tough sell. People hear “true story,” watch some men die and move on with their lives. “The Mosquito Bowl” made me think about how we send babies to war. Was that part of the inspiration?

No. My dad was a Marine. He served in Korea. Buzz Bissinger, who wrote the book, his father was a Marine. Buzz and I are cousins. Our dads were good friends. We both grew up sort of understanding the Marine Corps, understanding what it means when you ask Marines to go. Because when you ask them to go, they will go.

When I was writing the script, I spent a lot of time at Parris Island with these 17-, 18-year-old recruits, real recruits today and I started writing the script at a little motel right off the base. Their youth was something that was really seared into me, as it was from the book.

But after Parris Island, what really fired me up to make this film the way I did was I went to Okinawa, where the final battle of the movie takes place, where these young men, Butkovich and Schreiner and Bauman and Murphy, all died. The Marine historians took me on a tour of the island. I started in the center of the island, where the invasion started and I went over Hacksaw Ridge and into the caves. There are still marks on the walls from where the Japanese soldiers pulled the pins on their grenades and blew themselves up because they didn’t want to surrender. You can see where those suicides took place. Then I went all the way to the southern tip of the island, where it ended and there are these cliffs where Okinawan women were throwing themselves off, holding their babies. And then there’s the peace memorial, with the names of the people who died on that island. The power of that experience was really quite awesome inside of me, and that got me going.

And the kicker was, after they toured me, the Marines said, “Would you like to see our current military bases on Okinawa?” I said, “Sure.” And I didn’t realize that we have Marines and current military on these massive bases right now, today. While we’re having this conversation, they’re there. We have aircraft carriers, troop movers, attack aircraft, attack helicopters, missile systems. There is a massive military operation going on right now.

I met with the commanding general of the base, and we’re in his office and there’s a big window behind him and there are F-35s flying constantly behind him. I said, “Sir, can I ask you a question?” He said, “Yes.” I said, “What the fuck is going on here?” Literally how I asked the question. I had no idea, and I’d wager that most people who read you don’t know this is happening. He says, “I’ll tell you, that’s a good question.” He gets up, and he has a giant map of the South China Sea. He goes, “It’s all about this” and he points to this tiny little island, Taiwan. He goes, “They want it” and he points above it on the map, China. “It’s my job to make sure they know they can’t have it.”

And I’m thinking, OK, a war movie. Do we need another war movie? Have we learned our lesson? Seventy-five million people were killed globally in World War II. All it takes right now is for a Chinese aircraft and an American aircraft to play games with each other and clip wings, and we could be in a really bad spot.

So for me, seeing the young Marines today on the island of Okinawa, ready to go do exactly what the Marines did in the 1940s, ready to go fight, made me think, they’re young. War is fucking hell. And it’s possible to hold the two truths. You can respect the military. You can understand the need for a military and make sure we protect and honor those men and women who are willing to go. That’s truth one. And truth two is we need to do everything we fucking can to not go to war. We need diplomats. We need peacemakers. We need to understand what happens when you send young men into war.

That’s why I made the film the way I did. I didn’t turn it into a character study of those guys. I wanted to immerse the audience in what it looks like when young men get their faces blown off. Some critics have responded very well to that, and others haven’t and that’s fine by me.

There’s such a disconnect now. So much of modern war is fought from a distance, almost like a video game.

For sure. We’re being told that drones and AI weapon systems are great because they’re saving lives. But think about it this way: In 1943, when we would go fight on these islands in the Pacific, you had 5,000, 10,000 Japanese on an island. You had 10,000 Marines ready to go breach that island and have this fight. They didn’t have drones back then, but they had massive naval artillery bombardments, which you can think of as drones. Because the theory is, we won’t send men into an active war zone. We’ll use bombs and stay back and blow up the island and kill all the Japanese, and then the Marines can go in. That’ll reduce the human risk. But it didn’t work, because the Japanese just hid in the caves and then the Americans had to go and they had to fight to the death.

Well, the same thing’s happening in Ukraine now. The drones are keeping people back, but unfortunately, eventually, human beings must engage with each other, face to face or bullet to bullet. So these were the big ideas lurking underneath. Yeah, we’re making a World War II film. Yes, it’s a story of football players and these were beautiful men who died. But I was asking people to question war.

Man is violent. There’s always been war. But when you see what happened when Outlook crashed [on Nov. 12], this planet cannot absorb a world war. We can barely absorb what’s going on in the Middle East right now. We can absorb what’s going on in eastern Ukraine. But if we go at it with China, it’s game over.

People say we’re one person away from having a bad day and then ending everything as we know it.

There’s a quote I was thinking about when I made this film. Have you ever heard of Stewart Brand? He’s a big thinker, kind of like Oppenheimer-level. He had a simple quote. He said, “We are as gods and might as well get good at it.” And that’s never felt more true. We now have the power to utterly destroy this planet like that. Do we want to do that? I’m going to go out on a limb: No, we don’t. Look at where we are with the weapon systems, with the technology, with the hostility, with the greed. We are as gods. How do we want to behave?

I just got back three weeks ago from a funeral for a friend of mine who committed suicide as soon as he got out of the military. And I’ve been to several funerals. It’s a rough business, war. If there’s a scale between peace and war, I don’t think any one movie is going to tilt anything. I understand why deterrence is important and why we need to be strong and why we need a military, and I love these men and women and I am pretty unapologetic about that. I feel it for the young men and women at Parris Island right now who, for multiple reasons, are willing to serve in the United States Marine Corps.

When I screened the film at Camp Pendleton for 900 Marines, I told them I love them and I respect their job and I sincerely hope they move through their entire careers as Marines and they get great values and they learn grit and resilience and they make friendships. I hope they do all of that, and they never have to kill a person. They never have to fight. And they just looked at me and nodded.

I showed this movie to Admiral McRaven and General Petraeus, and afterward Admiral McRaven gave me a hug and he was emotional. He said, “This is what people should be seeing. This is what war does to people.” And he said something interesting. This is Admiral McRaven, a legendary American military leader. He said, “Peter, there is no greater pacifist on this planet than me.” And General Petraeus was nodding his head. These are guys who have to go to the funerals for people who’ve died. They have to go to the funerals for military personnel who’ve committed suicide on the way out because they couldn’t deal with it. They understand the cost. They understand the need for deterrence.

Bill Maher tried to tell me that I didn’t understand the need for deterrence. I’m like, yes, I do. I do. You can hold both truths. We have to have a strong hammer, but we have to also try and figure out other ways to function without using that hammer. Because once you use that hammer, what happens in our movie happens. Seventy-five million people died.

My brother served in the Army, and my nephew is serving now. I’m a fierce advocate for the military, but I feel like we treat our vets like shit.

Of course we do. We spend millions and billions of dollars making the soldiers, making the equipment and the second they’re done, take a number and go to the VA hospital.

I know a lot of vets. Ray Mendoza is a very good friend of mine. He directed “Warfare.” He came out of the Navy SEALs, and he worked on a lot of my movie sets and I watched him just create a career in this business and manage his stressors from being a Navy SEAL for 20-odd years. It’s not like all veterans need help. Marcus Luttrell, his brother Morgan Luttrell, who became a congressman. There’s a lot of veterans who can get themselves going, but there are a lot who can’t.

These were all reasons. People are like, “Why would you want to make another World War II film? ‘Saving Private Ryan’ was a pretty good film.” Yes, it was. Oh, yes, it was. But this is a different time.

“Deepwater Horizon” and “Patriots Day” are my two favorites of yours, and I’d argue this is your strongest ensemble yet. Are you directing actors better than ever, or did you just have a group doing something you’d never seen?

Maybe a little of both. I do feel like I’m getting more comfortable. I feel fortunate that I’ve been able to have a long career and succeed and fail and learn how to collaborate and find people I trust, and that made me better. So I’m more excited and comfortable just being a filmmaker. I really love it.

With the actors, I really credit the book and Buzz Bissinger, because when a script is born from the truth and you reference two films, “Deepwater” and “Patriots Day,” those were true stories. I was able to go into those worlds and meet the people and find the truth. I love nonfiction because I can find the truth. And once I find the truth, I can hopefully get it on the page and I can bring it to the set and I can talk to the actors and the actors can meet the real people and experience the truth and that helps make their performances more truthful. All of them were just really talented actors who responded to the truth of the story, and that made them lock in a little bit harder.

Ray Nicholson is particularly interesting to me, and I love him very much. I’ve had long talks with him. The whole nepo baby thing. Can you imagine trying to be an actor and your father is Jack Nicholson? Good fucking luck, bro. And you kind of look like your father.

Jack Nicholson is my favorite [actor]. “Cuckoo’s Nest” is my personal favorite film. And what I’ve said to Ray is, what’s so fascinating about where you are now and where you can go is that you have a chance to find your own way and be your own guy. There’s a lot of people who have not seen “One Flew Over the Cuckoo’s Nest” or “Chinatown.” You have your own unique energy. You’ll have that last name, but you are not your father and that gives you the opportunity to do something completely unique. And you do have your father’s genes in you, which is a good thing, because everyone who has not experienced the Jack Nicholson gene gets to get a taste of it, but in a different way. Hold your head up high.

The fact that he’s able to stand on his own two feet as his son is, I think, beautiful. I’m really impressed with how he’s able to own the reality of, yes, I am Jack Nicholson’s son and I am going to have my own career. That’s respect. I tip my hat to that young man.

This one is for me and my sister. “Shocker” is the movie that made us “friends” in the sibling sense because we watched it over 100 times. Are there movies that changed your life in ways their makers might never know?

One that comes to mind is “Billy Jack.” The quote from the movie is, “When I see her humiliated and embarrassed and hurt, well, sir, I just go berserk.” That was the first time I remember, as a little kid, just trembling in a movie theater and I quote that still to this day, all the time.

But I’ll tell you one funny “Shocker” story that I think you might appreciate. There are two things I took from that film. Wes Craven was just an incredibly sweet human being, and it blew my mind that this lovely, sweet, gentle man could have this imagination. That was kind of my first lesson: Don’t judge a book by the cover. I think he was a teacher for a while. Just the most gentle, lovely man and then he would come up with these wild, terrifying stories.

I was making a film in Mississippi, and I went out with the cast to a drive-in and we all went to see “Shocker.” I think Ally Sheedy was in the car, and Phoebe Cates and Kyle Secor, just a bunch of actors. And there’s hundreds of cars. It was an old-school drive-in theater, speaker in the window. And the car next to us had these four teenagers, Southern boys and they’re smoking weed, just clouds of weed and they’re mesmerized by the movie. They’re watching me fight Mitch, and they’re just so stoned and so locked in. I got out of the car, and I sneaked around to their window and I just knocked on it and it’s me. They turned and they looked at me, and they looked at the movie and they looked at me and they freaked out. Just screaming. It was the greatest actor moment of my career. Can you imagine? You’re a 17-year-old in Mississippi, stoned out of your mind, staring at this movie outside at a drive-in and the actor from the movie is in your face.

The “Friday Night Lights” reboot is in development at Peacock, and you’ve called it a complete reinvention. What does Dillon look like in 2026 or 2027?

If it’s a complete reinvention, it probably won’t be Dillon. If you’re going to do a reboot of “Friday Night Lights,” you have to look at what made “Friday Night Lights” work. If you trace that back to its genesis, it’s Buzz Bissinger again. In the mid-’80s, he moved his family to Odessa, Texas, lived with the Permian Panthers football team and moved into the community of Odessa for over a year. He’s an investigative journalist, and he went very deep. If you read the book, it’s about much, much more than football. It’s about community and family and educational systems and racism and love and hatred and winning. There’s some deep themes in that book. That led to the movie, which then led to the TV show, but without the book and without the soul of Bissinger in there, it wouldn’t have worked.

So for us to do a remake, what first has to happen and he knows this, is Buzz has to go into another community in 2026, 2027. It could be Texas, it could be Ohio, it could be Florida, anywhere football is played. It’s a different world right now. We didn’t have these cell phones. So we need to have the origin story done right, and that means before a script is written, there has to be something else written, whether that’s a book or a detailed, long-form piece of journalism. I don’t want to just do it to do it. But what does excite me is if somebody approaches it as a piece of cultural anthropology or a sociological exploration of a community in today’s Americana. Then I think you have the chance of success.

Not really. Little parts. What do you got for me?

Someone online suggested you’d make a great Buddy Ryan, the architect of the 1985 Bears defense. Call Michael Mann.

That’s amazing. Michael’s my mentor. He produced “The Kingdom”; he produced “Hancock.” He saw an early show I did called “Wonderland,” about a psychiatric hospital in New York that was killed instantly by ABC and he responded so well to that. He’s one of my favorite people on planet Earth. He’s a great human being.
