‘The Hunting Wives’ Stars and Creator Break Down Margo and Sophie’s Dangerous Love Affair in Season 2: ‘It’s a F—-ed Up Commitment’ to ‘Move Past’ Murder

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

SPOILER ALERT: This story contains spoilers for Season 2 of “The Hunting Wives,” now streaming on Netflix.

Earlier this month, Texas shocked the nation by electing Democrats to the governorship, the U.S. Senate and several more key positions during this year’s midterm elections. But the Lone Star State’s politics have always been seen as dynamic and complicated to those in the know — like fans of “The Hunting Wives,” Netflix’s juicy, campy drama series about a group of wealthy, gun-loving and sexually fluid women from East Texas.

“I love our show, because you see both sides of this coin, and we’re making fun of each side,” says “The Hunting Wives” star Brittany Snow about the series’ depiction of Texas politics. Co-star Malin Åkerman adds, “We’re not going to say that we had anything to do with it, but there’s all these different people saying, ‘The Hunting Wives’ had something to do with Texas going blue!’ It’s a fun little moment for us right now.”

Åkerman plays Margo Banks, the housewives’ confident ringleader whose Republican husband Jed (Dermot Mulroney) is a favorite to become governor. But, in Season 1, her beautiful house of cards is torn apart with the arrival of Sophie (Snow), an East Coast liberal who is shocked by the dirty underbelly of her new Southern home. Margo is full of secrets that she’s intent on keeping from Sophie, and thinks she’s in the clear when the two begin falling in love (and deep, deep lust). But Season 1 ended with Sophie discovering that Margo had gotten pregnant by a teenager, Brad (George Ferrier), before having an abortion and murdering Brad’s girlfriend, Abby Jackson (Madison Wolfe), for uncovering the truth. The only saving grace for Margo is that Sophie can’t do anything with the information, because when her brother, Kyle (Michael Aaron Milligan), attempts to intimidate Sophie on Margo’s behalf, Sophie hits him with her car and kills him instantly. Now, they’re both in trouble.

Because Sophie hid Kyle’s body and told no one what she did, Season 2 begins with Margo trying to get back into her good graces. Unaware that Kyle is even dead, she keeps calling his number, getting no response until a panicked Sophie uses AI to deepfake Kyle’s voice and keep the illusion up. In person, Sophie tries her best to avoid Margo, but she can’t control herself. So she uses Kyle’s voice to ask Margo whether any of their relationship was genuine. When Margo answers that it was “100% real,” not just a ploy to cover-up the murder, Sophie is finally ready to get back together. She meets Margo at the Banks’ lake house, where they have sex — which is rudely interrupted by Zelda (Dale Dickey), a woman from Margo’s past, who sneaks up and holds a gun point-blank to Margo’s head.

Zelda is the leader of a biker gang in Alba, Margo’s hometown. She suspects Kyle of impregnating and murdering her underage granddaughter, Ellie (Chloe Guidry), and is attacking Margo to get information about Kyle’s whereabouts. Zelda and the gang don’t believe Margo when she says she has no idea where he is until Sophie blurts out that Kyle couldn’t have killed Ellie because she killed him. Zelda pieces together that it was someone in the gang (Cam Gigandet as Gentle John) and shoots him in the head. Soon, the police show up and the gang gets shot to death. Margo and Sophie are escorted home before they can discuss what happened, and Sophie panics, worrying that Margo will tell the police what happened to Kyle. But she doesn’t. After ignoring several calls from Sophie, Margo shows up at Sophie’s apartment and forgives her — they’re even, since Margo is a murderer too. The two begin secretly dating again, but Jed and his staff keep Margo on a tight leash during his campaign for governor. So Sophie hatches up a plan: If Margo publicly admits that she’s had an abortion, she’ll be able to tank Jed’s campaign and leave him for Sophie while drumming up enough support and attention to maintain her wealthy lifestyle. But right before the couple is set to make a TV appearance, Jed tells Margo that he knows she killed Abby and that Sophie killed Kyle, and that she needs to be a “dutiful wife” if she wants to keep herself and her girlfriend out of prison. Margo does still confess to cheating on Jed and having an abortion on live TV, but instead of advocating for women’s rights, she calls herself a murderer and says that prayer has allowed her to find redemption. The move is received positively by Jed’s rightwing base, and Sophie freaks out. That night, she confronts Margo, who explains to Sophie that she did it to protect her and that they can never speak again. Sophie is enraged, and says that she’ll release her recording of Margo confessing to Abby’s murder unless Margo gives her $10 million. In a brief epilogue set two years later, Sophie is uber-rich but estranged from Margo. She receives a phone call from a mysterious number and only hears panicked breathing on the other end; it’s Margo, who is covered in blood.

Snow and Åkerman, in one interview, and series creator Rebecca Cutter, in a separate conversation, spoke to Variety about the absolute insanity playing out in their East Texan world.

Brittany Snow: When I read the first episode and realized that one of the first scenes is her having a panic attack in her car, and then she later starts hearing sounds on the phone that are not there — I realized she’s sort of losing her mind, and that embeds in her reasoning through the entire show. She’s losing her mind because she’s losing the piece that she’s addicted to, which is Margo. So he’s just trying to fix the negativity and the guilt and shame she feels. So she’s trying to save her marriage. Maybe that’ll fix it. Or maybe she’ll run away from Margot, and maybe that’ll fix it. Or maybe she’s gonna move into this tiny house. Nothing seems to be fixing this hole, so she’s in a very psychologically dark place at the beginning of this show.

Malin Åkerman: I don’t like when Margo’s not on top — but it is fun to play. Margo is in the doghouse. She’s not in the main mansion. She has these bikers from her past who are chasing after her brother. Everything that could go wrong is going wrong. She doesn’t have Sophie available at all. She’s not answering any of her calls, so we find her in a really tough position, and we just want her to get her power back and get back to the unapologetic, outrageous Margo.

Snow: The car scene in the rain was a really difficult scene because there had to be so many things at once happening. The push-pull of a toxic relationship: “Please stay in my car. I love you. I also need you to get out. Also, I killed your brother. Also, I’m gonna have a nervous breakdown.” And it’s all those levels while looking really, really terrible on my part. Like a drowned rat.

Åkerman: I loved that that was the first scene where we see them together, because there was so much going on and there are high stakes. Hopefully people feel the same way, like, “Oh my god! Just stay! Kiss! Get together!”

Snow: It was a good scene. That kinetic energy that we have. The anger that I have. You trying to win me over. It’s a lot!

Åkerman: I mean, Margo comes first. She’s always gonna have her own back. But I truly think that somewhere deep inside, this is the closest to a feeling of love that Margo has had for anyone. There’s something about Sophie that really draws her in, and she feels the most herself that she’s ever felt. There’s some version of true love, as well as her survival. She can’t turn that off yet because it’s not safe yet to turn it off, and she has to make sure that she’s not going to get called out. So there’s a big duality there.

Rebecca Cutter: It was reverse engineering to get Sophie and Margo back together. The biker gang is looking for Kyle, and [since he isn’t available], who can Margo go to when she’s threatened? Sophie’s the only one that knows she killed Abby. Sophie’s the only one that knows the real her. So having that threat come into her life, in the form of Zelda and the gang, sends her directly to Sophie. Then Sophie, having to interact with Margo through the AI deepfake, falls in love with her all over again and feels guilty about what she’s done. It was this perfect storm bringing them back together. The other thing of that makes their reunion possible is that Sophie has grown and changed a lot over the two seasons. She goes from being a very weak character who feels powerless in her life to being this really formidable woman who is an equal to Margo. And realizing that they’re both killers and they both have that same drive and instinct makes them fall in love all the more.

Snow: That scene is nuts. That was a nuts shoot. We also didn’t know that the song that was going to be behind it all was going to be, like — [she sings an twangy, upbeat melody] — so we’re acting our asses off, screaming and crying, and then when you see the scene, you’re like, “Wow, it’s kind of funny, actually! Comical!”

Åkerman: You just never know how it’s gonna be edited together. But shooting it was it was a lot, over the course of two or three days. A lot of emotion. Luckily, we shot the two things separately, the intimate scene when Dale Dickey’s character comes in and discovers us, and the living room scene where the shootout happens. It was probably good for us — one mindset to the other. It’s one of my favorite things that Dale Dickey gets to say, with the gun to my head. [While Margo’s head is in between Sophie’s legs, Zelda appears with a gun and says, “Well, lookee lookee what we got here.”]

Snow: The juxtaposition of the show is really encapsulated in that scene. The comedy, the drama, the thriller.

Snow: I hate that scene. Because you really see how desperate Sophie is in that moment. I don’t know if any more veins in my neck and forehead could have popped out. She’s desperate, and she doesn’t know if Margo’s going to forgive her, so the fact that she does is pretty crazy.

Åkerman: I loved that scene. You’re brilliant in it. All the veins. I love it all. I love it all.

Åkerman: It’s a lot, and but I do think that Sophie is the closest thing to family that she has after her brother. We also know that Kyle is very unhinged and unmanageable, and who knows what he may have done to Sophie if she hadn’t have run him over? She didn’t plan on it. There were a lot of factors that she had to think about. She herself is also a murderer, so she kind of understands.

Åkerman: I do think it was a lot to process. But she almost wins that cat-and-mouse chase, because she goes, “Well, now I’m back on top. I get to pull the strings and call the shots.” There are a lot of different elements to why she makes the decision to come in and say, “All right, this is how it’s gonna go. Now I’ve got you under my thumb again.”

Cutter: Margo is, above all else, a survivor and a schemer. In a very practical sense, she recognizes that Sophie has something on her, with Abby Jackson, and she has something on Sophie, with Kyle. It’s like Russia and the United States. It’s a cold war. But now we’re even, so it’s a great place for them to be.

Snow: Sophie’s coming from such a nervous breakdown that any forgiveness feels like such an oasis in her mind, and especially because it comes from Margo. There’s hope in that scene. When you see Kyle’s dead body [get discovered], you realize hope is never going to last long on “Hunting Wives.” But that scene puts us on the same playing field, and alludes to the idea that things are going to be OK in their weird world.

Åkerman: I don’t know if I’d say the purest. Afterwards, I think it gets purer. But in that moment, Margo holds onto a few other things other than loving intentions. There’s the power dynamic where she’s also deciding that this is OK, and she is now in charge. You’re not gonna call me out for killing Abby Jackson.

Snow: Our physicality in that scene is interesting, because you’re sitting there the whole time just watching me, and I’m pacing around, doing a tap dance. You’re just like, “Dance, monkey.”

Åkerman: But afterwards, I loved that part of filming. We love being in love. That is the closest we get to seeing what a real relationship could be like for these two. We go, “Oh my God, they might make it! They might run off into the sunset together, and everyone else gets killed off.” That’s not what happens. But we get to see a little more vulnerability from Margo, and I thought that was a lovely part of Season 2.

Cutter: It was important to show that the relationship is more than just sex, and that there is this deeper commitment that they’re making to each other to move past this, even though it’s kind of a fucked up commitment. I remember talking in the writers’ room about how Mandy became Margo not by dwelling on the past, but by always being willing to move on, shed her skin and transform. “We’re just gonna take this drink and act like nothing ever happened.” That’s so Margo. That’s what she has to do. She cannot dwell in any past trauma.

Snow: I think she can compartmentalize in a way that’s scary, especially when it comes to her son and her family and what she wants. She sees Margot as a beacon of who she wants to be and who she wants to be with. But she still has to be a good mother, and needs to divorce Graham. So she’s wrestling with the decision to, maybe for the first time in her life, choose herself. And that feels really good to her. She’s going to take a bubble bath in the middle of the day and show up for her girlfriend and do all these things. And then it comes crashing down.

Åkerman: Yeah, I think she had a hopeful moment where she thought maybe this could be their happy ending. There was a glimmer of hope, because that’s the thing that initially attracted her to Sophie. She’s bright and smart, and Margo noticed that about her in that very first scene when we see them together at the NRA party.

Åkerman: I may not have a great answer for you. I think it’s more about the suspense, because we want to believe she’s about to say this, and to give it that really great twist at the end. That was the one scene that I had a lot of trouble wrapping my head around, like, “Why would she admit that?”

Snow: But don’t you think she’s taking her power back? Like, “You can’t cage me, Jed. I’m still gonna say what I want.”

Åkerman: I guess you’re right. Thank you for that!

Cutter: It’s partly to mislead the audience, also because Sophie said something to her on the phone call earlier: “You could be a hero to half the country.” Margo is a very shrewd politician and understands that if she comes forward and gets ahead of it and frames it a certain way, it could actually add to her own power. She plays a card that could have backfired, but we see that it works — and I will be shocked if, someday, a Republican politician doesn’t play this card. Mark my words.

Snow: Trying to cover up another murder, which always brings them together. I mean, we leave off this season with a cliffhanger, where it’s open to the audience whether Sophie feels like she might hear Margo’s voice or maybe she doesn’t. And Margo’s covered in blood, so we get to see these women in totally different places, physically and mentally, and they’re on a level that makes them feel like equal adversaries in a lot of ways. I just hope for Sophie, in Season 3, to take a chill pill. Maybe some Xanax! To sit in her power and not be so anxious all day. I feel like it’s coming.

Snow: I think she’s going to own herself.

Snow: We’re gonna watch, and we’re gonna like it!

Cutter: I don’t watch that much reality TV, but I think I would have to try it. Imitation is the sincerest form of flattery.
