Review: Oboz Katabatic Storm GTX Winter Hiking Boots

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

The Oboz Katabatic Storm GTX is a new waterproof and insulated hiking boot from Oboz, known for its popular 400g Bridger 10″ and 200g Bridger 8″ insulated mid winter hiking boots. Modeled after cross-country ski boots, the Katabatic Storm GTX is a lightly insulated mid-height boot insulated with aerogel-based Solarcore bonded to a Gore-Tex liner, providing waterproofing and warmth without added bulk. The result is an active winter boot with about 80% of the warmth of Oboz 200g insulated Bridger boots, that’s robust enough for use with lite traction aids like Kahtoola Microspikes or Hillsound Trail Crampons.

If you’re a hiker, but you don’t hike in winter, you’re missing out on a lot of fun. Gone are the bugs, roots, and rocks that can make three-season hiking unpleasant. The trails are less crowded, and there are lots of new skills to learn and gear to play with. In the past 15 years, winter footwear has gotten lighter and lighter due to advances in insulation technology, material science, and manufacturing practices. The introduction of lightweight traction aids, like microspikes, was a real inflection point because they could be used with footwear that has lightweight, flexible soles, requiring no special training like rigid mountaineering boots and crampons.

Several companies, including La Sportiva, Hoka, and Salomon, have attempted to introduce lightweight mids and trail runners for winter use with mixed success, because there are so many different types of winter hiking outings, from trail running and snowshoeing to light mountaineering, all targeting different user types, temperature ranges, complementary gear types, and trail conditions.

With that in mind, the Oboz Katabatic Storm GTX is a winter hiking boot, not a trail-running shoe, because its sole is too rigid. It is lightly insulated, with about 80% of the insulation of a 200g Thinsulate boot, and is good for temperatures down to about 15 degrees as long as you are moving and not standing still. It has enough front toe-kick rigidity to work with traction aids like Kahtoola Microspikes or Hillsound Trail Crampons without causing friction on top of the toes, which can lead to blisters or cold toes because of reduced circulation. The boots have a gaiter hook, making the boots compatible with gaiters. The outsole has lugs that provide good traction on snow and frozen slush when you’re not wearing a traction aid.

Fitwise, the Katabatic Storm is true to size. That said, they’re patterned on cross-country ski boots, with a quick-lace system and a zippered top cover, which makes them a little time-consuming to put on and hard to adjust if you use an articulated lacing technique that requires different pressure at different points along the lacing track. I’m not a huge fan of either, but they fit me well enough, hold my foot securely, and don’t cause any hot spots inside.

I was a little leery of the Katabatic Storm GTX when I learned they only have a warmth value of about 80% of a 200g insulated winter hiking boot. The insulation they do have is only located in targeted areas such as underfoot, over the toe, and through the tongue, but not along the sides and around the heel. The shoe is designed for movement and managing the heat it generates, but not for standing around.

Why would that be an issue? As a winter hiker, I generally recommend buying insulated boots that will keep your feet warm when you’re not moving and are standing still.  This is especially true if you hike in the backcountry where you may have to wait a long time, say 8 hours or more, for search and rescue to come and help your injured hiking partner while you wait with them, or if you have to slowly walk an injured hiker out into the wee hours of the morning. That’s not the time to wear a lightly insulated boot designed only for movement. That might sound overly conservative, but I have participated in backcountry winter rescues that ran into the early hours of the following day, and we were all very glad we were wearing heavily insulated boots.

The insulated Oboz Katabatic Storm GTX boot is lightweight and comfortable, even on challenging hikes that require traction. I just wish they were available with more insulation that’s equivalent to the 400g Oboz 10″ Bridger insulated winter hiking boots I wear today. If they were, I’d wear them on every winter hike. As such, they’re ideal for shoulder season weather and spring conditions where there’s ice and slush on trails, but temperatures are warmer during the day. Because they are only lightly insulated, I reserve them for winter hikes on warmer days and close to accessible trailheads where local SAR crews can muster and arrive relatively quickly in an emergency.
