Brand statement from prison claiming his innocence ‘appalling’ and could breach rules, says No 10 – UK politics live

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

Brand’s wife Laura read out a message from him on their YouTube show

Peter Walker is a Guardian political correspondent.

Downing Street has described the broadcasting of a message from Russell Brand from prison claiming his innocence as “appalling”.

As the BBC reports, at the weekend Brand’s wife Laura read out a message from him on their Sunday Service show on YouTube in which the convicted rapist claimed he was “not guilty” of the offences for which he was jailed.

Asked about this at the daily lobby briefing, the PM’s spokesperson said:

double quotation markThis is clearly appalling and is being urgently investigated. We expect action to be taken if he is found to have broken the rules.

Prisoners are strictly banned from making statements that cause distress to victims and speaking to the media is only permitted in exceptional circumstances. Prisoners cannot get around these rules by having someone else publish their comments.

It is understood that, if a prisoner provides an offending statement of this kind to a relative knowing that it will be published, they are deemed to have broken the rules.

Hamish Falconer told the UK-EU parliamentary partnership assembly that the TCA (trade and cooperation agreement – the post-Brexit trade deal with the EU) has now been in force for five years.

The rest would provide an opportunity to” cut costs, support industries and create more opportunities for young people”, he said.

double quotation markAt the heart of what our teams have been doing are the detailed negotiations on the three new agreements we committed to last year – an agrifood trade deal, an agreement to link our emissions trading systems and a youth experience scheme.

These important deals will boost our respective economies. They will cut red tape, help our businesses grow and provide opportunities to young people denied since Brexit.

Over the course of this week, Maroš [Šefčovič] and I will be working hard to conclude those agreements and we hope to have good news for you come Friday summit.

The UK government will then bring forward primary legislation to implement those agreements.

We will also discuss a shared approach to protecting our economic security to ensure we do not unintentionally harm each other, and instead tackle shared economic challenges together.

That should be the floor, not the ceiling, of our ambition.

We want to go further to restore a higher level of growth and prosperity.

So beyond this summit, as the prime minister said, we will begin to look at options for the future and opening a national discussion on these options.

And that is obviously also a conversation we will want to have with our EU partners and friends about how Britain and the EU can work more closely together for our common security, prosperity and opportunity.

Hamish Falconer, the minister for EU relations, and his EU counterpart, Maroš Šefčovič, are both speaking and taking questions at a meeting of the UK-EU parliamentary partnership assembly in the House of Commons today. There is a live feed here. Falconer and Šefčovič have been heavily involved in the EU deals being announced on Friday, although it is not clear how much they will want to reveal this afternoon. Falconer started by saying he was appointed to his job while he was on honeymoon. He said he would not describe himself as married to Šefčovič, but they spend so much time together “it sometimes feels like the relationship is indeed very close,” he joked.

Downing Street has not ruled out the UK joining the European Union’s pilot scheme to send rejected asylum seekers to Rwanda, the Press Association reports. PA says:

double quotation markSeveral EU countries are reportedly set to announce a deal to send rejected asylum seekers to the east African country.

The government will watch the scheme “with interest” as part of efforts to find “innovative approaches” to kerb illegal migration, No 10 said.

The scandal-hit Rwanda deportation plan introduced under the Conservatives was axed by Keir Starmer immediately after he became prime minister.

It cost Britain £700m despite only four volunteers being sent to Kigali, then-home secretary Yvette Cooper said, as she branded the policy the “most shocking waste of taxpayer money I have ever seen”.

But the new EU pilot is said to differ from the Tory plan because it will only apply to asylum seekers who have exhausted all of their options, rather than all asylum seekers who arrive.

Asked about the scheme, the PM’s spokesperson said: “I’ve seen the reports on this. Obviously, it’s for the EU to make any announcements. I’m not going to get ahead of that.

“More broadly, though, you’ve got our position. The UK, like other European countries, is exploring innovative approaches to tackle illegal migration, including returns hubs, and will continue working with international partners to strengthen returns, remove those with no right to be here and restore control to our borders.”

Asked to rule out the UK joining the plan, the spokesperson said the UK would be watching the new initiative “with interest, learning from what went on in the past”.

While Andy Burnham may be nervous about making the debate about the future relationship with the EU a simple one about whether or not to rejoin (see 10.59am), his opponents are happy to have this argument. Brexiters have already launched a campaign called Stay Out. It is led by Oliver Lewis, who was deputy to David Frost when Frost was Boris Johnson’s chief Brexit negotiator.

Lewis was on the Today programme this morning. Making the case for the UK to stay out, he focused more on the argument that this would be a breach of promises made to the public (including by Andy Burnham during the Makerfield byelection campaign) than on the notion that Brexit has been a great success. In this, he was broadly aligned with what Kemi Badenoch has been saying.

But Lewis also went further than the Conservative party (see 12.20pm), explicitly criticising the deal set to be announced by Burnham on Friday. He said:

double quotation markIt’s always a good idea to promote closer cooperation with all of our allies, and there’s always more that you can do to facilitate trade with friends and neighbours. However, what I disagree with is a settlement that will involve us paying hundreds of millions of pounds into the EU and involve the imposition of huge amounts of red tape on all sorts of businesses across the country … I think it’s a really bad step backwards.

Lewis said that at the general election Labour had “very firm red lines” about not joining the single market or the customs union. But the deal to be announced on Friday would be “an attempt to water this down”, he claimed. He said that was “deeply concerning”.

At the lobby briefing, the PM’s spokesperson also played down the prospect of the government introducing a staggered age of consent. (See 11.30am.) Asked about the proposal, the PM’s spokesperson said the government would “always look at what more can be done to protect young people” but that changes in this area of law were “complex”. He also referred reporters to what Louise Haigh, the first secretary of state, said on this over the weekend. She told the Sunday Times:

double quotation markIt clearly would be a massive step to change the age of consent, but it deserves proper consideration and scrutiny.

At her press conference, Kemi Badenoch faced various questions about Brexit, and Andy Burnham’s reset deal with the EU. She dismissed Burnham’s plan to debate a closer relationship as a “distraction”. Here are some of her arguments.

Badenoch said Burnham’s deal with the EU was just a “big nothingburger” until the details were published, and she repeatedly described his call for a debate on the future relationship with the EU as a “distraction”. She said:

double quotation markAndy Burnham is not the one who started this EU reset. Quite a lot of what Keir Starmer went and did at the last EU reset has not come to pass … People have forgotten, but all he managed to do was give away our fish. Everything else is still unsettled.

So the fact is Labour going to the EU and talking about a big reset moment is a big nothingburger until we see what’s come back and when it’s actually implemented.

Until then, it’s just a talking point and a distraction from the day to day issues we need to deal with now.

(Badenoch may be keen to dismiss the debate about the future relationship with the EU as a distraction because, given polls show most people now regard it as a failure, the Conservatives, who implemented Brexit, feel awkward being forced to defend it.)

Badenoch backed David Cameron’s argument that the problems facing Britain have nothing to do with Brexit. She said:

double quotation markAndy Burnham still has not learned the lessons of the past. He is still living in 2016.

The problems that this country faces today have nothing to do with being in or out of the EU. France was literally on fire last month. It’s in the EU. It has a small boat crisis, as do many other European countries. They are in the EU.

We need to stop obsessing about Europe and whether to be in or out, and actually get on with doing the things that we are elected to do – sort out the economy, fund our defence, cut welfare, get jobs for people, make sure businesses can create those jobs. We don’t need to be in the EU to do any of that.

This is again a distraction from a Prime Minister who doesn’t know what to do.

She said Burnham wanted the opposition to talk about Brexit “because if we’re talking about that, then we’re not talking about the government’s failures”. She added: “I’m not falling for that trick.”

She said she agreed with Boris Johnson when he told the Daily Telegraph that, in a second referendum, people would again vote to be out of the EU. Asked about his comments, she said:

double quotation markI wasn’t an MP when we had the referendum. And I remember everyone assuming that remain would win and all the polls and stats showed remain winning. And then something different happened.

So all of the people bringing out stats and polls saying people would vote to go back into the EU – I would ask them to go and look at the news from ten years ago and familiarise themselves with that.

She refused to say whether she would support or oppose the deal Burnham is set to agree on Friday. She said:

double quotation markI speak as a former trade secretary. [Burnham] laid out all of his negotiating tactics and all of his asks [before agreeing the EU deal]. You never, ever do that when you’re negotiating because the other party can squeeze out more concessions.

Take something like youth mobility, for instance. I support youth mobility depending on the conditions. I negotiated youth mobility for countries like Australia … The issue is not youth mobility. It’s what are the terms that are going with it?

It’s the detail of the deal that we will be looking at. So simply saying, ‘Yes, we’re going to reverse it, no, we’re not going to reverse it’, let’s actually see what he brings up.

What we will always do is what is right for the country. I’m not interested in, you know, attacking bits and pieces of a reset deal that I haven’t seen. The bottom line is that Andy Burnham is not the one who started this EU reset.

Following the Russell Brand conviction, and the revelations about David Walliams, there have been calls for the government to introduce a “staggered” age of consent, which would make it illegal for adults over a certain age to have sex with 16 and 17-year-olds. In an interview yesterday Pat McFadden, the work and pensions secretary, said the government would look at this idea.

In her press conference this morning, Kemi Badenoch said that, while she could see the case for this, she did not think it was very practical.

double quotation markWhen I was 16, I remember reading the Peter Stringfellow case, a 55-year-old man dating a 16-year-old, and I thought it was the most disgusting thing ever.

I do think ... that there’s some common sense there in terms of a staggered age of consent. My issue is with how we will enforce it.

Badenoch also suggested it would be odd for the government to be raising the age of consent in some circumstances while also lowering the voting age to 16. She went on:

double quotation markPoliticians need to focus on building capacity, building prison spaces, renting spaces abroad, which is one of our policies, rather than creating more laws that cannot be enforced …

[The government is] all over the place. Let’s have an age of maturity and stick to it, and let’s have some coherence in policy.

When Andy Burnham told the Labour conference that he wanted to start a debate on a new relationship with the EU, because Brexit had done more harm than good, he raised the possibility that this could lead to Labour proposing to rejoin at some point after the next election.

But some ministers and Labour MPs are deeply unhappy about that prospect, believing that there would have to be a referendum and that this would be deeply divisive, and there are some signs that Burnham (who is on record as saying he would like the UK to rejoin in his lifetime) is starting to scale back his ambitions.

In his speech to the Welsh Labour conference on Saturday, Burnham repeated the argument he made in September about Brexit having done more harm than good. But he focused on making Brexit better, not overturning it. At one point in the speech he said:

double quotation markI don’t believe Brexit and the deal that they got has been good enough for Britain. I think we’ve lost more than we’ve gained over the last 10 years.

And actually, I think the Conservatives gave Britain a harsher and harder Brexit than people thought they were getting when they voted in 2016.

double quotation markPeople’s jobs depend on working together and collaborating.

And that’s our approach, and it will always be our approach. People working together in unions, trade unions, but also unions of nations, and that’s what makes us who we are.

And yes, working with the European Union, as I said, but at the same time, respecting the red lines in our manifesto and respecting the referendum, the referendum result.

The reference to “respecting the referendum result” could have just meant not rejoining before the next election. But given that this was a manifesto red line, and that he had already spoken about respecting that, he seemed to imply something more.

And today Burnham has announced three tests for his Brexit reset. (See 9.15am.) Although these are only tests for the deal he is announcing on Friday, not for the Brexit policy he will announce at the next election, the focus on improving the Brexit deal and bringing it “closer to what people thought they were voting for in 2016” might be a sign of where his thinking is heading.

In interviews this morning, Ed Miliband, the foreign secretary, also focused on the government wanting to make Brexit work better for the economy. He told Sky News:

double quotation markLook, here’s my starting point. My constituents, people across the country, voted for a range of reasons for Brexit, but one of them was one of the biggest, certainly for my constituents, was to make our economy better, positive economic change. We haven’t had that. The economy has got worse. And nobody really thinks this Brexit deal is a good deal.

On ITV’s Good Morning Britain, Ed Balls did not ask Miliband about Russell Brand but he did ask him repeatedly if he wanted the UK to rejoin the EU. (See 9.15am.) Miliband and Balls worked very closely together for many years and Balls was clearly assuming that the honest answer would be “Yes”. But Miliband said that he would not give his own personal view because he was abiding by collective cabinet responsibility. Balls said that Andy Burnham has been happy to say he wants the UK to rejoin in his lifetime, while John Healey (who is 10 years older) has said that he does not expect that in his lifetime. Balls asked Miliband what he expected to happen in his lifetime. Miliband still refused to answer. Balls urged him to be braver and less restrained – “Let your hair down, have a good time” - but Miliband still would not engage with the question.

The Tories have pledged to halt “screen-based” exams and cut coursework in a bid to limit the use of AI by schoolchildren if the party returns to power, the Press Association reports. PA says:

double quotation markNew guidelines for teachers would seek to ensure that laptops and tablets are not the default in classrooms, the party said.

Shadow education secretary Laura Trott said Britain had “sleepwalked into a situation where nearly a million children spend almost every lesson” on a device despite health professionals warning of the dangers of excessive screen time.

Under Conservative proposals, the amount of coursework in school assessment will be “minimised” to make sure the next generation of workers are “AI-resilient”, the party said.

“Screen-based” exams would also be stopped, with screen time for secondary school pupils limited to a maximum of two hours a day, including during homework.

In primary schools, devices would only be used at a “minimum” and where relevant in the curriculum, such as for computing, the party has said.

Kemi Badenoch and Trott will be speaking about this at their press conference starting shortly.

Ed Miliband has said he will not resign from the cabinet if the government approves the Rosebank oilfield, despite his previous condemnation of the scheme.

In an interview on the Today programme, it was put to Miliband that it would be logical for him to resign given that he is on record as saying in 2023 that Rosebank would be “climate vandalism” and a colossal waste of taxpayers’ money.

Asked if he would resign if Labour approves the project, Miliband replied: “No. Definitely not.”

double quotation markI obey collective responsibility. Miatta Fahnbulleh [the energy secretary] will make the decision on this. I know it’s a quasi-judicial decision. So she looks at all the evidence, and then reaches a conclusion. I’m not going to second-guess [her].

Miliband said he supported the decision she made to allow the Jackdaw gasfield to go ahead.

Pressed again on Rosebank, he would not say whether he had changed his mind since he condemned the proposal in 2023. But he said: “I’m a member of the government and I will abide by collective responsibility on this.”

As Ben Quinn reports, around 75 Labour MPs have already signed a private letter saying they are opposed to Rosebank going ahead and it is thought that number could rise to more than 100.

Good morning. There are two issues that will dominate the political news this week – the EU reset, and the Alan Milburn report on youth unemployment – and this morning there are new lines on both of these issues.

Andy Burnham has set out “three tests” for the deal that he is due to agree with the EU at a summit on Friday. Tonight he is giving the traditional prime ministerial speech to the lord mayor’s banquet in London, which by convention focuses on foreign policy, and, according to the overnight briefing from Downing Street, he will announce three tests. No 10 says:

double quotation markAhead of this week’s UK-EU Summit, the prime minister will set out three tests for any agreement:

-First, it must be consistent with the manifesto and the government’s red lines.

-Second, it must improve the Brexit deal and bring it closer to what people thought they were voting for in 2016, rather than the hard Brexit that no one ever seriously argued for in the referendum.

-Third, it must pass the Makerfield test by delivering real, tangible improvements in people’s everyday lives, particularly in communities that have felt neglected for too long.

That means cutting costs for businesses, easing pressure on prices for consumers and creating more opportunities for young people.

If the agreement delivers against these tests, the prime minister will sign it, demonstrating a new approach based on cooperation rather than confrontation.

There is no real doubt about whether the Friday agreement – which will largely focus on food export rules, but which will also cover energy policy (emissions trading rules) and a youth mobility scheme – will pass these tests. Much of it was already agreed when Keir Starmer was PM, and it is more or less a done deal. But Burnham’s “three tests” are interesting because they provide some clues as to how he will approach his plan to have a wider debate about a closer relationship with the EU.

In his speech, Burnham will also restate his view that Brexit has not worked – using a new simile. He will say:

double quotation markIt’s like the Emperor’s new clothes – where no one is prepared to say the obvious truth. Well, I say it as I see it. And to be frank, the Emperor has nothing on. The Brexit we were given has not worked. We have lost more than we gained.

Charlie Moloney has more on the Burnham speech here.

Alan Milburn is expected to say that employer national insurance for workers under the age of 25 should be cut in his report published tomorrow, the BBC is reporting. Many of Milburn’s recommendations have already been made public, but this is new, and it is likely to be welcomed by Kemi Badenoch, who is holding a press conference later, because the Tories have repeatedly been saying that Rachel Reeves’s decision to raise employer national insurance has been bad for jobs.

As Alexandra Topping reports, in an article for the Guardian, Burnham has pledged in a Guardian article to intervene as early as childhood to tackle youth unemployment.

Ed Miliband, the foreign secretary, has been giving interviews this morning. It is relatively unusual for Miliband to be put up for a full interview round and, on Europe and the Milburn report, his remarks were not especially newsworthy. But, on the Today programme, Nick Robinson did get a good line out of him when he asked him about Russell Brand, and Miliband’s decision to do a high-profile interview with Brand during the 2015 election campaign – when Brand had not yet been accused of rape, but when he was already widely viewed as disreputable.

Last week Ed Balls, who was shadow chancellor at the time, twisted the knife by telling his podcast that he had warned Miliband not to go ahead with the “ridiculous” interview. Balls is now a presenter on Good Morning Britain and this morning he was interviewing his old boss and colleague. Sadly, though, he did not ask about Brand.

On the Today programme, when Robinson asked about Balls’s comments last week and put it to Miliband that in 2015 he should have listened to his shadow chancellor, Miliband replied:

double quotation markLet me first say, Russell Brand’s crimes are heinous and appalling, and all of my sympathies are with the victims of Russell Brand, who have shown incredible bravery.

On the question of that interview, I’ve said … that if I had known of these allegations against Russell Brand, the allegations which he’s now being convicted [of], I would never have done that interview, and I deeply regret it.

Miliband claimed that he did not know at the time about Balls’s reservations about the interview going ahead, but he said Balls was “obviously right” about Brand being weird and untrustworthy.

But Miliband went on to defend doing the interview. He said:

double quotation markLet me just say one thing, because I do want to one thing you said.

Russell Brand was going around telling people not to vote in the general election.

I was not endorsing Russell Brand. I was taking him on and saying he was completely wrong in saying that people shouldn’t vote, that democracy didn’t matter and all those things. But as I say, I deeply regret doing that interview.
