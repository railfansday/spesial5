He's savoured silverware, snubbed the 49ers and set Derek McInnes on the path to managerial success... but all former St Johnstone chairman Geoff Brown wanted was to make the people of Perth proud

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Published: 17:30 EST, 27 November 2026 | Updated: 17:32 EST, 27 November 2026

The child is the father to many traits that inhabit the man.

Geoff Brown walked into his home more than 70 years ago to a mother crying because she feared she could not put bread on the table. Now, at 83, he knows that his children, grandchildren and great grandchildren will never suffer that anguish.

He was a child who lived in a cramped house with the family of eight at one time sharing three rooms. He made his business by building large family homes and constructing a huge one for himself.

He was a football player and coach in his childhood and as a young adult. He then bought a football team as a successful businessman and his unflinching tenure ensured it would survive and then prosper. He was born in St Johnstouns Nursing Home in 1943 and later would own St Johnstone.

As a child, too, he had a working horse, Prince. As a multimillionaire, he had a string of racehorses. He now focuses on providing horses and hosting events for Riding for the Disabled.

The foundations of his future were laid in his childhood.

Geoff Brown was chairman at St Johnstone for 38 years and retains a deep bond with the club

The most public achievement is his stewardship of St Johnstone. The club, simply, would not be still playing if Brown had not stepped in and run it for 38 years.

‘If I knew then what I later knew then I would never have got involved,’ he says briskly of his takeover in 1986. ‘No one would have touched St Johnstone with a bargepole.’

Brown, then, has lived a full life but its broad scope can shimmer with odd, illuminating moments. His conversation is serious but never dull. He tells of how the mobile phone bill of Derek McInnes was evidence he was an excellent manager, how his grandmother was his driving force, and how football made him indulgent without measure.

He sets out his philosophy in simple terms. The son of a struggling market gardener, he says: ‘People are what matter. All my life I have wanted to provide for my family.’

The Browns, with six children, lived in a house with no bathroom. There were two bedrooms for the children. The parents slept in the living room.

‘Even today, at the age of 83, I have a vivid memory of being 10 years old and seeing my mother in tears because we didn’t have money for bread.’

Brown and his son Steve, who took over as chairman, celebrate double cup success in 2021

The teenage Brown left school, became a joiner, started a business and built a portfolio of interests that all stemmed from his hard work and ability to make a deal.

He insists his greatest skills are ‘to be observant and have commonsense’. The first trait is exemplified by his aside when assessing his former manager, McInnes.

‘His phone bill during the summer was twice as much as any other manager we ever had,’ he says.

‘What does that tell you? Yes, he was working away trying to get players in and seeing what was going on. He never stopped.

‘Football is gossip. If you know what is going on then you are a step ahead. Derek was ready on day one of the season.’

Paul Sturrock, a firm friend, remains his favourite Saints manager but his appraisal of McInnes is stunning. Asked about the task at Rangers, he says: ‘I don’t see another manager in Scotland who could do the job he will do. He has a positive mindset. He makes decisions and then gets on with it.’

There is more than a bit of that in Brown’s personality. ‘Yes, I never had five-year plans or any ultimate strategy,’ he says. ‘I just get up in the morning and deal with what is in front of me.’

Brown is full of admiration for the managerial talents of Derek McInnes, whom he hired in 2007

This energy took him to St Johnstone. He made it clear from his first interest in the club that he was not going to be a collegiate manager. Brown was going to run it his way. ‘There was no way I was going to become involved unless I had complete control,’ he writes in his autobiography.

The club’s substantial debts emerged quickly. ‘I hadn’t seen the books and the directors hadn’t told me how bad things were. It soon became clear what a mess the club was in financially.’

He wrote a cheque for £100,000 immediately. ‘Aye, if I knew then what I know now…’

His decades at the helm of St Johnstone, in bad times and in glorious moments, have given him stark lessons. ‘With football it is money, money, money,’ he says.

The commonsense, he admits, was an early casualty. He had promised the players a communal bonus of £250,000 if they finished fourth and gained European competition in the 1998/99 season.

‘They then asked for £500,000 if they finished third and I said: “Okay, they will never do that”. Of course, they did and then we made nothing near that in Europe.’

Brown looks out over the club's old Muirton Park home before the move to McDiarmid Park

In the qualifying round for the UEFA Cup, the Saints beat Finnish side VOS but were beaten in the first round proper by Monaco.

‘I felt I was too soft in football,’ says Brown, ‘As I say, it was all about money and people can get swept along.’

He sidesteps any praise for the Scottish Cup win in 2014 and the domestic cup double in 2021, pointing out they occurred under the reign of his son, Steve. He pays tribute, too, to Stewart Duff, who was a stalwart as managing director in those times.

Yet Brown was a constant presence and a huge influence at the club until he sold it to Adam Webb in 2024. The deal was done on a handshake and Brown later learned that the 49ers group, who subsequently took over Rangers, were keen to talk. ‘There were no discussions,’ he says. ‘I shook Adam’s hand and a deal is a deal. I would not have talks with anyone else once I had given my word.’

He adds: ‘One thing is odd, though. Adam had to give up his share in Cambridge before the SFA would allow him to progress with the deal. Yet the 49ers still have a share in Leeds.’

He worries about Scottish football. ‘It’s difficult for clubs. They are all in tough positions,’ he says. This is primarily in reference to finances but he has deep concerns about the dearth of young players coming through.

Brown with new club chairman and owner Adam Webb, who bought the Perth outfit in 2024

Asked what he would do first if he was at the Scottish Football Association, he answers quickly: ‘I would reintroduce primary school football. That is where all the great players learned. I ran an under-13 team at St Madoes and that was the progression for primary schools.

‘The club academies have not worked. Players are rejected too early and for the wrong reasons. Someone who is small at ten can be tall at 15. Players progress at different rates and once there was that path from school to boys’ club to Juniors or above.’

The problem is visible. ‘I watched three games recently: Switzerland v Scotland, Wales v Portugal and Motherwell v Rangers. Wales were much better than us, good to watch. In the Motherwell game, there was not a single Scottish player on the park,’ he says.

He has other interests to comfort him. He is a keen golfer and does a bit of shooting. He also has retained his interest in horses. He no longer has thoroughbreds in training but has devoted time and money for disabled people, particularly children, having a place to ride horses.

In 2014, he created a charity and refurbished a large barn at Castle Farm, Kinfauns, to be fully compliant with the needs of Riding For the Disabled.

Brown proudly shows the Princess Royal around his Kinfauns stable in Perthshire in 2016

He and his family never took a wage out of St Johnstone. The money for the riding at Castle Farm is generated by Brown. The horses are his.

So what was his reward for decades in sport? Was it the joy of being in control at a football club?

‘No, that was a bit of a bind, to be honest. It took far more time out of my life than I ever thought,’ he says.

But surely providing pleasure to generations of Saints fans, giving them days of glory in finals and seeing them play in Europe, that must mean something?

‘The thing I have always wanted was to give something back to the people of Perthshire,’ he says. But he does not expand.

Horses have played a central role in Brown life and he helps run Riding for the Disabled charity

In the book there is a poignant scene of Brown watching a disabled girl being helped from a horse back into her wheelchair.

‘Aye, I always remember her smile,’ he says to me. ‘That is payment in full.’

Geoff Brown: always observant but blessed with more than commonsense.
