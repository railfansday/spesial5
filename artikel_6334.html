Scientists just documented Bolivia’s first Amazon river dolphins, though local fishers knew about them all along

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Scientists just confirmed Bolivia’s first Amazon river dolphins after three sightings along the Acre River at Cobija, even though local fishers knew about them all along.

On the Acre River, in the Bolivian Amazon, fishers have been seeing pink dolphins for years. Until recently, scientists had no confirmed record of even one sighting.

The first confirmed record came in April 2025, when two pink river dolphins appeared in the river at the city of Cobija and a biologist photographed one of them.

In a new study, researchers describe that sighting and two later ones as the first documented evidence in Bolivia of the Amazon river dolphin, often called the pink river dolphin.

The finding extends the species’ known range into a river where scientists have been suggesting since the 1970s that it might live.

The biologist was Ximena Morales Mamani, the study’s lead author. She is with the Shimanú Amazon Conservation Program in Cobija.

Her co-authors are from Shimanú, the Universidad Mayor de San Andrés in La Paz, the group Juventud Proactiva de la Amazonia, and the Wildlife Conservation Society.

So far, the team has photographs, video, and fishers’ accounts but no genetic tests. That means scientists still have to confirm which species these dolphins are.

The April sighting came during the City Nature Challenge, a yearly event held in the last week of that month.

People in cities around the world photograph and record the wildlife near them. They upload the results to iNaturalist, a citizen science website.

The 2025 event was the tenth, and the first that Cobija joined. On April 27, two dolphins were seen in the city’s Junín neighborhood.

Morales Mamani photographed one of them while it was near the riverbank.

In an interview with Earth.com, the biologist said she trained in Cobija and has lived and worked there for many years. Adding to what is known about the region’s wildlife, she said, “is especially meaningful to me.”

The Shimanú program is a group of young volunteers from different professional backgrounds, led by biologists, she added.

Fishers recorded the next two sightings. On November 28, 2025, a local television channel broadcast video that fishers had filmed near Puerto Alto.

On May 3, 2026, fishers posted images on social media of two dolphins near the Frontera neighborhood.

Together, the three records show that dolphins were in the Acre River during two rainy seasons in a row.

Until now, Bolivia’s known river dolphin was the Bolivian river dolphin, which is found in the Mamoré and Iténez river basins. Many specialists count it as a separate species.

Rapids and waterfalls on the upper Madeira River, in Brazil, separate that dolphin from Amazon river dolphins. The barrier has limited breeding between the two.

The Acre River doesn’t have a barrier like that. It flows into the Purús River, which flows into the main channel of the Amazon.

Because of that open route, scientists suggested as early as 1973 that Amazon river dolphins might be in the Acre.

Robert Wallace, a senior conservation scientist at the Wildlife Conservation Society and a co-author of the new study, raised the same possibility in 2010.

Local residents reported dolphins in the river from time to time, but none of those reports came with evidence that scientists could check.

The open route is also the team’s reason for calling these animals Amazon river dolphins. The authors wrote that the species should be confirmed with genetic tests or a study of the animals’ anatomy.

The record adds to what is known about Bolivia’s mammals, Morales Mamani told Earth.com, and shows “there is still much to discover in Pando,” the department that includes Cobija.

In May and June 2026, the team surveyed 34 fishers who work along the Acre River in three areas of Cobija. The survey was designed to record their local knowledge of dolphins and other river animals.

Some of the fishers had worked on the river for a few months, and others for more than 30 years.

Only five of the 34 said they had never seen a river dolphin. Twenty of the fishers, or 58.8%, had seen dolphins between one and five times.

The fishers’ answers agreed with the photographs and video. Dolphins may be in the river more often than three records suggest, the authors wrote.

Asked how many dolphins they saw at a time, 44.1% of the fishers said two, which was the most common answer. Another 20.6% said one.

The team noted when a sighting took place only if a fisher mentioned the timing unprompted.

“What surprised us most was learning that fishers had been observing these dolphins repeatedly for many years. One account even describes a first sighting more than 30 years ago,” Morales Mamani said.

About two-thirds of the fishers, 64.7%, said they see dolphins more often in the rainy season, which runs from October to May. All three recorded sightings fell in those months.

In the middle section of the Acre, the water level rises and falls by 39 to 49 feet (12 to 15 meters) with the seasons. That’s about the height of a four-story building.

The authors wrote that Amazon river dolphins may regularly use parts of the Acre, especially in the rainy season. The dolphins can get there because the Acre connects to the wider Amazon basin.

The river also ran unusually high in both rainy seasons covered by the records, which may partly explain why no records existed before, they added.

Several fishers also told the team they had begun seeing dolphins more often after floods that affected Cobija and other border communities, Morales Mamani said.

She called the fishers’ account “an interesting observation for future research” and said there’s no scientific evidence yet that the floods and the sightings are related.

All of South America’s river dolphin species are threatened, the authors wrote. To keep dolphins in the Acre, they called for protecting riverside forests, maintaining water quality, and keeping the river connected to the rest of the basin.

Forest loss in the Amazon and changes in river levels caused by climate change may alter river habitats, they added.

The Acre River forms part of the border between Bolivia and Brazil. Morales Mamani said a first step would be cooperation agreements between the two countries to conserve the species.

“It is also essential to involve local communities,” she said.

Fishers told her team they wanted to help protect the dolphins and the river. The fishers proposed one idea themselves: planting trees along the riverbanks.

Wallace wrote in an email to Earth.com that the Acre River needs conserving anyway, for what it provides to those same fishers and to Cobija, the capital of Pando.

“The Amazon river dolphins are a nice symbol and potential important catalyst for those conservation efforts,” he added.

The study can’t say how many dolphins use the Acre, where they travel, or how closely they are related to dolphins elsewhere in the Amazon.

The records also say little about the dry season, from June to September.

The authors called for surveys in those months to learn whether dolphins stay in the area all year, keep to particular habitats, or move as the water level changes.

No new sightings have been documented since the study was written, Morales Mamani said.

Her team wants to set up monitoring to record future sightings. The team will need resources to keep that work going over the long term, she said.

The fishers have said they’re willing to help with that monitoring.

The full study was published in the journal Neotropical Hydrobiology and Aquatic Conservation.
