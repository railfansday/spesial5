Empathy isn’t just about pain – we share joy and embarrassment too

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

Empathy includes sharing someone's joy and embarrassment. In a new study, sad stories prompted the strongest wish to help.

A friend gets good news, and you feel happy too. Someone tells you about an awkward moment, and you cringe as though you were there.

Both experiences involve empathy, but researchers have spent much more time studying how we share another person’s pain. Most lab tests focus on sad stories.

A new study adds happy and embarrassing stories to the mix.

People who watched strangers tell these stories shared their feelings and said they were more willing to help than after hearing everyday stories, though sad stories prompted the strongest response.

Eva Landmann, a doctoral researcher, and Anne Böckler-Raettig led the work at the University of Würzburg in Germany. Böckler-Raettig is a professor of research methods and social cognition.

Psychologists distinguish between feeling what someone else feels and working out what they think. They call the first skill empathy and the second theory of mind.

Sharing joy seems at least as common in daily life as sharing pain, the authors wrote.

A 2021 study followed a representative group of American adults for seven days and found that most chances to empathize involved positive feelings.

Embarrassment is trickier because it can bring mixed feelings. When someone describes a mishap, you might want to laugh and wince at the same time.

Earlier brain scans found that reading about people breaking social rules activated areas also linked to empathy for pain.

Still, the authors knew of no published test that compared sharing embarrassment with sharing other emotions.

Böckler-Raettig helped introduce a video test called EmpaToM in 2015. It shows amateur actors telling stories as if the events happened to them.

The original test used everyday stories about work or hobbies and sad stories about losing a job or a relative’s illness.

The team added happy stories, such as meeting old friends again, and embarrassing ones, such as reaching a checkout without a wallet.

The researchers registered their plan before the main tests and ran two preliminary rounds of testing.

The main tests involved 115 adults online, with 60 watching the version with happy stories and 55 watching the version with embarrassing stories.

Six in ten participants were women. The videos were in German, and each person watched 48 clips lasting 12 to 15 seconds each.

After each clip, viewers rated how they felt, from very bad to very good. Those taking the embarrassment version also rated how embarrassed they felt.

They then answered a multiple-choice question about either what the speaker was thinking or a fact from the story. The thinking questions tested theory of mind.

Finally, viewers used a scale from zero to nine to rate how much they would invest in doing something good for the speaker.

They did not actually give anything, so this measured willingness to help, not action.

The new videos produced the feelings the researchers expected.

Viewers felt better after happy stories than after everyday or sad ones, and more embarrassed after embarrassing stories than after either of the other kinds.

Embarrassing stories left people feeling slightly worse than everyday ones but better than sad ones.

The authors saw this as a sign that viewers were not enjoying someone else’s misfortune, perhaps because the stories involved no rivalry or serious wrongdoing.

Sad stories prompted the greatest willingness to help, while everyday stories prompted the least. Happy and embarrassing stories fell in between.

Helping someone who is already happy might seem puzzling. Landmann suggested that people may want to keep that good feeling going.

“In positive contexts, people may help in order to maintain their own sense of well-being,” Landmann said.

The researchers suggested another possible reason for helping someone who feels embarrassed.

Showing embarrassment may signal that the person cares about social rules, which could make others more willing to approach them.

People who reacted strongly to sad stories also tended to react strongly to happy ones, a link the authors described as strong.

Sharing sadness also went with feeling more embarrassment for the speakers, though that link was weaker. The results suggest that a person’s tendency to empathize can carry across different feelings.

Feeling someone else’s emotions and understanding their thoughts looked like separate skills.

People who felt more strongly for the speakers did no better or worse on the thinking questions, matching earlier findings from the original test.

There was one small exception: people who felt more embarrassment for a speaker scored slightly higher on those questions. The authors cautioned that this could have happened by chance.

People who responded more strongly to happy stories said they were more willing to help the speakers. The same was true for sad stories in the first experiment.

In the second experiment, the link between sharing sadness and wanting to help was weaker and could have happened by chance.

How much embarrassment a viewer felt had no link to how much they wanted to help.

The authors suggested that watching someone else’s awkward moment might pull us in two directions. We may want to approach the person and avoid them at the same time.

Questions after happy and embarrassing stories were slightly harder.

Viewers answered about 68% of questions correctly after happy stories and 65% after embarrassing ones, compared with about 75% after everyday and sad stories.

Each video included a face and a voice, so the study could not tell which mattered more for sharing feelings.

Earlier research with the original test found similar empathy from sound alone, but faces may matter more for joy or embarrassment, the authors wrote.

The tests depend heavily on language, making them unsuitable for children.

The study also did not establish typical empathy scores or check how the tests compared with other measures.

The authors suggested making English versions. These could help researchers study larger groups that better reflect the wider population.

Böckler-Raettig and Landmann said clinical psychologists could eventually use the tool to explore whether some mental health conditions affect certain kinds of empathy.

Someone might share another person’s pain but struggle to share their joy.

This study tested healthy adults, so it cannot show whether patients have that pattern. Researchers would need to run the tests with people who have those conditions to find out.

The study is published in the journal Behavior Research Methods.
