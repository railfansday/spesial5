What do you mean that cash performed better though? Certainly you must be referring to investing in something like treasury bills, or getting some other type of interest rate.
Correct, usually something like 3-Month TBills.

At least from one perspective, that is the closest thing to a "risk-free rate". I'd actually argue liability-matched TIPS can be even lower risk, but that really complicates the analysis since every investor has their own liability structure.

OK, so holding that issue aside, courtesy of Portfolio Visualizer, this is total returns for gold in blue, cash (3-month TBills) in green, and US stocks (AQR until 1992, VTSM after) in grey, starting in 1975 and inflation-adjusted:



Gold bought in 1975 was under cash for a lot more than just 1990 to 2005. It was actually under cash for the large majority of the possible end points. This is illustrating what I mean about it not exactly being neutral to pick 2020-present as your end point range, since that range happens to be above the cash line. Picking many other possible end points would put you below the cash line.

OK, so conventional analysis would score gold during this period as very risky. Indeed, as I remarked before, it is actually hard to find a major risky "asset class" which has spent so much time below cash, aka 3-month TBills, aka a "risk-free rate".

And I included US stocks just to illustrate that point. US stocks are also riskier than cash, at least over short time periods (see previous aside about liability matching), which explains why they have a higher expected return, aka an equity risk premium. Over this period, the excess return of US stocks over cash has been quite substantial, so those willing to accept exposure to US equity risk have been compensated quite well.

And in fact, that chart is a log chart, which is good for some purposes but doesn't do a good job illustrating relative cumulative effects. So here it is in linear scale instead:



So that gives you an idea not only how much US stock purchasers in 1975 were compensated for holding something riskier than cash, it also gives you a more realistic illustration of how much more they were compensated for taking on that risk than 1975 gold purchasers were compensated for taking on their risks relative to cash.
