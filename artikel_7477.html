The data is in: News creators and influencers are a major source of news for Americans, especially people under 30. This is the latest edition of Creators of Record, an occasional series of interviews with popular creators about how they do their jobs.

On a September afternoon in Manhattan’s Union Square neighborhood, Shreyas Sinha is sitting on a toilet, wearing a blue suit. Looking into the camera, he asks, “Why is it that everything I’ve been eating this year is giving me diarrhea?”

Sinha is about to link the 2026 cyclospora outbreaks to decades of problems at the Food and Drug Administration. “I’m only allowed to make three diarrhea jokes in this video,” Sinha says. “I live in tyranny.”

The toilet video is one of the tamer stunts that Sinha and his co-creator, Gaurav Srivatchan, have pulled for their YouTube channel, A Show About The News, a weekly deep dive into major business and politics stories (tagline: “Critics are calling it the worst alternative to mainstream media, and those are just the critics I’m related to.”)

In a video about why young people can’t afford to buy homes, Sinha pops out of a cardboard box on the street and takes former New York City mayor Eric Adams to a studio apartment filled with men. Other recent videos include a 45-minute interview on the street with former White House communications director Anthony Scaramucci, an explainer on prediction market ads, and a rap music video on why retirement investments are safer than get-rich-quick schemes.

Sinha, 25, and Srivatchan, 22, met in 2023 when they were students at New York University’s Stern School of Business. They teamed up to make short vertical explainer videos, but those didn’t take off as much as they’d hoped. In 2024, Sinha quit his finance job to pursue A Show About The News full-time, and they transitioned to creating long-form deep dives with a dash of their own humor.

I chatted with Sinha and Srivatchan about how they launched their media careers, how they use humor to explain the news, and what they think business opportunities are for creators today. Our conversation has been edited for length and clarity.

Tameez: Why did you start creating news videos? Sinha: Gaurav and I started making videos together during the spring 2023 semester, when I was a senior at NYU and he was a freshman. I had been doing a newsletter for a while, I had been doing stand-up comedy for a few years, and I was interning at CNN. It seemed like everything was [pointing to], “Hey, you should start making short-form videos.” And then Gaurav was like, “Hey, I’m actually really good at editing videos.” We were not really thinking of it as a YouTube channel, but as making short-form stories. At the time, we were called Fifty50 Media. We were all about trying to distill the world into bipartisan perspectives. We eventually started focusing more on deeper-dive stories. We renamed ourselves to A Show About the News. It’s very easy to put out a political opinion that goes viral, but we really wanted to focus on good stories. Eventually, we started making videos for Morning Brew, and we joined Morning Brew full-time as a franchise in August 2025.

Sinha: We literally got zero responses…then an opportunity came to make a video for [Morning Brew] and it was a great experience. We made another and another, and then they were like, “You guys should just do this for us.” Srivatchan: Morning Brew provided us access to this office. Tameez: Tell me about your workflow, from video ideation to posting. Sinha: We don’t do short-form clips [from longer videos]. We script our shorts. That takes a ton of time. [But] long-form is the priority. It takes much more time, but you really get to have a relationship with the audience. Srivatchan: When it comes to shorts, either Shreyas or I will have something that really interests us for the week. We start scripting it, then we switch the scripts and each of us does a pass on the other’s. Then we revise them and film them. That takes roughly two out of five workdays. We spend the rest of the time on long-form. We’re also doing a weekly livestream to discuss the headlines with the audience and build that audience connection. So our output is two native short-form, one long-form, and one live stream per week.

Sinha: Do we actually have something new to say about it? There’s no point in making a video that someone else has already made.

A lot of people have made videos about how people aren’t having kids anymore. That doesn’t mean we wouldn’t [cover that topic], but we need to have something new to say. In our video, we talked about how governments have spent tens of billions of dollars but it hasn’t resulted in any more babies. We talked about demographic trends and got into studies. We brought in a bunch of 19- and 20-year-olds and asked them why they’re not having more sex. Then we went to a bunch of older people and were like, “So, you guys were doing it hella in the 90s. What’s the secret? Let’s give it to these kids.”

Sinha: We try to be funny, maybe to a deficit. In one of our worst-performing videos, we had a guy come in on an inflatable horse. Gaurav was wearing a Will Smith mask and slapped me. But sometimes it really works. We filmed this thing where we were having a shootout with Abraham Lincoln as he smoked a vape. That did great for us.

I listen to a lot of podcasts of serious, smart people. The way they talk, I’m like, how are you not ripping your hair off right now? How are you not just slamming your head against your desk?

Srivatchan: We have all these sources that we regularly read. For a lot of politics and inside scoops on Capitol Hill, Politico is great. For more niche stories, ProPublica is great. Each source has its own strength. We send stuff to each other all the time.

Once we’re in the research phase of a video, we do everything possible to learn about what the scope of the video is and what sources and articles can fall into that.

Sinha: We do a lot of our own fact-checking. Corrections often occur in the way of Gaurav coming to me and being like, “Shreyas, you moron, you said this and it’s actually this.” Then we record a voiceover, or we change it in the captions, or we just put it up as text.

Sinha: If you go on YouTube right now, high production quality is the expectation. It’s not like when I was young and my favorite YouTubers were clearly filming in their bedrooms. There’s so much money that is being invested into it, which is awesome, but if you want to have authentic relationships with your audiences, you need to do more.

Srivatchan: Earlier this year, TBPN [Technology Business Programming Network] was sold to OpenAI for $100 million. Their whole thing was live-streaming. They didn’t get that many viewers concurrently on their livestreams — everything was clips, targeting a very specific niche — but we saw how they grew through clips.

Sinha: When we started making these videos, I was writing articles on the side and freelancing so that I could pay rent. Eventually, this was able to pay rent.

By no means are we rich. But we really believe in the business model of digital media. It’s important to incentivize people to invest in media that is fact-checked, that is telling stories that are new and not just polarizing or viral for virality’s sake. You can’t ask people to only think of [media] as a charity expenditure or as a nonprofit, because those are the first things that go away when the market goes down, and the first thing people are going to stop cutting checks to if they don’t see the ROI.

We spend most of our time thinking about content, but it’s important to be creative about our business models. We always tell ourselves not to think about ourselves as employees or YouTubers, but to keep reminding ourselves that we’re founders of a startup that is trying to do something for the world.

The prevailing narrative that news media is doing poorly is because news media for generations was run by journalists, and that was fine because they could get cable carrier fees. But the reason they were able to do that was because they had a monopoly. You could only watch CNN, CBS, and ABC News because that’s all you had.

[I asked Sinha and Srivatchan to answer a few final questions via email. Their answers are below.]

Sinha: It’s hard to say what’s legacy and what’s new these days. Morning Brew has never had a TV broadcast or print media arm, but it’s owned by a media conglomerate, Axel Springer. Other news companies, from CNN to The Wall Street Journal, have pretty successful social media presences and journalists who’ve made a name for themselves.

The crisis for news companies that dominated in the 20th century is that they relied on fixed distribution: cable, broadband, local stations, and papers. A random Joe couldn’t get their ideas placed in those, so the competition for viewership was highly limited.

Today, competition is high. Legacy journalists who struggle often blame the changing tides of information consumption, when in reality they’re just struggling to compete for attention. Many have been successful in making the transition, so we don’t think it’s fair to paint all of legacy journalism as having something to learn from news creators. But our message overall would be that traditional news shouldn’t blame consumers for not maintaining their expensive newspaper and cable subscriptions, but instead [should] figure out how to compete and win in this new age.

Srivatchan: For journalists who have been successful in their careers but want to transition to social media, we think they severely underestimate how well-prepared they are to make the jump. They’ve already been writing strong articles and hooks to get people to click. We think journalists underestimate how that skill can translate on a platform like YouTube, where everything is dependent on the packaging that gets someone to click on your video.

Often, when someone from traditional media starts a new Instagram or YouTube page, they make very lo-fi content, talking to their phone with their headphones mic. I think they see influencers doing that and think viewers want authenticity. That works for some people, but our view is that investing in strong production is the baseline expectation on social media.

Instead of seizing the social media opportunity, often, legacy journalism is falling into the trap of liquidating its reputation for more attention and to better monetize its declining assets. It’s partnering with gambling apps — CNN’s partnership with Kalshi, or [Dow Jones’] with Polymarket — and inviting inflammatory internet voices rather than the trusted ones that educate.
