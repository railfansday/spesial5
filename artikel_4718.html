When Your Sense of Familiarity Falls Apart

“Phony,” the new television series streaming on several networks, follows a plot closely tied to a neurological condition. Sonny, after waking up from a car accident, perceives that his mother is a phony. He believes she is not his true mother. This, of course, puts this young man in a spin. The question is, what happened that led to Sonny’s uncomfortable feelings about his mother?

Among several conditions that could explain this phenomenon, Capgras syndrome is a strong contender. As a neurologist, I’ve written about it and included it in lectures about collecting. The logic is that if the art piece we purchase is a “phony,” we collectors value it less. This has parallels to Sonny’s feelings for his mother in the movie after his car accident.

Whatever the cause, the afflicted individual can misinterpret not only people previously close to them as fake, but also pets and even inanimate objects. For example (hypothetical): Jane has a favorite cat called Ernie. He sleeps on the dining room table in the sun when she is not home and begs for food at the same table when she dines. Though these habits of Jane’s cat may seem repugnant to some, they suit Jane just fine.

But things changed after her bike accident. She was in a coma for three days and then had to go to rehabilitation therapy for several months to regain her cognitive skills. When she returned home, she insisted Ernie was not Ernie. She claimed the cat, who had the same preferences as before Jane’s accident, was not him, even though she felt she was the same. Jane said Ernie might look like and act like her old feline, but he was an impostor.

This is the known explanation. Due to faulty brain wiring after a car accident or head trauma, or dementia/Alzheimer’s disease or schizophrenia, delusions can surface, suggesting Capgras syndrome. Emotional distress can be another contributor, plus other medical conditions have been reported as being associated with it.

So, was Sonny afflicted by Capgras syndrome? I’ll leave the reader to enjoy the television series and decide.

In the meantime, we collectors have to use every tool possible to avoid fakes, which collectors are likely to devalue compared to authentic pieces. According to Artnet News, over 50 percent of art on the market is forged or misattributed. In other words, phony.
