The Holidays on a Tight Budget

“Although we had no money, I was rich as I could be, in my coat of many colors, my mama made for me.” —Dolly Parton

In the song “Times A-Getting Hard, Boys,” Pete Seeger famously captured the tune from Carl Sandburg’s collection, revised by Lee Hays, referring to the debt-ridden farmers who abandoned their land and migrated to California. It was the Dust Bowl, yet another Depression-era tragedy.

Granted, we are not close to the Great Depression now. But the wealth gap is near historic highs, and for much of the nation, economic hardship is on the front burner, just in time for the holidays.

Bottom line: Families are paying more for just about everything that matters most. So how can we travel, host, and preserve our holiday traditions under this kind of financial stress?

We are all influenced by the idyllic Hallmark portrayal of the holidays on TV and social media. It’s easy to fall prey to ideals of perfection, only to have our expectations dashed by reality.

Let’s face it. Even in great economic times, the holidays are stressful, laced with conflict and inevitable disappointment.

We begin with the adults. As the flight attendant says, “If the cabin pressure drops, put your own oxygen mask on first, then help the person next to you.” We are better at managing our kids’ stress when we first take care of our own.

Kids don’t worry about money the way adults do, but they feel our stress, and it has an impact on them.

For all of us, adults and kids alike, self-care is essential: eating well, exercising, getting restorative sleep, and making time for meditation or relaxation. In short, finding (free) ways to reduce stress.

Stress due to financial strain is not all that different than stress induced by other psychosocial factors.

But one thing is different. Even in therapy, talking about one’s finances can be rare. Clinicians will hear about all sorts of complex issues, but some research has shown that rarely, if ever, does a patient talk about money—their income, net worth, debt, and attendant issues for the family.

So, how can we have meaningful conversations with our kids and other family members about what we can afford this year?

Tailor the conversation to your child’s age. Remember that younger kids don’t need details about the family budget, but they do need reassurance. School-age kids can understand trade-offs, and they often like being part of the solution. While teens can handle more nuanced conversations about financial matters, they’re also most likely to feel the sting of comparison with peers.

Before we change anything this year, we need to know what our kids, young and old, are thinking.

Gatherings are super important: Coming together does more than we think. A review of 148 studies following more than 308,000 people found that those with stronger social relationships had a 50 percent higher likelihood of surviving over the study’s period than those with weaker ties, and this finding remained consistent across age, sex, and even initial health. Gatherings save lives.

Remember that giving is as vital for the giver as the receiver. In a study during the COVID-19 pandemic, 777 people were asked to perform three acts of kindness each week for two weeks. Those assigned to be kind to others experienced significant decreases in depression, anxiety, and loneliness. Here are some ideas to kick around for giving.
