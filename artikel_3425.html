7 Masterpiece Fantasy Book Series, Ranked

Starting a fantasy series is a big commitment. Some of the genre’s most beloved sagas run for thousands of pages, with maps, family trees, and made-up languages that can feel overwhelming before the story even begins. So many fantasy series get lost in endless exposition and keep bombarding readers with information before giving them a single reason to care.

By the time the story actually gets going, most people have already put the book down. However, a good fantasy series knows exactly how to strike the balance between building a rich world and telling a compelling story with characters that the readers instantly fall in love with. Here are the seven greatest fantasy book series of all time that can turn even the most casual of readers into lifelong enthusiasts of the genre.

Robert Jordan’s The Wheel of Time is one of the most expansive fantasy series ever written — one that rewards its readers’ commitment every step of the way. The story begins in a quiet farming village called the Two Rivers, where a mysterious woman named Moiraine arrives searching for someone. She belongs to the Aes Sedai, a powerful order of women who can channel magic. Moiraine believes that one of the village’s young men, possibly a humble shepherd named Rand al’Thor, is the Dragon Reborn: a legendary figure destined to face the Dark One. The problem is that the same prophecy says the Dragon could also break the world all over again.

The series spans 14 main novels, and its world is magnificent in scope, with dozens of nations and cultures that all feel distinct instead of ever blurring together. The story doesn’t leave a single loose thread and handles its big question of destiny with immense responsibility. Rand al’Thor has to carry the weight of a fate he never asked for, and since male magic-users in this world slowly go mad, his power is as much a curse as a gift. The middle books are famously slow, but the payoff is worth it in the end. Jordan passed away before he could finish the series, and author Brandon Sanderson completed the final three books using his notes. The Wheel of Time remains a towering achievement, with an ending that ties together 14 books’ worth of prophecies in a way that left longtime fans completely satisfied.

Brandon Sanderson is one of the most popular fantasy authors still writing today, and The Stormlight Archive is his magnum opus. The series is set on Roshar, a harsh world battered by massive storms so powerful that the plants and animals have evolved to survive them. Its heroes are a group of broken people. Kaladin is a former soldier sold into slavery, while Shallan is a young scholar hiding a dangerous secret. Dalinar is a battle-hardened warlord trying to make up for the terrible things he’s done. As an ancient threat returns, each of them is drawn toward the long-lost order of warrior-magicians known as the Knights Radiant.

For a plot so grand, The Stormlight Archive makes sure that it never takes away from the characters’ struggles. Kaladin’s fight with depression is one of the most honest portrayals of mental health in fantasy, and it grounds the world’s fantastical elements in something that plenty of readers can relate to. Sanderson is also famous for his carefully designed magic systems, which follow clear rules and make every battle feel like a clever puzzle. The books are complex, but they move fast, and each one builds to a huge climax that fans call a “Sanderlanche.” Five books in the series are out so far, with five more planned, which means the best may still be yet to come. The Stormlight Archive is the series modern fantasy fans can’t stop recommending, and for good reason.

Discworld proves that fantasy doesn’t have to take itself seriously to be brilliant. Terry Pratchett’s series is set on a flat world that rests on the backs of four elephants, which in turn stand on a giant turtle swimming through space. Over 41 novels, Pratchett fills this ridiculous world with unforgettable characters, including the bumbling wizard Rincewind and Commander Sam Vimes of the city watch. Death himself is a recurring character in the story, who speaks entirely in capital letters.

The genius of Discworld is that it starts as a fantasy parody and slowly turns into something much more meaningful. Pratchett uses his silly world to poke fun at politics, religion, and human nature, and his jokes often sneak up on readers with surprisingly moving ideas. The series also has a unique structure, since it is made up of several smaller storylines that can be read in almost any order, which makes it easy for newcomers to jump in at any given time. Pratchett was diagnosed with Alzheimer’s later in life but kept writing until he died in 2015. Discworld is funny and wise in equal measure, and it’s the most comforting series on this list.

Four worlds and four completely different ideas of what a good life looks like. This is where you belong, what it would ask of you, and what you would get back in return.

You belong somewhere old enough that the mountains have names and somebody can still tell you who gave them. Middle-earth is for people who think the right thing is worth doing even when it will not be finished in their lifetime.

You want the sea, a crew, and absolutely nobody telling you where you are allowed to go. The Grand Line is the world for people whose main ambition in life is finding out what is over there.

You would rather be feared and useful than liked and useless. The Continent is a world where the monsters are the straightforward part of the job and the people are where it all gets complicated.

You want the myths to be real and loose in the actual world, and you want to find out at about thirteen that you were never anywhere near as ordinary as everybody kept telling you.

Long before Game of Thrones took over TV, George R.R. Martin’s A Song of Ice and Fire had already started reshaping the fantasy genre. The story is set in Westeros, where powerful noble families fight a brutal war for control of the Iron Throne. The Starks and Lannisters scheme and battle, while the exiled princess named Daenerys Targaryen is building an army across the sea. All of that is threatened by an ancient danger that is stirring in the frozen north. Of course, that is only half the story, and A Song of Ice and Fire covers a lot more ground, with storylines that converge and diverge all the time. Every chapter is told from a different character’s point of view, so readers see the war and power struggle from all sides.

The biggest shock of the series is how willing Martin is to break the rules. Characters who seem like the hero can die at any moment, and good people often make terrible choices. That realism made the books feel unpredictable in a way fantasy rarely had before. Martin also writes morally gray characters better than almost anyone, and some of the most hated figures in the early books slowly become fan favorites, which is a testament to the author’s brilliant storytelling. The big catch is that the series is still unfinished, with fans waiting more than a decade for the next book, The Winds of Winter. Even so, A Song of Ice and Fire is the series that made fantasy feel dangerous, and its influence is everywhere.

Decades before Hogwarts, Ursula K. Le Guin sent a young boy to wizard school. A Wizard of Earthsea follows Ged, a goatherd with a natural gift for magic who grows up on an island in a vast world made almost entirely of oceans and small archipelagos. The only problem is that Ged knows how good he is, and his pride often leads him right into dangerous situations. While he is still a student, Ged tries to prove himself by performing a forbidden spell and accidentally unleashes a terrifying shadow creature into the world. He spends the rest of the novel being hunted by it, until he finally has to turn around and face it.

What makes Earthsea special is how thoughtful yet simple it is compared to most fantasy books. In this world, magic works by knowing the true name of things, and every spell comes with a cost, so even the most powerful wizards are careful with their gifts. Le Guin was also ahead of her time in deliberately making most of her characters, including Ged, people of color. All the books in the series are short and beautifully written, and they explore big ideas like balance and death in a way that’s easy to follow. Earthsea has inspired countless writers, and it’s a must-read for anyone who wants to understand where modern fantasy came from.

Everyone knows about Harry Potter at this point, because it has become a permanent part of modern pop culture. J.K. Rowling’s seven-book series follows Harry, an orphan who discovers on his 11th birthday that he’s a wizard and has a place at Hogwarts School of Witchcraft and Wizardry. There, he makes lifelong friends in Ron Weasley and Hermione Granger, and he slowly learns the truth about the dark wizard Lord Voldemort, who killed his parents and has never stopped trying to kill him.

The reason why the Harry Potter series still resonates with the entire world is that it grows up with its readers. The first books are full of wonder and whimsy, but the series gets darker and more mature with every installment, until the final chapters cover a full-blown war. Rowling also had a gift for mystery, and each book is built around a central mystery that keeps readers guessing until the last chapter. The friendship between Harry, Ron, and Hermione is the heart of this fantastical story that readers just can’t get enough of. The series turned an entire generation into readers, and with a new HBO series on the way in December 2026, Harry Potter is not losing its magic anytime soon.

The Lord of the Rings is the series that has defined practically every other book on this list. J.R.R. Tolkien’s epic is often published as three volumes, but the author actually wrote it as a single story. Now, he obviously didn’t invent elves, dwarves, or epic quests, but he turned them into the blueprint the entire genre still follows. The story picks up years after The Hobbit, when a young hobbit named Frodo Baggins learns that the plain gold ring his uncle Bilbo left him is actually the One Ring, forged by the Dark Lord Sauron to control the world. The only way to stop Sauron is to carry the Ring into his kingdom of Mordor and throw it into the volcano where it was made. Frodo then sets out with a fellowship of companions, including the wizard Gandalf and his loyal friend Samwise Gamgee, as war spreads across Middle-earth.

What makes the series so powerful is that its real villain isn’t just Sauron, but the Ring itself, which slowly tempts and corrupts anyone who holds it. Tolkien fought in World War I, and his experience shows in the way the books treat war as something that leaves lasting scars. The world Tolkien creates feels extremely real. Middle-earth comes with its own languages and thousands of years of history. That depth gives The Lord of the Rings a sense of age and meaning like no other. Peter Jackson’s films brought the franchise to a new generation, but nothing comes close to the brilliance of the source material.
