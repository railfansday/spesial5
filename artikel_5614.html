New Biologics May Be Able to Change the Course of Sjogren's Disease

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

— Experts discuss promising dazodalibep and ianalumab data presented at ACR

In this exclusive roundtable video from MedPage Today, three rheumatology experts discuss promising new treatments for Sjögren's disease, highlighting emerging data presented at the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) annual meeting.

Moderator Hanna Zembrzuska, MD, of the University of Iowa in Iowa City, is joined by Nancy Carteron, MD, of the University of California Berkeley, and Thomas Grader-Beck, MD, of Johns Hopkins University in Baltimore. In this first of four episodes, they examine phase III OASIZ-301 results for dazodalibep and long-term NEPTUNUS extension findings for ianalumab, including whether improvements in salivary flow could signal the potential to preserve salivary gland function.

Following is a transcript of their remarks:

Zembrzuska: Hello, and welcome to MedPage Today Roundtable on the most recent American College of Rheumatology meeting. My name is Hanna Zembrzuska. I'm an adult rheumatologist in Iowa City, Iowa, and I'm joined by some colleagues here who I will have them introduce themselves. Go ahead.

Grader-Beck: Hi, my name is Thomas Grader-Beck. I'm an associate professor at Johns Hopkins Rheumatology. I'm a member of the interdisciplinary Sjögren's center and I'm very excited to be here.

Carteron: Hi, I'm Nancy Carteron. I'm a professor at University of California at Berkeley and UCSF [University of California San Francisco], and I'm based at a Sjögren's center in Berkeley, and I'm happy to be here.

Zembrzuska: Yeah, I'm so happy to be here with Dr. Carteron and Dr. Grader-Beck, and we are going to do a virtual roundtable covering Sjögren's news from the most recent ACR meeting.

This is the most promising era in the history of Sjögren's disease research. For decades, care was limited to managing symptoms like eye drops and saliva substitutes with zero FDA-approved targeted therapies. But over the past year, multiple late-stage clinical trials have succeeded, bringing the first disease-modifying drugs to the brink of approval. So very exciting.

At the recent American College of Rheumatology annual meeting, there was a late-breaking oral abstract session that discussed dazodalibep, which is a CD40 ligand antagonist, and the results from its phase III OASIZ-301 trial. What were your main takeaways from this trial?

Carteron: I'll jump in. I think seeing yet another product meet the endpoints in Sjögren's historically was terrific. I think the fact that it also targets an additional pathway in the disease pathogenesis is promising.

Grader-Beck: I will say one other thing, maybe. I think the side effect profile overall was good. There was a little bit of a signal of hypertension and there wasn't much additional information about that, but maybe that's something to just keep in the back of the mind to see if that is something to really be concerned about. But otherwise, I thought it looked great.

Carteron: I would also add that in their study, they included a pretty significant number of patients that also had another coexistent autoimmune disease. So both lupus and rheumatoid arthritis plus Sjögren's patients were allowed in the study. And I think it was maybe even up to 20%.

I don't think there was any data presented separating that out, but I think that will begin to give us a little more information about real-world use of this particular kind of product. And I think the other point really is it's not a monoclonal antibody, so it's a different technology of inhibition. So it's actually a fusion protein that's very targeted.

But to get to Thomas's point about safety profile, it really does look like the newer technologies have figured out a workaround against some of the thrombotic effects that we saw early on when that particular pathway was inhibited. But intriguing the point that he's making about the hypertension, so stay vigilant.

Zembrzuska: So at ACR, long-term results from the NEPTUNUS extension study for ianalumab were also presented. This study had high retention rates and it reported data on stimulated salivary flow with improvements maintained through week 96. What does this mean exactly? Does this mean that sustained stimulated salivary flow indicates gland preservation or is that too strong of a statement? What do we think this means?

Grader-Beck: Yeah, I mean, it's very, very interesting and it's exciting to see that we see a consistent longer-term signal. So in the extension study, of course, everyone is on the drug and then you have some patients from placebo switching over to drug and you can start to see similar effects in the placebo group in terms of improvement of disease activity. And then also there was a little bit of a signal of the salivary flow.

I think it's too early, it's too early to say. We really need some more dedicated studies to tease that out. But if you look at the more mechanistic studies -- and there was data also presented at ACR this year, and last year actually too -- where you measure, for example, the vascularity in the glands before and after treatment. And you can clearly see a decrease of inflammation if you measure it by vascularity, you can see improvement of infiltrates, you can see actually improvement of salivary flow, but those were not placebo controlled. These are mechanistic studies.

So certainly that all points towards a possible effect. And the way it looks like, it would be patients that are actually earlier in the disease course that have more residual saliva may be the ones that may benefit more.

Carteron: I think we have data showing a decrease in glandular infiltrates of lymphocytes, but predominantly B cells, with deep B-cell depletion from a number of different products looking at it when they have. And so I think the overwhelming evidence is that there can be improvement, and I think even a small amount of improvement in salivary function may translate into a significantly meaningful outcome for patients. Whether it then translates into decreased dental damage or other things, clearly we need more.

But I think that for quality of life, not even well established, but even early on for patients, I find it particularly exciting. And also anecdotally for patients that were within the study that elected to continue and go into the long-term extension study because of a number of factors, but one of them also was increased oral comfort.
