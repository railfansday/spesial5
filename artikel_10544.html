Samsung’s paid Galaxy AI plan has a giant Gemini problem

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

The relationship between Samsung and Google has, over the years, waxed and waned depending on their respective interdependence. Samsung is Android’s physical emissary, powering a plurality of all its handsets globally. But Google, and in particular its services, is the glue that holds Samsung’s smartphone experience together.

The back and forth is over a decade old; back in early 2014, Samsung shipped a refreshed magazine-style skin with its Galaxy tablets that eschewed many of the design tenets Google had been pushing its partners to adhere to. Not only was Samsung willfully defying its most important partner, but it made a point of barely mentioning Android during its press conferences.

Around the same time, it transitioned its smartwatches from Android Wear to Tizen and began shipping duplicative apps on its devices (many of which persist to this day). The goal was to distance itself from Google as much as possible.

While we don’t know exactly what happened behind closed doors, we saw the outcome quite clearly: Magazine UX lasted one product cycle, and the two companies signed a 10-year cross-license patent deal, which may have helped Samsung prevent any future lawsuits with Apple. Google then sold Motorola to Lenovo, clearing the way for Samsung to continue building its burgeoning hardware business on the back of Google’s mobile ecosystem.

Since then, the two companies have grown even closer. In 2021, they co-announced a new Wear OS along with an overarching wearables partnership that has since grown to include Android XR as well. And Google is still the default search engine for Samsung devices, despite reported inroads made by Microsoft in 2023 to convince the Korean giant to switch to a ChatGPT-powered version of Bing Search. (Samsung’s ongoing relationship with Microsoft, which bundles its Office suite on most Galaxy devices, could be seen as a point of leverage for negotiations with Google, too.)

This brings us to today, and to AI. Samsung was among the first companies to adopt Google’s full suite of Gemini Intelligence products, and its phones often get new AI features before anyone else. But like a decade ago, when Samsung shipped Bixby to kind-of-sort-of compete with Google Assistant, it still wants to offer its own services alongside Google’s.

So a rumor that Samsung may launch a paid Galaxy AI Premium subscription next year for $5/month piqued my interest for the same reason Magazine UX did 12 years ago. Galaxy AI features today are a blend of first-party and third-party capabilities, but none of them are especially useful, particularly when compared to Google’s own Android equivalents powered by Gemini Intelligence. Now Bar and Now Brief, two heavily promoted features in Samsung’s marketing, are wildly disappointing.

At I/O this past May, Google announced Spark, an agent that goes out and performs actions on your behalf. And while it’s since been somewhat eclipsed by more capable mobile options like Meta’s Muse, it’s obvious that Google is working on a follow-up that will perform functions both on the phone and inside apps.

That’s exactly what Samsung is rumored to be working on with Bixby Action, a spiritual successor to its namesake that, when it debuted, prioritized on-device tasks like turning on Wi-Fi over web-based actions Google Assistant did better.

According to leaks from SamMobile, Galaxy AI Premium will offer agentic upgrades for phone users to “create commands triggers and system routines for daily tasks” or “automate routine daily tasks using Galaxy AI.” Gen Video will reportedly “convert static photos into dynamic video clips,” while a new Daily Briefing likely does what it says on the tin.

Will Samsung users see enough value in this hodgepodge of features paywalled only because they use a surfeit of compute? And what models will power them on the backend?

Right now, Google is in the enviable position of being able to offer its AI at three distinct layers: the model itself, which is invisible to the user; the OS level, which can promulgate Gemini across every Android phone that runs Google services; and the hardware level, where Pixel lets it specialize and optimize.

Samsung, not particularly known for its software prowess, will likely need to step gingerly around Google’s AI shadow while also justifying the cost to shore up its much-needed services revenue. Based on this leaked roadmap, though, I’m not confident it will work out.

Instead, Samsung may want to acquire one of the many up-and-coming AI agent startups whose valuations are rising more quickly than their user bases. While Muse and OpenAI’s Dots have taken up most of the recent conversation, I’ve been using Hark, Town, and Instinct pretty regularly for a month or so and could see them being brought in-house by Samsung and offered either as standalone products or customized for Galaxy devices. As Google is doing with its own AI plans, Samsung could provide Galaxy users a year of free service, after which they would be expected to pay.

Samsung doesn’t have a great history of building software, but many of its acquisitions, from SmartThings to Viv Labs (which became “next-gen” Bixby) to LoopPay (which became Samsung Pay) to Whisk (which became Samsung Food), have proven successful. Given the current urgent state of mobile AI, I’d forgo the hodgepodge and bring in some outside expertise.
