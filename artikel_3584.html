Why did Rockstar Games make empathy a key mechanic in GTA 6? "Because it's fun" says head of development Rob Nelson

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

That, and parts of RDR2 became "too hard to ignore or walk away from".

There's been plenty of chatter about Grand Theft Auto 6's 'honour system', so to speak, implemented here via what the game calls your Criminal Profile.

Our own Dom just wrote that they prefer to turn its notifications off, for instance, in favour of fully soaking into the immersion. Personally I quite like the pop-ups - I find the act of figuring out systems and how they're manipulated almost as fun as the games they're a part of. And pop-ups or no, the fact that GTA 6 can arguably be portrayed as 'rewarding' empathy and 'discouraging' the series' famous rampages of mass violence has sparked a fair bit of debate.

Some of that is genuinely interesting - I'm dubious as to whether that's really what this system is doing, for a start. Is it incentivising and disincentivising certain in-game behaviour, or just putting a label on it? And then, actually: does the act of labelling "psycho" behaviour alone actually become a disincentivising factor in itself?

Either way, until now Rockstar hasn't said a great deal about why, exactly, this system - seen by some as one that builds empathy into GTA's chaotic world - was implemented in the first place. But during an interview I had with Rob Nelson, Rockstar Games' head of development, just before GTA 6's launch earlier this month, a little more on that came to light.

A little context is necessary here: Nelson's response was actually following a question I had about the overall approach to design that Rockstar took, and how its wider themes and intentions came about. As a part of that, he turned to this system - and how, in a way, it exists here because of the work the studio simply didn't want to leave behind after Red Dead Redemption 2.

Nelson explained that this all began when the team was in the early phase of exploring what type of game GTA 6 would be, in 2015 and 2016, while much of the studio was still working full-tilt on RDR2 - a game which itself didn't release until late 2018. The team was looking to "build up the sophistication of the NPC population, and also allow for more player choice than we had [before], and more options in terms of the way you interact with the world beyond just violence," Nelson told me.

The idea would be that "it's still a world of violence - but you could threaten somebody by aiming a gun at them and just robbing them, and they have a chance to comply. So you don't have to beat them to death to get their money." Ideas that were "simple things" in principle, he said, "but they create a lot more work in terms of development of the sophistication of the systems."

Then, as systems were gradually implemented, it became a question of "trying to marry up what you're doing in the open world with who we're saying you are in the narrative - more than we had, I would say, previously, in Red Dead 2." It's here where the system now referred to as the Criminal Profile started to form.

The thinking turned to "the nature of 'honour', which wasn't always a part of it" with Red Dead Redemption 2, Nelson continued, "but became part of it as we were working on this story and finding that the 'redemption arc' didn't feel so good when it was foisted upon you - and feelings that we had playing Red Dead sort of early on." (Those feelings, primarily, were around getting you to invest in a gang led by Dutch Van der Linde, who was fundamentally unlikable from the off - or plain "crazy", as Nelson put it. The implementation of more player choices and agency was part of Rockstar's way to build investment in Arthur, even as he followed Dutch.)

That meant moving the choices away from key story moments, where many games tend to put them. Instead, Rockstar built a system that ran alongside the story via smaller, moment-to-moment choices you might make. "We tried to take all of those off the main flow and make them optional. So again, they're a choice."

"Usually they involve some form of slight sacrifice or more effort on the part of the player," he added. The hope, then, was that you as a player would both "feel something", and ultimately also feel "you got the outcome that you deserved."

That was the initial thinking behind this kind of system as a whole. Coming to GTA 6, it then combined with a wider desire to "evolve" Grand Theft Auto as both the team's storytelling and the available technology matured. "You're thinking about story and character set, but we're also thinking about what we want to do with GTA as a series, and how you want to evolve it," Nelson said.

"I think as the resolution goes up, it sort of asks different new questions," he continued. "As the resolution goes up, you want the sophistication of the experience to go up, and obviously the sophistication of the mechanics. So it just naturally flowed that we give players choices to interact with the world beyond just violence, beyond just 'down the barrel of a gun', because it's fun."

"It's fun for some people like myself to just sort of role-play in the world, and be in the world, and restraint becomes something you hadn't thought about," he continued. That tied back into the more sophisticated NPCs and reactions the team were making.

"If you think about the NPCs, even back to [GTA] 5, you would draw a weapon - first of all, they didn't react if you drew an invisible shotgun out of nowhere, or a rocket launcher," he said. "They don't react unless you aim it, and then they flee in terror, and you're just sort of this demigod stomping through the world, you know? And that's fun, but for how long?

"The team then started thinking of ways to build a bit more sustained enjoyment from it. "What if you can't do that?" Nelson continued. What if, instead of whipping an RPG out of nowhere, "you have to call your car. You have to pop a car trunk and get it, and now I'm walking, and now people are noticing, but they're sort of sidling away, and it's more believable. And then they're still fleeing in terror, but then there's people who also have guns, and they're shooting at you, or you try to hold somebody up and they pull a gun, or they grab it, and then you have to deal with them.

"And then, he continued, "you choose. You're trying to jack somebody off a motorcycle, and they pull a gun on you, and you have to blow their head off and it's like, 'You shouldn't have made me do that!' And so then it has more meaning, and it's more funny in a way when it's a little bit more pointed. And you can still go on those rampages and basically smash your Criminal Profile until it breaks and then you enter this irredeemable state, which basically says 'I reject your criminal profile system! I don't want it.'"

That Irredeemable profile level, for what it's worth, was another element born of Red Dead Redemption 2. "We had that with Red Dead, where people would sort of have low honour, and then 'greet' their way back up to high honour. And so this was an attempt of sorts of [making something] for people that just want to go crazy and almost opt out of the system. They can opt out of it - and you can also turn off the notifications in the system."

It's notable, in fact, just how interlinked the thinking was with GTA 6 and Red Dead Redemption 2. As Nelson put it at one point: "There was maybe more emotional resonance in the story of RDR2 than we had had, certainly in a GTA before. So we liked that idea." What Rockstar did with GTA 6 was then in a way a natural next step. "We didn't necessarily come to GTA 6 going: 'We must have emotional resonance,' but all of those things combine. It's more that those systems and that element of player choice and agency, and the way that affected the narrative, was a hard thing to ignore and walk away from."
