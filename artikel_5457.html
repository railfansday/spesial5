How the U.S. Emergency Alert System gets warnings to your TV, radio and phone

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Free daily briefing on global business news.

How the U.S. Emergency Alert System works, from the agencies that run it to the codes, phone alerts and tests that get warnings to the public

A tornado warning from a National Weather Service forecast office can reach a car radio, a cable TV channel and a smartphone. That reach depends on shared digital codes, a federal gateway run by FEMA and legal obligations that bind broadcasters and cable systems. The Emergency Alert System carries warnings over radio, TV and cable. Wireless Emergency Alerts carry them to phones.

Knowing how the U.S. Emergency Alert System works explains why its messages look and sound the way they do. The two-tone sound heard during a broadcast alert is called the attention signal. A digital header sent with the alert carries instructions for receiving equipment. Those instructions identify the type of event, the counties affected and the time window the warning covers. Phone alerts take a different route from TV and radio alerts. They reach handsets by cell broadcast rather than by text message. Phone users can switch off some categories of alert, but not Presidential alerts.

The system's predecessors date to the Cold War. CONELRAD began in 1951, the Emergency Broadcast System replaced it in 1963 and the Emergency Alert System took over in 1997. Today the system carries a wide range of messages, including tornado warnings, flash flood warnings and AMBER alerts for abducted children. Federal, state, local, tribal and territorial agencies can all send messages through FEMA's gateway, but only after they are approved as senders.

The network has weak points. In January 2018, a false ballistic missile alert reached phones and broadcasts in Hawaii. Retracting it took 38 minutes, according to the FCC. The episode exposed the system's reliance on authorized senders getting messages right. It also depends on people having a working device turned on and nearby when a warning goes out.

The 10 slides that follow break the system into its working parts. They cover the three federal bodies that share responsibility for it and its history since 1951. They also explain which media providers must carry alerts, the SAME code carried in broadcast warnings and the relay chain that moves national messages from station to station. The remaining slides look at IPAWS and the Common Alerting Protocol, Wireless Emergency Alerts on cellphones and the National Weather Service's role. They close with the weekly, monthly and nationwide tests that check equipment and the limits that remain.

No single agency owns the Emergency Alert System. Three federal bodies run it jointly: the Federal Communications Commission, the Federal Emergency Management Agency and the National Weather Service.

Each agency covers a separate function. The FCC sets and enforces the rules. FEMA operates the national alerting infrastructure. The National Weather Service supplies weather warnings. One alert can involve the work of more than one of these agencies before it reaches the public.

The Federal Communications Commission, or FCC, is the regulator. It writes the rules that govern how the EAS operates, and it enforces them. Those rules apply to radio and TV broadcasters, cable systems, satellite radio and TV providers, and wireline video providers. Each of these participants must carry alerts and keep EAS equipment. They must also run weekly and monthly tests.

The Federal Emergency Management Agency, or FEMA, handles the national layer. It operates the national alerting infrastructure, and it is the agency that can trigger a national-level alert. FEMA designates the Primary Entry Point stations that receive national alerts first. Those stations pass the message on to other stations in a daisy chain.

FEMA also runs the Integrated Public Alert and Warning System, known as IPAWS. Authorized federal, state, local, tribal and territorial agencies use it to send one message over several channels. Those messages are written in the Common Alerting Protocol, an XML-based format.

The National Weather Service, or NWS, issues the system's weather-related alerts. Its forecast offices originate warnings such as tornado warnings and flash flood warnings. Those warnings feed both the EAS and Wireless Emergency Alerts, which reach phones by cell broadcast. NOAA Weather Radio is a related channel for weather warnings.

A flash flood warning shows how the roles fit together. An NWS forecast office issues it. Broadcasters and cable systems carry it under rules the FCC writes and enforces. A national-level alert takes a different route, starting with FEMA and the Primary Entry Point stations it designates.

The Emergency Alert System replaced the Emergency Broadcast System in 1997. The Emergency Broadcast System had itself replaced CONELRAD, a Cold War warning system that began in 1951.

That sequence makes today's system the third national broadcast warning system the U.S. has used. CONELRAD came first. It started in 1951, in the early years of the Cold War.

The Emergency Broadcast System took over from CONELRAD in 1963. CONELRAD had been in service for 12 years.

The Emergency Broadcast System had a much longer run. It stayed in place for 34 years before the Emergency Alert System replaced it in 1997.

The two earlier systems covered 46 years in total, from CONELRAD's launch to the arrival of the Emergency Alert System.

The Emergency Alert System is the system in use today. The name of the national warning system has changed twice since 1951. Its purpose has stayed the same: getting official warnings to the public through broadcast channels.

Radio and TV broadcasters, cable systems, satellite radio and TV providers, and wireline video providers are the required participants in the Emergency Alert System. Each one must carry alerts and keep EAS equipment in place to receive them.

The obligation has two parts. A participant needs working equipment that can pick up an incoming alert. It also has to pass that alert along to its own audience. Owning the gear without airing the message does not meet the requirement.

The Federal Communications Commission sets these obligations and enforces them. The same agency writes the rest of the system's rules. Individual companies do not decide on their own whether to take part.

These categories cover the main ways people receive radio and TV at home or in the car. A person watching a satellite channel and a person listening to a local radio station are both reached through participants held to the same requirement.

Participants also serve as the links in the alert chain. A national alert passes from station to station in a daisy chain, with each participant's equipment receiving the message and delivering it to its own audience. State and local plans set out how alerts move between participants in each area.

The equipment requirement comes with a testing requirement. Every participant must run weekly and monthly tests. Those checks show whether its equipment can still receive an alert and pass it along.

Mobile phones are not on this list. Warnings reach phones through Wireless Emergency Alerts, a separate channel with its own rules. A household that relies only on a phone gets alerts through that system rather than through broadcast, cable or satellite participants.

Every Emergency Alert System message starts with a digital header built on Specific Area Message Encoding, known as SAME. The header tells receiving equipment who sent the alert, what kind of emergency it describes, which counties it covers and how long it applies.

SAME is data rather than speech. It travels as a coded signal at the front of an alert, ahead of the audio message or on-screen text that the public hears and reads. Broadcast and cable equipment reads that code before anything else in the alert.

The first field is the originator code. It identifies the type of sender behind the alert. Equipment down the line can then tell where a warning came from. A weather warning from the National Weather Service and a message from a local emergency agency carry different originator codes.

A three-letter event code follows. It names the hazard in compact form. TOR, for instance, stands for a tornado warning. With a separate code for each type of event, equipment can tell a tornado warning apart from other alerts without anyone listening to the audio.

Location codes come next. SAME uses FIPS codes, which identify individual counties. An alert can list several of them. A flash flood warning for two neighboring counties, for example, would carry two FIPS codes. That lets a warning name a specific area instead of an entire state.

The header also carries a time window. That field sets the period during which the alert is in effect.

The part of an alert meant for human ears begins with the attention signal. It combines two tones, one at 853 Hz and one at 960 Hz, sounded together. The combined sound marks the start of the spoken or written warning.

Every SAME message closes with an end-of-message code, NNNN. That code tells receiving equipment the alert is over.

The header and the tones do different jobs. The digital codes tell machines how to identify and sort an alert. The two tones tell people to stop and pay attention.

A national alert enters the Emergency Alert System through Primary Entry Point stations, which FEMA designates. From those stations, the message moves outward from one station to the next in a daisy chain until it reaches local audiences.

FEMA operates the national alerting infrastructure and can trigger alerts at the national level. Primary Entry Point stations are where such a message first lands. FEMA decides which stations fill that role. Those stations receive the national alert before any other station in the broadcast chain.

The relay works by handing the message along. One station carries the alert, and the next station picks it up and passes it on. Each handoff extends the warning into another station's coverage area. A message that starts at the entry points can spread across the broadcast network this way.

Under this structure, a station does not need its own direct connection to FEMA to carry a national alert. It needs only to receive the message from the station ahead of it in the chain. The trade-off is dependence on the links upstream. A station farther down the line can relay an alert only after the stations before it have passed the message along.

The national entry points handle only the first stage. State and local plans decide how an alert moves within a particular area. Those plans set up the local relay paths that carry a message the final distance to the stations serving a given community.

This station-to-station relay is the broadcast side of national alerting. Alerts on phones travel through a separate channel, Wireless Emergency Alerts, rather than being handed from one radio or TV station to the next.

The full route has three layers. A national message starts at the FEMA-designated entry stations and moves through the daisy chain. It then follows the local relay arrangements that each state and locality has set up.

IPAWS, FEMA's Integrated Public Alert and Warning System, is the gateway that lets an approved agency write one warning and send it over several alerting channels. The Common Alerting Protocol, or CAP, is the shared message format that makes this single-entry approach work.

The gateway is open to agencies at every level of government. Federal, state, local, tribal and territorial authorities are all eligible. A county emergency office and a federal agency can reach the public through the same system. Once a warning enters IPAWS, it follows the same route whatever the size of the agency that wrote it.

Access is not automatic. An agency must be approved before it can send alerts through IPAWS. That approval step controls who can put a message into the national alerting infrastructure, which FEMA operates. It also means every message in the gateway comes from an agency that has already been cleared to send.

CAP is an XML-based format. XML is a text-based markup language that computers can read and process. A CAP message packages a warning as structured data rather than free-form text. Each piece of information sits in a defined place within the message. Details such as the type of event, the area affected and the time the warning expires each have their own labeled field.

That common structure is what lets one message travel to more than one channel. Every approved sender uses the same format. An alerting official writes the warning once and submits it to IPAWS. The gateway then handles distribution. The message does not have to be rewritten for each outlet. Broadcast equipment and phone networks draw on the same core content. A warning on a TV screen and one on a cellphone therefore describe the same event for the same place.

IPAWS grew out of a 2006 executive order. The system that came from it sits between the agencies that write warnings and the channels that deliver them to the public.

Wireless Emergency Alerts, the phone-based layer of U.S. public warning, began in 2012. The messages reach mobile phones through cell broadcast technology rather than as ordinary text messages.

That difference shapes how the alerts behave. A text message is addressed to one phone number and delivered to that number alone. Sending a warning that way would require a list of every number in an affected area. Cell broadcast skips that step. One message goes out from cell towers to the compatible phones connected to them in the area being warned.

For the person holding the phone, that means there is nothing to sign up for. The sender never needs to know who is in the area or what their phone number is. A compatible phone that is switched on and connected to the network can display the alert.

Wireless Emergency Alerts are one of the channels that IPAWS, FEMA's Integrated Public Alert and Warning System, can reach. An agency must be approved as a sender before it can issue one. That rule applies to federal, state, local, tribal and territorial agencies alike.

Wireless Emergency Alerts fall into three categories. Presidential alerts are reserved for national emergencies and are issued at the direction of the president. Imminent Threat alerts cover hazards that put lives or property at risk. Tornado warnings and flash flood warnings issued by National Weather Service forecast offices fall into this group. AMBER alerts concern child abductions.

The categories follow different rules on the user's side. People can opt out of Imminent Threat and AMBER alerts in their phone's settings. They cannot opt out of Presidential alerts.

Phone alerts work alongside the broadcast side of the Emergency Alert System rather than replacing it. The same warning about a tornado or a missing child can appear on a television screen, interrupt a radio program and light up a phone. A person away from a TV or radio can still get the warning on a phone. Someone without a phone can still hear it on air.

National Weather Service forecast offices originate many of the warnings that reach the public, including tornado warnings and flash flood warnings. Those warnings feed both the Emergency Alert System and Wireless Emergency Alerts. One forecast office's decision can therefore reach broadcast audiences and phones.

A weather warning starts with meteorologists, not with broadcasters or phone carriers. The forecast office responsible for the threatened area decides that a warning is needed and issues it. From there, the warning moves outward through several channels at once.

Weather is the National Weather Service's share of federal alerting. The meteorological judgment behind a tornado or flash flood warning comes from the weather service. The agencies that write the rules and operate the national alerting infrastructure do not make that forecast call.

On the broadcast side, a weather warning enters the Emergency Alert System. Radio and TV stations, cable systems, satellite providers and other required participants can then pass it to their audiences. That route puts a weather alert onto TV screens and radio speakers. A flash flood warning takes the same broadcast route as a tornado warning.

On the wireless side, the same warning can reach phones as a Wireless Emergency Alert. A person who is not watching TV or listening to the radio can still receive it. That includes someone who is outdoors or away from a television when the forecast office issues the warning.

NOAA Weather Radio is a related channel for these warnings. It belongs to the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, the parent agency of the National Weather Service. Listeners hear it through a dedicated weather radio receiver rather than an ordinary broadcast station.

Together, these routes give a single weather warning more than one path to the public. Someone who misses a broadcast alert may still get the message on a phone or a weather radio.

Every participant in the Emergency Alert System must run required weekly and monthly tests. The U.S. has also held nationwide tests, starting with the first nationwide EAS test on Nov. 9, 2011.

The routine tests fall on the outlets that carry alerts to the public. Broadcasters, cable systems, satellite providers and wireline video providers must all keep EAS equipment. The weekly and monthly schedule gives that equipment a trial run between real emergencies.

A test checks the system at a moment when no one is in danger. Gear that sits idle until a crisis could fail without anyone noticing. A regular schedule gives an outlet the chance to find a fault before a tornado warning or an AMBER alert has to pass through the same equipment.

Nationwide tests work at a different scale. A routine test at one station confirms that its own setup works. A nationwide test sends a single message meant to reach audiences across the whole country. It shows whether the system holds together as a national network, not only whether its separate parts function.

The Nov. 9, 2011 test took place before Wireless Emergency Alerts existed. WEA, the system that delivers alerts to phones, began in 2012. As a result, that first nationwide test covered TV, radio and cable audiences but not phones.

Later nationwide tests added phones. The tests on Sept. 20, 2018, and Oct. 4, 2023, involved WEA alongside the EAS. On both dates, the test message was meant to reach mobile phones as well as people watching TV or listening to the radio.

Adding phones to a test widens what it checks. The broadcast portion shows whether stations and cable systems pass a message down the line. The WEA portion shows whether the same alert reaches the devices people carry with them.

A test message that appears on a screen or plays over a speaker is a check of the system, not a warning of real danger. The weekly and monthly tests cover individual outlets, while the nationwide tests cover the network as a whole.

The Emergency Alert System depends on two things: people need a working device that is switched on and nearby, and authorized senders have to get the message right. Hawaii's false ballistic missile alert on Jan. 13, 2018, showed what happens when the second condition fails.

That morning, a warning of an incoming ballistic missile went out to people across Hawaii. It reached them on their phones and through broadcast outlets. No missile was on the way.

Taking the message back proved harder than sending it. The FCC investigated the incident and found that the retraction took 38 minutes.

The episode showed a trade-off built into the alerting chain. The technology moved the false alert quickly. It reached the same broad audience a real warning would have reached. Speed and reach are the purpose of the system. They also mean a mistake travels as fast as an accurate alert does.

Sender approval does not remove this risk. Federal, state, local, tribal and territorial agencies must be approved before they can send through IPAWS, FEMA's alerting gateway. Approval decides who may issue a warning. Getting each message right still falls to the sender.

The device condition is just as basic. A warning carried over radio and television reaches only people who have a set turned on. A Wireless Emergency Alert reaches only phones that are powered on and connected to a cell network. The owner also has to be close enough to notice it. Someone asleep with a phone switched off, or away from any screen or speaker, can miss an alert entirely.

These limits do not change the system's basic design. Broadcasters, cable providers, satellite services, phones and weather radio each give a warning another route to the public. That overlap raises the odds a message gets through. It cannot make up for an inaccurate sender or an audience with no device on hand.
