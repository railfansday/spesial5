A 10-step response plan for when a website you use is hacked

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Knowing what to do after a data breach limits the damage, from changing reused passwords to freezing your credit, plus how AO3 users can protect a pen name

A breach notice usually arrives after the damage on the company's side is already done. The data has been copied, and nothing a customer does can pull it back. What a person controls is the window that follows. The hours and days after a leak decide whether a stolen email address and password stay a single problem or become a key to many other accounts.

That is the core of knowing what to do after a data breach. The main danger to other accounts does not come from the hacked website itself. It comes from credential stuffing. This is the practice of taking leaked email and password pairs and trying them automatically on other sites. Banks, email providers, shopping accounts and streaming services all become targets if the same password protected them. A breach at a small hobby forum can end with someone inside a person's main inbox.

The right response also depends on what was exposed. A leaked email address alone calls for caution about scam messages. A leaked password calls for changes across every account that shared it. A leaked Social Security number, card number or date of birth raises the stakes to fraud and identity theft. The tools for those problems are different.

Some exposures are personal rather than financial. Archive of Our Own, the fan fiction archive known as AO3, is run by the nonprofit Organization for Transformative Works. Its accounts are tied to a username and an email address. Many writers there publish under pen names. On any site built around pseudonyms, the most sensitive piece of data may be the email address. That address can connect a pen name to a real person. The same risk applies to other fan archives, gaming communities and anonymous forums, whether or not a given site has ever been breached.

The 10 steps that follow move in a deliberate order. They start with confirming that a breach actually happened and finding out what it covered. They then turn to securing accounts with new passwords and two-factor authentication. Next come monitoring tools, including breach-lookup services, credit freezes and credit reports. The list closes with reporting identity theft if it occurs and reducing how much any single site knows about you. Each step works on its own. Readers can start with the one that matches what leaked.

Before changing anything, confirm the breach on the company's own website, its official social media accounts or in a notice it sent you directly. A verified announcement tells you what was exposed, and that detail decides which later steps you actually need.

News of a breach often arrives secondhand. A screenshot circulates on social media, a forum thread claims a database is for sale, or an email warns that your account is at risk. Some of these reports turn out to be accurate. Others are exaggerated, recycled from an older incident or written by scammers hoping to catch people in a panic.

The safest route is to type the site's address into your browser yourself rather than following a link. Companies typically post breach information in a press release, a blog post, a help center article or a status page. Many also email affected users. If the only evidence is a message you cannot match to anything on the company's own channels, treat it with suspicion.

A site going offline is not proof of a breach either. Archive of Our Own, the fan fiction archive known as AO3, has had outages caused by distributed denial-of-service attacks. Those attacks flood a site with traffic to knock it offline. That is a different problem from attackers stealing account records.

A useful notice explains what happened and roughly when it was discovered. It lists the categories of data involved, such as email addresses, usernames, passwords, payment card numbers or Social Security numbers. It should say whether stored passwords were hashed. Hashing makes passwords harder to recover, but it does not make them safe to keep using. The notice should also describe what the company is doing and what it wants users to do.

That list of exposed data shapes your response. A leak limited to email addresses mainly raises the risk of targeted scam messages. Exposed passwords mean resetting credentials. Leaked financial or government ID numbers call for stronger protections against fraud.

Legal rules push many companies toward disclosure. Every U.S. state has a breach notification law. In the European Union, the General Data Protection Regulation requires organizations to report qualifying breaches to regulators within 72 hours of becoming aware of them, where feasible. The U.K. applies the same deadline under its own version of the regulation. Timing and detail still vary from company to company. Check back for updated notices as an investigation continues. Early announcements are sometimes revised as more is learned.

Reset the password on the affected account as soon as the company confirms the breach. Make the change by typing the site's address into your browser or opening its official app, not by following a link in an email or text.

A new password matters even if the company says stored passwords were hashed or encrypted. Hashing makes stolen passwords harder to read. Weak or common passwords can still be cracked, though. Changing yours closes that gap regardless of how the site stored it.

The replacement should be long, random and used nowhere else. Length does more work than a scattering of symbols. The U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology takes that position in its digital identity guidelines, published as Special Publication 800-63B. Those guidelines favor longer passwords over complicated composition rules. A passphrase built from several unrelated words is one way to get length that is still practical to type.

Avoid small edits to the old password. Adding a digit at the end or capitalizing a different letter produces something an attacker holding the leaked version can guess. The new password should share nothing with the one that was exposed.

After the reset, look for a setting that signs you out of other devices or ends active sessions. Many services place it near the password option in their security settings. Ending old sessions cuts off anyone who logged in with the stolen password before you changed it. If the site keeps a log of recent sign-ins, check it for locations or devices you do not recognize.

Review the account's recovery details next. Confirm that the recovery email address and phone number are still yours. Someone who briefly controlled the account could have swapped them to regain access later. If the site uses security questions and their answers may have been exposed, change those answers as well.

On the fan fiction archive Archive of Our Own (AO3), users change their password from the account's Preferences. The same approach carries over to any site. Find the account or security settings, make the change there and confirm you can sign in with the new password.

Update the password anywhere it is saved, such as a phone app or a browser's built-in password store. A stored copy of the old password will no longer work. Leaving it in place can produce repeated failed sign-in attempts. Some sites treat those as suspicious and respond with a temporary lockout.

If the leaked password also protects any other account, change it there too, and give each account its own unique password. Attackers routinely test stolen logins on other sites. A reused password can turn one breach into many.

That testing has a name: credential stuffing. Attackers take email-and-password pairs from a breached site and enter them on the login pages of other services. The attack succeeds only where a person used the same pair in more than one place. They do not need to break into the second site. If the same pair works there, they simply sign in as you.

The stakes of the breached site do not set the limit on the damage. A password first created for a recipe site, a game forum or a retailer's loyalty program may have spread to accounts that hold money or personal records. The breached site may never have stored anything sensitive. The accounts sharing its password might.

Start with your email account. Email is where password reset links arrive. Anyone who controls your inbox can reset the passwords on most of your other accounts. Next, move to banking, payment and brokerage accounts. Then cover shopping sites that store a card number or shipping address. Social media and cloud storage accounts come after that. Leave forgotten or rarely used accounts for last, but do not skip them.

Small variations do not count as unique. Adding a digit or swapping the year at the end of a known password leaves a pattern. Attackers can guess the new version from the leaked one. Each replacement should be unrelated to the old password and to every other password you use.

Few people can memorize dozens of unrelated passwords. A password manager handles that job. It generates long random passwords, stores them in an encrypted vault and fills them in on the matching site. Standalone options include 1Password and Bitwarden. Google $GOOGL Password Manager is built into Chrome and Android. Apple $AAPL builds a similar password tool into the iPhone and Mac.

Several of these tools can also flag passwords you have reused across sites. That report gives you a working checklist for this step. The only password you then need to remember is the one that unlocks the manager. It should be long, unique and used nowhere else.

Turn on two-factor authentication wherever a site offers it. With a second check in place, a password stolen in a breach is no longer enough on its own to open the account.

Two-factor authentication, often shortened to 2FA, asks for something beyond the password before it lets anyone in. That second factor is usually a code sent to a phone, a code generated by an app or a physical device the account owner holds. An attacker holding a leaked password still has to get past that extra step.

Start with the email account. Most other services send password reset links to an inbox. Whoever controls the email address can often take over everything connected to it. Banking, payment and shopping accounts come next, followed by any account that stores personal details or links to a real name.

The methods differ in strength. Text message codes are the most widely offered and the easiest to set up. They are still an improvement over a password alone, but they have known weaknesses. A criminal who persuades a mobile carrier to move a phone number to a new SIM card can receive those codes. That scam is known as SIM swapping. A person can also be tricked into typing an SMS code into a fake login page.

Authenticator apps such as Google $GOOGL Authenticator and Microsoft $MSFT Authenticator generate short codes on the phone itself, typically refreshing every 30 seconds. The codes never travel over the phone network. A SIM swap therefore does not hand them to an attacker.

Passkeys go a step further. A passkey replaces the typed password with a cryptographic key stored on a device and unlocked with a fingerprint, face scan or PIN. It is tied to the real website's domain. It will not work on a lookalike site. Physical security keys that plug into a computer or tap against a phone work on the same principle. Both authenticator apps and passkeys resist phishing better than SMS codes.

Setup usually sits in a site's security or account settings. Many services issue a set of single-use backup codes when 2FA is switched on. Store those somewhere safe and offline, or in a password manager. They are the way back into the account if the phone is lost or replaced.

Not every site offers a second factor. For accounts without one, a long, unique password remains the main defense against someone logging in with leaked credentials.

Have I Been Pwned is a free breach-lookup service. You type in an email address and see which known data breaches included it. Running that check after a breach notice shows whether the same address has turned up in other leaks too.

Security researcher Troy Hunt created the service. It collects data from breaches that have become public and indexes the email addresses they contain. A search returns a list of the breached sites tied to that address. Each entry typically describes what kinds of data the leak held, such as passwords, usernames, phone numbers or physical addresses.

Those descriptions matter. Each entry's list of exposed data shows which follow-up steps apply to that particular leak.

The results have limits. The service can show only breaches that have surfaced publicly and been added to its database. A clean result does not prove your data was never exposed. It means the address has not appeared in any breach the service knows about. An incident a company has not disclosed will not show up. Neither will a leak that never circulated.

The search can also turn up accounts you forgot you had. Old forum logins, retired shopping sites and abandoned apps often keep your data long after you stop using them. If one of those appears in the results, log in and change the password. Close the account entirely if the site allows it. Each dormant account you delete is one fewer place your details can leak from.

The service also lets you sign up for alerts. It emails you if your address appears in a breach it adds later. That gives you notice even when the affected company is slow to tell its users.

The results also show how widely a single email address has spread. An address that appears in several breaches is tied to accounts at each of those sites. That matters most on platforms built around pen names. On Archive of Our Own (AO3), the fan fiction archive, many writers publish under pseudonyms. Every account there still requires an email address. If a personal address registered on AO3 turns up in the results from another site's leak, that address is already circulating in breach data. Anyone who wants a pen name kept apart from a legal name can use that finding to choose a fresh address for the pseudonymous account.

Expect scam emails, texts and phone calls that mention a recent data breach, and never hand over a password or payment details in reply. A legitimate breach notice normally asks for neither.

Scammers watch breach headlines. Once a company announces a leak, fake notices can appear that copy its logo, tone and wording. Some claim your account will be locked unless you verify it. Others promise a refund, a settlement payment or free identity monitoring in exchange for personal information.

Treat every link in a breach-related message as untrusted. Instead of clicking, type the company's web address into your browser or open its official app. The same rule applies to phone numbers. Call the number printed on the company's website or on the back of your card, not the one in the message.

A few warning signs show up again and again. Pressure to act within hours is one. A sender address or link that resembles the real domain but differs by a letter, a hyphen or an extra word is another. A request to type a password, a one-time code or a full card number into a linked page is the clearest signal of all. Attachments that claim to contain breach details deserve the same suspicion.

Text messages follow the same pattern. A short text with a link and a warning about your account is a common format. A text can be harder to inspect than an email. On a phone screen, the full web address is often hidden.

Leaked data can make these messages more convincing. A scammer who holds your username, a pen name or an old order detail can drop it into a message so it looks official. On a pseudonymous fan site such as Archive of Our Own (AO3), a message that names your AO3 username is not proof that it came from the archive. Accurate personal details show only that the sender has some of your data.

If you already clicked a link or typed in a password, change that password from the real site right away. Then check the account for settings or contact details you did not change. Report the message through your email provider's phishing option, and delete it.

If a breach exposed your Social Security number or financial details, place a credit freeze at Equifax, Experian and TransUnion. A freeze stops lenders from pulling your credit file, which blocks most new accounts opened in your name.

A fraud alert is a lighter option. Since September 2018, U.S. federal law has required the three nationwide credit bureaus to offer freezes free of charge. Fraud alerts cost nothing to place either.

A freeze restricts access to your credit report. Most lenders check that report before they approve a credit card, a loan or another line of credit. If they cannot see it, the application usually stalls. A thief holding your name, birth date and Social Security number then has a harder time turning them into new debt.

Each bureau handles its own freeze. A freeze placed at Equifax does not carry over to Experian or TransUnion. Full coverage means contacting all three. Each bureau takes requests online, by phone or by mail.

A freeze does not lock you out of your own credit. Before applying for a mortgage, a car loan or a new card, you can lift the freeze temporarily or remove it, then restore it afterward. Each bureau provides an online account or a PIN to manage this. Asking the lender which bureau it plans to check can spare you from lifting all three.

A freeze also has limits. It does not lower your credit score. It does not stop your existing creditors from reviewing your file. It does not block fraudulent charges on cards you already hold.

A fraud alert works differently. It flags your file and asks any business checking your credit to take extra steps to confirm your identity before opening an account. An initial fraud alert lasts one year. You request it from one bureau, and that bureau must notify the other two. Your credit stays fully available while the alert is active. Confirmed identity theft victims can request a longer extended alert.

A leaked Social Security number, bank account number or card number is the main reason to use either tool. Some people place a freeze and a fraud alert together.

Both tools belong to the U.S. credit system, and rules differ elsewhere. In the U.K., the fraud prevention service Cifas offers Protective Registration, which flags a person's details to its member organizations.

Pull your free credit reports from AnnualCreditReport.com, the federally authorized source, and read your bank and card statements line by line. Those two records show whether anyone has used exposed details to open accounts or spend money in your name.

AnnualCreditReport.com gives access to reports from all three nationwide credit bureaus: Equifax, Experian and TransUnion. Each bureau keeps its own file. A lender may report to one bureau but not the others. A new account can show up on one report and be missing from the other two. Reading all three gives the fullest picture.

Start with the personal information section. An address you never lived at could be an early sign that someone has applied for credit with your details. A name variation you do not use or an employer you never worked for can point to the same thing.

Next, go through the list of accounts. Every credit card, loan and line of credit should be one you recognize. An unfamiliar account needs a closer look. So does a collections entry for a debt you never owed, or a balance that does not match your own records.

The inquiries section matters too. A hard inquiry appears when a lender reviews your file after a credit application. Hard inquiries from companies you never applied with can mean someone tried to borrow in your name. Checking your own report does not count as a hard inquiry and does not affect your credit score.

If an entry is wrong, the Fair Credit Reporting Act gives you the right to dispute it. You can file the dispute with the credit bureau and with the company that supplied the information. The bureau must investigate and correct or remove information that cannot be verified.

Credit reports list accounts and balances, not individual purchases. Bank and card statements fill that gap. Small charges from unfamiliar merchants need a closer look. Criminals sometimes test a stolen card with a minor purchase before attempting a larger one.

Contact the bank or card issuer as soon as you spot an unauthorized charge. Use the phone number on the back of the card or on the issuer's official website. The issuer can block the card and send a replacement with a new number. Many banking apps also offer alerts for each transaction. Those alerts make unfamiliar activity easier to catch between monthly statements.

If someone has used your stolen information to open an account, take out a loan or make purchases in your name, report it at IdentityTheft.gov. The Federal Trade Commission runs the site. It turns your report into a personalized recovery plan.

This step is for misuse, not exposure alone. A notice saying your email address or password leaked in a breach does not mean your identity has been stolen. Identity theft begins when someone acts on that data. Warning signs include a credit card you never applied for or a collection notice for a debt you don't recognize. A tax return filed under your Social Security number is another, as is a medical bill for care you never received. If nothing like that has happened, securing your accounts and watching them closely is the appropriate response.

Once misuse appears, the site walks you through questions about what happened. It asks which accounts were affected, when you noticed the problem and what information you believe was used. Your answers shape a checklist of next steps that fits your situation rather than a generic list.

The report has practical value beyond the record itself. Completing a report through IdentityTheft.gov produces an FTC Identity Theft Report. You can give that document to businesses and credit bureaus when you dispute fraudulent accounts or charges. It shows that you have formally reported the crime. Some people also file a report with their local police department. Certain creditors may ask for one.

Creating an account on the site lets you save your recovery plan and return to it later. You can mark steps as finished and update the report if new fraud turns up. Cleaning up identity theft can stretch over weeks or longer. One central plan keeps the work organized.

Contact each affected company directly. Call the fraud department of every bank, lender or retailer where a fraudulent account was opened or a charge was made. Ask them to close the account. Request written confirmation that you are not responsible for the debt.

Keep a written record of every step. Note the date of each call, the name of the person you spoke with and what they agreed to do. Save copies of letters, emails and any documents you send. Mailing important disputes by certified mail with a return receipt gives you proof that the company received them.

IdentityTheft.gov serves people in the U.S. and works within U.S. law. Readers in the U.K. and other countries should report fraud through their own national police or fraud reporting service instead.

On a pseudonymous site such as Archive of Our Own (AO3), the biggest breach risk is often personal rather than financial. A leaked email address can tie a pen name to the real person behind it. A separate email account used only for fan activity limits that exposure.

AO3 is a fan fiction archive run by the Organization for Transformative Works, a nonprofit. An account there needs a username and an email address. Many writers and readers post under pseudonyms. Some keep their fan work entirely apart from their professional or family lives.

That separation only holds if the details behind the account stay apart too. An email address built from a full name, or one also used for work, banking and social media, acts as a bridge. If any site's account records leaked, anyone holding the list could match that address to a public profile elsewhere.

A dedicated email address breaks that bridge. Create one that contains no part of your legal name, your birth year or your workplace. Reserve it for AO3 and similar community sites. Some email providers also offer alias addresses that forward to a main inbox, and those serve the same purpose.

Handles create a second link. A pen name reused on Tumblr, Discord, Reddit $RDDT or a personal blog lets someone connect accounts by searching a single word. A different handle on each platform makes that matching harder. At minimum, keep the AO3 name off any account that carries your real identity.

Profile details matter as well. Location, age, job title, school and photos can narrow a pseudonym down to one person even without an email leak. Leaving those fields blank reduces what a stranger can piece together. Keeping personal anecdotes out of author notes does the same.

These habits carry over to any hobby or community site, from craft forums to recipe boards. The risk is not limited to fan fiction. It exists wherever a person shares interests they would rather keep apart from their name.

AO3 has faced other kinds of incidents that differ from an account-data breach. The site has gone offline during distributed denial-of-service attacks, which flood servers with traffic rather than steal stored records. Its publicly posted works have also been scraped for AI datasets. Neither is the same as a breach of account data.
