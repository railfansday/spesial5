What If We Told You She’s a Mastermind? A Tale of Pop Stardom

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

A SIDE A child is born in 1989 and grows up on a Christmas tree farm south of Reading, Pennsylvania. She is destined for the stage. She plays Sandy in Grease, Kim in Bye Bye Birdie, Maria in The Sound of Music. A computer repairman teaches her guitar, and she strums untiL her 12-year-old fingers bleed. At 15, she signs with a newly opened record label in Nashville. It’s soon clear that she’s a generational talent. Her name foretells her future fate: the Latin root of celebrity is celere, meaning “rapid,” “fast,” “swift.”

She has romantic associations with celebrities—some of whom treat her ­badly, including a member of a boy band who breaks up with her over a 27-second phone call. By that pOint, she is a celebrity herself. But fame has not warped her the way it has nearly everybody else: She doesn’t even have a manager! What she has are her many great friends. She tells them everything and is pleasantly surprised when they do not put her on blast.

At 29, the record company from Nashville betrays her by selling her life’s work to her archnemesis’s manager. She would have bought the rights to her own songs, only she was never afforded the chance. The sale between the two men occurs without her knowing. When she finds out, she appeals to her loyal fans, who defend her in her moral outrage. With their support, she wins the rights to her music. By then, she’s met her shining knight. After a happy and very public courtship, your English and your gym teachers wed.

When the curtain falls on humanity, they will say that for a time, she defined American sonic life—that her music owned the ’10s and the long ’20s.

B SIDE A child is born and grows up on an 11-acre Christmas tree farm that her third-generation banker father bought from a client at Merrill Lynch, where he is a vice president and manages a portfolio around $270 million. When she is 10 years old, the family moves to a 3,560-square-foot, five-bedroom, three-and-a-half-bath Georgian Colonial in Wyomissing, Pennsylvania. A visitor calls the house a “six-star hotel.”

Her mOther is “definitely a theater mom, very aggressive,” says Sandy Carbone, whose son directs The Star in Grease, Bye Bye Birdie, The Sound of Music. The mom asks Carbone’s other son, a computer repairman named Ronnie Cremer, if he might teach her daughter to play a 12-string guitar. “She used to complain a lot about the guitar chords,” Cremer says, “that it hurt her hands.” Cremer relays this to the parents. “Just keep worKing her,” they reply. “She needs this to get better.”

Once, during a lesson, the mother returns home with Taco Bell. The girl, who is not yet a star, scampers to the kitchen, but salad is her lot. “Nobody wants to see a fat pop star,” Cremer recalls the mother saying. (Cremer first told the New York Daily News this story in 2015. The Star declined to comment then. Vanity Fair sent The Star multiple questions while reporting this story; she declined to comment.)

Her father’s connections lead to his daughter singing the national anthem before a minor league baseball game, which leads to another performance, which he videotapes and sends to a marketing manager for the Philadelphia 76ers. Which is how she ends up singing the national anthem for them, wearing red, white, and blue.

Four months into her guitar-playing career, according to Cremer, her father begins driving his daughter to various gigs, giving away tickets to business associates and other acquaintances. Cremer says her father asks that they cheer his daughter loudly so she always seems to be playing to a full house. He captures these performances on tape to send to Nashville, before moving the family there.

Her fame grows. She becomes a Star. Her erstwhile suitors grouse that the songs she writes about them are misrepresentations—that their relationship lasted only a few weeks, or that the call was 27 seconds because she hung up first. In lieu of a manager, what The Star has is her mother and a closed board of stakeholders. Her many friends do not dare speak ill of her—dare not speak of her at all.

By 2006 the father has a stake in the Nashville record company, after making a financial investment worth a reported $500,000, according to Music Business Worldwide. He makes $15 million when it sells. In 2023 she becomes a billionaire on account of her tour, the highest grossing of all time. Her fans do not mind. “Of all the billionaires,” a fan says, “she is my favorite billionaire!”

When the curtain falls on humanity, they will say that for a time she defined American sonic life—that her music owned the ’10s and the long ’20s.

A SIDE The Star is beloved. ­Universally, famously. She is friendship bracelets. She is sold-out stadiums. She is honorary degrees. She is a wedding reception for 1,000 and $26 million to charity for the inconvenience to any. She says vote and tens of thousands register. She wears a bandeau “nap top” and midi skirt, and everyone else buys a bandeau nap top and midi skirt. She is our most gracious homecoming queen, our Miss Americana. In these divided times, everybody agrees: The Star is power.

B SIDE Across her 12 eras and interviews with over 100 people who worked with her, who Work with her, who were her confidants, contemporaries, scholars, critics, other anoraks, nemeses, neighbors, classmates, teachers, and lover, what emerges as The Star’s most enduring identity is not musician, not artist, not celebrity, but a combination of victim and victimizer.

The dissenting chorus has penned its own folklore about how The Star often seems to present herself as persecuted even as she sits on a throne of gold and diamonds. Cold, they say, is the steel of her ax to grind. She may not have explicitly ordered her detractors to be banned from parties and other social occasions, or banned her friends from collaborating with her perceived opponents, but tHe results are just that.

Her highness is controlling and manipulative, they say—of charts, of public opinion, of social dynamics. They say she has been known to crucify those who cross her. Others say The Star weaponizes her sense of victimhood, that by foregrounding her own suffering, she believes she is speaking from a position of superior virtue. “She is punitive,” says a music industry professional we’ll call The Exec. “She loves to smack people in the heAd” figuratively, says a person familiar with the matter who used to be close with The Star. We’ll refer to them as The Person Familiar. “This is her way of feeling like she can discipline people.”

A lawyer with knowledge of The Star declined to be interviewed using their own phone, choosing to speak instead through an intermediary. A manager asked for our correspondence to be deleted. A pasTor from her childhood church said they could not speak without express permission from The Star’s family. When these mortals did speak of The Star in public—in line, at the airport, mid-flight, on foot, on the job, at pickup, on the way to grocery stores, parks, or yoga—it was softly, using initials. A Washington Post reporter who recently wrote a book about The Star has noted that countless sources refused to go on the record even to say nice things about her.

Someone who previouslY collaborated with The Star—and asked that their profession not be mentioned because The Star had worked with so few with this specific job—advised I turn down this assignment. Did they think The Star would read the article, though? “Oh yes.”

The Star’s Manichaean notion of the world means “if you’re in the circle, she takes amazing care of you”—according to a musician who toured with her and spoke from behind the scrim of a nondisclosure agreement.

If you’re not, you face social censure. “If you look at history,” says The Person Familiar, “she’s always had villains. Back to her high school days.” Or earlier.

A SIDE When she was young, The Star told Katie Couric in 2009, she had no friends. Or rather, “I had that group of friends when I was about 12. [Then] they all just decided they didn’t wanna hang out with me anymore. I would go and sit dOwn at the lunch table with my friends. And they would get up and move their trays to another table.” If one definition of artist is “a person uniquely sensitive to the vicissitudes of life, from whence their great art comes; someone for whom much happens, and mUch more is expected in return,” then the original pain of The Star appears to be this early sense of loss: the agony of being passed over or feeling lost among peers. No amount of later validation, it seems, would allay it.

B SIDE “That wasn’t true,” says Sharon Luyben. “We had assigned seating in junior high.” (One former classmate of The Star recalls assigned seating at lunch; another isn’t sure. The Wyomissing Area School District did not respond to questions.)

Luyben—Mrs. Luyben to The Star—had been the music teacher at Wyomissing Area Junior/Senior High for 34 years. She taught The Star in seventh and eighth grades and then again on Sundays at their church. They first met during a 2002 production of The Sound of Music in which The Star played an older von Trapp child. (She played Maria, the lead, in a different production.)

Chanice Skinner, who sat next to The Star in Luyben’s class, recalls The Star being “always part of the popular crowd,” among preppy kids who wore Ralph Lauren shirts with the collars popped. “If you weren’t part of her friend group,” Skinner says, “you didn’t exist to her.” Years later she was watching a Target commercial in which The Star talked about being bullied. “I was like, You were part of that. What are you talking about? If anything, you were one of the bullies.”

In Skinner’s estimation, The Star’s success, like so many aspects of American life, was overdetermined. “That’s what happens when you have a faMily that is already well-off and able to back you, [to] fund your dream.” Did it seem, though, that The Star carried exceptional promise? “We all had promise,” says Skinner, who starred in plays with The Star and now sells industrial saws in West Reading. “It’s just that she had money.”

Arielle Peppers was a year behind The Star and sat on the other side of Skinner in Luyben’s class for multiple quarters. She remembers The Star coming to class in Ugg boots. Peppers had transferred from the nearby city of ReAding after her neighbor was stabbed in the neck. She asked her mother for a pair of the same boots. “Do you know how much they cost?” her mother said. A pair of Uggs amounted to half her single mother’s paycheck working variously at a bakery, at a dairy mill, and as a caretaker. Peppers recalls The Star owning multiples of Uggs: “She had the brown one, the black one, the blue one.”

Peppers, who works as a caretaker while maintaining her music practice (under the stage name A.Star), remembers The Star being teased on a single occasion: at a school talent show, when she sang about her crush on a classmate called Corey. (In the aforementioneD 2010 Target commercial, The Star recalls having a crush on a boy named “Corey Robertson” when she was in fourth grade.) The Star had brought her own sound system to perform a song her classmates remember being about “Corey’s eyes.” The Star’s mother helped set it up. The Star won.

The Star’s debut album, released in 2006, includes a song called “Stay Beautiful” that starts like this: “Cory’s eyes”—spelled without the e—“are like a jungle / He smiles, it’s like the radio.”

A SIDE Every Starbucks lover and their mother—or their manager—knows the story. The Star convinces her parents to move to Nashville so she can try her luck in country music. Her music reportedly finds its way to The Machine—an executive from Nashville—in part through his wife, who gets it through a music executive The Star’s father knows. Fame follows.

In time, The Machine decides to sell the label. He tells The Star of his intention and says that he is willing to part with her master recordings—accounting for 50 to 80 percent of the label’s revenue—only if The Star agrees to makE new music in exchange for old, the same bad deal that stymied musicians like Prince and Janet Jackson. The Star has fought many a battle in her time, a great deal of them against men who mean her harm. It is impossible, she cries, to be a woman in the music industry; history proves her right over and over.

A high-profile Manager representing top talent—some of whom have feuded with The Star, including Justin Bieber—hears the news and calls The Machine. On June 28, 2019, The Machine’s board approves a sale, giving The Manager control of The Star’s masters. Two days later The Star takes to Tumblr.

B SIDE Her kingdom terrorized, The Star rises to defend her doMinion. She takes special umbrage with The Manager, accusing him of “incessant, manipulative bullying.” The Person Familiar alleges The Star only ever spoke fondly of The Manager, while The Manager alleges he met The Star only three times, according to The Free Press.

In her Tumblr post, she also mentions an illegal recording of a phone call between her and an archnemesis. The Person Familiar contends they told The Star at the time it was a bad idea to let the nemesis use her name in a certain famous song. Eventually, it seems The Star would come to agree with them.

The Star’s fans send death threats to The Manager. The Manager relays this to The Star’s team; he does not hear from her. He does hear from others who were allegedly smote by The Star for their perceived treachery. They welcome him into the fellowship of the chastened. Mutual friends of both The Star and The ­Manager—a ­singer, a talk show host, an actor—try to help them make up to no avail.

Meanwhile, The Star begins rerecording her old albums, depreciating the value of the originals enough to buy them all back eventually. A hitherto unprecedented feat, it is declared a victory for the people. It’s certainly a victory to The Star, who, in making herself a metonym for all womEn, successfully equates criticism against her as criticism against womenkind.

A SIDE Since her debut in 2006, The Star has been celebrated for her very brilliant songwriting—often about the marriage plot or the pains and possibilities of living in the snakeskin of an ended relationship, but also about historical figures, or literary characters, or herself. There are only so many ways to say “Love me” or “Treat me right,” and she says them all, with tremendous emotional fluency. And she does it all in rhyme, that hooked-up sound so pleasing to the human ear. “There is a reason,” poet Michael Robbins says, “we don’t say penis pic. We say dick pic.”

Also: She is generous to fellow musicians, many of whom open for her, then ascend to new heights of fame. Many remain in her orbit.

Years after opening for The Star on tour, another pop star—let’s call her The Brat—releases her sixth studio album, where she explores the vulnerability she feels next to The Star. Despite the jab, in August 2024, The Star tells New York that she has been blown away by The Brat’s “melodic sensibilities,” calling her lyrics “surreal and inventive.” She adds, “I love to see hard work like that pay off.” In February 2025, The Star is seen dancing jubilantly to The Brat as she performs onstage. The Star is benevolent; she rises above.

B SIDE The Star has historically urged her listeners not to separate the art from the artist, openly turning her life into lyrics and lyrics into hits. It is verboten, however, for others to use her as their source material. Only on her watch may one be casually cruel in the name of being honest.

In 2018, after The Brat opened for The Star, The Star began platonically courting The Brat, inviting her into her inner circle. The Brat shied from The Star’s advances—because...brat—even when she and The Star dated men in the same English rock band.

In 2024, The Brat released a song about her insecurities around the world’s biggest pop star: “I couldn’t even be her if I tried,” she sings. “I’m opposite, I’m on the other side.”

In 2025, The Star seemed to clap back to The Brat by releasing a song of her own about their relationship. In it, she accused The Brat of consuming illegal substances—which is fair enough.

In the time since The Brat released a song about The Star, The Exec says, The Star has “done this performance that she’s supportive of her...but she’s not.” In fact, says The Exec, artists who seem earmarked as The Star’s intentionally DO not collaborate professionally with her rivals, who also seem to be barred from taking part in her social world.

The incident recalled other entangle­ments with other brunettes. A Protégé was affiliated with The Star until 2021, when she sent her debut album to the wife of her archnemesis. Seven-ish weeks later, The Protégé credited The Star on a song, which one occasionally does to ward off litigation and fan outcry, according to copyright lawyers.

In 2023, The Protégé released a song that appeared to intimate a falling-out between her and a once-beloved mentor: “Trust that you betrayed, confusion that still lingers / Took everything I loved and crushed it in between your fingers.” But then, at the 2024 Grammys, The Star was seen vibing to The Protégé’s performance.

Before that was The California Girl—an adult pretending to be a child—and her feud with The Star, who might be a child pretending to be an adult. And, of course, the Kardashian affair, the details of which we are excluding from this narrative. (Yet another protégé, let’s call her The Blonde, remains in The Star’s good graces.)

“It’s truly Regina George,” The Exec says.

There once lived a music critic who dared pan The Star. Around then, members of her friend group, one of whom she lived with, were getting courted by The Star. The Critic felt The Star was “ordering friends from a catalog.” After her negative review ran, The Critic’s friends—professional musicians all, who previously credited The Critic for their early success—shunned her. Her roommate, seemingly of her own accord, asked her to move out of their shared duplex. The Star had won.

A SIDE In a 2020 documentary about herself, The Star repeated a story she has often told about how a big part of her feels frozen at 16, when she became famous. Though if she is frozen at 16, she has repeatedly—and mightily—fought against stasis. In her younger years, she avoided electoral politics, could not see how articulating her opinion was necessary. Later, she found her voice, making statements and endorsements for Democratic candidates, including Kamala Harris for president. She speaks in favor of LGBTQ+ rights, gun control, and making Juneteenth a national holiday. She becomes someone on whom nothing is lost.

B SIDE You only become famous if you really, really want to. Lana Del Rey said as much to the BBC in 2024. And The Star really, really did. “She wants it,” Del Rey said. “She’s told me so many times she wants it more than anyone.”

But if fame is a fickle food, as ­Emily Dickinson—The Star’s sixth cousin, according to Ancestry.com—observed, it is also impunity. You might end up like Barbra Streisand, cloning Coton de Tulears and building a mall in your basement. Or like Jim Carrey, watching YouTube videos all day from your estate in Maui. Or like whatever Elon Musk is doing right now.

Fame also fractures trust. A life grows and shrinks in inverse proportion to the size of its celebrity.

When they were close, The Person Familiar says, The Star’s professional life was run by a cabinet of three, including her mother, who acts as The Star’s manager; her longtime tour manager; and her publicist, to whom we only beg for mercy.

“Nobody is beyond reproach with her,” says The Person Familiar. Perhaps not even The Star’s sometimes close and dear friend who, after their private correspondence spilled across the tabloids as part of a public lawsuit, was not seen at The Star’s wedding. “If you don’t see it her way, she comes after you.”

“It’s always the same,” The Person Familiar says. “They say something she doesn’t like and they are dead to her. She’d be at dinner at [redacted]’s, then three weeks later, ‘Oh no, I hate her guts.’ ”

A/B SIDE Okay, so The Star has a reputation for being punitive. She seems too invested in her own victimhood. Where might she have learned to be so?

Stars function, as cultural theorist Stuart Hall observed, as a mirror. “I want you to know / I’m a mirrorball,” The Star sang in a 2020 hit. “I’ll show you every version of yourself tonight.”

The Star holds up a mirror to us, and what the mirror says is this: If churches were once our society’s primary source of community, identity, and meaning, in these secular times we turn instead to celebrities. And we look, especially, to singers, because who among us is immune to music? The way a song can pitch you forward in space, sustain you through time; the way you swear some songs are written just for you.

A SIDE Stars, they’re just like us. Despite her life in perfect meter, lasting love had long eluded The Star. But it turned out she was a princess, this was a fairy tale, and we were in Hollywood. Her latest beau—regretfully we must call him Redwood—brought to her life a new form of intensity, a golden light, as they courted at a stadium show, a podcast interview, more shows, in Instagram posts. They have so much in common, The Star points out: They both perform at stadiums. He practices; she rehearses. He plays games, she plays shows.

And all she had ever wanted from the world was that it celebrate her milky goodness, that her altruism not be mistaken for covert narcissism. Ahead of her wedding, the happy couple donates $26 million to local charities. Since then, The Star released four new songs, launched an Oscar campaign, including performing at the Academy Museum Gala, and got a haircut.

B SIDE Summer 2026. The world’s most famous sexy baby—who insists that she is really a monster on a hill—chooses, as the site of her nuptials, midtown Manhattan during a heat wave. Her guests nibble on buffet food as thousands of New Yorkers lose power.

It is not lost on them that her $26 million giveaway, apart from being a tax write-off, is akin to a person making $81,228—the median household income in New York City—giving away $1,055. (In fall 2025, when this article was commissioned, The Star was worth $1.6 billion. By the time this issue goes to press, she will be worth over $2 billion, or the GDP of Eritrea.)

“To become a billionaire,” The Star’s ex Matt Healy once told the Doomscroll podcast, “you don’t really care that much about culture.” He wasn’t speaking specifically about The Star but the deadening effects of modern wealth. Beyond need lies want. Beyond want, greed.

A/B SIDE And beyond greed was a tricked-out walled garden for the enchanted, her betrothed, and 1,000 of their family and friends, including, according to news reports, at least one government contractor who did not respond to multiple requests for comment. (In March his company took over our nation’s largest ICE detention facility. Earlier this year a father of three was pronounced dead there, after struggling with five guards, the second death at the camp in as many months. His name was Geraldo Lunas Campos.)

In other news, the wedding reportedly featured a raffle where a lucky guest won a Chanel bag. Another allegedly won a 1970 Chevrolet Chevelle. Madison Square Garden Entertainment Corp. reportedly locked out its own security staff for the occasion, like it was a Mob bar—the kind with no windows. Not even members of the New York Police Department were allowed their own phones on site.

Love may be free, but the performance of it is not. It requires crates, trucks, officers on overtime, private jets, SUVs, HVACs, all the queen’s horses, all the queen’s men, a Jumbotron. Conspiracy theories do not tell you the truth, but they do tell a kind of psychological truth about a place or its people. Had The Star and Redwood filmed the ceremony, perhaps for theatrical release or streaming—maybe on Disney+, which paid The Star a king’s ransom for the rights to another project? (A person with knowledge says the couple has no plans to drop a documentary about their big day.)

Around the barricades of Madison Square Garden, in the wake left by The Star, her groom, and their guests, her fans gathered. Some had been there for hours, wanting to be nearer to their pious majesty. A white truck passed. Pastries—from the merrymaking gentry presumably, though who can say for certain—were distributed, with an assist from a cop in uniform and gear.

If you listened carefully then to the sounds of the city—not for any semantic meaning, but if you were to really pitch your ears to the utopic promise of music—you might have heard the determined whoosh of a weighted blade dropping, dropping, dropping, the ax of public opinion falling in downward demotic motion.
