Saharan dust brings unexpected passengers to the Amazon rainforest

Tanggal: 2026-11-11

Saharan dust carried a distinct bacterial community across the Atlantic to the Amazon rainforest, where airborne endotoxin levels roughly tripled.

The air above the Amazon rainforest looks calm from the ground. Under a microscope, it’s teeming with pollen, fungal spores, plant debris, and bacteria.

The fungal community is usually remarkably stable, while bacterial communities vary with height and time. But during five days in February 2020, something foreign arrived.

Saharan dust, blown across the Atlantic over ten days, carried with it an entirely different bacterial community. Its arrival also coincided with a threefold increase in airborne endotoxins.

The study was led by Jens Weber and Bettina Weber at the University of Graz in Austria.

They worked with researchers from the Max Planck Institute for Chemistry and a network of Brazilian partner institutions.

The scientists collected aerosol samples at the Amazon Tall Tower Observatory.

This is a 325-meter research tower surrounded by hundreds of kilometers of unbroken primary rainforest in Brazil’s Uatumã Sustainable Development Reserve.

Africa’s drylands are the planet’s single biggest dust source. They pump 11 to 15 teragrams of mineral particles into the atmosphere each year – roughly half the global total.

Those plumes regularly cross the Atlantic, depositing phosphorus and iron that fertilize the Amazon basin.

What wasn’t clear was which microbes the dust carried and how its arrival affected the air over the rainforest.

The team installed high-volume dust filter samplers at two heights on the ATTO tower: 42 meters, within the forest canopy, and 323 meters, well above it.

They collected filters daily from 10 February to 2 March 2020, then ran genetic analyses to characterize the bacterial and fungal communities in the samples.

The five-day dust event hit between 18 and 22 February. Backward trajectory modeling confirmed the air masses had traveled from North Africa.

The change was dramatic. Mean aerosol mass concentrations jumped nearly eightfold – from about 2.7 micrograms per cubic meter under normal conditions to over 21 micrograms during the dust event, peaking at 45.78.

The particles’ calcium content was consistent with Saharan mineral dust.

Under normal conditions, the bacterial community at 42 meters reflected local canopy sources – microbes associated with bark, leaves, and lichens.

At 323 meters, it picked up more long-range material. But during the dust intrusion, bacterial communities at both heights converged sharply toward the same profile: organisms associated with dryland soils, biological soil crusts, and marine environments.

Endospore-forming bacteria belonging to the phylum Bacillota – minor players under background conditions – became the dominant feature at both heights.

The researchers identified a distinct signature of terrestrial soil genera including Clostridium, Brevibacillus, and Tumebacillus.

Cyanobacteria characteristic of biocrusts – Schizothrix and Trichocoleus – showed up too.

Marine bacteria made the journey as well. Synechococcus, detected roughly 1,000 kilometers from the nearest coastline, illustrated the dust plume’s capacity to scoop up ocean microbes during its transatlantic passage.

The fungi, by contrast, barely noticed. Wood-decomposing Basidiomycota dominated at both heights before, during, and after the dust event.

At 42 meters, they made up 93.8% of fungal sequences.

The researchers suggest that abundant local spores may have masked any fungal material arriving from Africa.

“Soils in arid regions and deserts are largely colonized by microbial communities, known as biological soil crusts,” Bettina Weber said.

“Genetically, the bacteria match organisms from the Sahara, which proves that they are transported from African biocrusts across the Atlantic by wind all the way to Brazil’s rainforest.”

The most immediately concerning finding wasn’t a particular species. It was endotoxins – lipopolysaccharides found in the outer membranes of Gram-negative bacteria, released when those bacteria die.

They’re chemically stable, resistant to heat, and capable of triggering inflammation in humans.

Endotoxin concentrations at 323 meters roughly tripled during the dust event, rising from 0.45 to 1.27 endotoxin units per cubic meter, with a peak of 2.10.

The levels correlated tightly with coarse-mode aerosol mass.

The researchers traced the signal to two Gram-negative sources: dead microbial material from African dryland soils and marine cell-wall debris picked up over the ocean.

“I was surprised by the large amount of endotoxins in the air,” Jens Weber said.

“Previous studies in the Caribbean have shown that asthma attacks often increase when Saharan dust arrives. Whether a similar correlation exists in the Amazon remains to be investigated.”

Those endotoxins are relevant not just for the forest ecosystem but for roughly 3.9 million people in Brazil’s Amazonas state, plus riverside and coastal communities sitting in the plume’s path.

One critical gap is that the study can’t tell us whether the bacteria are still alive. Amplicon sequencing detects genetic material regardless of whether the organism is viable or dead.

Endotoxins trigger immune responses either way – dead bacteria are perfectly capable of causing inflammation.

But whether intact, living microorganisms survive ten days of UV radiation, desiccation, and temperature swings over the open Atlantic is a separate question.

The team plans follow-up work using fluorescence microscopy and flow cytometry to test viability directly.

There’s also the matter of pathogens. Obligate pathogens weren’t enriched during the dust event.

Some opportunistic genera – Staphylococcus and Clostridium – did increase at 323 meters.

However, identification at the genus level could not establish whether disease-causing species were present.

The dust appears to deliver soil bacteria and immunostimulatory debris rather than an army of infectious agents.

That distinction matters, though it won’t be fully settled until viability testing catches up.

The study is published in the journal Communications Earth & Environment.
