The 6 Best Mini-LED TVs Deliver a Bright Screen and Great Value

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

When it comes to color and contrast, OLED is king, but a great mini-LED TV can give you a great picture, too, often for an excellent price.

Mini-LED is among the most versatile display technologies ever created. By shrinking down the LED backlighting that powered older TVs since flat screens first gained traction in the late 2000s, TV makers have found a way to deliver brighter TVs with richer, more accurate colors without relying on expensive new display technologies.

The best mini-LED TVs right now provide a brighter and more impactful picture than ever, especially since many of them get a performance boost from other display tech like quantum dots and the hot new multicolored backlight configuration, RGB LED.

Just as importantly, they often offer great bang for the buck, especially when you're looking for a larger screen size—let’s say 75 inches or higher. As malleable as they are cost-effective, mini-LED is the secret sauce behind some of the best TVs on the market, even outdoing premium OLED screens in metrics like brightness and color expansion.

We’ve tested the top mini-LED TVs around, from entry-level options to groundbreaking flagships, to help you find the best options for your money.

I picked the top mini-LED TVs after sampling the best the market has to offer from brands like LG, Samsung, Sony, TCL, and Hisense. I tested four of the TVs directly, while two of them were tested by Popular Mechanics staff and recommended based on that testing.

My evaluation was based on performance traits like brightness, color vibrancy and accuracy, image processing and upscaling, black levels, and overall contrast. For my testing process, I used objective measurements like brightness and color accuracy with a Portrait Displays C6 HDR5000 colorimeter, a G1 pattern generator, and Calman software, as well as subjective testing using a wide library of familiar content, from test demos to films and TV shows via 4K HDR Blu-rays and streaming. I also weighed usability features like smarts and gaming options, overall design, and size options for those interested in the ever-expanding big-screen TV market.

I’ve been reviewing TVs and audio technology for well over a decade at publications like Wired, Business Insider, Digital Trends, and others. I’ve reviewed virtually every kind of TV and display technology available, from budget screens to premium RGB LEDs. I’ve gone hands-on with the best new mini-LED TVs from brands like Samsung, LG, Sony, TCL, Hisense, Roku, and others, along with competing tech like OLED and QD-OLED, each of which has its advantages. Few things give me more pleasure than finding people the best tech for their money at every performance level and budget.

RGB LED, which uses tri-colored red, green, and blue light clusters for enhanced color expression, was the hot new display tech at the start of 2026. Virtually every major brand debuted some form of RGB Mini-LED, kick-starting a premium Mini-LED alternative to OLED.

When TCL introduced its own RGB TV for 2026, the RM9L, it seemed less excited about the RGB factor, and more interested in hyping up its exclusive new mini-LED tech, Super Quantum Dots (SQD). The QM8L makes it easy to see why.

SQD is an advanced version of regular quantum dots: tiny nanocrystals that emit pure, bright colors when exposed to light to enhance colors and brightness. Fancy tech aside, the results speak for themselves. The QM8L offers incredible brightness, far outshining its predecessor, the QM8K, alongside expansive colors that are as accurate as they are vibrant.

The new display offers other benefits, including fantastic black levels with very little light bleed, and reduced off-angle performance loss for most settings. Image processing has also improved, getting closer to the clarity and upscaling in top flagships from brands like LG and Samsung. The result is a premium picture that comes tantalizingly close to OLED displays in terms of contrast between the lightest and darkest images, and details that seem to pull you into the screen.

The TV’s Google TV interface is another plus, offering easy setup and extras like the ability to add albums from your Google Photos library as screen savers. Modern connectivity provides top-tier gaming features across inputs, and the sound isn’t bad either, thanks to built-in Bang & Olufsen speakers. Given that you can grab a 65-inch QM8L for as low as $1,000 or less on sale, it’s an easy pick for the best mini-LED TV available.

In many ways, entry-level TVs like the Hisense U6SF Pro are the best way to gauge how far TV tech has come in recent years. You just don’t expect a TV this cheap to look this good, from punchy brightness and naturalistic colors to minimal light bleed that lets you watch even challenging scenes in a dark room without being pulled out of the moment.

One sacrifice at this price is the TV’s black levels, which don’t look as rich as pricier mini-LED TVs, let alone self-lit OLED displays. Because of that, even though the TV’s brightness rises above anything you can stream, it can look less striking than step-up models. On the other hand, its matte-like screen does a great job of diminishing ambient light and even direct light reflections for a good picture day or night. That’s something I didn’t expect to see at this level.

You’ll get some handy features, including support for all major HDR formats and dual HDMI 2.1 ports with VRR (Variable Refresh Rate) up to 144 Hz for PC gaming, another impressive get at this price. I find the TV’s Fire TV smart system a little clunky, but it includes some cool extras, like Ambient Mode that displays free artwork. TCL’s QM6K is a good alternative thanks to its friendlier Google TV smart system, but it can’t match the U6SF Pro’s performance, which is the best I’ve tested at its price point.

The step-down model of our top-rated QM8L, TCL’s QM7L offers many of the same goodies that make its pricier sibling such a rock star, but at a much lower list price. It all starts with the same SQD display tech, for excellent brightness and color vibrancy, and the kind of dimensional contrast that pushes mini-LED closer to OLED.

The QM7L isn’t quite as much of a powerhouse as the QM8L, but it’s still put-on-your-sunglasses bright, blasting well past anything you can stream, and properly lighting high-quality 4K HDR Blu-rays. Its potency aids in remarkable color expansion for a deep dive into advanced films like Spider-Man: Into the Spider-Verse. Importantly, the TV also provides good color accuracy, along with deep black levels for excellent contrast. You’ll still see some minor light bleed if you look for it, and picture quality is reduced off-axis, but both are relatively minor concessions, especially for the price.

The TV offers a similarly loaded set of gaming features to last year’s QM7K, including options like VRR (Variable Refresh Rate) and ALLM (Auto Low Latency Mode) across two of its four HDMI inputs. Like the QM8L, its Google TV smart system is intuitive to set up and use. That’s all icing on a TV that comes about as close as I’ve seen to a premium display for the money.

Hisense’s mid-tier mini-LED TV doesn’t offer the latest display technologies like RGB LED or Super Quantum Dots, but it does offer seriously punchy performance, along with a design that feels much more premium than its price class suggests. That starts with Hisense’s latest anti-reflective matte screen that eliminates most ambient reflections, matched by slim bezels and a stylish pedestal stand with the kind of build quality usually reserved for flagship TVs.

Under the hood is a potent mini-LED display that employs regular quantum dots for rich colors and brightness that meets or beats anything in its class, including TCL’s QM7L. When paired with its rich black levels and reflection-repelling screen, you’ll have no trouble watching even darker shows in a bright room. Hisense’s image processing has also improved for enhanced detail and upscaling with low-quality content. I did notice that the TV’s local dimming may create some artifacts at its highest setting, but it’s a very minor issue. Like most backlit TVs, it also loses some picture quality when viewed from an angle.

Feature-wise, the U7SG is a gaming master, including VRR (Variable Refresh Rate) at up to 165 Hz across all four HDMI inputs, and handy extras like an intuitive game bar for adjusting settings on the fly. Add in its snappy Google TV interface, and you’re getting a whole lot of good stuff at a price that often falls well below $1,000 for a 65-inch model, and stays competitive up to a whopping 116 inches.

After skipping a year, Hisense’s flagship U9 series is back, now loaded with the latest RGB LED display tech for a striking makeover. The TV uses its tri-colored red, green, and blue mini-LED backlighting to great effect, providing rich black levels and sparkling colors that increasingly close the gap with OLED, alongside the kind of dazzling brightness that OLED can’t yet match.

The UR9’s screen gets an advanced version of Hisense’s matte-like anti-glare coating called “Obsidian,” which impressively repels ambient and even most direct light sources, making it a great option in sunlit rooms. Turn the lights off, and you’ll find great shadow detail and very little light bleed from bright objects on dark backgrounds.

Hisense outfits the TV with some premium extras, from a capable 4.1.2-channel speaker system to HDMI 2.1 support at up to 165 Hz across all three HDMI inputs. There’s also a DisplayPort for connecting a PC, though I would have liked to see a fourth HDMI 2.1 port as well. The TV has also gotten an update for the latest advanced HDR format, Dolby Vision 2, making it well-primed for the future. It’s all wrapped in a stylish design, for a posh taste of next-gen luxury at an accessible price point.

TCL was so confident in its entry-level QM6K that it was the first TV the brand released with its new mini-LED display tech in 2025, and the only major model that wasn’t replaced for 2026. That badge of honor is well earned, as the QM6K is one of the best-performing TVs I’ve tested in its price class, with naturalistic colors, rich black levels and contrast, and enough brightness to properly light up HDR scenes like a blazing sunset or a jet’s afterburners.

Maybe just as impressive are the issues the QM6K avoids. While most TVs in its class readily reveal issues like screen aberrations on solid backgrounds, light bleed from bright images, and awful off-angle performance, the QM6K mostly stays clear of those pitfalls so you can simply enjoy the picture without distraction. My only real performance complaint is the TV’s particularly bad sound, but given that nearly all TVs I test are much better off with a soundbar or speaker system, that’s not a big surprise.

When it comes to extras, the QM6K is well stocked with options like VRR (Variable Refresh Rate) at up to 144 Hz for blur-free action and a gaming bar to adjust features on the fly. And while the TV isn’t nearly as bright as my top budget pick, the U6SF Pro, its Google TV smart system is more intuitive and responsive for a better overall user experience. With a 55-inch model now available for under $500, the QM6K is tough to pass up.

Most TVs on this list are available from 55 inches to large-scale models at 98 inches or larger, and at increasingly reasonable price points. A big reason for that is, unlike OLED, mini-LED TVs are scalable at a relatively low cost, so screens fit for large home theaters are much more attainable. That’s one of mini-LED’s biggest advantages over other display tech, not to mention the potential for next-gen brightness and color coverage at lower pricing. That said, ultra-large screens more readily expose performance issues, so it’s important to balance screen size with quality so you don’t regret your purchase later.

All of the TVs on this list offer 4K resolution with HDR (High Dynamic Range), and you wouldn’t want to buy a TV with lower resolution these days unless you’re looking for a very small screen (around 48 inches or lower) for a bedroom or office. The majority of new content is shot in 4K with HDR, the latter of which provides higher brightness and better contrast between the lightest and darkest images, along with the potential for more vibrant colors. Some TVs still offer 8K resolution, but because 8K content is scarce and difficult to recognize with the naked eye, it’s essentially dead (or at least dormant) in the current TV landscape.

As for refresh rates, any TV with 120-Hz or higher is preferred for fast-moving content like sports and, especially, gaming. All of the TVs on this list offer 120 Hz refresh rates or higher with VRR (Variable Refresh Rate), designed to adjust to games dynamically to reduce lag and screen tearing/blur. Many TVs push to 144 Hz or better, but those refresh rates are only accessible with select gaming PCs. For consoles—PS5, Xbox Series S/X, and the Nintendo Switch 2—120 Hz is sufficient.

All of the displays on this list use mini-LED-backlighting, a smaller and more accurate version of traditional LED backlighting, paired with a liquid-crystal display (LCD). Because mini-LEDs are smaller, they can be used more prevalently to create better brightness and more dimming zones, which can enhance black levels for better contrast between the brightest and darkest images. Mini-LEDs can also be enhanced by features like quantum dots, nanoparticles that produce pure bright colors when exposed to light. Some advanced Mini-LED TVs also use RGB LED technology, which trades uniform blue or white mini-LEDs for multicolor red, green, and blue light clusters designed to produce purer, more expansive colors at the light source.

In contrast (no pun intended), OLED displays use organic material excited by electricity to light each of a TV’s millions of pixels independently. These self-lit (a.k.a. self-emissive) displays have distinct advantages, including the ability to create perfect black levels for enhanced contrast over backlit displays, along with much wider viewing angles. Like mini-LEDs, some OLED displays also use quantum dots: So-called “QD-OLED” TVs offer enhanced brightness and colors, among other new technologies to compete with ever-brighter and more advanced mini-LED TVs.

Both display types have their advantages. OLED is the best choice for those after a more balanced, nuanced, and uniform picture, especially if money isn’t a concern. However, because mini-LED is generally cheaper to produce, it may be a better choice for those on a budget who want the largest and brightest screen for their money. And in recent years, the best mini-LED TVs have increasingly advanced to better compete against OLED.

The most important connectivity option for gamers is HDMI 2.1, which offers higher bandwidth to get the most out of gaming devices. HDMI 2.1 allows for high refresh rates and features like VRR (Variable Refresh Rate) from devices like the PS5, Xbox Series X, and gaming PCs. If you’re only connecting a Blu-ray player or streaming device, HDMI 2.0 will suffice. All of the TVs on this list also support HDMI eARC/ARC, which allows you to easily connect newer soundbars and other audio devices at high resolution for enhanced audio performance.

WiFi 6 or 7 may also be helpful for wireless streaming, especially if you’re watching a lot of high-resolution content, though you’ll need a router that supports those formats. Most TVs also include an Ethernet connection for a mainline connection to your modem. Other connection options to consider include Bluetooth for connecting wireless headphones, ATSC 3.0 for features like over-the-air HDR from an HD antenna, and an optical connection for older audio devices like a legacy amplifier or soundbar.

Each TV also supports multiple HDR formats, which is important for getting the best performance. HDR10 is the baseline format, which all modern TVs support. Most TVs also support dynamic HDR formats like Dolby Vision and HDR10+ (many support both). These advanced formats can provide a more precise vision of a director’s intent by adjusting the brightest and darkest images scene by scene, and even frame by frame. Dolby Vision is the most common dynamic HDR format for streaming.

If you’re interested in voice control, you’ll want to look for voice assistants like Roku Voice, Amazon Alexa or Alexa+, and Google Assistant or Gemini. If you’re connecting an audio device that supports spatial audio, like a Dolby Atmos soundbar or A/V receiver, you may also want to ensure your TV supports 3D audio formats like Dolby Atmos and, for 4K Blu-rays, DTS:X.
