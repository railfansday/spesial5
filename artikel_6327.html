Alzheimer’s discovery reveals a breakdown in the brain’s defenses

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

A mouse study reveals how the Alzheimer’s-linked gene CASS4 helps brain immune cells surround plaques and limit nearby nerve damage.

An immune cell can’t do much for damaged brain tissue if it can’t get there. That problem seems to lie at the heart of a new study on CASS4, a gene linked to Alzheimer’s disease.

Mice without CASS4 had less contact between their brain immune cells and the protein clumps associated with Alzheimer’s.

Bigger clumps, looser packing and more damage followed, although the brain still had about as many immune cells to work with.

Microglia, as these cells are called, crawl about in brain tissue checking for damage and collecting waste. Those clumps are called plaques.

For over a decade, genetics studies have pointed to CASS4 without explaining much about its work in a living brain.

It belongs among the Alzheimer’s risk genes that do their work outside nerve cells, despite nerve cells bearing the damage most of us know about.

Adeline Walsh, a physiology graduate student at the University of Virginia, led the research with neuroscientist John Lukens. He heads a lab at the university’s Center for Brain Immunology and Glia.

“Despite CASS4 being an Alzheimer’s risk gene, really nothing is known about what this gene does or how it affects the brain during disease. Our lab sought to address this major knowledge gap with this paper,” Walsh said.

The work points to CASS4 as a possible drug target. Alzheimer’s affects more than six million Americans and ranks sixth among the country’s causes of death.

For the plaque experiments, the team needed mice that would grow amyloid deposits while still young.

They used mice carrying five mutations associated with inherited Alzheimer’s disease, then removed CASS4 from some of them.

A separate characterization of the strain found plaques arriving sooner and more consistently in females, hence the choice to use females throughout.

In mice without CASS4, the cortex and the memory-related hippocampus both had extra buildup by five months old.

The largest size group, over 1,000 cubic micrometers, accounted for more deposits in those brains.

More swollen nerve endings surrounded each plaque, and a second marker of nerve fiber damage was elevated in both brain areas.

At four months old, 10 mice from each group had a swimming test. Somewhere in the cloudy water was a platform to stand on, hidden just under the surface.

For four days, both groups found it at about the same pace, and both did equally well on a memory test the next day.

Then the platform moved to the far side of the pool, and the mice missing the gene began to struggle.

The mice went back to where the platform used to be, and swam farther from the new location than the other mice did.

The team took the platform out for a final test, and these mice still spent more time at the old position than the new one.

They covered as much water, just as fast, as the other mice, and two tests of activity and anxiety came out much the same.

Mice without Alzheimer’s mutations showed no such difficulty after losing CASS4, suggesting that the effect depended on the disease model.

An old habit can be hard to shake for people with Alzheimer’s, and researchers have much left to learn about it.

Of course, a swim in a pool doesn’t capture what it’s like for a person.

Differences in microglial numbers did not explain the findings. Mice without CASS4 had about as many microglia as their littermates with the gene intact.

A check of cell division, and of the signs of cells going on alert near a plaque, found much the same in each group.

The cells also swallowed labeled amyloid at much the same rate.

Within 10 to 20 micrometers of a plaque, though, the mice without CASS4 had much less microglial material. That distance is a fraction of the width of a human hair.

Reduced microglial contact was accompanied by less rounded, less densely packed plaques, with amyloid spread loosely around the core.

A 2015 study described that packing job. Microglia put a barrier around a plaque and squeeze the protein into a tighter clump, so less of it can reach the neighboring nerve cells.

There was still something to sort out. Every cell in the first mice had lost CASS4, which left open where the trouble really started.

In another group, two weeks of medicated food after weaning switched CASS4 off in cells bearing a particular surface marker.

After a few weeks, blood cells with working CASS4 had replaced the affected blood cells, but microglia live for years and stayed without the gene.

The second group again had more plaques in the hippocampus, more of the largest deposits and more damage to nerve fibers nearby.

Those plaques were less round and less densely packed, despite the brains having no shortage of microglial material compared with the controls.

“Many in the field initially presumed that CASS4 was likely functioning in neurons to influence Alzheimer’s. Our findings shift this paradigm by proving that CASS4 predominantly acts through the immune system,” Lukens said.

Pulling a cell across a surface is a job for tiny gripping points, and the protein made from CASS4 helps organize them.

The protein acts as a scaffold for structures called focal adhesions, which help cells grip their surroundings.

The team found 98 genes working at different levels in microglia without CASS4, with less activity in systems that build and tighten the fibers used to move.

A separate test with 1,318 antibodies picked out 93 differences in phosphate tags, little chemical additions that control proteins, with 17% involving proteins central to focal adhesion signaling.

Grown in a dish, these microglia spread less of a key gripping protein beneath them. A scratched layer of related immune cells also took longer to close the gap.

Through a window in the skull, the team watched how microglia responded to a tiny brain injury created with a laser.

Normal microglia sent branches toward it over the next 40 minutes, and the ones missing CASS4 hardly stirred.

People aren’t mice with CASS4 deleted. We inherit small differences in its DNA, and the team still needs to find out what those differences mean for the protein.

Mice with human versions of the gene would help, including versions that turn its activity up. They’d still be mice, of course, with all the limits of studying human disease in another animal.

“We are now actively exploring potential roles for CASS4 in multiple other neurodegenerative disorders and developing novel strategies to therapeutically target it,” Lukens said.

There’s no drug to boost CASS4 yet, or evidence that doing so slows Alzheimer’s in people.

Getting a treatment into microglia and following what happens to plaques over time would be necessary steps along that road.

The study is published in the Journal of Experimental Medicine.
