Is airline elite status worth it anymore? It’s an age-old question that’s back on plenty of travelers’ minds — especially after two of the four largest U.S. carriers overhauled their loyalty programs this month.

And one theme is becoming clear: To get the best rewards, members are going to have to spend ... a lot.

Starting in 2027, taking the most United Airlines flights won’t necessarily translate to the highest United MileagePlus status.

Instead, the airline's top elite members will be the customers who spend the most money — either on flights or the airline’s cobranded credit cards.

Top-tier 1K status will also be harder to earn.

Meanwhile, Southwest Airlines took its biggest step yet toward nudging flyers toward its lineup of credit cards.

Beginning next year, every dollar its members charge to their Southwest Rapid Rewards cards will count toward status.

Southwest also closed a key shortcut to its most alluring perk: the Companion Pass. Going forward, members hoping to score the "buy-one, get-one" flight pass will have to spend their way to the airline’s top status tier (or take 100 flights in a year).

These changes weren’t entirely surprising.

Over the past decade, airline status has become less about the time you spend in the sky and more about the cash you’re willing to spend.

For years, American Airlines and Delta Air Lines have awarded status based purely on the money their frequent flyers spend.

And across the industry, carriers have become more agnostic about whether those dollars come in the form of a flight booking or card swipe at the grocery store.

On some airlines, you can now technically swipe your way to status without ever setting foot on a plane.

It’s not just the eye-popping dollar amounts travelers must now spend to reach status.

Airlines have also made carrying a credit card a more important part of the overall loyalty experience.

United and Delta cardholders now get access to better mileage redemptions than members who don’t carry a card.

JetBlue will soon offer exclusive elite perks to JetBlue TrueBlue loyalty members who carry its top-tier card.

And even more airlines offer cardholders shortcuts to elite status.

Delta this month said it’s on track to pull in a record $9 billion through its banking partnership with American Express.

American expects to make $8 billion this year through its tie-up with Citibank.

Those are eye-popping numbers, of course.

Beyond the raw cash flow, though, airlines also see turning flyers into cardholders as one of the best ways to become part of customers’ day-to-day lives, even when they’re not on a trip.

“It really is about making sure that we are top of mind for our members every single day, whether or not they’re able to be on board our aircraft," Jarad Fisher, United’s president of MileagePlus, said in a recent interview.

On one hand, airline status still offers a host of ways to save money, get better seats and travel with more flexible policies.

And, in some ways, qualify has become more realistic for travelers who aren’t true “road warriors," since everyday spending counts toward status.

At the same time, free first-class upgrades have gotten harder to score and status benchmarks keep getting pricier to earn.

It's created a real dilemma for travelers who fly a lot — but only have so much money they're willing to put toward earning status: Is it worth dumping tens (or hundreds) of thousands of dollars onto your favorite airline’s credit card to reach your status goal?

Think about what you’re getting out of status, and whether it’s worth the investment.

If not, you could be a candidate to hop off the status "hamster wheel" and be an airline "free agent."

But if you've crunched the numbers and decided status is still worth it, you may need to double down on your favorite airline.

And that might mean prioritizing credit card spending on that carrier's card. Because in 2026, that is almost as important as flying when it comes to unlocking the best perks.

Of course, the most efficient way to earn status is by combining your card spending with flying.

Spending on flights does typically earn you more elite credit (and points) than you'd get with typical credit card spending.

While airline credit cards can offer strong value with that carrier, other cards may offer a stronger return for your spending.

For instance, there's basically no airline card that truly competes with the American Express® Gold Card on rewards for dining worldwide and grocery purchases.

And some Capital One credit cards offer 2 miles per dollar spent on every purchase.

Most of us only have so many dollars to dole out, and sometimes the best rewards to earn are the flexible points you can transfer to a wide range of partners.

Plus, as a frequent traveler, I also carry hotel cards that are key to unlocking perks once I arrive at my destination.

If you're rethinking your loyalty with a particular airline, there are status matches you can take advantage of to effectively “change airlines.”

Just remember: Most U.S. carriers are now playing the “spending to status” game in some form. So, you may encounter similar sticker shock with your new carrier of choice.

Plus, even after matching, you’ll likely have to hit an ambitious challenge goal to keep your status for the entire year.

While United and Southwest announced major overhauls to their loyalty and status programs, Delta opted to stay the course for next year.

Its Medallion qualification requirements will not change in 2027.

Delta’s last major shake-up to its Delta SkyMiles program came in 2023, which led to significant backlash among its SkyMiles members.

American likely won’t update its AAdvantage members for a few months since its loyalty year technically doesn’t begin until March 1.

So, it remains to be seen whether the status game will continue getting harder for more flyers in the new year.
