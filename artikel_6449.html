Worker or queen? A genetic advantage may shape a fire ant’s future

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

A study of fire ants suggests a “selfish” supergene influences body size, potentially giving some ants a better chance of becoming queens.

Most red imported fire ants become workers. A few become queens, the largest ants in the colony and the ones that lay eggs.

How large an ant grows during development helps determine whether it becomes a worker or a queen.

A new study suggests that genes also influence this process.

In colonies with several queens, ants carrying a particular version of a large group of genes were more common among the biggest workers and future queens.

Haolin “Horace” Zeng and Brendan Hunt, insect researchers at the University of Georgia, studied this pattern in lab colonies.

Their work offers a clue to why these genes keep spreading, even though they can have drawbacks for the ants carrying them.

Some fire ant colonies have just one queen laying eggs, while others have many. A group of more than 500 genes helps determine which kind of colony develops.

These genes sit close together on a chromosome, a structure that carries DNA. They pass from parent to offspring together as a package called a supergene.

“We call them supergenes because the genes are tightly linked and inherited together, almost like one big gene,” explained Hunt, an associate professor in UGA’s entomology department.

The package comes in two versions. Scientists call the older version B and the newer one b, which appeared about one million years ago.

Ants in single-queen colonies have two copies of B, written as BB.

In invasive populations with several queens per colony, every egg-laying queen has one copy of each version, written as Bb.

Queens with the Bb combination weigh less and have more trouble starting colonies alone than BB queens. Yet the b version continues to spread.

Scientists describe it as a “selfish genetic element” because it improves its own chances of reaching the next generation, even when it does not help the ant carrying it.

Becoming a queen gives a female ant the opportunity to pass her genes to the next generation.

“If you want to pass down a gene, you’d better become a queen. You don’t want to become a worker,” Zeng said.

Young ants grow as larvae before becoming pupae, the stage when they change into adults. A natural chemical called juvenile hormone can delay the move out of the larval stage, giving them more time to eat and grow.

The researchers wanted to find out whether ants with the b version responded differently to this hormone.

In spring 2024, they collected colonies with one queen and colonies with several queens from Clarke and Oconee counties in Georgia.

They used these ants to set up small lab colonies without queens.

Each held about 2,500 workers, and all colonies received the same total weight of young ants at different developmental stages.

Some colonies received one meal containing methoprene, an artificial chemical that acts like juvenile hormone and is also used as an insecticide.

The others received a meal without it, and workers passed the food to the larvae.

Between 15 and 24 days after that meal, the team collected and weighed worker pupae. The middle weight, or median, was about three times higher in treated colonies than in untreated colonies.

The researchers then checked the genes of 1,049 worker pupae and 195 queen pupae from colonies with several queens. They compared the ants’ genes with their weight.

Ants carrying the b version became more common as body weight increased.

Here, ‘carrying the supergene’ refers specifically to the b version.

“Among the smallest workers, about half carried the supergene,” Hunt said. “That proportion increased to roughly 65% among the largest workers and 75% to 83% among queens.”

On average, BB pupae weighed about 19% less than Bb pupae. But the heaviest ants in each group weighed about the same, so the b version did not raise the upper limit on size.

Instead, ants carrying b reached the larger sizes more often.

The hormone treatment increased size by about the same amount in both groups, suggesting that a major difference in their response to this hormone did not explain the pattern.

The share of Bb ants also rose with weight at a similar rate among workers and queens.

That suggests the gene package affects body size, which then helps shape whether an ant becomes a queen, rather than having a separate effect on queen development.

“To me, it highlights that, through a developmental mechanism involving body size, a genetic element can alter caste outcomes and basically cheat its way into making a queen,” Hunt said.

The team next looked for genes that might explain the size difference. Scientists do not yet have a full record of which genes become more active as fire ant larvae grow into workers or queens.

The researchers therefore used existing data from pharaoh ants, which belong to the same ant subfamily.

Earlier work on adult fire ants had identified 191 genes that were more active in Bb ants than in BB ants.

Of those, 34 were also more active in pharaoh ant larvae developing into queens. That overlap was larger than the researchers would expect by chance.

One gene, called gas8, appeared in every comparison and sits within the fire ant supergene.

DNA near the start of gas8 is also more loosely packed in Bb fire ants, which fits with the gene being more active.

The team still does not know how the gene package changes growth.

Perhaps large BB larvae die more often before becoming pupae, or perhaps some Bb larvae spend longer eating and growing before moving to the next stage.

A computer simulation showed that extra growing time could produce the pattern seen in the study. Giving Bb larvae a better chance of continuing to a larger size could also explain it.

Workers might treat larvae carrying b more favorably, but that alone seems unlikely to explain the findings.

An earlier experiment found extra Bb queen pupae even when only BB workers raised them.

Researchers have not yet tested what gas8 does as ants develop. Experiments with fire ant larvae will be needed to find out.

Comparisons with other animals that carry supergenes could answer a wider question: do these gene packages often spread by changing how animals grow, or how long they spend growing?

The study is published in the journal Proceedings of the Royal Society B.
