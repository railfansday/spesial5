Why does Webb see so many giant galaxies in the early universe?

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

The Lumina simulation traces how stars and quasars transformed gas in the early universe and explores why Webb sees so many massive galaxies.

The first stars and galaxies gave off ultraviolet light that stripped electrons from hydrogen atoms between galaxies.

Quasars later began stripping helium of its last electron, a process that took more energetic light and was still underway long after most of the hydrogen had been ionized.

A new computer simulation called Lumina tracks how gas, dark matter and light interacted through both changes.

It models a region that would span about 1.6 billion light-years on each side at today’s cosmic scale, giving astronomers a way to test their predictions against observations from the James Webb Space Telescope.

Oliver Zier, a fellow at the Center for Astrophysics | Harvard & Smithsonian, led the project.

Aaron Smith of the University of Texas at Dallas was a co-author, along with colleagues at MIT, York University and the Max Planck Institute for Astrophysics.

“With JWST, the early universe has become an exciting new frontier in astrophysics,” Zier said. “To understand what we are seeing with this telescope, we need to translate our theories into detailed predictions.”

A simulation is that translation. Astronomers feed in the laws of physics and the universe’s starting conditions, then let a supercomputer calculate how everything develops over time.

They compare the result with real telescope images. A match gives astronomers more confidence in the model. A mismatch can show where its assumptions need more testing.

Lumina covers roughly the first 2 billion years, following the earliest galaxies and the thin gas between them.

The simulated cube is about 1.6 billion light-years on a side, more than 100 times the volume of comparable simulations.

The team divided its contents into 432 billion pieces of gas and dark matter, and each piece of gas has about 3.6 million times the mass of the Sun.

Galaxies grow where the dark matter gathers, strung along the filaments of the cosmic web.

The team ran Lumina on Frontier, a supercomputer at Oak Ridge National Laboratory in Tennessee.

The simulation used 7,536 graphics accelerators, just over 20% of the machine, and about 600 terabytes of working memory at its peak.

Smith described what that size makes possible.

“For the first time, we can follow along as both hydrogen and helium changed into their modern form across such an enormous volume of the universe, while still resolving the galaxies and other luminous objects driving that transformation,” he said.

Earlier simulations of this kind, including the Thesan project, stopped before helium began to clear. Lumina’s cube is 144 times the volume of Thesan’s.

The first stars and galaxies cleared the hydrogen slowly and unevenly. In Lumina, bubbles of cleared gas open around the biggest galaxies first and grow until they merge. Regions far from any galaxy stay foggy longest.

The team divided the simulation into 125 regions and tracked how quickly the hydrogen changed in each one.

In a typical region, nearly all of the hydrogen gas between galaxies had lost its electrons by about 1.1 billion years after the Big Bang. In the slowest regions, that took about 1.3 billion years.

Astronomers see the same late finish in the light of distant quasars, which still shows absorption by hydrogen gas at that time.

Stars did nearly all of this work. Quasars supplied about 15% of the hydrogen-clearing light near the end.

Clearing helium takes light about four times more energetic than clearing hydrogen, and ordinary stars give off very little of it. Quasars do.

A quasar is a supermassive black hole pulling in gas so fast that the gas shines with intense ultraviolet light.

In Lumina, the brightest quasars began stripping helium of its last electron about 900 million years after the Big Bang. The process was about halfway complete by 1.5 billion years.

By 2 billion years, the regions where helium still held its last electron accounted for less than 1% of the space Lumina modeled.

The bubbles around quasars grew larger than the hydrogen bubbles that formed around galaxies.

Each clearing also heats the gas. The thin gas between galaxies warms to roughly 18,000°F to 36,000°F (10,000°C to 20,000°C) while the hydrogen clears.

It cools as the universe expands, then warms again when the quasar light reaches it.

Measurements of the helium clearing are scarce. Most studies of it rely on fewer than about 25 quasars with a usable line of sight, and Lumina’s timing agrees with those measurements.

Lumina also grows galaxies, and here it fails a test. At about 1.2 billion years after the Big Bang and earlier, the simulation produces fewer very massive galaxies than Webb has found.

The shortfall grows the further back in time the comparison goes.

The team wrote that this confirms a known problem: galaxy models tuned to the nearby universe undercount the giants Webb sees in the early one.

Stars might form more efficiently in the earliest big galaxies, or the masses astronomers read off Webb’s images might be less certain than assumed.

The simulation can’t settle that on its own, but it marks where the standard picture falls short.

Some of Lumina’s agreement with observations is built in.

The authors set the share of each galaxy’s hydrogen-clearing light that escapes into space at 18%, a value chosen so that hydrogen clears on a realistic schedule. So the timing of that clearing is partly a tuning choice, not a prediction.

The team also slowed the speed of light in the calculation to a fifth of its real value, because the full speed would have made the run unaffordable.

That shortcut may keep hydrogen fog in remote voids a little too long. And the detailed comparison with real quasar spectra, the one that would say whether the helium timing is right, is still to come.

Zier’s team plans to release the full Lumina data in 2028. Companion studies are already using it to work out how patchy clearing marks the afterglow of the Big Bang, and how many quasars of each brightness the early universe held.

Observations from Webb and future surveys by the Roman Space Telescope will help astronomers test whether Lumina can explain how the earliest massive galaxies formed.

The full study was published in the journal Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society.
