From Westeros and Venice Festival Winner ‘Woman Unknown’ to ‘Star City’: Meet Baltic Craft Specialists Who’ve Worked on Int’l Productions

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

The Baltic countries of Lithuania, Latvia and Estonia are in focus at the 25th Industry@Tallinn & Baltic Event market, part of the Tallinn Black Nights Film Festival.

Whether such Hollywood films as Christopher Nolan’s Tenet, Venice 2026 winner Woman Unknown or such series as Paramount+ spy thriller The Agency, the BBC’s War & Peace, and Apple TV’s Star City – the Baltic countries of Lithuania, Latvia and Estonia have been among the locations that international productions have turned to, bringing global audiences a look and taste of the region.

Film and TV craft experts have brought their skills to international and local crews alike.

With the 25th edition of the Industry@Tallinn & Baltic Event (ITBE) market, part of the 30th edition of the Tallinn Black Nights Film Festival (PÖFF), currently shining a spotlight on the Baltic region, THR decided to check in with some creatives who have brought their talents to film and series you may well recognize. Meet some of the faces you want to or should know below.

Ingrīda Nagle, location manager, LatviaYou may have seen her work in: Woman Unknown (the first Latvian co-production in the main competition at Venice where it won the Golden Lion. The Latvian capital Riga is standing in for post-war Denmark); Gomorrah; Oleg; Dogs Don’t Wear Pants.

How long have you been working in your field, and what first inspired you to do this kind of creative work?I have been working in the film industry since 2009. It was a very interesting and challenging turn of events in my life, and I got into the industry through a fortunate coincidence. I was invited to work as a Production Assistant (PA) on the international biographical television film The Courageous Heart of Irena Sendler, which was shot in Latvia. In this role, I had to prove my abilities and versatility, which I handled successfully throughout the project. I received great recommendations and, inspired by my colleagues, began focusing specifically on location management. Over the years, working on many different and challenging projects, I realized that I truly enjoy this work. As my skills developed, I found it increasingly rewarding. It is worth noting that before starting my career in film, I graduated from the Riga Film Studio Acting School, so in a way, working in cinema was already waiting for me. I am deeply grateful for this twist of fate, which fundamentally changed my professional career.

What are some of the big international productions you have worked on? How did they find you and your skills, and how did they hire you?I have worked on a number of notable feature films and series. Among them are films such as Dogs Don’t Wear Pants; Oleg; The Exalted; Modris; the series Gomorrah; The Invisible Fight, and most recently, the award-winning feature Woman Unknown. For the most part, producers reach out to me based on recommendations regarding my ability to think out of the box, offer unconventional location ideas, as well as my multitasking abilities, communication skills, and professional attitude. In the film industry, accuracy, the ability to navigate complex situations, finding quick solutions, while maintaining creativity and a good sense of humor, are highly valued – and I believe I possess all of these qualities.

Do you focus on either local or international work now or how do you choose projects?Currently, I am working on several large-scale international projects. I am pleased to say that my professional capabilities and level of work have [earned] a high degree of trust. However, whenever time and capacity allow, I also really enjoy working with local, talented directors and on smaller-scale projects. In these projects, where the crew is compact and creative freedom is high, a special “film family” feeling is created, which brings immense satisfaction in being an important part of making the movie.

Do you have any tips for young people who want to work in your field?My main advice is: travel more around your country, keep your eyes open, walk, explore, and discover! Throughout my life, I have covered most distances on foot, and that is precisely how I have discovered truly wonderful and unique locations. Be open to challenges, read plenty of books to develop your communication skills. Don’t be afraid to make mistakes! Learn to look at places you have walked by a hundred times with “fresh eyes,” look up, notice the details, and let your imagination run wild. And be prepared to work hard – this role truly demands it.

What are you working on right now?Currently, I am working on three international projects, which, due to NDAs is not confirmed for publicity yet, but I am currently waiting for approval from the producers to name them.

Kaire Hendrikson, make-up designer and special effects make-up artist, EstoniaYou may have seen her work in: The Agency: Tenet; Azrael; Deliver Us; The Swedish Torpedo; The Missile; and the international drama series Estonia.

How long have you been working in your field, and what first inspired you to do this kind of creative work?I’ve been doing films for 22 years now. I lived in Italy after high school for years, and when I came back, one time, I thought that if I found something interesting in Estonia, I’d stay here for a while. Then I found a job as an assistant in film production company, and I fell in love with these people and the lifestyle. Then came the makeup academy, and the rest is history.What are some of the big international productions you have worked on? How did they find you and your skills, and how did they hire you?The Agency, the Paramount+ TV series with Michael Fassbender – I was supervising makeup artist during the Estonian shooting period, doing the actors with my U.K. collegues.Tenet by Christopher Nolan – I took care of the extras in Estonian shooting period.Burial by Ben Parker – I was a makeup designer on this U.K. production.Undergods by Chino Moya was a great adventure in Serbia, shooting a U.K., Belgium, Holland, Estonian co-production.The Swedish Torpedo – I really loved designing this Swedish movie.They usually find me through Estonian producers.Do you focus on either local or international work now or how do you choose projects?For the last 15 years, I’ve mainly worked on international productions. If I’m designing the makeup for a film, the script has to be interesting to me in one way or another – great story, interesting period to recreate, challenging SFX makeup, exciting character creating or very inspiring film director. I just refuse to be away from home and my family for months if there’s nothing that excites me about the project.When it’s a big international production like Tenet, just being part of a team that size and seeing how makeup artists from around the world work is inspiring in itself!Do you have any tips for young people who want to work in your field?It’s a lifestyle with a lot of traveling, a lot of new people all the time, a lot of not knowing what and where the be next project is going to be, a lot of uncertainty, a lot of quick adaption, a lot of team work. So basically you have to have a bit of a Gypsy soul in you to enjoy it. If this sounds exciting, go for it – all the rest is learnable! I love it!What are you working on right now?I’m recovering from this crazy year and enjoying the time with my family now! I am also prepping a Norwegian period movie based on real story about very brave/crazy woman – lots of super nice SFX makeup and very nice period to create! It’s going to be a busy year as there are also other super interesting projects waiting for the final green light.

Jurgita Globytė, makeup designer, LithuaniaYou may have seen her work in: Stranger Things; Vesper; Truth & Treason; Star City.

How long have you been working in your field, and what first inspired you to do this kind of creative work?I have been doing this work for about 20 years. Before that, I studied business management, but I did not feel it was right for me. Later, I decided to change direction and chose to study make-up for film and theater.After finishing my studies at the make-up academy, I entered the film industry quite naturally, through a recommendation. One of my teachers recommended me to a film make-up artist who was looking for assistants. That is how my journey began.If you are open to learning and quickly understand how working in film works, you have a good chance to find your place, learn more and later do your own creative work.

What are some of the big international productions you have worked on? How did they find you and your skills, and how did they hire you?When international productions come to Lithuania, local producers recommend us. There is often a selection process, and that is how they choose their teams. One of the most memorable and beautiful projects I worked on was the BBC series War & Peace.Do you focus on either local or international work now or how do you choose projects?I am very happy to work on local projects. When I have a choice, I prefer them. We are a small film community, and it is always a great joy to create something at home. Directors who know how I work, my skills and my style invite me to work with them.Do you have any tips for young people who want to work in your field?My advice to young people would be to stay curious, be dedicated and enjoy what you do.What are you working on right now?We are now finishing a project that is very important not only to me, but also to Lithuania. It is a historical epic about the fight for freedom, and I believe it will be a beautiful film.

Matis Rei, sound designer and supervising sound editor, EstoniaYou may have seen his work in: Tár; Black Mirror; Cabinet of Curiosities; The Kitchen.

How long have you been working in your field, and what first inspired you to do this kind of creative work?I have been working on films since 2009 and I think what led me into it was doing electronic music which has some similarities to film sound design. Then I was looking for things to study after high-school and found out they were accepting sound students into Baltic Film School. Film always felt magical dreamlike experience where sound creates a lot of that tonal emotional subconscious feel. I was very curious but had no knowledge about film sound at the time.

What are some of the big international productions you have worked on? How did they find you and your skills, and how did they hire you?I often work on smaller European films. Sometimes I also happen to work on bigger U.K. or U.S. productions. I like the variety of working on very different styles of films: doing minimal art house film for 4 months and then jumping to something completely different and boombastic.More famous projects that I’ve worked on over the past few years are Todd Field’s Tár and Black Mirror season 7. I lived and studied in the UK for a few years and met many great people back then – some of these people liked my work and hired me for their films. This has led to some great work relationships that are still fruitful.Do you focus on either local or international work now or how do you choose projects?I work both local and international. Ideally, a little bit of both. I’m grateful to be working on films and I just hope the best films find me, which they have done so far.Do you have any tips for young people who want to work in your field?Work hard always – every film is the most important one in your life, be grateful and hope the right people find you at the right time. Reach out to people or companies you want to learn from or enter a film school.What are you working on right now?I’m currently working on one HBO series which takes place in Westeros [in the] Game of Thrones universe. This one is lots of fun, comedy and some action. And then in late January, I will start a Swedish romantic drama that takes place in and around Stockholm in the early 1900s.

Liene Dobrāja, costume designer, LatviaYou may have seen her work in: The Chronology of Water; The Lightkeeper; Dice-Ching-o-Mat; Swallow.

How long have you been working in your field, and what first inspired you to do this kind of creative work?I have been working in film since I graduated from NYU in 2014, so that makes it 12 years. My primary focus beforehand was costume design for the stage, but once I got to Tisch, met some amazing collaborators, and got on set, I was hooked. I knew that film design suited my way of thinking visually much better.I have always been a big reader, ever since I was a little kid. I can disappear into a book, a magazine article, or anything written, really. Literature gets me to daydream and create visual images in my mind. Film is a medium that helps me translate those daydreams into something tangible. That first love of books grew into a love for research, and the psychological aspect of costume design endlessly interests me, too: What music does this character listen to? What was their childhood bedroom like? What do they believe in? And so on and so forth. I love digging deep into a character. We are very complex and multifaceted beings, and I find that incredibly inspiring.

What are some of the big international productions you have worked on? How did they find you and your skills, and how did they hire you?My biggest international production so far has been Kristen Stewart’s The Chronology of Water. She found me through a mutual costume designer friend, Olga Mill, whom I also met at Tisch. Kristen sent me the script; I absolutely fell in love with it, and one Zoom call later, we started working on the film. The projects I have found the most fulfilling, both creatively and professionally, have luckily come quite easily.The other big production would probably be Swallow – perhaps not in terms of scale, but in terms of the effect it has had on my career. Carlo Mirabella-Davis, our director, gave us full creative freedom, and we ran with it. I still see directors’ decks that include reference images from our movie – the DP was the incredible Kate Arizmendi. I have been hired for films and commercial work just because of that movie alone. I had collaborated with Carlo and Mollye Asher, our amazing producer, before at Tisch, so that’s how we found each other for this film.Do you focus on either local or international work now or how do you choose projects?My focus is usually on the script and the storytelling possibilities, and since the language of a film set is universal, the location of the work is not that important. Of course, having a small child means making some natural adjustments, but so far, I have been very lucky to have an amazing and resourceful village around me, including my directors and producers. I have been able to travel internationally for work with my child and be fully present for the films I work on.Do you have any tips for young people who want to work in your field?I think being and staying curious is the biggest thing. Being genuinely interested in people and storytelling is another. When it comes to practical tips: film is a community-based industry, so finding your people, who you want to collaborate with, will help you create opportunities and open doors.What are you working on right now?I am currently in talks about a few feature films that are gearing up to shoot next year, both co-productions with Germany and Spain, respectively. I am also eagerly awaiting the premiere of Tomas Vengris’ new film Barakuda, about the Lithuanian mafia in the ’90s, which we finished last year.But most importantly, I am preparing for the Christmas holidays with my son and wondering if it is too early to put up the tree.
