Scientists turn one of the world’s hardest-to-recycle plastics into high-performance lubricating oil

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Old plumbing pipes, discarded window frames, and expired credit cards could one day become valuable ingredients in engine oil. Researchers at Virginia Tech have developed a new chemical process that transforms one of the world's most difficult plastics to recycle into a high-performance lubricant material.

Led by chemist and chemical engineer Guoliang "Greg" Liu, the research team discovered how to convert polyvinyl chloride (PVC) into polyalphaolefin, a key ingredient in synthetic lubricants such as engine oil.

Published in the journal Nature, the research offers a potential solution to two environmental challenges: reducing the accumulation of stubborn plastic waste and finding more sustainable ways to manufacture valuable industrial lubricants.

PVC is particularly difficult to recycle because of its chlorine content and the wide range of chemical additives manufacturers incorporate into their products. These additives can give PVC different properties, such as flexibility, rigidity, and resistance to heat. However, they also complicate conventional recycling methods. Consequently, much of the world's discarded PVC ends up in landfills.

Turning Plastic Waste Into High-Performance Lubricants

Meanwhile, global demand for lubricants continues to grow, and producing these materials can come with significant environmental costs. Transforming discarded PVC into a valuable lubricant ingredient could help address both problems.

Although lubricants rarely attract much attention, they are essential to modern machinery. They reduce friction and wear between moving components, helping engines operate efficiently and extending the lifespan of mechanical equipment. Everything from lawn mowers and passenger vehicles to jet engines depends on reliable lubricants.

The Virginia Tech team's process could provide an alternative source of these important materials while helping address the growing problem of plastic pollution.

To make the lubricant, researchers place PVC of the kind commonly used in household plumbing, window frames, and credit cards into a solvent. They then introduce aluminum trichloride and alpha-olefins, a class of hydrocarbons used in the manufacture of synthetic lubricants. The mixture is heated to 158 degrees Fahrenheit for three hours, after which the researchers extract a relatively thick lubricating oil.

The resulting polyalphaolefin belongs to a family of synthetic base oils valued for their stability, lubricating properties, and ability to perform across a wide range of temperatures. These materials are commonly used as the foundation for high-performance synthetic engine oils.

"Number one, we have proved that it is feasible to use plastic waste to make high-performance lubricants. Number two, these lubricants are green, and they can meet the emerging needs for sustainability by the market," Liu said.

Building on Earlier Plastic Recycling Breakthroughs

The discovery grew out of previous research by Liu's laboratory, published in Science and Nature Sustainability. In those studies, the researchers demonstrated how other types of plastic waste could be transformed into surfactants, substances that help water interact with oils and grease and are widely used in soaps and detergents.

Encouraged by those successes, the researchers decided to tackle the more challenging problem of PVC waste.

"We want to help improve the recycling and upcycling of PVC," Liu said.

Unlike conventional recycling, which typically aims to recover materials for reuse, upcycling seeks to transform discarded materials into products with greater value or usefulness.

Liu recruited a group of graduate researchers to investigate this possibility. Eric Munyaneza Nuwayo, who was completing the final year of his doctoral program, was selected to lead the project. Connor S. Thompson, a chemistry graduate student who had been pursuing a different research topic, joined the effort after Liu suggested changing direction.

Abby Civiello, a graduate student in her first year, also joined the team and quickly became an important contributor to the research.

"I often called them the three musketeers," Liu said.

An Unexpected Discovery in Polymer Chemistry

The researchers initially experimented with ways to modify the molecular structure of PVC and convert it into more useful substances.

Like other plastics, PVC is a polymer, meaning it consists of long molecular chains made up of repeating chemical units. The researchers initially hoped that changing the chemical groups attached to these chains would produce a material with desirable properties.

"The idea was simple. PVC, as one of the most activated forms of polyethylene, ought to be easily converted into some other molecules by replacing the chlorine atoms with other groups," Liu said.

However, the early experiments consistently produced disappointing results. Instead of creating a functional material with the desired performance characteristics, the team ended up with soft, sticky substances that had little practical value.

Those frustrating results eventually inspired Liu to approach the problem differently. Rather than continuing to modify the long molecular chains, he wondered whether breaking them into smaller pieces might produce something more useful.

"One day I realized -- if this polymer is so gooey and so soft, why don't I just keep breaking the polymer chains down to smaller segments?" said Liu.

As the researchers refined their chemical process and evaluated the properties of the materials it produced, Liu began to recognize that the team had discovered something with implications extending well beyond plastic recycling.

They had found a potential new route for manufacturing valuable industrial lubricants.

Bringing Recycled Plastic Lubricants to the Market

To better understand the properties and commercial potential of the new oil, Liu enlisted the expertise of researchers at several institutions.

He provided samples to Ali Erdemir at Texas A&M University, where researchers evaluated the performance of the finished materials. William Goddard at the California Institute of Technology, or Caltech, contributed chemical computations to help investigate the underlying chemistry.

At Virginia Tech, Xi Chen examined the economic and manufacturing aspects of the technology, developing models to explore how the oil could be produced on a larger scale.

The research establishes that discarded PVC can serve as a starting material for producing high-performance lubricants. However, expanding the process beyond the laboratory remains an important next step.

Liu hopes to further improve the environmental sustainability of the technology while making the resulting lubricants more widely available. Ultimately, the approach could give one of the world's most problematic plastics a valuable second life and provide industries with an alternative source of essential lubricating materials.

"Lubricants are the silent hero out there. We often don't recognize they exist, but they are out there working quietly. We want to be able to produce the oil on a larger scale to reach more people in the world," Liu said.
