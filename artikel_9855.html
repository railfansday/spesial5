Hey devs! If you still add “think hard” to your Claude prompts, try leaving it out.

In its Opus 5.5 tests, Anthropic found that removing instructions like “think hard” and “think carefully” made replies start sooner, with no clear drop in quality.

That caught my attention because it is an easy habit to keep carrying from one model to the next.

On November 12, 2026, Anthropic published its guide to using Claude Opus 5.5 for knowledge work. It covers seven prompting habits to revisit.

I tried the examples in Claude. Below, I’ll show you the prompts, the effort setting, and what happened when I asked it to turn a chart into a report.

You can also watch my full video walkthrough on YouTube.

Give Claude access to the material the task depends on.

If you need a report based on a spreadsheet and a meeting note, provide both. Tell Claude which files to use and what you want it to produce.

You can upload the files directly or use a connector to the app where they live. Connecting an app is useful when Claude needs to find several related documents.

On Team and Enterprise plans, an owner must enable a connector before members can use it.

Start with the sources. A detailed prompt still leaves Claude guessing if the spreadsheet it needs is missing.

In your first message, describe three things:

Claude asked who the chart covered, whether I had exact figures, what an annual data point meant, and who would read the report.

I told it to keep the context generic, use its approximate readings, treat the annual point as an annual average, and write for management.

After those answers, it created a report with a summary and a chart.

The useful part was seeing the assumptions before they became part of the report. The finished file still needs checking against the source data.

Thinking is always on for Opus 5.5. Anthropic says the model adjusts how much it thinks based on your request and the effort setting.

Compare these two prompts from my walkthrough.

The first repeats the thinking instruction but leaves the task vague:

What does “wrong” mean here? Which period should Claude analyze? What should it return?

This gives Claude something concrete to work toward.

My screenshots illustrate the prompt change; they are not a measured comparison of answer quality. For your own task, check whether the response answers the question and supports its findings with the supplied data.

Anthropic recommends starting Opus 5.5 at Medium and increasing the effort when it stalls on your task.

To change it, click the model name next to the send button, open Effort, and select Medium.

Before changing the setting, check whether Claude has enough context.

Did you attach the correct file? Did you explain the output? Is a decision missing?

More thinking time won’t supply a missing spreadsheet.

If you have a chart, screenshot, or diagram, attach it and explain what you want to learn.

Claude described the axes and series, then created a table of approximate values. It also noticed that the chart mixes annual, monthly, and quarterly points.

Check the labels, units, and values it reads.

If exact numbers matter, provide the original spreadsheet too. Numbers extracted from an image can look precise in a table while still being estimates.

Once Claude had described the chart, I used the one-page report prompt from the earlier section to continue the task.

“Make it look modern” leaves a lot open to interpretation.

In the walkthrough, I asked Claude to redesign the chart and avoid a cream background.

That gives it a specific visual choice to change.

This is an example you can adapt, rather than the exact wording in my screenshot.

A reference image also helps when you already know the layout or visual style you want.

If you want the output to sound like you, give Claude examples of your writing.

For a presentation, attach your template. For an article, share a sample and explain which parts of its style to follow.

Point to the sentence, chart, or slide that needs changing and explain why. “This paragraph repeats the introduction” gives Claude a clearer edit than “make it better.”

Pick a report, document review, or chart you already need to work on.

Supply the files, describe the finished result, and start at Medium. Then check the output against your brief before changing the prompt or effort setting.
