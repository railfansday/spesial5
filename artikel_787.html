London’s people-first approach to smart-city development

Benjamin Plackett is a science journalist based in London.

A virtual-reality model of London’s main financial district, the City of London, has mapped the size and location of every building.Credit: Mike Kemp/In Pictures via Getty Images

London has risen to fourth place in the IMD Smart City Index 2026, up from sixth place in 2025 and eighth place in 2024. The index — which is published by IMD, an independent Swiss business institute — draws mostly on residents’ perceptions of infrastructure and technology, not a tally of sensors. That focus on perspective is important, says Trevor Dorling, managing director of DG Cities, a private urban-innovation consultancy spun out of the Royal Borough of Greenwich local authority, which works to make better use of data and introduce new technologies in the southeast London borough.

“We increasingly describe our work as ‘city innovation’ rather than ‘smart cities’, because in many people’s minds ‘smart’ is associated with gadgets and gizmos,” says Dorling. “The aim is to use technology to create better places for people. It shouldn’t be the technologists who define what ‘better’ is, but the communities that live there.”

There is plenty of evidence that London has the resources and knowledge to apply research solutions in such a people-centred way. In the 2026 Nature Index Science Cities table, London is ranked 21st overall, but it jumps to 5th place for health-sciences output, for instance.

The Smart Mobility Living Lab, created by a consortium of public and private organizations, including DG Cities, spans 24 kilometres of roads in Greenwich and the Queen Elizabeth Olympic Park in Stratford, and is one example of London’s efforts to create societal impact with its innovation.

The area has more than 1,000 different road features, from speed bumps and pedestrian crossings to one-way systems and bridges, that allow companies to test new vehicles and services in busy, real-world complexity. “The driving force for this was the rapid development of self-driving technologies and exploring the opportunities this presented for safety and accessibility, as well as the challenges,” says Dorling.

Trials to test self-driving shuttle buses and delivery vans have put the technology in public view, with residents invited to ride in the test vehicles, an openness that Dorling says has helped overcome scepticism and brought local authorities into the development process at an early stage.

London’s digital-twin projects are similarly pragmatic. The local authority in Harrow (northwest London) uses high-resolution drone imagery to inspect and measure roofs and buildings remotely and to build interactive maps of parks. The council used this approach in April 2026 to make a 3D model of Headstone Manor and Museum — a restored medieval complex and local-history museum — saving £12,000 (US$16,260) by avoiding a conventional roof survey with scaffolding and manual inspections.

In London’s main financial district, known as the City of London, a virtual-reality model created by the local authority in collaboration with researchers and digital spatial mappers has captured the size and location of every building, window, lamppost and traffic light within the area to within two centimetres of accuracy, allowing planners and the public to properly scrutinize building proposals.

Elsa Arcaute, a complexity science researcher at University College London, cautions against too much faith in digital twins, as a city with all its complexity and interplay between urbanity, nature and human behaviour cannot be completely duplicated.
