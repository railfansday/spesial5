America shines before sunrise in a stunning astronaut photo

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

An astronaut’s predawn photo captures Midwest cities shining beneath colorful airglow, revealing what nighttime lights can tell us about Earth.

In the darkness before dawn, city lights sketch a glowing map of the American Midwest.

Cities shine across the dark landscape, and a faint glow traces the atmosphere above them.

Today’s Image of the Day captures this meeting of electric light and natural light from the International Space Station.

An astronaut took the wide-angle photograph on October 18, 2026, at 4:54 a.m. Central Daylight Time, or 09:54 Universal Time.

With clear skies across much of the eastern United States, the camera recorded an expansive view of communities still waiting for dawn.

Chicago and Detroit form two of the scene’s brightest concentrations.

Between and beyond these major metropolitan areas, smaller clusters mark cities including Indianapolis, Cincinnati, and Louisville.

Illuminated routes help connect those bright centers, showing how settlements extend across the region.

From orbit, these corridors reveal connections among cities that can be difficult to appreciate from the ground.

The broad perspective also changes how individual cities look.

Chicago appears as a largely uniform patch of pale yellow in this photograph, although closer views reveal differences in the colors of its lighting.

Those variations matter. Different kinds of lamps give off different mixtures of light, leaving clues that researchers can examine in detailed astronaut photographs.

Above the cities, the photograph records light with a very different origin.

Along the left side of the horizon, green and orange bands mark airglow, a faint radiance produced by gases in Earth’s upper atmosphere.

Solar energy sets the process in motion, and atoms and molecules release light as they shed energy.

Airglow can persist through the night. Although its colors may resemble an aurora, the two phenomena have different drivers: auroras arise from energetic particles, while airglow draws its energy from ordinary solar radiation.

On the right, sunlight brightens the horizon as morning approaches.

At the time of the photograph, sunrise was still roughly an hour away along the East Coast and about two hours away in Chicago.

Daylight photographs show the Midwest through its fields, waterways, and buildings.

At night, artificial lighting draws attention to a different set of features: where people have built communities and how those places connect.

Large cities dominate the view, but the smaller patches of light help fill in the picture.

Together, they show a region made up of many distinct communities, with illuminated corridors stretching between them.

Dark areas require more careful interpretation. A place without obvious lights is not necessarily empty.

Land used for farming, for example, can support substantial human activity while producing relatively little nighttime illumination.

The photograph shows where light reaches the camera, rather than providing a complete inventory of what lies below.

Scale also shapes what viewers can learn. This wide view makes regional patterns easier to recognize, while detailed photographs allow researchers to investigate differences within a single city.

Moving between those perspectives helps turn a beautiful scene into useful information.

The broad image shows how the pieces fit together; closer observations reveal details that disappear when an entire metropolitan area becomes one bright patch against the night.

Views like this offer more than a striking portrait of Earth after dark. Repeated observations help scientists examine how human settlements change over time.

Researchers using NASA’s Gateway to Astronaut Photography of Earth archive have developed a method for identifying lighting types within nighttime images.

Measuring the color, brightness, and spread of urban illumination can help document changes in the built environment.

Satellite observations add another way to follow those changes.

NASA’s Black Marble products provide records of nighttime lighting at daily, monthly, and yearly intervals.

Scientists have used these observations to track power losses and the return of electricity after major storms.

This photograph preserves a quieter moment: cities glowing beneath an atmosphere that produces light of its own.

Soon, daylight would make both harder to see, concealing the luminous patterns that briefly bring the region into focus.
