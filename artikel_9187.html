Childhood Trauma and Grief: Lessons From Batman and Two-Face

The difference between a heroic caped crusader and a criminal mastermind may come down to who was waiting in the doorway after a very bad childhood.

Harvey Dent (Two-Face) and Bruce Wayne (Batman) both carry the weight of living losses: grief from a traumatic childhood that never fully ends. Bruce becomes Gotham’s protector, while Harvey becomes Two-Face, a sadistic supervillain who mirrors the abuse he internalized as a child. Their stories show how loss is shaped by the context in which it occurs.

We usually think of grief as something that follows a death, but some of the deepest grief comes from what never was, such as a safe, loving, and reliable family. Grief researchers call this nonfinite loss, or living loss. It is re-experienced throughout life and keeps demanding adjustment.1

In "The Dark Knight," Harvey is a driven, idealistic district attorney who becomes Two-Face after violence takes the woman he loves.2 The comics reveal an earlier story: a father who abused him, with outcomes decided by the flip of a coin.3,4 Long before that night, Harvey had lost security, trust, innocence, and a sense of his own authenticity. These are intangible losses, which are symbolic, invisible, more existential, and easy to bury under professional achievements.

His story is also less rare and outlandish than it seems. The World Health Organization estimates that about six in ten adults in North America and four in ten in Europe have had at least one adverse childhood experience (ACE).5 ACEs are correlated with increased risks of being involved in the criminal justice system, health-harming behaviours, poor psychological health, and poverty or homelessness, and higher mortality rates, in addition to chronic illnesses.

Early relationships teach us how to regulate ourselves and what to expect from the world.6 When caregivers become a source of threat, a child adapts. Parts of our psyche hide, go numb, or freeze. Freeze is a psychobiological conservation strategy that results in having fractured developmental egoic states; we adapt by hiding, repressing, or emotionally isolating certain aspects of ourselves, analogous to how we have been made to feel by others.7

Jungian analyst Donald Kalsched describes how a traumatized psyche will survive in pieces when it is not safe to be whole.8 Two-Face is what happens when that adaptation becomes literal and hardens into identity: a polarized psyche, split between idealism and rage. He demonstrates how unexpressed grief does not disappear, and how traumatic loss can tear us apart from the inside.

Bruce has his own devastating losses.9 The difference is Alfred, a secure attachment figure who is consistent, attuned, and present when it matters. With Alfred, Bruce could grieve beside someone instead of alone, and being able to access the right support allowed Bruce to engage in his grief in a more integrative way than Harvey.

While Bruce nonetheless demonstrates his own childhood attachment injury from the death of his parents, his relationship with his parents is portrayed as positive in the Batman films and comics. This highlights how Bruce had internalized what it means to be protected, co-regulated with when distressed, and supported by loving attachment figures in expressing his own autonomy or interdependence at a young age.

This illuminates how loss experiences are always shaped by the various relational contexts in which they occur. In practice, that means finding our own Alfred, as we all need a trusted and caring person who can help us tend old losses. It also means cultivating an inner Alfred within our own imagination—a steady, compassionate aspect of ourselves that helps us stay grounded when the pain surfaces, even when it is not possible to find such a person externally to us.

This piece draws on my book chapter, Shadows in Context: Two-Face, Batman, and Losses Associated with a Traumatic Childhood in Superhero Grief and the Multiverse of Loss: Considerations and Case Studies edited by Jill Harrington and Darcy Harris (2026), which is a new collection published by Routledge that uses superheroes and the multiverse to explore the impact of non-death loss on our psychology.10
