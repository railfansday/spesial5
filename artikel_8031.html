Rodents Keep Destroying Your Hiking Gear? Here’s How to Keep It Safe

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

A chipmunk chews its way into a hiker's backpack. (Photo: Stanislav Sablin/Getty Images)

One of my favorite parts of being outdoors is the wildlife, and that includes rodents. I love watching squirrels chase each other around tree trunks, and I may or may not have had a “pet” chipmunk in one of my cabins during a horseback guiding season. I talk to marmots when I’m alone, and hearing the chirp of a pika makes my day. But I draw the line at the little buggers crawling over my face in a shelter, chewing through my trekking pole straps, or getting into my food bag.

This is a common occurrence in the backcountry, whether you’re hiking a populated trail or posted up at some far-flung campsite. While no one I know has a perfect mouse-proofing strategy, here are a few tips and tricks to help keep rodents at bay and protect your pack pockets, straps, and food.

The most vulnerable parts of your gear are the deliciously salty pack straps, trekking pole straps, and pockets where you’ve forgotten you left food. While keeping your straps clean is easier said than done after days of sweaty hiking, reducing the tantalizing salty odors will work wonders in keeping sodium-fiending rodents away. If you can, dunk your straps in running water a few times throughout the day, and especially at night when you get to camp.

You can also drip a few drops of hand sanitizer onto trekking pole straps to make them less appealing, and tucking a dryer sheet into salty pockets or around hammock straps can also work as a rodent deterrent. Finally, always stash your trekking poles upright in the ground to keep the straps less accessible.

The bear hang is a time-honored backcountry art form, but if you’ve ever seen an acrobatic mouse shimmy down the rope and land a perfect 10 on your backpack, you know this protective measure doesn’t translate perfectly to rodent-proofing. The solution, as many of us have seen in high-traffic trail shelters, is something I call the Mouse Swing. This is simply your standard piece of paracord or bear-hang rope, but with a barrier tied in the middle.

I’ve used everything from a (clean!!) tuna can to a quart-size yogurt container lid. Punch a hole in the can or plastic lid, run your string through the hole with knots to secure it in place, and you’ll have a lightweight, highly functional barrier between your pack and the string. Make sure your bag is a few feet above the ground or shelter floor to prevent high jumpers from making the leap.

Along with salty straps, mice, rats, and squirrels are highly attracted to food smells. The worst pocket destruction I encountered was when I carefully hung my pack from the rafters of a shelter but forgot to remove the remains of an energy bar from the pocket, and an industrious mouse chewed right through the material. Don’t forget to check your pockets for lip balm, energy gels, or the remains of your lunchtime protein bar.

While a hard-sided bear canister is the best protection against animals getting into your food supply, I prefer not to carry the bulky, heavy contraption unless it’s required in the area. The next best thing is the Ursack Allmitey, a heavy-duty food bag made from Spectra, a specialized material with one of the highest strength-to-weight ratios out there. Once tied properly closed, it’s next to impossible for critters to chew through or squeeze through the opening. If you’re in an area where rodents are the only concern, you can size down in bulk to the Ursack Minor, a Kevlar bag that isn’t tough enough to stand up to bears, but can keep smaller toothy critters at bay.

I’ve also used RatSacks for food drops in the desert. They’re heavier than the Ursack Minor, but have proven virtually indestructible against desert rodents, and some national parks like Canyonlands lend them out for free. These are made from stainless steel mesh with a heavy-duty Velcro closure at the top, and while they aren’t bearproof, I’ve never had a smaller animal bust through the dense metal mesh.

Finally, if scent control is your primary priority, you can further streamline your gear with LokSak OPSAK bags. These won’t prevent sharp little teeth from chewing through them, but the polyethylene material and secure zip tops are completely odorproof and can keep curious squirrels and chipmunks from testing them in the first place.

I hesitate to write this since it’s such a Big If, but if you’re camping somewhere that the risk of running into a bear is zero, you can bring smelly items into your tent. This isn’t foolproof—you run a risk of an unusually bold mouse chewing into your tent—but by and large, most small rodents will be deterred by your massive sleeping form and won’t bother trying to break in.
