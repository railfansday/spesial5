Five key projects by Dezeen Awards emerging architect of the year Kuma&Elsa

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

This year Kuma&Elsa was named emerging architect of the year at the Dezeen Awards. Here, they select five projects that demonstrate the ethos of the studio.

Led by Japanese architect Shohei Kuma and French-Spanish urban designer Elsa Escobedo, Kuma&Elsa has completed a wide variety of projects from their bases in France and Japan.

“We believe it is important not to impose a common consistency across our projects,” Kuma told Dezeen.

“Every project is unique, shaped by a one-off combination of site and client. Preserving that uniqueness requires an open mind. Personally, I quickly lose interest when things are planned in advance.”

“That said, there are certain topics, many of which are shared by our generation, such as feminism, the care for natural resources and a shared public space, that interest us on a personal level, and I imagine they find their way into our work,” added Escobedo.

“We try to approach them with a sense of joy.”

Although they have already designed a wide range of projects, from installations to houses and public toilets, the duo would like to work on more varied schemes and unusual projects, along with completing their own, long-running renovation.

“It would be nice to actually finish this self-renovation project at some point…” said Escobedo.

“More seriously, I’d love to experiment with the reuse of materials on a larger scale, or in a European context, working with European craftspeople.”

“I would love to work on overlooked building types and uses, especially those that have little place in architectural history and attract few expectations,” added Kuma. “Designing a toilet pavilion for the Expo, a building type that nobody expects to become architecture, is one example.”

Along with the variety in projects, both architects agreed that the wide range of daily activities is what they most enjoy about being an architect.

“The variety, the diversity,” said Escobedo, when asked about her favourite part about being an architect.

“One day you’re on a construction site with incredible Japanese carpenters, the next you’re discussing a detail with your favourite thermal engineer, the following week you’re taking part in a conversation with colleagues you admire, before finding yourself struggling to install the plumbing and tiles in a shower because you had the brilliant idea of taking on a self-build project.”

Kuma also added that he enjoys the intensity of working on site.

“The construction site,” he said. “Nothing is more stressful or demanding, with all the struggles involving people and money. Yet every time I see a building taking shape, I feel glad I chose architecture. There is something addictive about it.”

Read on to find out five key projects by Dezeen Award-winning studio Kuma&Elsa, as described by the duo:

“The client’s childhood home, a traditional Japanese house, has an engawa gallery open to the garden. The new residence occupies the top two floors of a building owned by the client. She, along with her two sons and their families, will move into three apartments located on the sixth and seventh floors.

“At the centre of each apartment, we inserted a hut-like volume that gathers the quieter rooms: a small living room and the bedrooms. In the space carved out around it emerges an intermediate zone – an engawa that serves both as a place to stay and as a passage.

“Balcony, engawa, and hut thus form three spatial layers that resonate with the childhood home. The structure consists of Y-shaped modules made of lightweight steel sections and bracing cables, typically used in shelving systems.

“These modules are arranged two by two in mirrored pairs along a regular grid, with occasional inversions. From these new dwellings, one can look down across the street at the engawa of the childhood home. This project does not seek to nostalgically replicate that memory-laden place, but rather to build a new home nourished by the memories of the old one.”

“One water is a bathing and rest pavilion for the Osaka World Expo 2025. Toilets are at first glance a pragmatic facility, dedicated to human physiology. But they are also intimately linked to contemporary issues such as the use of natural resources and gender.

“One water uses the path of rainfall on the accessible roof and its subsequent accumulation to create encounters between water and the public, and contribute to the supply of the toilets.

“The design of the pavilion allows for the reuse of materials and structural components, and makes no gender distinction in the toilets, because, after all, ‘we are all fish in one water’.”

House by the temple, Saitama Prefecture, Japan, 2024

“The house is opposite the entrance of a temple that is surrounded by a park of ancient trees and a wall, as if it belongs to another world. Reflecting this closed composition, we designed the house as an everyday world for a family.

“The main building is located at the back of the plot and has a quarter-circle plan to reduce its own shadow. The street facade is a single-storey hut, which creates a continuity between the open space of the park and the garden of the house.

“On the ground floor, the park is the backdrop to the house. On the first floor, each room offers distant views of the temple, like an observatory.”

“A daily public space in a residential area. We create a plaza in a small residential area where human life is still at human scale.

“The house is like the background that forms the square. The composition consists of various couples: the smooth black mortar of the outer wall and the rough crushed black basalt ground, the emerald entrance hut and the crape myrtle tree with white flowers, the interior spaces and the outside square.”

Water Flower Courtyard, Montpellier, France, 2019

“The life of a flower is short: we wanted to express the fragility of a concentrated life from flowering to dispersal. This project was open to the public for a week during the Montpellier architecture festival Festival des Architectures Vives in June 2019.

“Eighteen lightweight units made of two-millimetre-thick aluminium sheets are laid out together as a forest.

“The tube is a vase that contains water and peony flowers, the bowl contains water and slate stones. All structures are self-supporting and their stability works as in a roly-poly toy. After a few days, petals start falling on the surface of the water.”
