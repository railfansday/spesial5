Band of baby sea turtles swim into ocean after historic California nesting

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Hatchlings emerge after discovery in September of first sea turtle nests ever to have been recorded on US west coast

Two months after the discovery of the first sea turtle nests ever to have been recorded on the US west coast, the eggs have hatched – and California’s first group of 75 native-born olive ridley sea turtles have been released into the Pacific.

The eggs incubated at the Aquarium of the Pacific in Long Beach after the historic discovery of two separate nests in September at southern California beaches, a shift likely driven by this year’s El Niño conditions.

On Saturday, the Aquarium of the Pacific announced the turtles’ first emergence in a social media post: “The hatchlings are here! We are excited to share that some of the olive ridley sea turtle eggs from Seal Beach successfully hatched after a 58-day incubation period. Working with our partners, we carefully released them from the beach in the dark early hours of this morning.”

A day later, the Aquarium added: “Even more egg-citing news! A second group of olive ridley sea turtle hatchlings, this time from the Huntington Beach nest, was released into the ocean early Sunday morning.”

In total, 54 turtles were released from the Seal Beach nest and 21 from the Huntington Beach nest.

On 16 September, an olive ridley sea turtle was observed digging a nest and laying eggs on Seal Beach in the first-recorded instance of any sea turtle nesting on the west coast. Three days later, a second turtle was spotted nesting on Huntington Beach.

Typically a tropical species, the olive ridley sea turtle normally resides in the Pacific, Indian and Atlantic oceans, but has been known to nest on the southern tip of Baja California.

“It is not known why these turtles chose southern California beaches as nesting sites, but it indicates an important historical change that could have long-term implications for the species and for local sea turtle management and protection,” the Aquarium of the Pacific said in a social media post.

“The behavior of these turtles was probably influenced, at least in part, by the incoming El Niño, along with the recent tropical storm, range expansion, or perhaps navigational mistakes. The species is also known for its behavioral flexibility.”

As Hurricane Polo began to form in late September, researchers with the aquarium and its federal and local partners relocated the 204 eggs from the two nests to incubate in the safety of the aquarium’s animal care facility.

“As animals adapt in unexpected ways to changes in their natural world, humans will need to adapt and make space to ensure endangered and threatened species are given a fighting chance,” said Jeffrey Flocken, president and CEO of the Aquarium of the Pacific.

“If they successfully hatch, these baby turtles will likely one day return to nest to carry on future generations on these very same beaches, giving us a remarkable opportunity to help a species expand its range and thrive.”

Even as the Huntington Beach and Seal Beach turtles have begun hatching and returned to the ocean, researchers are continuing to advise the public to be on the lookout for more nests. On 1 November, a third nest was recorded in Ventura county although those eggs were swept away by unusually high tides – likely also caused by El Niño.
