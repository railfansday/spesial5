How to Investigate Suspected Bot Traffic in GA4

A spike in Direct traffic is easy to notice. The harder one to question is a spike that looks like a win. The new visitors scroll through articles, trigger plenty of events and sometimes rack up multiple page views. In a monthly performance review, that can look like audience growth.

That question matters in the age of AI search, when visits from people clicking links in AI tools and requests from automated systems can both be part of the picture. A GA4 spike can tell me where to investigate. It can’t tell me which tool generated the traffic, or whether an LLM was involved.

No single signal proves bot traffic. I look for the same sessions matching several of them, then compare reports with and without that segment.

In GA4, I start in Reports → Acquisition → Traffic acquisition and compare the spike with a comparable period. I check which session default channel group changed, then break down the increase by landing page and country. I’m looking for where the extra sessions came from. A sitewide average can hide a surge concentrated on a single URL.

Direct means GA4 lacks a clear referral source. Unassigned means the traffic did not match a channel rule. Missing campaign tags, redirects and other attribution gaps can put legitimate visits into unexpected buckets. Google’s explanation of Direct traffic · GA4 channel definitions

For a publisher, one of my first checks is Search Console → Discover, if the report is available. If Discover clicks climb on the same articles on the same dates, that gives me a plausible explanation for at least part of the lift, especially if referral information is missing in GA4. I compare the timing and URLs because Discover clicks and GA4 sessions are different measurements. Search Console performance reports

I add Screen resolution as a breakdown in a GA4 Exploration or check the Tech details report. I pay attention when a square resolution such as 1366 × 1366 or 1600 × 1600 goes from rare to accounting for a large share of the increase. The interesting part is the concentration. How common was that resolution before the spike? Does it also dominate the same landing page, browser or country? GA4 Tech details report

If the same cluster shows substantial volume and zero or almost zero returning users over a meaningful date range, I take a closer look. Automation can start with a fresh browser profile each time, and it can also reuse one. Real visitors can look new when GA4 can’t recognise a previous visit, and a successful acquisition campaign can bring in a genuinely new audience. I compare the suspicious group with similar traffic over the same period. GA4 user metrics

On a subscription site, I check whether the extra sessions produce any verified logins or subscription activity. A SaaS team could make the same comparison with sign-ups or account activity. If a site uses a custom User type dimension, a wave of (not set) values tells me to check when the tag sends that value. It might be missing for genuine anonymous readers or because the implementation only sets it after login. I get more confidence from a mismatch between thousands of extra interactions and flat account activity, especially where comparable traffic normally produces some.

If a UK-focused publication suddenly gets a large share of its Direct sessions from Singapore or China, I check whether that lines up with distribution, coverage, or a campaign. Geography helps narrow the investigation. I’m more interested when the same sessions also share a rare resolution and repeatedly enter through one page. Country on its own is too broad a reason to exclude traffic.

Views per session below 1 is another prompt to inspect the events. GA4 counts both web page views and app screen views in this metric. If I see a low ratio, I separate web from app traffic and check whether events are arriving without their corresponding page_view or screen_view. Missing tags or an event-only implementation can explain the same pattern. I check the instrumentation before attributing it to automation. GA4 dimensions and metrics

GA4 records engagement time while a page is in focus or an app is in the foreground. An engaged session can qualify by passing the engagement-time threshold, recording a key event, or reaching two page or screen views. The default enhanced-measurement scroll event fires when 90% of the page becomes visible. Browser automation can navigate to a second page, keep a page in focus and scroll deep enough to trigger the same event. These metrics describe what the browser reported. They can look healthy even when I still have questions about who generated the activity. GA4 user engagement · Sessions · Automatically collected events

I also check unusually long engagement against the metric’s denominator. More than a day of total engagement across a large group can be ordinary. More than a day of average engagement per session is a reason to inspect event timestamps and tracking. The GA4 default of 30 minutes, adjustable up to 7 hours and 55 minutes, is an inactivity timeout. It measures how long a session can sit idle before expiring, so it doesn’t set a hard ceiling on the length of an active session. GA4 session timeout

Here’s how I set that up in a GA4 Exploration:

Where I have access, I ask the web team to compare the same URLs and time windows against CDN or server logs. Repeated request sequences or an existing bot classification can strengthen the case. Until I have that corroboration, I label the segment suspected automation. I keep the original totals visible alongside the adjusted view so stakeholders can see how much the rule changes the story.

In an illustrative example, Direct sessions fell from 9,250 to 3,820 once the segment was excluded, while all 104 verified logins and 14 subscriptions remained in the adjusted view. The outcomes didn’t move, which told me the extra sessions weren’t new readers.

Once you know which patterns deserve attention, you can use GA4 custom insights to get an email when traffic changes unexpectedly. An alert gives you a reason to investigate using the signals above.

When an alert arrives, compare the spike with campaigns and Discover performance, then examine screen resolution, returning users, geography and account outcomes. The same alert could also flag a successful article or a tracking issue. Google's guide to Analytics Insights

In a publishing report, I’d rather explain why sessions rose while verified subscriptions and returning readership stayed flat than turn that spike into an audience-growth claim. The question I bring back to the dashboard is simple.

If we take out the sessions that all behave the same way, would we still make the same decision?

Yes, for known bots. Google Analytics automatically excludes traffic from known bots and spiders, and you can’t turn this off or see how much was excluded. Automation that isn’t on a known list can still reach your reports, which is why the signals above are worth checking.
