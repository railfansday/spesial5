As a primary port of pathogen entry, the intestinal mucosa has developed a sophisticated set of defences to ward off infections. Investigating the human-specific intestinal barrier responses to pathogens is limited by the lack of models integrating the necessary architectural complexity with an autologous immune compartment1. Here we developed engineered barrier models of the human small intestine and colon combining a multilineage epithelium, mucus layer, accessible microbial compartment and autologous tissue-resident immune cells. These tissues recapitulate morphological and functional properties that are region specific and compare with those of the native organ. We use the model to characterize the human epithelial response to Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica serovar Typhimurium at the single-cell level, uncovering the distinct gene regulatory networks that underpin luminal versus intracellular pathogen presence. Invaded epithelial cells initiate an IL-18 signalling axis that triggers cytotoxic T cell activation, resulting in epithelial shedding and barrier disruption. Overall, this work allows for the modular integration of epithelial, microbial and immune compartments, providing a versatile system for studying human intestinal physiology and pathologies.

The human intestine coordinates nutrient absorption with immune surveillance of luminal commensal and pathogenic microorganisms2. Controlled host–microbe interactions shape diverse aspects of human biology, including epithelial maturation and systemic immunity while maintaining a protective barrier against infection3. Conversely, dysregulated interactions contribute to pathologies such as inflammatory bowel disease and colorectal cancer4.

This context-dependent crosstalk is executed through a multi-barrier system comprising a patterned epithelium, a protective mucus layer and specialized tissue-resident immune cells5. Integration of these three components is required to meaningfully capture intestine–microbe interactions in a human-relevant setting1.

Intestinal stem cell (ISC)-derived organoids provide key inroads into human epithelial biology6. However, intestinal organoids face limitations recapitulating intestinal physiology, owing to an inaccessible lumen, culture heterogeneity, lack of biomimetic morphology and absence of non-epithelial lineages7,8. Although bioengineered 3D extracellular matrix (ECM) scaffolds have helped to overcome some of these challenges by establishing crypt–villus architecture and apical–basal access9, physiological integration of epithelial, tissue-resident immune and microbial compartments remains a major challenge1,10.

Here we established engineered immunocompetent human intestinal models composed of autologous tissue-resident memory T (TRM) cells and small intestinal or colonic ISC-derived epithelia amenable to luminal manipulation. Multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) characterization of these barriers reveal region-specific spatial niches, mucus layer formation and immune–epithelium interactions. Applying this system to Salmonella Typhimurium (S.Tm), we provide a single-cell multiomic view of the epithelial responses to luminal and intracellular infection. Finally, we modelled the pathogen–epithelial–immune axis, demonstrating that TRM cells respond to S.Tm invasion by cytokine release and concomitant epithelial damage, which may facilitate pathogen clearance and infection resolution.

To model the human intestinal epithelium, we developed a fluidic device and microstamping technology to impart crypt-mimicking topography on an embedded ECM-like hydrogel (Extended Data Fig. 1a). Human intestinal organoids were seeded into the top reservoir of the device, where they formed a tight, polarized barrier comprising crypt units sustained by basal nutrition (Fig. 1a–d and Extended Data Fig. 1b–e).

To characterize the morphology, cellular composition and patterning of the epithelium, we developed a 3D-printed ‘histomold’ for simultaneous embedding and perpendicular sectioning of up to 50 engineered epithelia (Extended Data Fig. 1f). Haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and mIF staining showed histological and cell-type patterning similarity to donor-matched primary tissue (Fig. 1e and Extended Data Figs. 1g and 2a–c). In addition, a duodenal dual reporter line stably labelling chromatin (H2BC4–mTurquoise2) and LGR5+ stem cells (LGR5–IRES-NLS-mScarlet3) confirmed LGR5 ISC localization to the crypt bottom (Fig. 1f). By day 10, the thick epithelium contained proliferative SOX9+ and Ki67+ cells primarily confined to the crypt, whereas mature enterocytes (APOA4+ in the duodenum, and FABP6+ in the ileum) and colonocytes (CA1+) were enriched within inter-crypt regions (Fig. 1g and Extended Data Fig. 3a–f), with αDEF5+ Paneth cells detected in some small intestinal crypts (Extended Data Fig. 3g). EdU pulse-chase labelling revealed that cycling cells, initially localized in the crypt base, progressively migrated towards the apical surface over 4 days, similar to homeostatic turnover in vivo11 (Extended Data Fig. 3h and Supplementary Video 1). These data confirm that imposing in vivo-inspired geometries on human intestinal organoids drives reproducible cell-type regionalization and migration akin to the intestinal crypt–villus axis9.

Multiplexed scRNA-seq demonstrated region-specific stem, absorptive and secretory lineages across seven donors at day 10 (Fig. 1h–j, Extended Data Fig. 4a,b and Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). Cell-type composition was similar to primary tissue counterparts and proportions were consistent across donors, highlighting the fidelity and robustness of the system (Fig. 1k and Extended Data Fig. 4c). Gene Ontology analysis of enterocyte and colonocyte marker genes revealed enrichment of distinct physiological programs concordant with the primary intestinal regions12,13 (Extended Data Fig. 4d and Supplementary Tables 3 and 4). Proximal small intestinal enterocytes showed higher expression of genes involving lipid, xenobiotic and iron metabolism (Fig. 1l and Extended Data Fig. 4d). Pseudotemporal ordering of the duodenal stem-to-enterocyte trajectory confirmed enterocyte maturation levels, approaching the AQP10+ expression observed at the villus tip in vivo14 (Extended Data Fig. 4e). Distal small intestinal enterocytes expressed bile acid and vitamin B12 transport genes, whereas colonocytes were enriched in cell–cell interactions and ion transport13,15 (Fig. 1l and Extended Data Fig. 4d). We confirmed differential protein expression of the transporters SLC5A1, SLC10A2 and SLC26A2 between the regions (Fig. 1l and Extended Data Fig. 4f). Among secretory lineages, enteroendocrine cells and goblet cells were differentially abundant, with substantial heterogeneity in enteroendocrine cells (CHGA+) including EC (TPH1+), MX (GHRL+ and MLN+), D (SST+), K (GIP+), I (CCK+) and L (GCG+ and PYY+) subpopulations that match tissue distribution16 (Extended Data Figs. 2 and 4g–j).

Collectively, these data show that the engineered epithelium retains region-specific cell composition, spatial organization and gene expression, providing inroads to explore nutrient, xenobiotic and host–microbe processes in a human context.

The mucus, a layered network of mucins, cellular material, antimicrobials and structural factors, provides an interactive barrier between the microbiome and intestinal epithelium. Characterizing the properties and functional role of the mucus at the host–microbe interface remains largely under-explored in vitro. To facilitate rapid accumulation of mucus, we reduced the apical volume over the course of approximately 4 days, which resulted in a fibrous mucus network covering the epithelium (Fig. 2a,b and Extended Data Fig. 5a,b). Shed cells were present throughout the mucus layer, where they contribute to viscosity17 and can exert an antimicrobial role18 (Extended Data Fig. 5b,c). Longitudinal sections revealed an irregular mucus profile, with higher colonic thickness and crypt plumes similar to observations in primary tissue19 (Fig. 2c–e and Extended Data Fig. 5d–g). AlcianBlue–Periodic Acid–Schiff (PAS) co-staining showed regional differences in secretion and glycosylation, with higher ratios of acidic, negatively charged groups towards colon (Fig. 2f and Extended Data Fig. 5h,i). These patterns were comparable with the primary tissue and may be influenced by relative levels of fucose, sialic acid and sulfate groups20. Proteomic analysis of the colonic luminal content identified the core mucus layer proteins21 (MUC2, FCGBP, TFF3, AGR2, CLCA1, ZG16 and MUC5B) as major constituents, alongside additional mucins, antimicrobials and lectin-binding proteins (Fig. 2g, Extended Data Fig. 5j and Supplementary Table 5). Using the scRNA-seq data, we identified seven non-proliferative (MKI67−) goblet cell clusters (C0–C6) representing regional diversity and differentiation state (Fig. 2h, Extended Data Fig. 5k and Supplementary Table 3). Colonic goblet cells (C1) were enriched in intracellular vesicle transport, protein glycosylation and aerobic respiration, suggesting a higher secretion machinery consistent with thickness measurements (Extended Data Fig. 5l and Supplementary Table 4).

Next, we benchmarked the expression of membrane-bound mucins, key glycocalyx components that fortify the epithelium but remain difficult to recapitulate in vitro22. We observed a strong cell-type and region-specific gene expression comparable with the reference tissue (Fig. 2i). MUC6 and MUC5AC, which encode stomach mucins that are often expressed under pathological and metaplastic conditions23,24, were nearly absent. MUC12 and MUC17 staining in the engineered epithelium and tissue counterparts confirmed the specificity and maturation of the glycocalyx, revealing in vivo-like levels by day 12 of culture (Fig. 2j,k and Extended Data Fig. 5m). Finally, we functionally assessed the mucus upon colonization with the commensals Enterococcus faecium or Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus, which proliferated without compromising epithelial integrity (Fig. 2l and Extended Data Fig. 5n). Together, these results indicate that the platform can accumulate a complex mucus layer with in vivo-like and region-specific properties, opening new avenues for the study of human mucus and host–microbe interactions.

Unlike commensal microbiota, the foodborne pathogen S.Tm exploits motility and adhesion mechanisms to penetrate the mucus25, triggering an invasion-dependent inflammatory response that facilitates pathogen colonization26. Although extensive research has characterized the fundamental mechanisms underlying S.Tm infection, the exact epithelial responses and the resulting epithelial–immune signalling remain unresolved at the human level and at single-cell resolution.

Motivated by the higher mucus level and its potential role against pathogenic bacteria, we infected colonic epithelium with S.Tm 14028 in conditions with varying densities of mucus. At 18 h post-start of the infection (hpi), S.Tm triggered strong damage in conditions with less mucus (Mucuslo), whereas the epithelium remained largely unaffected in the presence of dense mucus (Mucushi; Fig. 3a–d and Extended Data Fig. 6a–d). Furthermore, the denser mucus led to a decrease in microbial burden, cell invasion and cytokine release, while preserving barrier integrity (Extended Data Fig. 6e–g). These protective effects were coupled with cell shedding, which predominantly localized at crypt openings, presumably impeding bacterial colonization (Extended Data Fig. 6h).

Next, we used single-cell transcriptome and accessible chromatin sequencing (scMultiome) to characterize the Mucushi colonic epithelium response at 24 hpi and epithelial breach (Extended Data Fig. 6i). We detected canonical cell types in control and S.Tm conditions with increased proportion of mature colonocytes upon infection, a shift reported in mouse tissue27 (Fig. 3e,f and Extended Data Fig. 6j–l). Of note, ‘colonocyte subtype 1’ (RNA clusters C7-1 and C7-2) and ‘subtype 2’ (RNA cluster C8) were nearly exclusive to the infection condition (Fig. 3f and Extended Data Fig. 6k). Overall, all cell types exhibited a pronounced response to infection with 3,056 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and 34,337 differentially accessible open chromatin regions (DARs) between conditions (Extended Data Fig. 6m–p and Supplementary Table 3). Pathway enrichment analysis highlighted hypoxia, NF-κB and barrier or cytoskeletal signatures, including upregulation of antimicrobials (LCN2 and PIGR), reactive oxygen species (DUOX2 and DUOXA2) and inflammatory cytokines (CCL20, IL18 and IL32; Extended Data Fig. 6q–s). Stem cells strongly upregulated LCN2, chemokines (CXCL1–3) and immunomodulators (TNF and LTB). Together, these results identify a complex innate epithelium response involving sensing, barrier maintenance and mucosal signalling.

We used the chromatin accessibility data to infer a gene regulatory network underlying the epithelial response (Fig. 3g). This network revealed transcription factor nodes and target genes that orchestrate a broad-spectrum response, along with clusters that specifically coordinate infection-specific states. We identified BHLHE40, ZNF292 and RBPJ as the top differentially expressed transcription factors in the colonocyte subtype 1, which exhibited a hypoxic signature that appears conserved under infections and inflammatory-related diseases28. STAT3 emerged as a central node of the colonocyte subtype 2. Phosphorylation of STAT3 (pSTAT3) has been linked to intracellular S.Tm infection29 via the virulence factor SteE30,31, leading to an anti-inflammatory transcriptional program that supports the bacterial niche. Co-staining of pSTAT3 and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) revealed a strong colocalization both in colonic and small intestinal samples, with no pSTAT3 detection in non-infected conditions (Fig. 3h and Extended Data Fig. 7a–c). These results, in addition to the expression of the STAT3 negative-feedback regulator SOCS3, suggest that this population is S.Tm specific and contains cells with intracellular bacteria.

Finally, we classified DEGs into co-expression gene modules based on expression differences across cell types between conditions (Supplementary Table 3). We found that the colonocyte subtype 2-specific gene module exhibited increased reactive oxygen species, endoplasmic reticulum stress, unfolded protein response and ferroptosis (Extended Data Fig. 7d and Supplementary Table 4). Furthermore, we observed a possible antagonistic interaction between host cell and pathogen, with non-cannonical inflammasome signalling (CASP4, CASP5 and IL18)32 and genes with anti-inflammatory roles (SBNO2 and TNIP3; Extended Data Fig. 7e,f). BACH1, a key oxidative stress and ferroptosis regulator competing with NRF2 (NFE2L2)33,34, was also among the top upregulated transcription factors (Extended Data Fig. 7g). Binding motifs of STAT3 and BACH1 were over-represented in S.Tm DARs, indicating that both might be regulators of this population (Supplementary Table 6). Finally, we identified putative gene regulatory regions with increased accessibility upon infection, including intronic and distal regions of IL18 and SLC7A11 in the presumably invaded cells (Fig. 3i and Extended Data Fig. 7h). Collectively, these findings identify a complex multipartite human epithelial gene regulatory response to S.Tm infection, confirm STAT3 activation in S.Tm-invaded cells, and demonstrate how engineered intestines can be used to illuminate human infection responses.

Central to S.Tm pathogenesis resides a specialized virulence machinery including two needle-like type III secretion systems (T3SS-1/2) that mediate invasion and intracellular survival26,35 (Fig. 3j). To study how virulence factors influence the host response to luminal and intracellular S.Tm, we performed short, high-load infections (approximately 3 h, multiplicity of infection of approximately 1:60, −mucus) using wild-type (WT) and mutant strains. Subsequent gentamicin treatment removed the luminal stimulus while maintaining intracellular S.Tm populations for several days (Extended Data Fig. 8a).

We observed that flagellum-driven motility (ΔfliC–ΔfljB (ΔFg)) contributed more to invasion than SiiE-dependent adhesion (ΔsiiF; Extended Data Fig. 8b). Consistent with our previous results (Extended Data Fig. 7a–c), internalized S.Tm predominantly localized basolaterally beneath the nucleus, presumably within Salmonella-containing vacuoles (SCV)35 (Extended Data Fig. 8c). Intracellular replication led to either cell extrusion events with luminal release or rupture of SCV-like structures that released S.Tm basally (Extended Data Fig. 8d and Supplementary Videos 2 and 3). The levels of IL-18 increased over 96 hpi and was a hallmark with WT but not avirulent ΔinvG–ΔssaV (ΔSPI-1/2) S.Tm, distinguishing this invasion signature from the broader luminal cytokine response (Fig. 3k and Extended Data Fig. 8e,f). In addition, the system demonstrated virulence sensitivity, as S.Tm SL1344 (ref. 36) elicited an exacerbated invasion-driven response marked by increased internalization, cell shedding and IL-18 secretion compared with S.Tm 14028 (Extended Data Fig. 8g).

To resolve host transcriptional responses induced by luminal or intracellular S.Tm, we performed scRNA-seq on GFP+ (invaded (In)) and GFP− (non-invaded (Out)) cells infected with WTGFP, ΔSPI-1/2GFP, ΔssaVGFP (ΔSPI-2) or ΔsteEGFP strains at 24 hpi (Fig. 3j,l and Extended Data Fig. 8h,i). Invasion occurred across all cell types but was lowest in stem cells, probably given their crypt-base localization (Extended Data Fig. 8j). The luminal response of non-invaded (GFP−) colonocytes was comparable between WT and ΔSPI-1/2, involving hypoxia, barrier and cytokine genes (Fig. 3m). A low-abundant colonocyte state appeared independent of invasion and was marked by IFN signalling (MX1 and ISG15), probably reflecting extracellular bacterial recognition37 (Fig. 3m, ‘depleted non-infected’, Extended Data Fig. 8i and Supplementary Table 4). The intracellular response of invaded (GFP+) colonocytes, which matched the colonocyte subtype 2 state from the multiomic analysis, triggered a profound shift (approximately 4,000 DEGs) characterized by pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory, bacterial recognition, cytoskeleton and oxidative stress genes with variable patterns along differentiation (Fig. 3m, Extended Data Fig. 8k,l and Supplementary Table 2). Although effector contributions may differ functionally, the attenuated gene signature observed in ΔSPI-2, and especially in ΔsteE-invaded cells, positions SteE as a key driver of the intracellular transcriptional landscape (‘enriched in GFP+’; Fig. 3m and Extended Data Fig. 8i,l,m).

Among transcription factors, STAT3 (STAT3, SOCS3 and pSTAT3) was attenuated in ΔSPI-2 and absent in ΔsteE-invaded cells, confirming its SteE-mediated activation31 (Fig. 3m and Extended Data Fig. 8l,n). Invasion by WT and mutants elicited coordinated upregulation of the antagonistic BACH1–NRF2 transcription factor pair33 that coincided with reduced expression of iron homeostasis regulators (GPX4, FTH1, HMOX1 and FTL), suggesting predominant BACH1 activity34 (Fig. 3m and Extended Data Fig. 8l,o). SLC7A11 was a notable exception to this repression, possibly through non-canonical BACH1 activation34, STAT3 regulation38 or an alternative mechanism. Although the exact BACH1–NRF2 interplay and roles remain to be fully elucidated, these transcription factors appeared functionally coupled to intracellular S.Tm, as observed upon small-molecule inhibitor treatments (Extended Data Fig. 8p).

Finally, we classified S.TmWT-invaded cells by bacterial burden using imaging flow cytometry, identifying single-colony to high-load groups at 24 hpi (Extended Data Fig. 8q). To link intracellular replication levels to host response, we performed single-cell total RNA sequencing (VASA-seq) on index-sorted cells from GFPlo, GFPint and GFPhi levels (Extended Data Fig. 8r). GFPhi cells, with up to 35 colony-forming units (CFU) per cell, most strongly correlated with our previously defined intracellular gene module, linking the host response to S.Tm activity and replication rather than invasion alone (Extended Data Fig. 8s,t). Uniquely, VASA-seq recovered not only non-protein coding host transcripts but also bacterial genes involved in metabolism (mgtB, gltA and feoB) and SPI-2 machinery, highlighting the intracellular adaptation of the pathogen (Extended Data Fig. 8u,v and Supplementary Table 3). Collectively, these data provide a high-resolution, multi-modal atlas that uncovers how virulence factors and intracellular burden shape the invaded epithelial response.

Our transcriptomic analysis identified epithelial signals triggered by S.Tm infection and confirmed the epithelium as a sensor of pathogen presence. Elucidating the functional impact of these signals requires the presence of their target populations. Given that S.Tm infection was associated with induction of lymphocyte-targeting cytokines (IL-18 and TNF) and chemokines (CXCL10 and CCL20), we focused on T cells as putative responders and introduced them into the intestinal engineered tissues. In the human intestine, TRM cells reside within the mucosa to safeguard barrier integrity39. We have previously showed that TRM cells integrate into ISC-derived organoids and respond to synthetic T cell receptor (TCR) engagement40. Building on this approach, we established immunosurveilled barriers by incorporating autologous tissue-resident immune cells into the ECM scaffold of our engineered tissues (Fig. 4a–c and Extended Data Fig. 9a–c). These cells were isolated primarily from the small intestine due to higher proximal mucosal immune cell density5. We first assessed immune cell interactions with the epithelium, in the absence of a pathogenic threat. As expected from their tissue-resident phenotype facilitating epithelial interaction (Extended Data Fig. 9d–h), intraepithelial lymphocytes infiltrated the epithelium at physiological rates and patrolling speeds comparable with the organ of origin41 without compromising barrier integrity (Fig. 4d–i, Extended Data Fig. 9i,j and Supplementary Videos 4–6). By contrast, blood-derived T cells barely integrated into the epithelium (Fig. 4f).

To resolve the cellular diversity and epithelium–immune crosstalk, we performed scRNA-seq after 1 week of co-culture. The immune compartment exhibited striking heterogeneity, comprising CD8 T (CD8 TRM) cells, CD4 T helper (CD4 TRM) cells, regulatory T cells, T helper 17 (TH17 TRM) cells, γδ T cells, natural killer cells, innate lymphoid cells (ILCs) and memory B cells (Fig. 4j), demonstrating maintenance of immune lineage fidelity (Extended Data Fig. 9k). TRM populations constituted the majority of the immune cell pool, remaining non-proliferative, consistent with their long-lived quiescent state in vivo42 (Fig. 4j and Extended Data Fig. 9d–g,k). Given their dominant representation, we refer to the entire immune compartment as ‘TRM cells’ henceforth. Although overall epithelial cell-type composition was unaffected (Fig. 4k and Extended Data Fig. 9l,m), immune cell presence induced epithelial antigen processing and presentation programs, marked by upregulation of MHC class I (MHC-I) components (HLA-B and B2M) and the MHC-II chaperone CD74 (Fig. 4l, Extended Data Fig. 9n–p and Supplementary Table 4). Transcription factor expression analysis in the enterocyte cluster linked gene expression changes to STAT1, IRF1 and IRF9, canonical hallmarks of IFNγ signalling (Fig. 4l), suggesting that immune-derived IFNγ facilitates this baseline immune priming43 (Fig. 4m). We confirmed IFNγ within culture supernatants at the protein level (Extended Data Fig. 9q).

Next, we found that engineered tissues recapitulate key epithelial–immune interactions, including IFNGR1/2–IFNG, IL15RA–IL15 (ref. 44) and TNFRSF14–CD160 (ref. 45) (Fig. 4m and Supplementary Table 7). Furthermore, introduction of resident immune cells drove upregulation of the butyrophilin-like (BTNL) molecules BTNL3 and BTNL8 within the epithelium, probably in a HNF4G-dependent manner46 (Fig. 4l). These epithelial BTNL molecules signal back to the immune compartment, facilitating maturation of Vγ4+ γδ T cells and establishing a protective mucosal circuit, whose disruption is linked to intestinal pathology47.

Together, these data suggest that engineered intestinal tissues preserve physiological immune cell behaviours and foster in vivo-like epithelial–immune interactions.

Having incorporated TRM cells into the engineered tissues, we next asked how they interpret epithelial-derived signals to orchestrate mucosal defence upon S.Tm infection. We challenged immunocompetent barriers with high-load S.Tm for 3 h following gentamicin treatment (Fig. 5a). Histological analysis confirmed bacteria within close proximity of intraepithelial lymphocytes (Fig. 5b). The presence of resident immune cells accelerated mucosal damage compared with epithelium-only controls, leading to increased epithelial shedding and loss of barrier integrity (Fig. 5c and Extended Data Fig. 10a–c). Attributing elevated epithelial damage to TRM cell activation, we analysed the basal compartment for inflammatory mediators observed in patients with gastroenteric Salmonella infection48,49. We identified two temporally distinct regimes of immune and epithelial responses. A rapid (10 hpi) first wave of IL-2 and IFNγ secretion, indicating a canonical CD4+ TH1 response, occurred concurrently with epithelial secretion of IL-18 (Fig. 5d). This initial response was followed by release of cytotoxic and antimicrobial effectors, including GzmB, IL-6 and TH17-associated cytokines (IL17A and IL-22). TNF, IL-13 and IL-1β were modestly induced (Fig. 5d and Extended Data Fig. 10d,e). Although facilitating pathogen clearance (Extended Data Fig. 10f), this TH1–TH17-driven TRM cell response was associated with mucosal injury.

To determine whether TRM cell activation requires epithelial invasion or only luminal exposure to the pathogen, we leveraged the S.Tm mutants introduced previously (ΔFg, ΔSPI-2 and ΔSPI-1/2; Fig. 3). We found that the extent of mucosal injury and the magnitude of TRM cell response correlated with S.Tm invasive capacity and epithelial IL-18 secretion (Fig. 5e,f and Extended Data Fig. 10g). Furthermore, in line with the stronger epithelial response described earlier (Extended Data Fig. 8g), S.Tm SL1344 induced a more rapid and severe immunopathology than S.Tm 14028 (Extended Data Fig. 10h). Together, these data link TRM cell responses to pathogen virulence, invasive capacity and subsequent epithelial cytokine secretion. Although TRM cell responses are classically defined by antigen-specific TCRs, their capacity for innate-like bystander responses is a critical yet poorly understood feature of human mucosal immunity. We sought to understand whether S.Tm-mediated TRM cell activation is driven by secreted epithelial signals rather than direct TCR binding to epithelial MHC. Inhibition of PI3K and p38 MAPK, essential for innate-like IFNγ induction50, significantly quelled the response, whereas TCR-dependent LCK inhibition51 showed no effect (Fig. 5g). Likewise, surface TCR expression remained stable during S.Tm infection, contrasting the expected downregulation triggered by synthetic TCR engagement via an EpCAM–CD3 bispecific52 (Fig. 5h). Conditioned media from infected epithelium-only barriers were sufficient to activate TRM cells, whereas direct stimulation with bacterial pathogen-associated molecular patterns (LPS and flagellin) failed to trigger degranulation (Fig. 5g and Extended Data Fig. 10i). We note that this non-cognate response appeared to be a hallmark of tissue residency, as allogeneic and autologous blood-derived T cells remained unresponsive to infection, despite exhibiting substantial CD45RO+ populations (Extended Data Fig. 9d), high viability and IL-2 induction capacity (Fig. 5i and Extended Data Fig. 10j,k). Together, these results establish S.Tm-induced TRM cell activation in this system as a TCR-independent bystander response that requires inflammatory signals relayed by the epithelium. The induction of IL-18 upon S.Tm invasion closely coincided with the kinetics of the T cell response (Fig. 5d), implicating this cytokine as a key epithelial orchestrator of T cell activation. This was supported by high expression of IL-18Rα on CD4+ TRM cells, which conferred pronounced responsiveness to exogenous IL-18 and S.Tm infection (Fig. 5j and Extended Data Fig. 10l,m). To assess its role as an epithelial messenger, we generated IL18 knockouts in intestinal organoids using CRISPR–Cas9, resulting in ablation of IL-18 expression at baseline and upon challenge (Fig. 5k and Extended Data Fig. 10n). Indeed, IL-18 levels in WT S.Tm-infected mutant barriers were comparable with those of the avirulent ΔSPI-1/2 infection and non-infected control (Fig. 5l). When assessing the TRM cell response, IL-18 deficiency significantly dampened the inflammatory response (Fig. 5l,m), attenuating intracellular IFNγ levels in the cytotoxic IL-18Rα+CD4+ responder population (Extended Data Fig. 10o). Critically, the loss of IL-18 protected the barrier from pathological shedding and thinning (Fig. 5n and Extended Data Fig. 10p) despite functional T cell responder potential, linking IL-18-driven T cell activation to immunopathology.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the epithelium orchestrates antibacterial mucosal defence by secreting IL-18 to trigger non-cognate, innate-like responses of the TRM compartment.

We have reported bioengineered, luminally accessible human intestinal tissues comprising epithelia with periodically repeating crypts that maintain LGR5+ stem cells, in vivo-like cell composition and patterning, and retain transcriptomic signatures across multiple donors. Identical medium and biophysical culture conditions yield region-specific signatures due to stem cell origin. Cell-type maturation and a reduction in total apical volume enable the formation of a region-specific mucus layer with mature glycocalyx. In addition, autologous TRM cells patrol the barrier and engage in bidirectional crosstalk with the epithelium, conferring an immune-responsive state within epithelial cells via IFNγ signalling.

The modularity of the system enabled new inquiry into S.Tm infection in a human context, contributing to collective efforts to refine physiologically relevant enteric infection models53. Basolateral S.Tm localization within polarized cells presumably facilitates dissemination into the lamina propria, a phenomenon that has been reported in vivo54,55. In addition, the phenotypic divergence observed between SL1344 and 14028 strains could expand to clinical isolates and human-restricted pathogens (for example, Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica serovar Typhi, Shigella flexneri and Vibrio cholerae), including the study of host factors influencing bacterial gene expression and antibiotic susceptibility. We mapped the gene regulatory networks underlying luminal and intracellular S.Tm at single-cell resolution. The epithelium orchestrated inflammatory, metabolic and structural responses that accompany S.Tm colonization26 and demonstrate an innate-sensing capacity that coordinates multi-lineage immune recruitment in vivo. S.Tm invasion reshaped the host transcriptome through a SteE-dependent STAT3 gene regulatory hub that extends results in macrophages and immortalized lines29,31. The reduced SPI-2 contribution may stem from non-transcriptional effector roles or delayed activation resulting from the SPI-1-inducing pre-culture protocol56. In addition, changes in oxidative stress and iron homeostasis suggest a host–pathogen interplay associated with other intracellular infections57, warranting further study. Overall, these responses were highly influenced by the intracellular burden of the pathogen.

Advancing our previous work40 to an apically accessible immunocompetent system, we demonstrated that epithelial IL-18 upon S.Tm invasion is sufficient to drive TRM cell activation and cytokine release, reflecting clinical gastroenteric salmonellosis48,49. The system allowed us to elucidate the functional effects of this bystander T cell activation, aligning with previous reports of innate-like polyfunctional capabilities of TRM cells58,59,60. TRM cells intensified infection-mediated epithelial shedding, which probably contributes to pathogen clearance61 and is consistent with the rapid onset of diarrhoea during S.Tm infection. Although mouse studies have established the necessity of IL-18 for CD4+ T cell defence upon S.Tm infection62, it has been shown that enteric neurons, rather than the epithelium, serve as the critical source of IL-18 for intestinal protection63. We demonstrated that the human intestinal epithelium can potently activate TRM cells via IL-18 to drive defence responses, at least in an in vitro setting. Of note, S.Tm infection64 and IL-18 (ref. 65) have been linked to the onset of inflammatory bowel disease. Our data raise the possibility that this association may be underpinned by the appearance of a cytotoxic IL-18Rα+CD4+ T cell population58,62.

Although our platform provides a unique window into human intestinal in vitro modelling, several limitations remain. The engineered tissues lack villous structures, specialized Paneth or M cells, and myeloid immune lineages, crucial mediators of immunity against infection. The lack of fluid exchange might affect biophysical characteristics of a mucus layer accumulated by apical media reduction. Furthermore, S.Tm invasion rates probably exceed physiological levels due to mucus protection. Future studies may circumvent these limitations through bioengineering approaches9, mucus secretagogues, cytokine supplementation or the inclusion of additional commensal and immune lineages.

Beyond host–pathogen interaction, the engineered tissues and analytical framework expand what can be addressed ex vivo. The mature, patterned epithelium opens research directions in region-specific drugs and nutrient absorption, mucus biology and epithelial–TRM cell interactions implicated in inflammatory bowel disease pathogenesis. Overall, the platform brings the field closer to capturing complex, organ-level processes in a human setting that is controllable and tractable.

During the work on this project, a large number of microdevice prototype versions were fabricated using a range of microfabrication techniques, including cleanroom soft lithography, 3D printing, two-photon polymerization (2PP) lithography, computer numerical control (CNC) milling and plastic injection molding. The final chip is designed in a slide format (75 × 25 mm) containing three independent chips per slide. Each chip contained a central hydrogel chamber flanked by two side medium reservoirs and one top medium reservoir. The hydrogel precursor was injected through a loading port leading to the central gel chamber. A cylindrical stamp with a microtopography (for example, an array of biomimetic micropillars) on its base was placed onto a stamp-holding edge of the top reservoir before gel injection. After gel polymerization (see details in a separate section), the stamp was removed, leaving an inverse microtopography (for example, an array of microwells) on the hydrogel surface. The top and side reservoirs were then filled with PBS or cell culture medium. The top reservoir was used for cell seeding and subsequently served as the apical medium reservoir after epithelial coverage of the hydrogel and barrier formation. Medium from the side reservoirs passively diffused through the gel, supplying the epithelial tissue with nutrients from the basal side. Early prototypes were fabricated using conventional soft lithography and polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) molding workflows established at the Center of Micronanotechnology (EPFL). The 3D chip design was split into two photomasks that were sequentially aligned to enable the creation of pseudo-3D features and different heights for phase-guiding micro-pillars. Chip layouts were patterned onto chrome-coated fused silica substrates via laser lithography (VPG200, Heidelberg Instruments). Three layers of SU8 GM1075 (Gerlteltec) were sequentially spin coated onto dehydrated silicon wafers to achieve a total mold thickness of 750 μm. The first two 250-μm layers were soft baked, exposed to UV through the first photomask (MA6/BA6, Süss MicroTec) and post-baked. Then, the third SU8 layer was spin coated, soft baked and the second photomask was aligned to the existing pattern using dedicated alignment marks. After a final post-exposure bake and development with propylene glycol monomethyl ether acetate (PGMEA; Sigma), the molds were hard baked at 135 °C for 4 h and silanized overnight using vapour-phase trichloro(1H,1H,2H,2H-perfluorooctyl) silane (Sigma-Aldrich). PDMS (Sylgard 184, Dow Corning) was mixed at a 10:1 base-to-curing agent ratio, poured onto the molds, degassed under vacuum and cured at 80 °C for 24 h. PDMS replicas were cut, punched and plasma bonded to glass-bottom dishes. The strength of the soft lithography pipeline is in fabrication of high-resolution microfeatures and with relatively optimal throughput, as 10–20 chips can be produced from a single wafer. However, fabrication of the models taller than 500 μm is challenging and only step-like, pseudo-3D features from a single side of the PDMS layer can be fabricated. To overcome these limitations, we explored several other complementary fabrication strategies. Commercial 3D-printing techniques such as fused deposition modelling and stereolithography were used for the production of larger models (Formlabs Form 4). However, these methods lacked the resolution and surface finish required for high-quality PDMS molding and were suitable only for some early-stage proof-of-concept prototypes. Instead, here we used 2PP lithography using the UpNano NanoOne printer to fabricate detailed 3D features of the chip. In brief, 3D models of the microchip were created in Autodesk Fusion (latest version), exported as STL files and processed using UpNano Think3D software. The models were printed on glass coverslips using UpPhoto resin (UpNano). NanoOne stands out as the most developed printer for mesoscale manufacturing, enabling printing of large structures (10–20 mm in xy and up to 40 mm in height) due to several advanced technologies: a submerged printing mode in a large resin reservoir, high-power 1,000 W, 780-nm laser for fast printing with 5 × 0.25 numerical aperture (NA) objective and adaptive resolution technology that significantly reduces print times (up to 450 mm3 h−1). After printing, the coverslips were washed in isopropanol and silanized overnight using vapour-phase trichloro(1H,1H,2H,2H-perfluorooctyl) silane (Sigma-Aldrich). PDMS was cast, degassed, cured at 80 °C and then replicas were cut and plasma bonded to glass-bottom dishes. This method allowed us to fabricate high-quality prototypes for validation of the 3D microstructural design elements. However, the main limitation is the upscaling technology for fabrication of the large number of chips. More importantly, process flow is still resulting in the 3D topography features only from one side of the PDMS replica. To overcome the latter problem, commonly some laboratories fabricate multi-layered PDMS chips. In this approach, individual PDMS layers are fabricated separately, aligned and plasma bonded together. This direction was explored early on for fabrication of a few prototypes; however, the alignment process is tedious and unsuitable for large-scale chip manufacturing with the acceptable quality. To address this, inspired by industrial injection molding technology, we developed a cassette mold for PDMS curing. Molds were manufactured by CNC milling of acrylic, aluminum or steel, depending on the desired feature size and quality. Each cassette consisted of two mold parts bearing microtopographies of the bottom and top surfaces of the chip. When assembled, the parts fit precisely, enabling accurate alignment of both sides of the device. PDMS was injected into centre space of the mold, degassed in a vacuum chamber and cured in an 80 °C oven. After curing, the mold was opened and the PDMS sheets were cut and bonded to glass-bottom dishes. This process overcomes many of the limitations of previously described techniques and enables scalable production of hundreds of high-quality chips in a single batch. The main drawback is the need to adapt design features for CNC milling: some of the smallest features must be enlarged and sharp corners rounded. However, we did not consider this as a drawback as concurrently we began exploring industrial plastic molding technologies to eliminate the last remaining problem: the PDMS material itself. Although PDMS is ideal for prototyping, its high absorption of small molecules makes it unsuitable for translational applications and drug testing. Many of the learnings and design optimizations from the PDMS cassette-molding process were directly translated into the design of the final injection-molded plastic chip. Currently, the chip body is fabricated from cyclic olefin copolymer (COC) due to its low shrinkage, chemical resistance and optical transmission. The main body was fabricated as a single piece, followed by bonding of a 170-μm COC film to the bottom surface. A matching lid converting the slide was later designed and produced separately also via a COC injection molding process. After assembly, chips were packaged and sterilized using ethylene oxide gas. Large-scale chip production was outsourced to an external manufacturing facility.

Stamps featuring crypt-mimicking microtopography were fabricated from PDMS via replica molding of 2PP-printed models. First, arrays of colon crypt-mimicking micropillars were designed in Autodesk Fusion (latest version). Several topographies were tested and optimized, the final microtopography design consisted of the slightly conical pillars, ranging from 70 to 120 μm in diameter (top to bottom) and 700 μm in height. The 3D model was printed using a NanoOne (UpNano) 2PP printer on glass coverslips with UpPhoto resin and a 10 × 0.4 NA objective. To generate a master mold with inverted topography, the printed structures were plasma activated and silanized overnight using vapour-phase trichloro(1H,1H,2H,2H-perfluorooctyl) silane (Sigma-Aldrich). PDMS (Sylgard 184, Dow Corning; 10:1 base-to-curing agent ratio) was poured over the master, degassed under vacuum and cured at 80 °C for 12 h. The resulting PDMS replica was cut, plasma activated and silanized overnight as above. This PDMS negative was then used repeatedly as a mold for casting final PDMS stamps using the same procedure (Sylgard 184, 10:1 ratio, cured at 80 °C for 12 h).

To generate the ECM for the chip bovine TeloCol-6 type I collagen solution (5225-50 ml, Advanced Biomatrix) was neutralized following the manufacturer’s instructions and then mixed with Matrigel (356231, Corning) in 87.5:12.5 (v/v) ratio. A PDMS stamp with crypt-mimmicking array of pillar topography was placed in the central top reservoir. After the gel injection through the gel-loading port, the hydrogel was polymerized on a 37 °C hotplate for 10 min. After ECM polymerization, all chip reservoirs were topped up with PBS or cell culture medium. Prepared chips with hydrogels can be stored for multiple weeks at 37 °C at 5% CO2 (hereafter referred to as ‘incubator’) or in the +4 °C fridge before use. Hydrogel stiffness was measured using an MCR 702e MultiDrive oscillatory shear rheometer (Anton Paar) in a parallel plate configuration with a PP25/P3 measuring plate. The Young’s modulus (E) was estimated from the storage modulus (G′), assuming the hydrogel is elastic and nearly incompressible, using the relation E = 2 G(1 + ν) ≈ 3 G′, where Poisson’s ratio (ν) is approximately 0.5. Across multiple batches and replicates, the hydrogels yielded a Young’s modulus of 3,000 ± 500 Pa.

The accumulation of mucus was facilitated by reducing the total apical volume from approximately 120 to 100 µl of chip medium (Supplementary Table 1) to either approximately 30 µl or about 5 µl, based on the experimental needs. Permeabilization was followed by a blocking step with blocking buffer (10% donkey serum (S30-M, Sigma-Aldrich) and 0.01% Triton X-100 in DPBS 1X) for 4 h at room temperature on an orbital rocker or 16 h at 4 °C. Primary antibodies (Supplementary Table 1) were applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C on an orbital rocker. Afterwards, samples were washed three times for 1 h each at room temperature with 1X DBPS on an orbital rocker. Anti-species secondary antibodies (Supplementary Table 1), DAPI (1 µg ml−1; 62249, Thermo Scientific) and phalloidin (Alexa Fluor 568 phalloidin A12380; Alexa Fluor 488 phalloidin A12379; Alexa Fluor 647 phalloidin A30107, Thermo Fisher Scientific) were incubated for 4 h at room temperature or 16 h at 4 °C on an orbital rocker, covering the sample with aluminum foil. Samples were washed three times for 1 h each with DPBS on an orbital rocker before imaging.

For engineered tissue, samples were washed once with 1X DPBS before fixation with 4% PFA in the chip slide. After 2 h of fixation at room temperature, the engineered intestines were washed three times before using a biopsy puncher (3 mm in diameter) to detach the ECM and tissue. In parallel, the engineered intestines histomold, specifically designed to facilitate co-planar embedding of up to 50 engineered intestines, was prepared as previously described8. In brief, liquid histogel was dispensed into the chip histomold and allowed to polymerize for 20 min at 4 °C. Following polymerization, the histoarray was demoulded. Engineered intestines were then transferred with a thin tweezer into the slits of the histoarray. After careful glueing of the engineered intestines in the wells of the histoarray (see Harter et al.8), the histoarray was completely filled and distributed into biopsy cassettes. Samples were dehydrated overnight using a vacuum filter processor (HistoCore PEARL, Leica). The following day, samples were embedded in liquid paraffin.

For primary intestinal tissue, samples were washed three times in 1X DPBS and fixed in 4% PFA at room temperature. Fixation was performed for 24 h, although reduced to 3 h for smaller specimens to prevent over-fixation.

The basal compartments of the engineered intestines were washed once with 1X DPBS before fixation with 4% PFA. The apical reservoir was neither washed nor fixed to preserve the integrity of the luminal content. After 2 h of fixation at room temperature, the engineered intestines were washed three more times only in the basal compartment. Next, the bottom glass of the chip slide was detached by applying mechanical pressure and the matrix was punched from the bottom of the hydrogel in upwards direction, keeping the epithelium and luminal content. The punched sample was then carefully covered with 8 µl of TeloCol-6 type I collagen solution (5225-50 ml, Advanced Biomatrix) neutralized according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Finally, the sample was incubated for 15 min at 37 °C before the transfer to the slits of the histoarray, as described before.

Samples were dehydrated using a graded ethanol series as previously described8. In brief, tissues were held in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 5 min, followed by sequential dehydration steps: two washes in 70% ethanol (30 min each), one wash in 80% ethanol (1 h), and two washes in 96% ethanol (30 min each). Next, two steps of two 1-h incubations in 100% ethanol were performed, followed by two 45-min xylene baths. Finally, samples were held in paraffin until regular formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) setting.

For all datasets, raw counts were normalized using the ‘NormalizeData’ function with a scale factor of 10,000. Three thousand highly variable genes (HVGs) were identified using the variance-stabilizing transformation (‘vst’) method. Data were scaled and principal component analysis was performed using these variable features. The first 20 principal components were used as input for ‘FindNeighbors’ and dimensional reduction with UMAP. The resulting graph was used for ‘FindClusters’ with the Louvain algorithm at resolution 1.0.

For the multiplexed data, after inspection of regional marker gene expression, we excluded the potential foregut sample (‘don55’) marked by SOX2 and a sample with mixed ileal and colonic identity (‘IF17ile’, FABP6+ and SATB2+). We then removed ribosomal genes, mitochondrial genes, genes located on sex chromosomes and genes without proper gene symbols to avoid influence of these confounding factors to the integration of this dataset derived from multiple donors and reran the pipelines from normalization to clustering.

Epithelial cell-type annotation was initialized on cluster level based on expression of canonical intestinal cell type and regional markers, including: stem cells (LGR5, OLFM4, SMOC2 and ASCL2), absorptive cells (APOA4, FABP6 and CEACAM7), goblet cells (MUC2 and FCGBP), enteroendocrine cells (CHGA), transit-amplifying cells (MKI67), BEST4+ cells (BEST4), epithelial regional identity markers (SATB2, GATA4, CDX2 and PDX1) and leukocytes (PTPRC). Clusters with similar expression patterns were merged and resulted in a coarse-grained annotation. Differential features between proximal, distal and colonic enterocytes and colonocytes were derived from the top 500 marker genes using the ‘wilcoxauc’ function of the ‘presto’ R package on normalized RNA expression values (Wilcoxon test, logFC > log(1.1), adjusted P < 0.05). Enrichment for ‘BP’ (biological process) and ‘MF’ (molecular function) Gene Ontology was performed with the function enrichGO from the clusterProfiler package. Detected genes were used as background and all terms in the org.Hs.eg.db database were used. The enrichment score for each term was calculated by subtracting the significant −log10-transformed hypergeometric test Benjamini–Hochberg-corrected P value of the region of interest and the average of those from the other two regions. Missing values or not undefined numeric results for one region were set to the minimum of the other two regions for visualization.

Enteroendocrine cells (EECs) subtype annotation was performed following subclustering. Subtype-specific signatures (‘EC’: TPH1; ‘MX’: GHRL and MLN; ‘K’: GIP; ‘L’: PYY and GCG; ‘D’: SST; ‘I’: CCK; and ‘Prog’: NEUROG3) were calculated using the AddModuleScore function in Seurat on normalized RNA expression values. Cells were assigned to the identity with the highest score, provided the score exceeded a minimum threshold (0.1). Cells that did not meet this criteria were categorized as ‘EEC unassigned’.

For the multiplexed data and the sorted infection dataset, which did not resolve a cluster representing distal small intestine stem cells, we extracted stem cells from the continuum to absorptive cells based on summarized expression levels of stem cell marker genes. For the multiplexed data, we selected the top ten genes with the highest correlation with summarized scaled expression of LGR5, SMOC2 and ASCL2 across stem cells and absorptive cells and calculated the stem cell score of each cell by summing the scaled expression of selected genes. We defined cells with stem cell scores higher than the 60th percentile of positive scores as stem cells and others as absorptive cells. To reduce the noise of classification solely based on scores of individual cells, we performed high-resolution clustering (resolution = 5), and defined cells belonging to the high-resolution clusters with more stem cells than absorptive cells were defined as stem cells, regardless of their individual scores. For the sorted infection dataset, we subsetted two initial clusters (resolution = 1 from the whole dataset) with LGR5+ cells. We selected the top five genes with the highest correlation with summarized scaled expression of LGR5 and SMOC2 across stem cells and colonocytes and calculated the stem cell score of each cell by summing the scaled expression of selected genes. We defined cells with stem cell scores higher than the 90th percentile of positive scores as stem cells and others as colonocytes. The distribution of the manually extracted stem cells fits the visual inspection on the LGR5 feature plot. For the colonocyte-subtype annotation in the sorted infection dataset, colonocyte clusters with more than 80% cells from the GFP+ samples were collectively defined as ‘enriched in GFP+’, the colonocyte cluster with less than 0.1% cells from the non-infected sample were defined as ‘depleted in non-infected’, and other colonocyte clusters were defined as ‘shared’.

To resolve the goblet cell heterogeneity in the multiplexed data, we subsetted goblet cells from the entire dataset based on their coarse-grained cell-type annotation. The subset was re-normalized, and variable features were recalculated and reclustered (resolution = 1). We excluded clusters with enriched expression of MKI67 to get non-proliferative goblet cells and performed subclustering. We identified cluster markers using the wilcoxauc function implemented by the presto package and defined significant cluster markers with Wilcoxon rank-sum test Benjamini–Hochberg-adjusted P < 0.05, expressed cell percentage difference (pct_in) > 10%, expression logFC > 0.1.

TRM cell-type annotation was performed on the cluster level. We first annotated epithelial cells (EPCAM+), B cells (CD79B+) and proliferative CD4+ T cells (MKI67+), then annotated non-proliferative T cell subtypes based on mapping to a public adult human intestine tissue immune cell reference with two complementary approaches. We identified cell-type markers of the in vitro and tissue data separately using the wilcoxauc function with cut-off Wilcoxon rank-sum test Benjamini–Hochberg-adjusted P < 0.05, expressed cell percentage difference > 10%, expression logFC > 0.1. We quantified the marker gene overlap significance between in vitro and tissue cell types using −log10-transformed Fisher’s two-sided exact test nominal P value. For each in vitro cell type, we got the top three tissue cell types with the strongest overlap, or the subset of top three if the significance difference between pairs of top three was larger than 10. In parallel, we calculated Spearman correlation between our in vitro and tissue cell types based on the markers defined in both systems. For each in vitro cell type, we got the top three tissue cell types showing the highest transcription program similarity, or the subset of top three if the significance difference between pairs of top three was larger than 0.1. We then annotated the in vitro T cells as the overlap of overlap-based and similarity-based matched tissue cell types. On the basis of further inspection of individual canonical marker gene expression, we manually renamed activated CD4 T, activated CD8 T and MAIT-like CD4 as CD4 TRM, CD8 TRM and CD4 TRMTH17, respectively.

To construct an adult human intestine tissue transcriptome reference, we collected the count matrix and cell-type annotation of epithelial cells from three public adult intestine multi-region scRNA-seq datasets79,80,81. All datasets were processed using Seurat (v4). After merging the datasets, we performed LogNormalize normalization with a scale factor of 10,000. We identified HVGs independently for each dataset using the vst method, selecting 3,000 HVGs per dataset. We then selected integration features across datasets using the SelectIntegrationFeatures function. We ran principal component analysis using the selected integration features and retrieved the top 50 principal components. We integrated the data with Cluster Similarity Spectrum (CSS)82 stratified by sample, and default setting for other parameters. We performed dimension reduction with UMAP, and Louvain clustering based on CSS embeddings using the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions at a resolution of 1.0. We compared the data without batch effect correction, integration with Harmony or with CSS integration, and chose CSS-integrated results based on visual inspection of canonical cell-type marker gene expression.

To establish a consistent cell-type annotation scheme across datasets, we created a mapping between original annotations and standardized categories. We classified cells into major epithelial lineages: absorptive cells (enterocytes and colonocytes), goblet cells, BEST4+ cells, EECs, follicle-associated epithelium, stem cells, M cells, Paneth cells, secretory progenitors and tuft cells. We further refined these annotations by incorporating regional information. We defined the G2M-phased cells defined by the CellCycleScoring function in Seurat within the clusters with enriched expression of MKI67 as the transit-amplifying cells. Cell-type identities were validated using established marker genes. For rare cell populations (for example, Paneth cells and tuft cells), we calculated expression-based scores using canonical markers (DEFA5 and DEFA6 for Paneth cells; RGS13, AVIL and ALOX5 for tuft cells) and assigned cells based on these scores. To be more specific, we identified the top five positively correlated genes with summarized DEFA5 and DEFA6 scaled expression profiles across epithelium among the 3,000 HVGs as feature genes of Paneth cells. We summed up the scaled expression levels of the selected top five Paneth-associated genes for each cell as the Paneth cell feature score, and defined cells with scores exceeding 97.5th percentile of all positive scores as Paneth cells. We applied a similar procedure to identify tuft cells, with 10 tuft cell feature genes and using 95 percentile as the selection cut-off.

To establish a tissue reference for colonocyte subtype annotation in the Salmonella dataset, we subsetted to non-small intestine transit-amplifying cells, stem cells and colonocytes, integrated the data with CSS and performed subclustering based on the CSS embeddings at resolution = 0.4. We identified cluster markers using the wilcoxauc function in the presto package, with cut-offs adjusted P < 0.05, pct_in > 10 and logFC > 0.1. We selected the top 100 markers ranked by logFC from each cluster and unified them as features for comparison between in vitro and tissue colonic stem cells and colonocytes.

To further reconstruct the differentiation trajectory from stem cells to colonocytes in primary tissues (excluding transit-amplifying cells), we computed a diffusion map using the DiffusionMap function in the destiny package on the CSS space and we defined the rank of the first diffusion component (DC1) as the inferred differentiation pseudotime. We ordered the cells along the pseudotime, partitioned them into 25 bins and calculated the average gene expression of each bin. We calculated the Pearson correlation coefficients between the bin average expression levels and the bin order for each gene. The 50 genes with the strongest positive correlations were selected as pseudotime-positive correlating genes, which show higher expression levels in mature colonocytes, and the 50 genes with the strongest negative correlations as pseudotime-negative correlating genes, which show higher expression levels in stem cells and early colonocytes. We then applied these tissue-derived differentiation-related genes on the sorted infection dataset. We used the AddModuleScore function in Seurat based on normalized RNA expression values of the pseudotime positively and negatively correlating genes separately. A composite differentiation score of the cells in the chip was defined as the difference between the positive and negative module scores. We ordered the colonocytes according to the differentiation score from premature to mature, partitioned them into 25 bins and calculated the average gene expression of each bin. The obtained bin average gene expression profiles are defined as expression profiles along the reconstructed pre-mature-to-mature colonocyte differentiation trajectory.

To create a unified peak list across samples, we called open chromatin-accessible regions in each sample using MACS2 (v2.2.9.1) with the CallPeaks function and then quantified them with FeatureMatrix in Signac. We only kept regions located on standard chromosomes. We annotated peaks to most nearby genes using the annotatePeak function in ChIPseeker based on the Ensemble (v98) annotation. We only retained cells with ATAC UMI count between 200 and 50,000, RNA UMI count less than 20,000, RNA gene number between 500 and 5,000, mitochondrial percentage less than 50%, nucleosome signal (calculated by NucleosomeSignal) less than 1, transcription start site enrichment score (calculated by TSSEnrichment) less than 1 and hg38 genome blacklist ratio less than 0.5%. We ran the same pipeline of scRNA-seq data from read count normalization to dimension reduction and clustering as described in the previous section (‘Preprocessing and cell-type annotation of scRNA-seq data’). For the scATAC-seq data, we performed term frequency-inverse document frequency normalization on read counts using the RunTFIDF function. We defined the regions with over 1% detection rate as top features and RunSVD based on the top features. We projected cells into two-dimension UMAP embeddings and FindNeighbors based on top 2–50 latent semantic indexing.

We performed major epithelial cell-type annotation on RNA cluster level based on canonical cell-type marker expression. We subsetted to colonic stem cell and colonocyte clusters, calculated the Spearman correlation coefficients between in vitro and tissue stem cell to colonocyte clusters using the in vitro-expressed colonic stem cell and colonocyte feature genes defined in the tissue reference. We performed hierarchical clustering on the in vitro clusters based on the similarity profiles and classified them into stem cells and four colonocyte subtypes indicating differential maturity accordingly. Colonocyte clusters with mostly exclusive presence in the Salmonella-infected sample were defined as infection-specific colonocyte clusters. We transferred RNA-level cell-type annotations to ATAC data, and merged subtypes that could not be clearly resolved by ATAC data as annotations on the ATAC level.

We performed differential analysis for co-clustered S.Tm infection and control cells in each cluster of stem cells, colonocytes and goblet cells, which have sufficient cell numbers for robust estimation. For infection-specific clusters, which have less than 20 control cells in the clusters, we first identified matched control sample cells for each infected sample cell using get.knnx of the FNN package with k = 1 in the top 20 principal component space. We identified DEGs between matched control and infected cells using the wilcoxauc function by the presto package, with cut-offs adjusted P < 0.05, pct_in > 10 and logFC > 0.1. We applied the same procedure to identify DEGs in the epithelium induced by TRM cell co-culture except that we did not need to use the k-nearest neighbour search approach to get matched control cells as there was no condition-specific cluster.

To identify DARs between Salmonella-infected and control conditions, we implemented a statistical framework based on linear regression modelling of binarized chromatin accessibility data, with sequencing depth (UMI counts) as covariate. Specifically, for each open chromatin region i, we fitted:

Null model: glm(e ~ UMIcounts, family = binomial)

Full model: glm(e ~ UMIcounts + condition, family = binomial)

where e represents the binarized accessibility state vector for region i across all cells. We applied a Chi-squared test comparing the two models using ANOVA. Regions were considered differentially accessible if they had Benjamini–Hochberg-adjusted P < 0.05 and showed at least a 0.5% difference in the proportion of cells with the region accessible between Salmonella-infected and control conditions.

We reconstructed the gene regulatory networks from the scMultiome data along the stem-cell-to-coloncyte trajectory and each of the stem cell, colonocyte and goblet cell types shared by both control and infection conditions using Pando83 separately. We used the infection-induced DEGs and 5,000 HVGs of each data as input HVGs for Pando. We constrained our candidate regulatory regions to be either conserved in mammals (defined in phastConsElements20Mammals.UCSC.hg38 provided by Pando) or overlapped with ENCODE regions (defined in SCREEN.ccRE.UCSC.hg38 provided by Pando), and showing significantly correlated profiles with HVGs as output by LinkPeaks in Signac at the default distance threshold of 500 kb. We ran find_motifs and used the Pando-provided transcription factor-binding site (TFBS) motif database compiled from JASPAR and CIS-BP as reference for Pando to identify TFBS in candidate regulatory regions. We ran the infer_grn function with a Gaussian generalized linear model (model = ‘glm’) to infer regulatory coefficients from log-normalized transcript counts and binarized peak counts summarized to SLM algorithm-defined ATAC clusters (resolution = 10). We identified transcription factor–target gene modules using the find_modules function with default settings. We combined the modules from different datasets as inferred transcription factor–target pairs.

For visualization, we selected the condition-dependent DEGs with the top 500 absolute value of expression log-transformed fold change between conditions and the union set of the top three-correlated Pando-predicted transcription factors of each selected DEGs. We calculated pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients of expression profiles across cell types and conditions between selected genes, reduced the dimension of obtained correlation matrix to 20 principal components using prcomp_irlba in the irlba package and further projected genes to two-dimension embeddings using umap in the uwot package with the obtained principal components as input.

We ran the AddMotifs function in Signac to add the TFBS motifs information on our detected open chromatin regions and obtained a motif occurrence matrix. To identify TFBS motifs over-represented in open chromatin regions with increased accessibility upon infection in each cell type, we ran FindMotifs in Signac on the top 200 upregulated accessible regions, with 30,000 open chromatin regions with matched overall GC content retrieved by the MatchRegionStats function as background.

We defined three comparisons to characterize the luminal effects: (1) ΔSPI-1/2 non-invaded (ΔSPI-1/2Out, GFP−) versus non-infected, (2) WT non-invaded (WTOut, GFP−) versus non-infected, and (3) WTOut versus ΔSPI-1/2Out; and three for intracellular effects: (1) WT invaded (WTIn, GFP+) versus WTOut, (2) ΔsteE invaded (ΔsteEIn, GFP+) versus WTIn, and (3) ΔSPI-2In versus WTIn. We subsetted to colonocytes. Genes detected in at least 50 colonocytes were retained, excluding features with Ensembl-style identifiers beginning with ‘ENSG’. Pairwise comparisons were conducted between predefined experimental conditions within each colonocyte cluster. Comparisons were performed only when at least 20 cells were available in at least one condition. When one of the conditions contained fewer than 20 cells, we supplemented with cells from other clusters of the same condition that were defined as nearest neighbours of the other condition, using get.knnx of the FNN package with k = 1 based on Euclidean distance in the top 20 principal component space. Differential expression testing was performed using the wilcoxauc function of the presto R package on normalized RNA expression values. We identified DEGs between conditions per cluster using the wilcoxauc function by the presto package, with cut-offs adjusted P < 0.05, pct_in > 10 and logFC > 0.1.

We then pooled genes defined as significant DEGs in at least one comparison in at least one cluster as the union set of condition DEGs. We then calculated the colonocyte average expression levels of each condition. We calculated the expression difference between WTOut and non-infected—namely, diff_WT_vs_NI, where positive values indicate upregulation in WTOut versus non-infected—and between WTIn and WTOut—namely, diff_WT_GFP_vs_WT, where positive values indicate upregulation in WTIn versus WTOut. We ranked the gene according to diff_WT_vs_NI, took the top 50 genes as feature genes and used the AddModuleScore function to calculate the luminal effect gene module score, and applied the same procedure for diff_WT_GFP_vs_WT values for intracellular effect gene module score.

We used CellChat (v2)84 to infer intracellular communications between co-cultured intestinal epithelial cells and TRM cells. Normalized expression data were extracted from the epithelium and TRM cell combined Seurat RNA assay, and cell-type labels were used to group cells for interaction analysis. We used the built-in human CellChatDB database as reference for ligand–receptor interactions. To reduce computational cost, we subsetted the expression data to include only signalling genes present in the database. Overexpressed genes and interactions were identified using the identifyOverExpressedGenes and identifyOverExpressedInteractions functions. Communication probabilities were computed using the computeCommunProb function with type = truncatedMean and trim = 0.05. We subsetted to interactions between proximal small intestinal enterocytes and immune cell types, excluding proliferating CD4+ T cells for visualization.

Read alignment, gene expression quantification and cell filtering of the VASA-seq data were performed by Single Cell Discoveries. In brief, raw sequencing data were processed using Cutadapt (v4.9) to remove adapter sequences and homopolymers, followed by Ribodetector (v0.3.1) to filter out ribosomal reads. Processed reads were then aligned to the reference genome using STARsolo (v2.7.11b), with the parameter–soloFeatures set to GeneFull_Ex50pAS. The human reference genome used was GRCh38, ENSEMBL release 103. The S.Tm reference genome was downloaded from EnsemblBacteria Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica serovar Typhimurium str. 14028S str. LT2 14028S (GCA_000022165) release 62. The resulting single-cell-level gene count matrices were used as inputs for downstream analysis in R.

To improve the gene annotation for the S.Tm genome, we performed sequence alignment for the genes in the S.Tm strain 14028S annotation against the S.Tm strain 14028 annotation (GCF_028596185.1_ASM2859618v1). Out of the 1,542 genes with Ensembl-style identifiers beginning with ENSB, 242 were mapped to annotated genes with common names in the S.Tm 14028 annotation by blastn85,86 with more than 90% of sequence similarity.

Cells without GFP signal, UMI count of less than 3,500 or External RNA Controls Consortium (ERCC) counts of less than 450 were excluded. Raw counts were normalized using the NormalizeData function in Seurat with a scale factor of 10,000.

According to FACS index sorting values, cells with GFP < 500 were defined as GFP−, 500 or more and less than 2,000 as GFPlo, 2,000 or more and 10,000 or less as GFPint, and more than 10,000 as GFPhi. For human genes, only genes detected in at least 10% of cells in at least one GFP signal group were included in the differentially expressed test. For each retained gene, expression was modelled using two nested linear models. The reduced model included log-transformed total UMI counts per cell and the percentage of mitochondrial reads as technical covariates, whereas the full model additionally included the GFP signal group as a factor. The contribution of the GFP signal group was assessed by ANOVA comparing the full and reduced models. Genes with Benjamini–Hochberg-adjusted P < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed. To assign directionality, we calculated the difference in average expression between the GFPhi and GFPlo groups and assigned the directionality accordingly.

For S.Tm, we applied the same differentially expressed test strategy, but only included GFP+ cells for comparison. Given the sparsity of the bacteria transcripts, we reduced the detection rate cut-off to 1%. After further excluding ERCC transcripts and features with Ensembl-style identifiers beginning with ENSB, 3,137 genes were left as input for the differentially expressed test. We used nominal P < 0.05 as the cut-off to get marginally significant bacterial DEG.
