Hershey Review: Chocolatier Biopic Is Inoffensively Bland

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Though it has all the ingredients to be a heartwarming movie, Hershey is an all too tame experience, weighed down by its frictionless storytelling.

If you grew up in the US, odds are, you grew up with Hershey chocolate. It's one of those rare few brands that can be used as shorthand for the American national identity. So, it's only natural that Angel Studios, whose "values-based" output seems to represent the intersection of patriotism, Christian faith, and the Hays Code, would be drawn to a movie about its founder. Hershey, from journeyman comedy director Mark Waters, is a pretty standard biopic of Milton S. Hershey that surveys nearly 50 years of his life, from his humble beginnings in Pennsylvania Dutch country through the establishment and growth of his confectionery empire.

It's also quite tame, and not just in terms of its content. Biographical films have the frustrating tendency to be approached like the cinematic equivalent of a layup, and it's true that if they keep to the fundamentals, they offer a reliable viewing experience. Even at their best, though, such aggressive conventionality just lowers the ceiling of what these movies can achieve – and Hershey is not their best. There is a hokey quality to much of the film, stemming from its script but emerging in various other places, that prevents the kind of immersion expected from these stories.

The movie benefits from a general pleasantness of tone, which its target audience will surely appreciate, as well as its recreation of the period setting. Though far less rough around the edges, it offers a similar appeal as Kevin Costner's Horizon films, in that sometimes it's just nice to be transported back to another time through a mythic, Hollywood lens. But Hershey is far too bland for its own good. It takes a potentially inspirational success story and suffocates it with mild storytelling, so that all the opportunities to really feel something drift right on by.

In the film's telling, young Milton Hershey (James Kelly) was truly the product of his parents. His Pennsylvania Dutch mother, who still speaks German to her children at home, is a firm believer in hard, modest work. Her words before sending Milton to work for the local printer: "Playtime is over." His father (Alan Ruck), meanwhile, is the kind of dreamer that will run his family into the ground. He's always blowing money on the next venture, to the extent that his son feels the need to bury his jar of hard-earned pennies on their property. (A piece of dialogue that sums up his business sense: "Unicycles are the future.")

Combine those two people, and you get someone who dreams of building a life as a candy maker, and who will pursue that one dream relentlessly. This is one aspect of Hershey's life that the film captures and is the primary focus of the first third, showing his luck of learning the craft under the kindly Mr. Royer (Richard Kind) as a teen and, once he's a young man (Finn Wittrock), his struggle to open a successful shop of his own. The other areas of focus are Milton's relationship with Kitty (Alexandra Daddario), who would become the love of his life, and their eventual founding of Hershey, Pennsylvania, the company town that would turn his business philosophy into a long-lasting legacy of philanthropy.

There's little tension that isn't resolved soon after it's introduced, or that doesn't resolve in the most straightforward way possible.

It should come as no surprise that The Hershey Company was involved in shaping this movie from the ground up. Hershey includes such blatant acts of brand maintenance as having Milton balk at his wife's suggestion to name their town after him. (He sure got over that hang-up, huh?) But with the tone the movie is going for, and the feel-good nature of Milton's actual story, the unwillingness to critically portray its subject isn't really the problem. His and Kitty's romance is a good example. Their love is sweet, fated, and fairly uncomplicated, but also kinda nice. The way Daddario carries herself in this role is my favorite performance touch of the movie.

Hershey's problem is that its story is just not very well told. Everything required to be heartwarming and inspiring is here on paper, but the script plots out its moments as a straight chain of events, more invested in moving from A to B than building an emotional journey for us to follow. There's little tension that isn't resolved soon after it's introduced, or that doesn't resolve in the most straightforward way possible. It's a kiddie coaster built to deliver Milton S. Hershey facts efficiently.

This recalls another 2026 film I had the chance to review: John Travolta's Propeller One-Way Night Coach, which is more or less a story about being a kid who loved air travel in the early 1960s. There's a pleasant quality to marinating in a rose-colored version of the past, and in the production and costume design that realizes it, but there's little else to experience. Except where Travolta's one-hour oddity ultimately landed on the side of lightly charming, the 110-minute Hershey sticks around long enough to underwhelm.

Hershey releases in theaters nationwide on Wednesday, November 25.
