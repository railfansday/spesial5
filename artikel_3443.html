10 Books Like ‘Dungeon Crawler Carl’ You Should Read Next

There’s nothing quite like Dungeon Crawler Carl, Matt Dinniman’s hilarious, inventive, and surprisingly emotional book series about a man in his underwear forced to fight his way through a livestreamed game show based on dungeon-crawling video games that takes place after an alien takeover of Earth. (Did I mention he's accompanied by his ex-girlfriend’s suddenly talkative cat?)

The premise should not work, but it sure does—and it makes more sense if you know it fits into a popular genre known as "LitRPG," a blend of "literature" and "role-playing game." If you've torn through all eight books released so far and you're looking for more reads that blend surprisingly deep hilarity with tabletop and video game rules (or just similar feels), here are 10 books you should check out.

An isekai story that lands in the sweet spot for DCC fans, He Who Fights with Monsters kicks off with Jason—a completely normal, kind of boring middle manager—waking up in a strange hedge maze, completely nude (even his hair is gone). He has magic that works like a video game, and has to deal with quests that slowly give him clothes (it’s a relief when he finally acquires some pants), force him to survive cannibals, and hunt down various monsters that definitely need killing. It’s got the same “gaming rules” structure as DCC and a similarly absurdist tone, with just the right spice of darkness.

The Cradle series by Will Wight isn’t a LitRPG, and it doesn’t have Carl’s focus on humor, but it’s another progression fantasy with enough game-like elements to be a great match. Lindon is an “unsouled,” incapable of gaining any of the sacred arts and doomed to a life without a future. His lack of power makes Lindon ferociously determined to find an alternative path to power and success, and that’s what drives this story. He has to find creative ways to move forward, and slowly this leads him to a path that just might make him the most powerful person in his world. DCC fans will love the progression and the tone.

One of the key elements of any video game is the respawn—you get killed, but you pop back up to try again, sometimes at the beginning, sometimes at a save point. Django Wexler leverages that mechanic to great effect in How to Become the Dark Lord and Die Trying. Davi has been summoned to a fantasy world to be the hero, the person who defeats the Dark Lord and saves the day—but she’s failed (and died) so many times, she’s fed up, and decides that on this run through, she’s going to switch sides and become the Dark Lord, using all her knowledge gained from endless replays to her advantage. The humor is pitch-black the way DCC fans like it, and the progressive structure is a perfect fit.

Is Princess Donut your favorite part of Dungeon Crawler Carl? Of course she is. In that case, check out Andrew Seiple’s Threadbare series. Threadbare is a stuffed bear brought to magical life in a failed experiment. He’s a foot tall, full of stuffing, and extremely dumb when the story begins. But when he’s rescued by a little girl and plunged into a horrible series of dangers, Threadbare has to find a way to level up. Like DCC, Threadbare starts off leaning into the absurdity and humor, then slowly gains emotional gravitas as the story progresses.

If you play tabletop RPGs, you’ve encountered a Murderhobo or two: A character who solves all their problems by killing—even members of their own party. Luke doesn’t aspire to be a Murderhobo—he just wants to take over the family business and hang with his buddies. But when an invasion threatens the kingdom, everyone over the age of 17 is ordered to be tested for Potential. Luke’s friends all test high and join the Ascender Corps, traveling to different planes for training. Luke tests ... very low, and when he steps through a portal to engage in his training, he finds himself without any guidance or training in a strange, deadly world where his only chance of survival is to murder his way through it. It’s dark, darkly funny, and a progression fantasy like no other.

If you’re looking for a book series that combines the dark, chaotic humor, absurd game mechanics, and surprising emotional depth of Carl, here’s Ryan “Quicksave” Romano, a man who has the unique ability to literally create a save point in his existence that allows him to go back and redo things whenever he dies. Searching for a childhood friend, he becomes embroiled in a violent gang war that lets him make good use of his power—but underneath the snark and the insanity of Ryan’s existence is a surprisingly affecting story of a man who has suffered, and suffered countless tragedies that are definitely taking their toll.

If your fave bit of DCC is Carl himself—a mediocre dude in an extraordinary situation—you should read Dennis E. Taylor’s fantastic Bobiverse books. Bob is a software engineer who hits it big and promptly dies, waking up to discover a century has passed and he’s been repurposed as an AI controlling a space probe searching for new planets for humanity to inhabit. If he fails in his mission (and he’s not the only probe out there), he’ll be turned off for good. Luckily, he can make copies of himself, and he’s pretty smart. The humor here is smart and soaked in pop culture, making it a perfect match for DCC fans.

Want more of Carl’s absurdist humor? Mayor of Noobtown is the LitRPG you’re looking for. Jim dies and wakes up in a video game-esque universe and finds himself stuck in the zone designated for newbies—players with no experience, skill, or hope. The area fell out of the mainline progression mechanic a long time ago, so there doesn’t seem to be a way to gain power, and the monsters are not at all concerned about the unfairness of it all, so Jim has to figure out how to advance in a universe that seems designed to kill him (again).

If you want to lean into the meta side of things, check out Scathing Reviewer, written by an author who goes by the unusual pen name "BananaDragon." Liu Peijin is the author of a sprawling LitRPG web novel, and she’s used to the bad reviews and complaints from readers—and quite combative about it. When her books’ universe blossoms into a very dangerous new reality, she should be perfectly positioned to survive, having written the story in the first place. But her story becoming real also means her insufferable protagonist is real, and all those nasty critics are now passing judgment on her entire existence. It’s a dark, funny takedown of the entire genre itself, and DCC fans will love it.

If you want a story that parallels the dungeon crawling aspect of DCC, Andrew Rowe’s Arcane Ascension series is the ticket. The Serpent Spire is a tower of constantly changing traps, puzzles, and dangerous creatures. If you enter and survive, you emerge with magical powers. Corin Cadence’s brother entered the Serpent Spire years ago and never returned—and now Corin is determined not only to survive, but to make it to the top, where legend says a goddess will grant you whatever you wish. And what Corin wants is his brother back. It’s a literal progression combined with LitRPG rules, game-inspired puzzle-solving, and a complex magic system that fans of Carl will definitely enjoy.
