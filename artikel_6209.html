Inside the 2026 Condé Nast Traveler Points of View Summit

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

On November 12, our ninth annual Points of View Summit brought together trip designers, luxury planners, and on-the-ground experts in New York City for a day exploring the latest in travel. While unrelenting rain poured outside, the atmosphere inside was anything but dreary and gray.

Global editorial director Divia Thani kicked things off with opening remarks on big-picture trends: how American travelers are driving luxury travel; the transfer of wealth to a younger generation; and a shift from spending on products to lived experiences. These overarching themes gave the audience a preview of what would come later in the day on panels and in breakout sessions. From a talk about Hilton Head’s Gullah culture and a run-through of reasons to visit Morocco—yes, this bit was accompanied by a post-lunch tea ceremony—to an exchange on all-inclusives with Hyatt, and a sit-down unpacking food and memory with Antoni Porowski, the day was full of thoughtful exchanges that reminded us why we journey in the first place. After much applause, the summit concluded with a cocktail party featuring wines from San Miguel de Allende and a taste of Lowcountry South Carolina by way of shrimp, grits, and fried green tomatoes—there was lots to celebrate, with a decade of Hilton Head being on our Readers' Choice Awards and its Savannah/Hilton Head International Airport drawing visitors from all over. Below, explore all that went down at our 2026 Points of View Summit.

Our editors discuss next year's editorial coverage themes, including spotlight destinations and how we're tapping into pop culture.

The day kicked off with a roundtable over what’s been on our minds and what'll be on our editorial calendar next year, just in time for our 40th anniversary. Our features director Rebecca Misner led the conversation with Thani, global features director Erin Florio, global executive director of video Dana Kravis, senior features editor Megan Spurrell, and associate editor Charlie Hobbs, who all agreed that a demand for substantive, long-form storytelling is back (though did it ever truly leave?). Spurrell shared an early preview of the now-live Best Places to Go in 2027, including destinations like the Albanian Alps for hiking, Memphis for museum openings, Yunnan for a slew of new restaurants, and more. Kravis spoke about cross-platform stories such as our new video series Made In, exploring craft in a particular destination, and upcoming video iterations of series like On Location, which unpacks beloved film and show locations. On the topic of pop culture, Hobbs highlighted a continued focus on cultural happenings, sporting events, and festivals that we're keeping on our radar as reasons to travel. Ultimately, going into 2027, Florio said, our coverage will explore “what it is that drives us to travel, as opposed to where it is we want to go"—not only the destination for destination's sake, but the experiences and impulses underlying travelers' choices.

Eduardo Schutte and Madison Flager discuss the future of the all-inclusive stay and Hyatt's rapidly growing portfolio.

Next up, commerce director Madison Flager spoke with Eduardo Schutte, Hyatt Inclusive Collection’s commercial SVP, on how all-inclusive stays have evolved and expanded far beyond your typical beachside bubbles to meet globetrotters of all kinds. In an age of optimization, it's only natural that travelers would value the ease and confidence of a bundled vacation, with less time spent planning so that they can enjoy their destination sans stress. Schutte observed that Hyatt Inclusive Collection has grown from two properties in 2013 to over 120 today, with expansion well underway in Europe, the Middle East, Africa, and Asia. “You're going to know exactly how much your vacation is, so it's much more predictable and easier to plan,” Schutte said. Afterwards, one lucky guest won a trip to a Hyatt Inclusive Resort. Perhaps the all-inclusive is a remedy for the times.

Erin Florio helms the conversation on summer travel, from increasing shoulder season trips to less crowded destinations.

We recently unpacked why it seems everyone took a fallcation this year, and spotlighted the year-round appeal of off-season Greece. Though the once-sweltering days of the summer months might be all you’re yearning for as winter fast approaches, increasingly unpredictable weather and overcrowded destinations are shifting and expanding the typical high season. Florio led this panel with Jack Ezon, founder and managing partner of Embark Beyond; Catherine Heald, CEO and founder of Remote Lands; Jade McBride, president of Ted Turner Reserves; Scott Jordan, vice president of sales–East at Delta Air Lines; and Mei Zhang, chairman and founder at WildChina.

To Ezon, it's less about the timing and more about the value of a trip; entry-level Mediterranean rooms can cost $4,000 to $6,000 per night, raising questions about whether the experience delivers value beyond price. Reframing a destination’s appeal beyond one season is key—going to Amalfi in October for wine or December for Christmas markets—as per Jordan, who stressed the value of meeting travelers where they're headed. He noted Delta is extending and adding seasonal service, including earlier flights to Porto and routes to Copenhagen, Sardinia, and Prague. Still, the group wondered whether greater access to Sardinia might relieve pressure on Amalfi and Como or simply congest another destination.

Zhang said her clients aren't choosing between European hotspots at all; they're set on Asia, the choice becoming whether to visit Japan or China. In 2025, China saw sweeping growth in international visitors, which Zhang credited to new visa policies, renewed country perceptions, and a global interest in regional lifestyles. “I just think that's part of wellness. It's drinking hot water, wearing slippers, playing mahjong, being social with family," she said. "A lot of these concepts are promoting China as a way of living, rather than as a destination.”

Katie Mathews and Omolola Campbell on Hilton Head Island's Gullah culture

For a chat presented by Hilton Head Island, senior creative producer Katie Mathews sat down with Omolola Campbell, executive producer of The Spirit We Move With, a short documentary about South Carolina's Gullah community. The Gullah people are the descendants of West Africans who were brought to the Carolinas, Georgia, and Florida, in the 1700s and 1800s for slavery. A sixth-gen Gullah descendant herself, Campbell spoke with Mathews on the importance of leading the narrative and memorializing these stories today. In South Carolina’s case, it’s about hearing from Gullah people themselves. Campbell uses her gift shop on the island, Binya, as a space for educating customers through handwoven sweetgrass baskets, cookbooks, prints, and more. “People say that storytelling is a way of preservation. I like to say conservation, because to preserve something is to put it on a shelf, [but] Gullah culture is still alive and thriving,” Campbell said.

In between panels, Mei Zhang and others tried Moroccan tea.

Hannah Towey and Ms. Siham Fettouhi discuss lesser-known Moroccan cities and itineraries.

After breakout sessions with editors and travel specialists, guests were served Moroccan tea before settling into a chat with news editor Hannah Towey and Ms. Siham Fettouhi, North America executive director of the Moroccan National Tourism Office. Beyond Marrakech and the desert, Fettouhi noted the country's appeal lies in its Atlantic and Mediterranean coastlines and can't-miss surfing spots. "We're talking about a country that offers you the possibility to ski literally in the morning and enjoy sunset in the beach in the afternoon," Fettouhi said. “Morocco has never been this accessible from North America," she continued, mentioning nonstop flights now available from North America through Royal Air Maroc, Air Canada, Air Transat, Delta, and United. And domestic travel is also getting an update, as Morocco's high-speed rail network, Al Boraq, has extensions planned toward Marrakech and Agadir ahead of the 2030 World Cup.

An attendee captures Lale Arikoglu and Antoni Porowski's culinary conversation.

We closed out Points of View with a chat between Lale Arikoglu, director of special projects, and Antoni Porowski, Emmy-winning producer, Queer Eye veteran, and host of National Geographic's Best of the World series, which aired in June. While his other Nat Geo series No Taste Like Home traces family histories through food, this more recent show highlights the people behind well-known destinations' institutions and traditions—think a mustachioed clockmaker at Big Ben's Great Clock or a family-run cheese cave in Paris. “There’s always a beautiful nerd that’s behind this larger-than-life thing that millions of us have had a chance to experience,” Porowski said with a laugh, referencing folks not unlike himself, dedicated to that one experience and sharing it with others.

For Porowski, it's food that has offered a way to connect people with their roots and family histories, inspired in large part by his own journey toward embracing his Polish heritage. While planning a retirement trip for his dad several years ago, Porowski said his dad insisted they visit the Polish mountain village Zakopane. “We tried soups that I hadn't had since I was six or seven years old and got to connect through that,” he recalled. Porowski wrapped up the conversation with a handful of anecdotes from filming No Taste Like Home; a particular favorite was being in Senegal with Issa Rae, where he learned about teraanga, or the concept of generosity and sharing so integral to the West African country.
