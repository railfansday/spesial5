Tokenized Deposits Could Become Blockchain’s Killer Enterprise Use Case

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

A tokenized bond that settles through a conventional bank transfer is only half tokenized. The asset can move in seconds, while the cash leg remains tied to cut-off times, correspondent banks and separate reconciliation systems.

Tokenized deposits present a potential solution. By putting commercial bank funds within the programmable world of digital securities, collateral and trade documentation, the key innovation does not involve digitizing balances, which banks do anyway. Rather, it means making the payment an integral part of the transaction process without having to convert corporate funds into a new kind of private currency.

For this to happen, banks need to overcome a difficult challenge of treating tokens from different banks as a single form of money.

A tokenized deposit is essentially a digital version of a claim by a customer on a commercial bank. In case a company decides to tokenize $1 million of its deposit in the conventional form, a corresponding liability appears on the balance sheet of the bank.

Thus, a tokenized deposit does not have any other reserve pool, as a regular stablecoin would have. This is just money that appears on the bank’s balance sheet. The process of issuance and redemption must remain in sync with the bank’s internal ledger system to ensure that the same balance cannot be used twice.

Such an approach makes it possible to implement identity checks, transfer limitations, and compliance rules as part of wallet permissions. Smart contracts can decline all transactions with addresses not authorized for such transfers or that cross predefined limits.

In the end, this is programmable commercial bank money, not some independent currency.

The advantage of technology becomes evident when the money and the asset are in a suitable programmable environment.

In a classic securities transaction, trading, clearing, and settlement can happen on different systems. Each party updates its database, there is communication among intermediaries, and there is reconciliation to check if the databases are consistent. The security might come first, or the money might be set aside until the securities transfer is complete.

Tokenization can help with delivery-versus-payment using a smart contract. The smart contract checks that the parties have presented the necessary assets, locks them, and executes both transfers as one state transition. Otherwise, no transfer happens.

This pattern could be used for more than just securities. For example, in a trade finance workflow, payment could be executed after an electronic bill of lading has been provided. A manufacturer could pay a supplier if an appropriate system indicated that the goods had been inspected. The property transaction could transfer money only once the title requirements have been satisfied.

Blockchain is not valuable since it holds the information about the payment. Its value lies in providing a state machine where several organizations can perform interdependent actions without having multiple transaction versions.

If both subsidiaries have tokenized deposits with Bank A, transferring money between wallets means changing the ownership of liabilities of one bank only, without any interbank settlement.

A treasury arrangement could sweep excess liquidity, ensure minimum balances of subsidiaries or satisfy an authorized margin call out of regular clearing times.

The more complex transaction happens when a Bank A customer makes payment to a Bank B customer. Transferring the token means that the receiving party has a claim on Bank A but no deposit on Bank B.

There is a solution which involves burning the Bank A token, transferring the reserves between banks and minting a Bank B token for the receiving customer. This process needs to occur atomically, because the payer could end up being debited without crediting the receiver or creating a token before transferring the reserve.

Tokenized reserves of the central bank act as a shared settlement asset for different competing bank tokens. Bank for International Settlements claims that this approach would enable "singleness of money," meaning equal parities between deposits of different banks.

Continuous treasury may be the first major enterprise application. Multinational corporations generally keep liquidity at different banks, subsidiaries and currencies. Despite the total amount of liquidity being sufficient, the required liquidity is often not available in the correct account to make a payment or satisfy a margin call.

Tokenized deposits would enable sweeps, intragroup transfers and rules-based allocation of liquidity outside regular business hours. A treasury contract would ensure that minimum balances are kept, obligations are paid and excess liquidity is transferred without waiting for separate instructions to different banks.

Cross-border payments are another application with strong potential. The current correspondent banking requires payment instructions and accounts to pass through various institutions. Every institution screens the transaction and keeps its own records and may hold funds until the other party provides the necessary information.

Project Agorá of the BIS involves tokenized commercial bank deposits and tokenized central bank reserves on a common multi-currency platform. The project’s 2026 real-value testing covered 17 scenarios, including corporate, interbank and payment-versus-payment transactions. The test showed that the average time from initiation to completion was about 80 seconds, even without integrating with existing core banking and real-time gross settlement systems.

J.P. Morgan, Citi and HSBC place the boundary between blockchain and conventional banking in different locations.

The comparison exposes an unresolved choice. Public-chain deployment offers proximity to tokenized assets. A bank-controlled network simplifies governance. An internal treasury ledger reaches production sooner. None alone solves universal interbank movement.

Placing bank money behind smart contracts creates risks that conventional payment controls were not designed to address. A coding error could lock funds, release them prematurely or apply an incorrect exchange rate across thousands of transactions.

External data creates another weakness. A contract cannot independently determine whether cargo arrived or a borrower satisfied a covenant. It relies on an oracle, API or authorized institution to write that information to the ledger. If the source is compromised, perfectly executed code can still produce the wrong financial result.

Production systems therefore need contract audits, transaction limits, emergency suspension controls and clearly defined upgrade procedures. Banks must also decide which transactions can be reversed and who has the authority to intervene.

Tokenized deposits will be a set of bank-specific solutions unless the institutions decide to share a common approach to identity, messaging and settlement. Tokenization interoperable with ISO 20022 can facilitate the integration of token networks into the existing payments infrastructure. Still, even interoperability of messages does not ensure that a transaction on one ledger will be triggered from another ledger.

Ledgers require ways of cross-ledger finality, asset locking, replay prevention and failover. The issue of irrevocability needs to be addressed legally.

This may seem less sexy than issuing a new token, but it will define whether tokenized deposits will become a useful infrastructure or just another family of blockchain experiments.

Tokenized deposits will matter if banks can make regulated money execute atomically with assets and obligations across institutional boundaries.

If they connect, blockchain could provide something a faster payment API cannot: a shared execution layer in which the asset, payment and conditions binding them settle as one transaction.
