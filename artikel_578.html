8 Best Space Heaters (2026): Tested, Measured, and Mistreated

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

The Best space heaters are winter’s rear guard, the last line of defense against goose bumps and cold feet. Though not a great way to heat a whole home, space heaters can be indispensable for a late-evening movie night, a workbench in a chilly garage, or the home’s odd corners that refuse to stay warm. And if you live in an apartment building where you don’t control the heat, they may be your only way to feel OK in January.

Even so, these little machines do bring about serious safety and reliability concerns, along with a need to be able to parse dodgy and opaque marketing claims. (For example, no matter what the marketing specifies, large space heaters usually aren’t more powerful or more effective than small ones.) So we turned down the thermostat and tested dozens of space heaters to find the ones that’ll warm you up as quickly, quietly, and safely as possible.

Alongside naming the top pick overall—the blessedly balanced and quiet Dreo Whole Room Heater 714—we also pinpointed which heaters are best poised to suit specific needs, from the best bathroom heater and a space heater that doubles as an air purifier to an option that’ll warm your towels. For those on a tight budget, the bare-bones Honeywell 360 Surround often goes on sale.

This Dreo is the best answer to what I’ve been looking for in a space heater ever since I started testing them. I want simple things: the promise of safety, with reliable tip-over and overheat protections; quiet and effective functioning; and the ability to use the full capacity of an American outlet to heat quickly and evenly around the room. I like a remote control, but if I lose it, I still want to be able to use the heater. I also like precise digital-temperature control, with a thermostat that actually works. I also don’t want to hate how the thing looks, considering it’ll be with me all winter.

The counter-spiral grill, similar to competitor Vornado’s models, creates good heat distribution around the room. Oil-filled radiators like this Dragon from De’Longhi are a different beast from the resistive heaters that make up the rest of this list. The Dragon contains a reservoir of temperature-stable, diathermic oil that circulates within the radiator. The oil takes a little while to heat up, meaning it may be an hour before the temperature stabilizes in a room. But once the Dragon achieves this baseline, the large thermal mass means that the heat stays stable and even. There are trade-offs: The device is huge, at 2 feet tall and more than a foot deep, and its entire surface gets quite hot to the touch.

So why would you prefer it to other options? It is utterly silent, for one. Anecdotally, oil-filled radiators seem to dry out the air less than a fan-blowing ceramic. And its stability of heat for long periods makes it a good pick for bedtime. The hot oil inside the radiator keeps emitting heat even after you’ve turned it off, which means you can turn it on an hour before bed to warm up the room, then shut it off before you go to sleep and remain toasty. Additionally, the antifreeze feature makes it a great tool for greenhouses and basements: You can set the Dragon to turn on whenever the ambient temperature reaches 41 degrees Fahrenheit or lower. —Matthew Korfhage

Are Large Space Heaters More Powerful Than Smaller Ones?

Nope! It may seem counterintuitive, but large space heaters don’t necessarily heat any better than smaller options. In fact, most space heaters in the US and Canada deliver about the same amount of heat, regardless of size. Space heaters top out at 1,500 watts, which is the highest safe operating load for a 15-amp household circuit. And aside from some low-power personal heaters here and there, most space heaters available are capable of 1,500 watts.

What’s more, electric space heaters are all pretty much 100 percent efficient at converting that electricity to heat. Whether directly or indirectly, nearly all of that energy eventually becomes heat.

So if space heaters share basically the same power and efficiency capacity, why are some 1,500-watt heaters advertised for small rooms and others for large rooms? This likely has as much to do with marketing as science. Pretty much every ceramic, electric-coil, and positive-temperature-coefficient (PTC) heater has about the same amount of juice and, thus, the same ability to heat a room … at least eventually.

What differs among heaters is how and where the heat gets distributed and how quickly. All other things being equal, a heater's efficiency mostly focuses on whether it puts the heat where you want it to go.

What Are the Different Types of Space Heaters?

All electric space heaters essentially do the same thing: They take electricity off the grid, usually 1,500 watts of it, if you're in the United States, and turn that power into heat.

The vast majority of space heaters, including ceramic heaters and PTC heaters, are electric-resistance heaters. This means they run an electrical current through something that creates resistance: maybe an electric coil or maybe a specially designed ceramic. This quickly turns electricity into heat.

Most space heaters use a fan to disperse this heat out into the world, where we all live. These forced-air convection heaters tend to be the fastest at quickly raising the temperature of the surrounding air. The air then heats you. While this variety heats quickly, it can take quite a bit of electricity to warm an entire room.

Oil-filled radiators, in contrast, heat oil that circulates inside the device, which then emits heat to the surrounding room. These tend to heat up more slowly than electric-resistance heaters, but they offer even, lasting heat and are very quiet. These radiators operate on the same principle as steam radiators in buildings with a boiler room. And just like steam radiators, they can get hot to the touch.

Radiant heaters, such as infrared heaters, operate much like the sun or a campfire: Rather than heat the air, they directly heat objects or people by radiating energy in their direction. Infrared devices heat the air only indirectly, via the objects they heat. The glow is immediately palpable as warmth, but you’ll need to be in the path of the radiation to feel it, much like the feeling of being in direct sunlight versus the shade. Likewise, the thermometer might not immediately register the warmth you perceive. Infrared heaters tend to be superior for providing warmth without expending much energy, making this type common in large, lofty spaces or outside on a patio.

Though they deliver heat differently, both infrared and oil-filled heaters are subject to the same wattage limitations as electrical-resistance heaters and share efficiencies.

The same is not true of heat pumps, a newer technology that’s expected to provide the next generation of household heat. Heat pumps don’t use energy to create heat. Instead, they move heat energy from one place to another. Accordingly, they require far less energy than other space heater types. But household systems are costly to install, and while more affordable window and portable heat pumps are ramping up, they’re not yet broadly available.

Space heaters are much safer than they used to be. Modern space heaters are heavily regulated, especially regarding exposed heating elements and automatic shutoff switches to prevent overheating. But all heating devices require caution, and so do all devices that pull a lot of energy over long periods.

The vast majority of residential heating fires start with actual fire—specifically in fireplaces and fuel burners. But portable heaters, including electric space heaters, accounted for more than an average of 1,600 fires per year in the US between 2020 and 2022, according to the United States Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC). These fires were associated with an average of 70 deaths and 150 injuries annually during that time period.

So, treat your space heater with the caution and wild skepticism that it deserves. See WIRED's Guide to Space Heater Safety for a full rundown of how not to start fires or cause other hazards with the machines. That guide follows recommendations from federal experts at the U.S. Fire Administration (USFA) and the CPSC, as well as the Association of Home Appliance Manufacturers (AHAM).

Don’t leave a space heater unattended. "Unattended” includes sleeping, y’all! Might we suggest a wonderful down comforter instead? When possible, safety experts also recommend you don't leave a portable heater running unattended in a room with a baby, nor a person with mobility issues.

Plug your heater directly into the outlet. Don't use extension cords or power strips with space heaters, lest you build up too much electrical load and risk a fire.

Don’t plug another appliance into the same outlet or circuit as the space heater.

Place a space heater only on the floor or on a level surface. Heat rises. You don't need to prop up a heater on an unstable chair or table. The exception is the rare heater designed to be mounted on a wall.

Keep your heater at least 3 feet from flammable objects. This includes bedding, drapes, furniture, clothes, papers, and books.

Keep space heaters away from water and dampness, whether in bathrooms or in moist basements prone to dripping or flooding.

Read the manual. Manufacturers have good advice.

Testing space heaters involves answering a few key questions: How safe is it? How loud is it? How quickly and evenly can it heat a room?

During intensive testing of dozens of space heaters, I lived for weeks in a home with the thermostat set to a chilly 65 degrees Fahrenheit.

I tested how well and quickly each device heated a medium-size room (14 by 14 feet), and also how well it heated a small seating area in a larger, open-format space. To test each heater’s thermostat and also measure the evenness of heating, I placed thermometers at three or more locations in each room, including behind the heater.

I gauged the loudness of each device with a decibel meter at 3 feet away and verified power usage with a wattmeter. To understand whether the heater’s exhaust put out painful levels of heat, I used an infrared thermometer to collect precise surface temperatures. When in doubt, I put my own fingers on the line.

Finally, I tested safety features and basic durability. I looked at independent safety certifications, of course. But I also rudely knocked over each device to verify how its tip-over protections functioned and confirmed that the heater started working again when set back upright.

To test overheat protection, I smothered each device with an old sheet to see whether the device shut itself off or adjusted its power output to keep internal temperature low. In the event that the heater turned off, I then checked how soon it could turn back on, if it ever did. (Some heaters gave up, in permanent complaint!)

If any device fails the tip-over or overheat tests, I don’t recommend it. If it’s significantly louder than 50 decibels at 3 feet, I don’t recommend it. If the heater breaks during routine operation, I don’t recommend it. Maddening rattles and squeaks are also disqualifying.

I continually test my favorite models to track how they hold up over time and subject new heaters to this testing regimen to see if they can oust the current top picks.
