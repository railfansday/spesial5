Laurent Correia on the Crucial Difference Between Earning Wealth and Holding Onto It

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

Laurent Correia treats making money and keeping it as two separate skills. After years in business and Dubai property, he says the second one took him much longer to learn.

A good month in business feels like a turning point. Invoices get paid, sales pick up, and plans that looked a bit fanciful a quarter ago start to seem doable. Then a few months pass, and you’re under the same pressure to keep earning as before.

Often the issue isn’t how much came in. Each rise in income tends to bring a new commitment, whether that’s a bigger team or higher personal spending, and by the end there’s little left to show for the good stretch.

Laurent Correia describes making money and keeping it as two different skills. Having worked across business ventures and property investment, he says the second took him longer to learn.

Revenue is the number most business owners watch because it’s right there. A completed sale proves a customer was willing to pay, and a few strong months make it tempting to decide the idea has found its audience.

It leaves a lot out, though. Operating expenses, commitments you’ve already made, and the cost of simply keeping the business running all come out of that figure before anything is left over. You can run a growing operation and still have very little room to move.

Correia’s distinction gives you a second question to ask after a sale: what has this income made possible beyond the sale itself? You don’t need a complicated model to answer it. Could the business get through a quiet month? Could it handle an unexpected bill without panic? Can the owner make a decision without waiting for the next payment to land?

None of that shows up in turnover, but it tells you a lot more about whether things are getting better. Plenty of owners earn more every year and still feel just as stretched because the extra money goes out as fast as it comes in.

Property became central to Correia’s longer-term thinking, with Dubai at the center of that activity. He describes the move toward owning assets as a major stage in going from entrepreneur to investor.

What stands out to me is the change in timescale. Running a business mostly means reacting to what’s in front of you this week. Deciding what the proceeds should pay for over the next several years is a different kind of thinking, and it raises questions that day-to-day trading never does.

The answers depend on the person. The SEC’s guide to asset allocation says your choices should depend on how long you have before you need the money and how much risk you can stomach. Both of those are personal.

Correia’s path shows how one person worked it out, but your situation isn’t his, so it won’t copy across cleanly. What you can take from it is a habit: let the future shape what you do now. Words like freedom or security don’t mean much until you pin them to something.

For instance, how many months of bills you’d want covered, or what you’d like to do without worrying about it. Write that down, and you’ve got something to plan around.

Correia says becoming a father changed what success means to him. These days his ambition is tied up with freedom, security and what he can leave his children.

That makes earning versus keeping money feel personal. A target carries more weight when it’s attached to something you want your work to pay for, and a bare number usually isn’t.

Try it on your own last good stretch. Set the income figure aside and ask what actually got better. You might have more breathing room, or rely less on one large customer, or find you can plan further out than before.

If nothing comes to mind, that tells you something as well, and it’s a decent starting point for your next goal. Instead of just aiming higher, ask what a good year ought to leave you with.

The next sale will always need attention, and so will whatever you do with the money after it.
