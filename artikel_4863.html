Why Reconciliation Fails (and What Actually Makes It Work)

It seems family estrangement is showing up everywhere lately on social media. In particular, we see numerous headlines of adult children going “no contact." And, to some extent, the statistics support this observation.

A 2024 Harris Poll showed about half of American adults are estranged from at least one close relative, with 35 percent estranged from an immediate family member, such as a parent or sibling. Cornell sociologist Karl Pillemer estimates that 67 million Americans are estranged and that more than 4 in 10 will experience it at some point in their lives.

With this growing reality, reconnection often becomes a focus for one or both parties after estrangement, raising two very important questions. Firstly, why does family reconciliation so often break down, and secondly, what helps reconciliation succeed?

If you're estranged and searching for a way to reconnect, you may be feeling and carrying around a particular type of hope. Despite previous unsuccessful attempts to close the gap, you picture it going differently this time. You work to convince yourself that if you could only find the right words, everything would be fixed. But it isn’t.

After more than a decade of working in this field, I've discovered that words might not matter as much as we think they do. What actually determines how these conversations go is whether two people can stay emotionally present when words start to hurt, and to stay open, as opposed to shutting down or exchanging salvos, when things get tough.

What I see is that when one person describes your behavior in a way that’s painful to hear, you feel discomfort. In many cases, your body reacts instantly, before your mind has a chance to process the words. The other person hasn’t even finished their sentence, and you're already overwhelmed with the urge to respond, in all likelihood, with a counterattack. And most of the time, it doesn't feel like defensiveness from the inside. It probably feels like protecting yourself.

However, for the person who has worked up the courage to say the hard, painful thing, the message becomes that being honest isn't safe. And, therefore, the relationship isn't safe. And, until it is, repair cannot happen.

This is where I see many reconciliation attempts falter and stall. The fact that it’s even attempted underscores something incredibly important. Someone cares enough to give it a try. The unfortunate reality is that the well-intentioned bid for reconnection fails because of an inability to hear the other’s experience without the need to defend, deny, or deflect. This is at the heart of the problem. Only when it’s recognized and addressed can a space for genuine reconnection be created.

Let me give you an example. A woman had chosen estrangement from her mother. Over time, her mother would reach out periodically. Each time, conversations started like an apology but quickly disintegrated into explanations that felt like justifications. Recollections only accommodated one set of experiences, the mother’s. Following each conversation, the daughter felt like nothing had changed. Each failed attempt increased the gap even further.

In order to make sense of this recurring pattern, the daughter turned to therapy and, through that process, became aware that she’d allowed her own healing to become dependent on a mother who might never want to or be able to hear her in the way she needed.

She longed for her mother to listen, acknowledge her childhood experiences, and take responsibility. But, repeatedly, that craved-for moment remained painfully elusive. So, she decided to stop waiting for something external that might never come and started healing anyway.

When the next reconnection attempt eventually happened, there was little to no expectation. Once she let go of needing her mother to own the past, things changed. She still received the same excuses, and the defensiveness remained, but now the daughter viewed both in a different light. She saw armor that had always been there. Armor that, importantly, she came to realise, had nothing to do with her.

In reality, the relationship remained far from what the daughter really longed for. For years, she’d hoped her mother would turn up to the conversation without armor and had weighted her expectations accordingly. Ultimately, it had become clear to her that her mother might never be capable of staying emotionally present and open. The only way to break the deadlock was to be a different daughter.

In making that decision, she and her mother embarked on a new relationship, one where she had created her own conditions for safety. One where she was emotionally present and open, and where, little by little, repair became possible.

Strangely, this isn't unusual. Research from the Family Estrangement & Reconciliation Project found this pattern spanning hundreds of successful reconciliations. Often, the people who rebuilt their relationships weren't the ones who finally got the listening, recognition, and understanding they'd been waiting for. They were the ones who embraced the importance of letting go of never-to-be-met expectations from an irreconcilable shared past.

So, on one hand, reconciliation could be seen as an attempt to reclaim and redirect the past. On the other hand, it can also become the foundation for something new and different. And, with the latter of these two possibilities, it doesn’t mean saying everything that happened in the past was fine. Nor does it mean that one’s experiences are unimportant. It simply means that one can finally put down things that should never been carried in the first place, so that healing and growth can happen.

I don't talk about this from theory alone. I have been estranged from a parent myself. I know how long the road back to rebuilding a stable relationship can be.

It might seem counterintuitive, but wherever you are in your journey, travel as lightly as possible. Carry fewer expectations, explanations, and justifications. Before ever considering the words you might use to try and bridge the gap between you and an estranged loved one, take a look at what you’re bringing with you.

To what extent the other person meets you halfway is largely beyond your control. That said, turning up to the conversation armed with a deeper degree of self-awareness, emotional grounding, and a commitment to healing that isn’t conditional on what the other person does or doesn’t do can often bring reconciliation within reach.
