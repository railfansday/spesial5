Strength-Train Your Hair

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Can the slew of products designed to fortify weakened strands really made a difference? Dermatologists and beauty scientists detail what to look for when you’re shopping the haircare aisle

WE’D ALL LIKE to imagine our hair as strong, resilient, healthy. And there’s no shortage of products marketed toward reaching that end point. You’ve got your bond-building treatments, your protein masks, your strengthening serums—heck, there are now entire product lines geared toward promoting robust, rugged strands.

But what exactly does it mean to have ‘strong’ hair?

We tend to equate strength with the kind of glossy, thick, bouncy strands you'd see in a shampoo commercial. But there’s more to the science than that. For starters, hair that looks healthy isn't necessarily strong, and hair that's naturally fine or thinning isn't automatically weak. Plus, there's a difference between making hair “feel” stronger and actually improving its ability to withstand breakage.

Meet the experts: Sherry Maragh, MD, is a board-certified dermatologist based in Virginia. Nathan Puksta is the founder and CEO of FILAMENT Sciences, a haircare brand focused on the science of stronger hair.

So, what counts as strong hair, and can all these products really deliver on their promises? We asked two experts to explain.

Scientifically speaking, hair strength is all about resilience, meaning how well an individual strand withstands stress from brushing, stretching, styling, and other everyday activities without breaking. That strength largely comes from two parts of the hair shaft: the cuticle, which acts as its protective outer layer, and the cortex, which contains keratin proteins responsible for much of its structural integrity, explains Sherry Maragh, MD, a board-certified dermatologist based in Virginia.

Another important factor is elasticity, or the ability of hair to stretch and return toward its original shape without snapping. One of the most useful measurements is tensile strength, which quantifies how much force a strand can withstand before breaking, says Nathan Puksta, the founder and CEO of FILAMENT Sciences, which creates products and ingredients dedicated to the science of stronger hair. Think of it as the difference between a brittle piece of dry spaghetti and a flexible rubber band.

Tensile strength is an important part of this conversation because it’s something that laboratories can actually measure, using calibrated equipment that pulls individual hair fibers until they break. Other tests evaluate elasticity, breakage, and resistance to combing. But strength isn't synonymous with thickness, density, or shine. "Hair can absolutely be strong while still being fine, thin, or less reflective," says Dr. Maragh. "Similarly, hair that appears thick and glossy is not necessarily resistant to breakage." In other words, those naturally fine strands might be remarkably resilient, while locks that epitomize the “glass hair” trend could be surprisingly fragile.

Unfortunately, the things we regularly do to make our hair look good can also compromise its strength. Puksta identifies five major contributors to weakened hair: chemical processing, mechanical damage, heat, environmental exposure, and aging.

So, can you actually make your hair stronger? Yes, but there's an important distinction between improving damaged hair's performance and returning it to its original, undamaged condition. "The visible hair shaft is made up of nonliving cells, so damaged hair cannot biologically heal in the way skin does," says Dr. Maragh.

That doesn't mean strengthening products are useless. Certain ingredients can improve hair's physical properties, reduce friction, reinforce weakened areas, and help prevent additional breakage, with some technologies even working beneath the cuticle. Puksta explains that bond-building treatments target chemical interactions within hair, while certain peptides and proteins can help reinforce its keratin structure. However, effectiveness varies considerably between formulations.

Dr. Maragh cautions that an ingredient's ability to penetrate the hair shaft doesn't automatically prove that it repairs structural damage. So, what can different strengthening products realistically accomplish?

And remember that a product claiming 80 percent stronger hair isn't necessarily saying what you think. Dr. Maragh recommends examining what was measured, the comparison group, and whether results came from laboratory testing or consumer feedback. Here's the good news: You don't need naturally thick, glossy hair to have strong hair. And if your strands are damaged, there are meaningful ways to improve their resilience. The key is understanding what products can—and cannot—accomplish. A conditioner that reduces breakage through easier detangling is valuable, even if it doesn't rebuild internal bonds. Likewise, a treatment that improves measurable tensile strength isn't necessarily reversing every aspect of previous damage (translation: it can’t recreate virgin hair).

Ultimately, maintaining strength is as much about preventing damage as treating it. Consistent conditioning, gentler styling practices, and choosing products suited to your hair type can help protect existing strands while healthy new hair grows.

And that's worth remembering the next time you see a bottle that promises stronger hair: The goal is helping the strands withstand whatever you put them through—not just have shampoo commercial-worthy hair.
