Two decades of tracking data on Manx shearwaters shows the seabirds actually get jet lag just like humans do

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

GPS tracks of 1,045 Manx shearwaters over 20 years show fast eastward flights leave their body clocks up to an hour behind, bending their sun compass.

Manx shearwaters from Skomer Island, off the Pembrokeshire coast of Wales, sometimes fly about 621 miles (1,000 kilometers) west into the Atlantic to feed. On the flight back east, some go fast enough to get a kind of jet lag.

The sun comes up earlier the farther east a bird is. After a long day flying east, a shearwater’s body clock can run up to an hour behind.

Oxford biologists saw the effect in the birds’ tracks. The steering errors went up to about 15 degrees. The team does not yet know if the lag does the birds any other harm.

Lewin and 23 co-authors published the paper in Science on October 29, 2026. It used 20 years of GPS data from 1,045 Manx shearwaters on 3,688 foraging trips.

Professor Tim Guilford, a co-author, belongs to the Oxford Navigation Group, which works mostly on Skomer and on Lighthouse Island in County Down.

The group’s GPS loggers were first designed for geotagging photos on backpacking trips, its project page says. Team members take them apart and wrap them in heat-shrink plastic.

The birds came from seven colonies. The R code the team put on Zenodo lists Skomer, Skokholm, Ramsey, Lundy, Copeland, Rum and St Kilda. Skomer has nearly 350,000 breeding pairs, the Wildlife Trust of South and West Wales says.

In the code, every track was cut down to one GPS fix every five minutes. A trip home only counted if it started at least 31 miles (50 kilometers) out. It also had to end at least 25 percent closer.

Birds that use the sun as a compass also need to know the time. The sun moves across the sky all day. Normally they set their body clock at sunrise, the University of Oxford said.

A drawing released with the paper shows how this goes wrong. In it, a shearwater starts for home at 30 degrees west. Its clock matches local time there.

Six hours later the bird is at 15 degrees west, where the sun came up an hour sooner. The sun now sits about 20 degrees clockwise of where the bird expects it. So the bird turns about 20 degrees clockwise of its proper heading.

Birds in the GPS records drifted clockwise as they flew east. Dr. Patrick Lewin of Oxford’s Department of Biology, who led the work, said that is the pattern a clock running late would produce.

For each fix, the code works out where the sun would be from the spot where the bird saw sunrise that day. Crosswind and distance from the colony go into the models as well.

Magnetic values for every fix came from the International Geomagnetic Reference Field, via a program called geomag70. The sun model beat the magnetic one.

In Japan, streaked shearwaters were used to study how birds rely on an inner compass tied to magnetic sensing.

Lewin said it is “amazing to think that billions of migratory animals look up to the sun for direction.”

The code also checks whether a straight line from each fix to the colony crosses land.

A comment in the code says the team had to leave out points around St. David’s Head for Skomer and Skokholm. Without that step, every point to the north would count as having no clear line home because of a small stretch of land.

Along coasts the birds kept making the same errors, flying nearer the shore when the predicted error pointed toward land and farther out when it pointed out to sea.

Guilford, a co-author, said the thinking of wild animals is hard to study. Measuring their natural movements carefully, he said, “can reveal the underlying behavioral mechanisms.”

The team worked out one case for an adult bird. It started 621 miles (1,000 kilometers) out with an average error of 7 degrees. It corrected its course all the way.

That bird would fly only about 3.1 miles (5 kilometers) extra before getting within 3.1 miles of its colony.

A young bird on its first migration may not correct like that, Lewin’s group said. Its errors could add up to hundreds of kilometers. Caspian terns do not fly that first trip alone, because fathers lead young birds on their first migration.

Manx shearwaters spend the northern winter off Argentina, according to the Oxford group. Another Oxford-led study reported that the endangered Balearic shearwater changed its migration range mostly through individual flexibility.

People who fly fast across time zones get disturbed sleep and fatigue, according to the Oxford statement, and jet lag can also unsettle the gut and other organs.

The statement says more research is needed on whether wild birds suffer any effects on health, behavior or survival. The hundreds of kilometers for young birds came from a calculation.

Lewin wants to find out what the lag does to them. “Do they mistime critical behaviors? Do they experience physiological side effects like we do?” he said in the University of Oxford statement.

Lewin also said the shearwaters, which make long flights all the time, might one day help people deal with jet lag. For now, they keep racing an earlier sunrise back to Skomer.

The full study was published in the journal Science.
