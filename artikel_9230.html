Before comparing your balance to anyone else's, you need to know what the benchmarks actually include. Most retirement savings data from Vanguard, Fidelity, and the Federal Reserve covers:

What these numbers typically exclude: taxable brokerage accounts, real estate equity, HSA balances invested for retirement, pension values, and Social Security. For FI-minded savers, this distinction matters enormously — your total investable net worth is almost certainly larger than your "retirement savings" as defined by these surveys.

The gap between average and median retirement savings is one of the most important distinctions in personal finance. The average gets pulled upward by high-balance accounts — a single person with $5 million in their 401(k) dramatically inflates the average for their age group. The median (the middle number when all balances are ranked) gives you a far more honest picture of what a typical American actually has saved.

Notice how the gap widens with age. By 65+, the average is 3.3 times the median — meaning a small number of disciplined savers (or high earners) are pulling the average far above what most people actually have. The median tells you what a typical person in each age group actually holds. If you are above the median, you are doing better than half of Americans your age. But "better than average" and "on track for a secure retirement" are very different standards.

Vanguard's annual "How America Saves" report and Fidelity's quarterly retirement analysis provide the most comprehensive snapshots of what Americans hold in their workplace retirement accounts. These numbers reflect defined-contribution plan balances only — primarily 401(k) and 403(b) accounts.

Average balance: $13,265. Median balance: $5,400. Most savers in this bracket have been contributing for fewer than five years. The biggest advantage this group has is time — a 25-year-old contributing $6,000 per year with a 7% average return will accumulate over $1.2 million by age 65 without ever increasing their contribution. The FI community emphasizes that getting started early matters far more than starting with large contributions.

Fidelity's guideline of 1x salary by 30 and 10x salary by 67 is a reasonable starting point for traditional retirement planning. But for FI seekers, these benchmarks are the floor, not the ceiling. The ChooseFI community consistently demonstrates that saving 40-60% of income — rather than the national average of 4.6% — compresses the timeline from 40 working years down to 10-17.

If your balance is below the median for your age group, you are not alone — and you are not stuck. The math of compound growth means that aggressive action in your 40s and 50s can still produce a secure retirement or even an early one.

The IRS provides additional contribution room for savers age 50 and older. In 2026, the 401(k) catch-up contribution is $7,500 on top of the standard $24,500 limit, for a total of $31,000. IRA catch-up contributions add $1,000 to the standard $7,000 limit. HSA catch-up for those 55+ adds another $1,000. Combined with employer matching, a 50-year-old couple can potentially shelter $80,000 or more per year in tax-advantaged accounts.

Most financial advice focuses on investment returns, but the variable you control most directly is your savings rate. Moving from a 10% savings rate to a 30% savings rate has a dramatically larger impact on your retirement timeline than chasing an extra 2% of investment return. A household earning $120,000 that increases from 10% to 30% savings — a shift of $24,000 per year — adds roughly $600,000 to their retirement balance over 15 years (assuming 7% average returns).

The FI community's core insight is that savings rate is the primary driver of financial independence. At a 50% savings rate, you can reach FI in roughly 17 years regardless of income level. At 60%, it compresses to about 12 years. At 70%, you are looking at under 10 years. These numbers are independent of salary — they depend only on the ratio of savings to spending.

The average American contributes 4.6% of salary to their 401(k), receives a 3-4% employer match, and saves little outside of that. FIRE-path savers typically max every available tax-advantaged account, maintain savings rates of 40-60%, invest in low-cost index funds, and direct overflow savings into taxable brokerage accounts. The result: FIRE savers in their 40s commonly have $500,000 to $1.5 million in total investable assets — 5 to 10 times the national median for their age group. The difference is not income; many FIRE success stories come from median-income households that simply saved aggressively and avoided lifestyle inflation.

Most employers allow you to change your 401(k) contribution rate at any time. Increasing by 2% of salary is small enough that you will barely notice the paycheck reduction, but it compounds dramatically over time. Repeat this increase every six months until you are maxing out your 401(k).

Roughly one in four employees leaves free employer match money on the table. If your employer matches 50% of contributions up to 6% of salary, you need to contribute at least 6% to capture the full match. This is an instant 50% return on your money — no investment can beat it.

A Roth IRA provides tax-free growth and tax-free withdrawals in retirement, with no required minimum distributions. The 2026 limit is $7,000 ($8,000 if 50+). If your income exceeds the Roth limits, use the backdoor Roth conversion strategy to contribute indirectly.

A common guideline is 10-12x your pre-retirement income. For a household earning $100,000, that means $1,000,000 to $1,200,000 in retirement savings. The 4% rule suggests you can withdraw 4% of your portfolio annually with a high probability of not running out over 30 years — so $1 million supports roughly $40,000 per year in withdrawals.

It is never too late, but the urgency increases with age. A 40-year-old saving $20,000 per year at 7% returns will accumulate roughly $1 million by age 65. A 50-year-old has access to catch-up contributions ($7,500 extra in a 401(k)) and can still build $400,000 to $600,000 in 15 years with aggressive saving. The key is maximizing savings rate immediately rather than trying to make up ground through risky investments.

The average retirement savings benchmarks are useful directional guides, but they should not define your target. Most Americans are dramatically undersaved — the median 401(k) balance at retirement age is under $72,000, which supports only about $2,860 per year in withdrawals using the 4% rule. That is not a retirement plan; it is a supplement to Social Security at best. The FI community takes a fundamentally different approach: define your actual annual spending, multiply by 25, and work backward to a savings rate that gets you there. Whether your number is $750,000 or $3 million, the path is the same — maximize tax-advantaged accounts, invest in low-cost index funds, maintain a high savings rate, and let compound growth do the heavy lifting. If you are behind the benchmarks, the single most impactful action is increasing your savings rate, not chasing higher returns.
