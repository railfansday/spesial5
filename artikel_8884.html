What Happens When Grief Derails Your Triathlon Training

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Rachel Olson wins 2026 Ironman 70.3 São Paulo after struggling to train through the grief of losing her mother in November 2024. (Photo: Romulo Cruz/Ironman)

In late September, Rachel Olson crossed the finish line first at Ironman 70.3 São Paulo, nearly 10 minutes ahead of her nearest competitor. The race, by her own admission, was “not spectacular” for the longtime pro triathlete. But the support that followed was. The crowd embraced her, and athletes and fans who had followed her story cheered a little louder, as though they knew what it took just to get her to the start line.

Because in the months after her mother Janis died in November 2024 of liver disease, Olson could barely bring herself to work out. She did not want to disappear from triathlon. But she’d go to the pool and just sit on the edge and cry. She’d hop on her bike and feel a sudden urge to turn around. Running, always an easy way to manage big emotions, became a chore.

Then her body began to rebel: digestive issues, mysterious inflammation, bone-deep exhaustion, muscles that wouldn’t recover.

“My body was basically screaming at me that something was wrong, like it was constantly fighting something,” said Olson, 35, who lives in Boulder with her fiancé, fellow pro triathlete Andre Lopes. “Your mind is telling you to process, and your body is telling you to slow down.”

For endurance athletes, the instinct after a loss is often to train through the emotional pain. The adage goes that exercise helps with grief: Get outside, move your body, clear your head. Sometimes it does. But for Olson and many others, the very thing that once gave life structure can become one more demand on a body and mind already stretched thin.

“Grief doesn’t just live in the mind. It’s in the chest, in the restlessness, in this low-grade energy that has nowhere obvious to go,” said Kenza Haddock, president and co-founder of Oceanic Counseling Group in Murrells Inlet, South Carolina. “Endurance sport can tip from processing grief into outrunning it, using exhaustion to stay ahead of the feeling rather than move through it. It can just leave you more numb.”

Plenty of endurance athletes have turned grief into a mission. In August 2026, Matthew Hollingham, a 25-year-old doctoral student at Penn State, created an “Arctic Circle to Africa” triathlon in memory of his late fiancée Lovisa Arnesson-Cronhamre, who was killed in a hit-and-run while jogging on campus. Over 61 days and 3,545 miles, he swam in a lake above the Arctic Circle, ran a marathon a day for 37 days, and cycled just over 110 miles a day for 23. He raised more than $40,000 for two charities and kept her memory present.

Others have taken similar routes; at the 2026 London Marathon, Laura Coleman-Day, a 33-year-old mother from Lincolnshire in the U.K., went viral for changing into her wedding dress and running the final miles in it as a dedication to her late husband, Xander.

This kind of public mission can be cathartic, says Hilary Bucell, a psychotherapist and lecturer at Columbia University.

“Engaging in something like triathlon can become a ritualized space where the recognition of the loss is welcomed and at the same time, the sport’s necessity for present moment awareness also helps athletes acknowledge new life experiences continuing now beyond the loss,” she said.

Of course, racing to honor a loved one doesn’t erase grief — it just gives you a place to put it. It can also offer a healthy distraction from the deep ache of a devastating loss.

Some aren’t able to navigate their grief in such a public and profound way. There are those like Olson whose grief hits the body hard enough to derail training. In 2023, Gustav Iden, the 2022 Ironman World Champion, ended his season early to heal and regroup after the death of his mother, revealing “it was very emotional, and it was hard to find joy in training after that.” (He returned to racing about a year later.)

As one of her mother’s main caretakers, Olson spent two years before her death flying to her hometown in Texas for doctor’s appointments. Triathlon took a backseat: She parted ways with her coach and scaled back her training. Even when she did have time to work out, anxiety followed.

“I would think, ‘Am I going to be out on a bike ride and get a phone call saying she’s in the hospital? Are things going to go downhill?’”

After Janis died, that anxiety turned physical. Olson was never diagnosed with one specific issue, but testing showed elevated inflammation markers and low ferritin, requiring an iron infusion.

“Ultimately, I think my body was responding to trauma. It felt like I had an illness,” she said. “Trying to perform in a body that felt completely foreign to me was incredibly difficult. I didn’t understand why my body wasn’t doing what it had done before.”

Research suggests that grief can affect the body as well as the mind. The National Institutes of Health (NIH) lists impaired immune response and increased mortality from heart disease, especially in older, widowed patients, among the physical complications. The effects may vary, as not everyone experiences the same physical response.

Part of the explanation may lie in how the brain processes the loss of a loved one through interconnected systems involved in emotion, threat detection, and the regulation of stress hormones. Grief can flood the body with inflammatory signals. and increase cortisol, the steroid hormone produced by the adrenal glands that regulates the body’s response to stress.

“Grief is a neurological event that impacts your ability to learn and remember things, attend to things, and impacts your mood,” said Wilfred van Gorp, a Manhattan-based board-certified neuropsychologist. “Losing someone keeps your amygdala on high alert and elevates cortisol. It impacts you in your body as well as your mind.”

Grief and stress can also disrupt communication between the brain and the gut, which may contribute to everything from irritable bowel syndrome to acid reflux to ulcers, according to research.

For Olson, whose stomach swelled within days of her mother’s death, that brain-gut link was obvious.

“Only two days after [she passed], I woke up, and my stomach was distended about five inches. And then I kind of exploded in inflammation weight,” she shared. “Trying to get that down is not an easy process.”

Still, Olson pushed on. She’d qualified for her first Ironman World Championship slated for about 10 months after Janis died. She wanted to race there to honor her mom. She also didn’t want to lose her identity as a triathlete. Her training “wasn’t even close” to peak levels. In Kona, her stomach rebelled on the bike. After vomiting for three hours, she pulled out soon after starting the run.

“I held onto triathlon so tightly even though my body wasn’t ready. My mind wasn’t ready,” Olson said. “I kept pushing because I wanted it so badly. I attached too much weight to needing training [and racing] to go well and needing something in my life to be OK. Looking back, I realize I didn’t have to be OK.”

After Kona, Olson took a break. She focused on her other jobs as a triathlon coach and as a sales rep, and on envisioning what her wedding to Lopes could look like without her mom.

“I decided I wasn’t going to think about triathlon for a couple of weeks,” she shared. “If I still wanted it afterward, great. If I didn’t, that was OK, too.”

Spoiler: She still loved it. With the fog lifted, Olson saw a different future in the sport, starting with a new coach, Kasper Pedersen, who could build a sustainable load and understand her fragile state.

“I looked for a coach who understands that life happens,” said Olson. “Kasper is great at helping me push my boundaries, but he also knows that I may need to go at my own pace.”

They’re keeping her training volume relatively low as she re-establishes a baseline and repairs her frayed central nervous system.

“I really wanted to make my body and mind think, ‘This is fun. This is helping us,’ rather than setting off alarm bells every time I went out,” Olson shared. “There are still days where I have to take some time off. … Grief really is a roller coaster. But overall, we’ve done a really good job of figuring out what is working.”

Talking about it helps, too, though it doesn’t come easily. Olson describes her grief as a “cloud over her personal life,” and opening up about it has cost her: One friend drifted away, unable to understand what she was going through. Olson wonders if she was simply too much to handle. Still, she finds solace in speaking out, and in being a voice for others who may be grieving alone.

“As athletes, we want to put difficult things in the back of our minds and keep going,” she said. “But I’m glad I can be a voice for other people who are going through the same so they feel less alone than they think they are.”

If her recent win in Brazil is any sign, Olson is rounding a bend toward healing, with an approach to triathlon (and life) that’s less about control and more about leaning into the process, however unpredictable and messy it may be.

“I feel like I’m a different person than I was before [my mom died]. It will always feel like a part of me has been ripped away. But I also know that there’s literally no pain that hurts as bad as what I went through,” she said. “So I’ll keep telling myself, ‘Yes, I can do this thing.’”
