COROS Doubled the Price of Its Bike Computer. Can the DURA 2 Compete With Premium Rivals?

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

COROS kept the feature that made the original stand out and rebuilt nearly everything around it. Now the DURA 2 has to justify its place among other premium options.

The original COROS DURA stood out for two reasons: battery life and price. At $249, it was a (relatively) affordable alternative to the Garmin and Wahoo computers most riders already knew. Just with a solar charging feature that made charging almost optional. But it also felt like the first-generation product that it was. The DURA 2 is COROS’s attempt to move beyond that niche and compete with computers from Garmin, Wahoo, and Hammerhead.

The DURA 2 has a new, unusually slim form factor, a larger 3.5-inch touchscreen, updated maps, and redesigned controls that replace the original’s thumb dial. It also adds voice commands, music controls, and a built-in bike bell. The screen is a memory-in-pixel display, rather than the bright, phone-like panel found on OLED displays on the Karoo and latest Garmin head units. COROS’s priority here is a legible readout while minimizing the power demand of the screen.

Battery life remains the headline. COROS rates the DURA 2 for up to 120 hours in standard GPS mode, or 70 hours with dual-frequency GPS, before factoring in solar charging. After 30 hours of riding with paired sensors, occasional navigation, and the screen at full brightness, my unit still had 77 percent battery remaining.

The price of the DURA 2 has changed along with the ambition. The computer costs $499 on its own, or $549 with COROS’s aluminum mount—a steep jump from the original, though you can skip the bundled mount if you already have a compatible one. The question is whether the DURA 2’s much longer battery life, improved interface, and the COROS ecosystem make it a compelling alternative at this price. After riding it, I think the new DURA is a much more complete and compelling computer. But it hasn’t quite closed the gap to its competitors.

The DURA 2’s memory-in-pixel screen won't win anyone over with vivid colors or smartphone-like detail. That’s fine with me. I don’t use a cycling computer to watch videos or scroll social media. I want to glance down, find the information I need, and get back to riding. The DURA 2 does that well, with arguably the most legible screen in direct sunlight I’ve seen.

The screen struggles most in inconsistent, high-contrast light, such as bright dappled sunlight through tree cover. This is when I wish the DURA 2 offered any kind of meaningful backlight. To my eyes, changing the screen brightness from full to all the way off made no noticeable difference. This wasn’t a huge issue on basic numerical data pages, which stayed very legible, but when you’re trying to discern map detail, it felt a little tough, especially while riding.

Some riders will still prefer the brighter, higher-resolution screens on the Hammerhead Karoo or Garmin’s high-end computers. That’s a valid preference. How beautiful a screen looks isn’t a huge priority for me.

The maps themselves are a big improvement over the original DURA. In my experience, the Hammerhead is still the standard for map legibility and ease of on-device navigation, but the DURA 2 is now quite good. It shows street names at closer zoom levels, and the map responds quickly when you pan or zoom. Both the processing speed and touchscreen response are dramatically better than on the first DURA, and are now close to what I expect from Garmin and Hammerhead.

One screen quirk is that Coros has no way to manually lock the screen. COROS says it will automatically lock when it detects too much rain. I would still like to have a manual lock setting, even if the automatic system works as promised. I suspect COROS could add that option in a future firmware update.

Good riddance to the dial. I gave the original DURA’s dial a fair chance, and it could be useful once I got used to it, especially with gloves. But I also found it clunky and a pain to use while riding. The DURA 2’s new haptic buttons work as advertised, and the touchscreen is far more responsive than it was on the original.

The screen is a little less responsive with gloves on, but not dramatically less than other touchscreens I’ve used. The physical buttons help, and the new layout feels more straightforward. The DURA 2 is simply easier to operate on the bike compared to the original.

The offset Garmin-style mount connection is a small but useful design feature. Larger computers can run into clearance problems with some proprietary out-front mounts. Because the DURA 2’s connection point sits farther back, I’m less concerned about that here. I tested the computer without buying COROS’s optional mount, which I appreciate having as an option because I already have a bin full of included out-front mounts that I couldn’t give away if I tried. Being able to opt out of yet another one was actually kind of refreshing.

I’ve recorded 31 hours of riding on the DURA 2, and the battery is still at 82.4%. That’s with multiple sensors paired, occasional navigation, and the screen at full brightness.

Even with the caveat that battery usage will vary from user to user based on factors like location and use case, the battery performance is extraordinary. If you regularly do rides longer than 20 hours, this is probably the computer to get. No other current head unit I’ve tested comes close to this kind of real-world endurance.

For shorter rides, the advantage is less dramatic but still useful: charging becomes one less thing to remember. And Coros deserves kudos here for maintaining battery-life performance with the DURA 2 while adding a larger screen and more processing power to the unit.

Navigation on the DURA 2 with the updated maps is a clear improvement. The faster processor makes the device feel much less sluggish when zooming or scrolling the map, and the map is easy to read while riding. Street names at tighter zoom levels are a welcome addition compared to what the original DURA offered.

My main reservation is that rerouting still depends on your phone. I get the practical argument: most of us ride with a phone anyway, and the phone can handle it. But it still feels like a shortcoming in a $500 cycling computer. Other units can reroute on their own, and I’d rather not rely on a Bluetooth phone connection when I’ve already paid for a dedicated device.

The rerouting itself is kind of hit or miss. Most of my testing has been in Southeastern Pennsylvania, and so far the DURA 2 (or, I guess I should say, the Coros app on my phone) has sent me down a trail into a stream crossing on my commuter bike only once. Following a route I designed and loaded onto the DURA 2 has gone smoothly, as expected. But I wouldn’t trust the Coros-suggested routes when navigating to a point on a map or when rerouting in an unfamiliar place.

Finally, I personally don’t mind manually loading a route onto my head unit. But in 2026, route sync should be mostly automatic. Hopefully, they can add that in a future firmware update.

Sensor pairing and ride syncing have been trouble-free for me. I’ve had no issues with the sensors I use, and activities push to the app smoothly after a ride. Which is what I expect from a core cycling computer function in 2026.

Structured training is now more than passable. I don’t use my head unit to guide workouts, but I appreciate that COROS has made the feature more useful for riders who do. You can now pause an interval without pausing the ride recording, which feels like it should have been possible from the start, but I’m always happy to see improvements.

The COROS app and Training Hub are also strengths. In some ways, the experience is simpler than Garmin Connect, and I find parts of it easier to use. You can build routes, send rides to Strava and other platforms, and see your training data alongside recovery information from a COROS watch. If you already wear a COROS watch off the bike, that integration is useful. As with Garmin Connect, though, you get the most from the system when you use the company’s hardware.

The new music controls are great, and they’re seamlessly integrated. The electronic bell sounds like an actual bike bell, which is great. But it can’t currently be paired to a bonus button on Di2 or AXS, which Coros can fix in a future firmware update.

The voice control is puzzling to me. Garmin just unveiled this feature on its latest 1060 unit, and I’m just not sure why. I’ve spent 20 years riding without talking to my cycling computer. I would much rather companies perfect their UI and button interfaces so riders can quickly and simply access the functions they need instead of implementing voice controls. I’m sure some riders will find it useful, but I can’t see myself talking to my cycling computer any time soon. Especially since every time I tried to talk to the DURA 2 while riding, it couldn’t understand me, so there's definitely room for improvement.

The DURA 2 is a much better computer than the original DURA. It has a better screen, faster maps, more responsive controls, usable training features, and a more complete app experience. Most importantly, it keeps the exceptional battery life that made the original interesting in the first place.

The price has doubled, and there's really no getting around that. I think the improvements, plus the new form factor, justify an increase. But at $499, the DURA 2 is basically the same price as the Hammerhead Karoo 3 and not much cheaper than the Garmin Edge 850. Both units have their own strengths and trade-offs, but they're polished and fully functional. By contrast, I have written the phrase “hopefully this gets fixed in a future firmware update” three times in this review.

The DURA 2’s biggest strength is that it no longer feels like a budget computer asking you to overlook its rough edges. It feels like a serious head unit with exceptional battery life, and if you regularly ride 20 hours straight, it’s the easy pick. If you don’t, the DURA 2 is still a big step forward over the original, but the rough edges still need a bit more sanding given the jump in asking price.
