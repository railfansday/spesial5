Three Green Hydrogen Projects Slip Through The Trump Chopper

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

When US President Donald Trump swept into office last year, he set his choppers upon the $8 billion Regional Clean Hydrogen Hubs program, snuffing out plans to establish a diversified, domestic hydrogen market with a focus on decarbonization. Nevertheless, some stakeholders have continued to pursue the dream of producing green hydrogen from renewable resources — instead of squeezing it from natural gas. Now all that hard work is beginning to pay off, Trump or no Trump.

If you guessed the startup Plug Power is in the mix, run right out and buy yourself a cigar. Plug Power began life back in 1997 with a focus on electric forklifts powered by zero emission hydrogen fuel cells. In addition to replacing fossil fuels, Plug Power aimed to replace battery-powered forklifts, with fuel cells being a better fit for the fast-paced environment of shipping and logistics.

Plug Power soon pivoted into replacing gas-derived hydrogen fuel with so-named green hydrogen, pushed from water by an electrical current. After a series of ups and downs the company hit the jackpot in January of 2025, when the US Department of Energy tapped it for a $1.66 loan guarantee in support of the Regional Clean Hydrogen Hubs program, which was funded through the 2021 Bipartisan Infrastructure Law. If all went according to plan, Plug Power would have used the loan to finance up to six green hydrogen projects around the US, all affiliated with the Hydrogen Hubs program.

So much for that. The Energy Department announced the loan award at the beginning of the month, when former President Joe Biden was still in office. However, by the end of the month Trump was officially installed in the White House. He soon pulled the plug on the Plug Power loan, so to speak, sending it down the drain alongside many other federally funded clean power projects.

That was not the end of the story for Plug Power. After all, it’s a big world out there, and Plug Power continued to pitch its wares overseas. Last week the company also relaunched itself back into the US market, bigly.

On November 17, Plug Power announced that its new “GenEco™ Scale” green hydrogen electrolyzer system is up for grabs in the US market while concurrently announcing that its first customer is the Texas-based firm Aracdia eFuels. Arcadia will deploy 280 megawatts’ worth of GenEco Scale modules at its Project ARC campus in the Portland/Corpus Christi area in Texas. When fully up and running, Project ARC will deploy green hydrogen from the GenEco Scale system to produce about 23.2 million gallons of fossil-free aviation fuel per year.

While green hydrogen has its fans in the decarbonization field, the stubbornly high cost of electrolysis has raised a formidable obstacle against widespread commercial uptake. Plug Power is among the stakeholders pitching modular, integrated systems to help bring costs down into parity with conventional hydrogen sourced from natural gas or coal, and that’s where the GenEco system comes into play.

“Built around a standardized 20 MW block architecture, GenEco Scale combines Plug’s proven proton exchange membrane (PEM) electrolyzer technology with integrated balance-of-plant systems into a modular, pre-engineered solution for hydrogen production projects of approximately 100 MW and above,” Plug Power explained in a press statement last week. The company also noted that the “outdoor-ready” system avoids much of the cost and time related to the buildings and other infrastructure required of traditional large scale electrolyzer systems.

Arcadia eFuels CEO Amy Hebert also chipped in her two cents in support of the modular system. “Reducing the engineering burden on our EPC [engineering, procurement, and construction] partners and minimizing the complexity of bringing these systems together were important considerations for Project ARC,” Herbert emphasized.

Savings on land is another part of the cost-cutting plan. Unlike conventional electrolyzer system layouts, the GenEco model deploys a vertical configuration. The vertical system requires less than one acre per 100 megawatts of electrolyzer capacity, according to Plug Power.

Another Plug Power project has bubbled up in California, which was on track to host a suite of projects under the Hydrogen Hubs program with a focus on hydrogen derived from biomass as well as water electrolysis. The funding evaporated under Trump, but the state is still getting its hands on green hydrogen.

On November 19, Plug Power announced that its containerized 5-megawatt GenEco electrolyzer is up and running at a green hydrogen plant in Vernon, California, under the umbrella of the New Jersey-based firm Avina Clean Hydrogen.

Green hydrogen from this project will go to fulfill California’s plans for a zero-emission freight corridor, the plan for which includes fuel cell electric trucks as well as battery-powered trucks. “Located approximately 10 miles from the Port of Long Beach, the facility is designed to serve fleets moving freight through the Los Angeles–Long Beach port complex. It combines on-site fueling with tube-trailer deliveries to customers across the Los Angeles region,” Plug Power explains.

The station, also accommodate buses, and it can refuel a heavy-duty vehicle to more than 95% capacity in less than 30 minutes, according to Plug Power. That stacks up well against charging times for battery-powered heavy duty vehicles. On its website, for example, Tesla asserts that its new Semi electric truck can recover 60% of its 500 mile range in up to 30 minutes at a Tesla Megacharger station.

For the record, Avina has not placed all of its cards on hydrogen fuel cell trucks. The company’s roster of clean hydrogen projects also includes SAF (sustainable aviation fuel) and green ammonia, which is a key input for fertilizer among other chemical products.

Rounding out the trio of new projects is the Swedish firm PowerCell Group. The firm is better known for its work in the marine energy field, and now it is tapping into land-based markets as well. Last week PowerCell announced that its US branch is in motion to set up a green hydrogen system run by excess electricity from an existing solar array owned by a to-be-named client in the US. In effect, the hydrogen will serve as an energy storage medium. It will go to run a PowerCell PowerPod 3000 stationary fuel cell, which will kick into action at night to keep the clean kilowatts flowing after the sun goes down.

PowerCell describes the PowerPod 3000 as “a containerized power generation platform integrating our PS190 systems with power conversion equipment, cooling and DMC, PowerCell’s control and Energy Orchestration technology,” with an installed fuel cell capacity of 27 megawatts and 20 megawatts of rated power.

That’s a good question. Rather than let that excess solar electricity go to waste, the client could invest in a BESS (battery energy storage system) for daytime energy storage and night-time electricity. However, a typical BESS only lasts for a handful of hours. In contrast, PowerCell describes its fuel cell as a “long duration” storage system, which generally means eight hours or more.

PowerCell also points out that a fuel cell does something that a BESS cannot. Fuel cells generate electricity by combining hydrogen with oxygen in the presence of a catalyst. Water — H2O — is a byproduct. All else being equal, that opens up the potential for green hydrogen to serve as a water distribution medium, sucking up water in an electrolysis system at one location, then pushing it out in a fuel cell located elsewhere.

PowerCell is not making any specific promises for this particular project, though the company does emphasize that “the ability to generate both electricity and recoverable water adds another dimension of value to hydrogen-based energy storage.”

“The amount of water recovered, and its potential uses will depend on the final system configuration, operating conditions and water treatment requirements,” the company adds.

And, that’s where things get interesting. The client is a to-be-named electric cooperative, which is of interest because electric cooperatives (more specifically, Rural Electric Cooperatives) account for a large portion of the US grid, and their roots in the rural poverty fighting programs of the Great Depression provide for a distinctive public benefit mission that can, in some cases, allow more wiggle room for innovative energy solutions that generate broad benefits for local communities.

I’m reaching out to PowerCell for more details. If they can’t divulge the name of the client yet, perhaps they’ll be able to describe what they mean by long duration energy storage, so stay tuned for an update.
