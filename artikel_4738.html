Does Black Friday Make Everything Else Seem Expensive?

There is something rather irritating about seeing a product heavily discounted during the Black Friday sales, only to see it back at full price a few days later. Before the sale, the original price might have seemed perfectly reasonable. But once the excitement of the sale has died down, it feels expensive, and paying the full price seems almost painful.

Black Friday discounts might do more than encourage us to spend money during the sales. They could also change our perception of value and how much we're willing to pay for things long after the bargains have disappeared.

When we buy something, we tend to think that we're making a rational judgment about whether it's worth the price. But how do we decide what something is actually worth?

One way is by comparing the asking price with what we've paid before or what we've seen the product selling for elsewhere. This gives us what is known as a reference price, a sort of benchmark we use to decide whether something seems expensive or cheap.

Over time, and after many repeated purchases, we become accustomed to paying a certain amount for a particular product. We accept that amount as its normal price, and it becomes what we expect to pay. But then Black Friday happens, which nowadays may last for a full month rather than one day, and that same product is suddenly available for considerably less.

Even though we know perfectly well that the discount is temporary, that lower price starts to influence what we think the product should cost. It can become a new reference point against which we judge future prices.

When the sale ends and the price returns to normal, the amount we have become used to paying no longer seems quite so reasonable. The deal has changed our perception of value.

When a discount changes what we think something is worth, it can also change whether we're willing to buy it at all.

Across three studies, Guo (2026) found that consumers who had seen an extremely low price were less willing to buy the same product later at a higher price than those who had seen a smaller discount. What was interesting was why they didn't want to buy it.

Seeing the lower price reduced how much participants thought the product was worth. The prospect of paying the higher price also increased their anticipated regret. They expected to feel worse about buying something they knew had previously been priced for considerably less.

It didn't matter whether participants had previously bought the product at the cheaper price. Simply knowing it had once been available for less was enough to discourage a future purchase. We don't necessarily have to miss out on a bargain for it to change our perception of value, which the researcher called the postdiscount effect.

The findings raise an interesting question about Black Friday. If retailers spend weeks convincing us that their products are worth buying at heavily discounted prices, what happens when they expect us to start paying full price again?

This is where Black Friday might become a problem for retailers themselves. Black Friday is designed to encourage us to buy more by making products temporarily cheaper. But in doing so, retailers may also be changing what we're willing to pay for those products in the future. They could be inadvertently teaching us that their products are worth less than they originally claimed.

The more accustomed we become to seeing discounts, the more we might begin questioning whether the full price is ever worth paying. Why buy something today when it might be cheaper again next month in the post-Christmas sales? Why pay the normal price when we've seen exactly how little a retailer was prepared to sell it for last week?

There's nothing wrong with taking advantage of a good Black Friday discount. You've made a sensible decision if you've been planning to buy something and find it cheaper. The problem is when we allow that temporary price to determine what we think something should cost long after the sales have finished.

That's the irony of Black Friday. Retailers spend much of November getting us excited and convincing us that we're going to get extraordinary value for our money. In doing so, they might also be convincing us that their products were never worth the original asking price.

They may have saved us money today, but they've made everything seem more expensive tomorrow.
