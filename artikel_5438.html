How the Congressional Review Act lets Congress overturn agency rules

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Free daily briefing on global business news.

The Congressional Review Act lets Congress cancel federal agency rules, and these 10 explainers cover how it works, from major rules and Senate fast-track votes to the lookback period

Congress writes the laws, but federal agencies write many of the detailed rules that carry the force of law. Lawmakers routinely hand agencies the authority to fill in details on workplace safety, financial markets, energy use and much more. That delegation raises a basic question about accountability. Once an agency finalizes a rule, how can Congress take back a specific decision it never voted on?

The Congressional Review Act is Congress's statutory answer. Passed in 1996 and signed by President Bill Clinton, the law gives Congress a set procedure to overturn individual rules issued by executive-branch agencies. It does not require lawmakers to rewrite the underlying statute. Congress instead passes a short measure called a joint resolution of disapproval, aimed at a single rule.

Both chambers must pass the same resolution, which then goes to the president. That final step shapes much of how the law is used. The president can veto the resolution, and overriding a veto takes two-thirds of each chamber. Successful uses have mostly come when the president and congressional majorities share a party and the targeted rule came from a predecessor.

Timing also shapes the outcome. The law sets windows for introducing resolutions and for using a special Senate procedure that sidesteps the filibuster. Through the lookback period, rules submitted late in a congressional session can become targets for the next Congress. That leaves rules finalized late in an administration exposed when power changes hands.

A successful disapproval has lasting effects. The canceled rule is treated as though it never took effect. The agency also faces limits on issuing a similar rule later, under statutory language that has never been precisely defined.

The law produced no successful disapproval until 2001. That first success stood alone for years. That changed in 2017, when a Republican Congress and President Donald Trump used it to overturn many Obama-era rules.

The 10 slides that follow explain how Congress cancels federal rules under this law. They cover what counts as a rule, how agencies report to Congress, the special treatment of major rules and the procedures in each chamber. They also explain the president's role and what happens after a disapproval. Each slide stands on its own, so readers can start with whichever part of the process they need.

Congress passed the Congressional Review Act in 1996, and President Bill Clinton signed it into law on March 29 of that year. The law gives Congress a formal way to overturn rules that executive-branch agencies issue under powers Congress handed to them.

The act did not arrive as a standalone bill. Lawmakers enacted it as one part of a larger package, the Contract with America Advancement Act. Clinton's signature on that broader measure put the review process into federal law.

The statute is codified at 5 U.S.C. sections 801 to 808. Those eight sections hold the full framework, from how agencies report new rules to what happens after Congress acts on them. That placement gives the process a fixed legal address. Lawmakers and agencies work from the same set of provisions each time a rule comes up for review.

The law responds to a problem that starts with delegation. Congress often writes statutes in broad terms. It then assigns the detailed work of carrying them out to federal agencies.

Those agencies write rules that fill in the specifics. Congress does not vote on each of those rules before an agency issues it. The authority to write them comes from statutes Congress has already passed.

The 1996 law added a check rather than a rewrite of that system. Agencies kept the rulemaking authority Congress had already granted them. What changed was that Congress gained a set procedure for answering a finished rule.

The law's scope follows from that arrangement. It applies to rules from executive-branch agencies acting under authority Congress delegated. The branch that granted the power keeps a statutory path to cancel a particular use of it.

The Congressional Review Act covers a wide range of agency actions. Its definition of "rule" comes from the Administrative Procedure Act, the law that sets out how federal agencies make regulations.

That borrowed definition is broad. A document does not need to carry the label "regulation" to fall within it. What matters is whether the document meets the statutory terms.

The review law then carves out three categories. The first is rules of particular applicability. These are actions aimed at a specific party or circumstance rather than at the public generally.

The second exclusion covers rules about internal agency management. Such rules stay outside the law only if they do not substantially affect parties outside the agency. A directive that reaches no one beyond the agency's own operations would meet that condition.

The third exclusion covers rules of agency organization, procedure or practice. A similar condition attaches. These rules escape review only if they do not substantially affect the rights or obligations of outside parties.

The qualifier in the last two exclusions limits their reach. A procedural or management rule that changes what outside parties must do, or what they are entitled to, does not fit either exemption. Such a rule remains subject to review.

Guidance documents have been a point of dispute. Agencies issue guidance to explain how they read a statute or how they intend to apply it. Whether a given guidance document qualifies as a rule under the review law has been contested.

The Government Accountability Office has issued opinions on such questions. Those opinions address whether a particular agency document meets the statute's definition of a rule. Each opinion applies the statutory definition and its three exclusions to one specific document.

The definition sets the threshold for everything else in the statute. An agency action that falls outside it cannot be overturned through a joint resolution of disapproval under this law.

Federal agencies must send each new rule, along with a report on it, to both chambers of Congress and to the Comptroller General before the rule can take effect. That delivery opens Congress's chance to review the rule under the Congressional Review Act.

The Comptroller General heads the Government Accountability Office, or GAO. The submission therefore goes to three recipients: the House, the Senate and the head of the GAO. Each receives the rule itself and the agency's report on it.

The duty is not limited to the costliest regulations. It applies to every action that meets the law's definition of a rule, whether or not the rule is classified as major. A routine regulation and a sweeping one face the same delivery requirement.

Finalizing a regulation is not the agency's last step. A rule cannot take effect until the agency has completed this submission. The requirement ties a new rule's legal effect to Congress being told about it.

Congress treats receipt of the rule as the start of its review clock. The deadline for introducing a disapproval resolution counts forward from that date. The window for the Senate's expedited procedure also runs from receipt, together with the rule's publication.

The date of receipt therefore anchors the whole review. Every later procedural deadline depends on it. A lawmaker weighing a challenge first needs to know when Congress received the rule.

Rules that agencies never formally submitted have been a recurring issue under the law. The problem follows from how the review clock works. If Congress never formally receives a rule, the event that starts the review period has not happened. The delivery step is what links an agency's rulemaking to Congress's power to overturn it.

A major rule under the Congressional Review Act generally cannot take effect until 60 days after it is submitted to Congress or published in the Federal Register, whichever comes later. The waiting period applies only to rules that meet the law's definition of major.

An agency does not apply the label to itself. The Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs makes the determination. Its finding rests on what a rule is likely to do once it is in force.

The first trigger is a dollar threshold. A rule is major if it is likely to have an annual effect on the economy of $100 million or more.

A second trigger looks at prices. A rule also qualifies as major if it is likely to cause a major increase in costs or prices.

The third trigger covers wider economic harm. A rule qualifies if it is likely to have significant adverse effects on competition, employment, investment, productivity or innovation. Likely significant adverse effects on U.S. competitiveness also count.

Each trigger stands on its own. A rule with an expected annual effect below $100 million can still be major if it is likely to significantly harm employment or competition.

The 60-day clock runs from the later of two dates. One is the day the agency submits the rule to Congress. The other is the day the rule is published in the Federal Register. If an agency publishes a rule and submits it to Congress afterward, the countdown starts on the submission date.

The delay gives lawmakers time to examine a high-impact rule before it takes effect. Rules that fall short of the major tests are not held by this 60-day pause.

The Government Accountability Office works on a tighter deadline. It must send Congress a report on each major rule within 15 days. That deadline falls inside the 60-day waiting period.

A joint resolution of disapproval is the one tool the Congressional Review Act gives lawmakers to cancel a federal agency rule. The statute fixes its wording: Congress disapproves a named rule, and that rule shall have no force or effect.

The prescribed text is a single sentence with blanks. It states that Congress disapproves the rule submitted by a named agency relating to a stated subject, "and such rule shall have no force or effect." Drafters fill in the agency and the rule. They add nothing else.

That fixed text sets the resolution apart from an ordinary bill. Lawmakers do not draft new policy language or amend the regulation section by section. They name one rule and reject it.

The design narrows what a resolution can do. It cannot strike some provisions of a regulation and keep others. It cannot substitute different requirements. The only outcome it offers is disapproval of the named rule in full.

That all-or-nothing structure shapes the vote itself. A lawmaker who supports most of a regulation but objects to one provision cannot use the resolution to fix that provision. The choice is to keep the entire rule or cancel the entire rule.

The uniform wording also keeps the debate narrow. Every resolution follows the same template, so there is no policy language to negotiate. The question before each chamber is whether the named rule should stand.

Because the prescribed text names one rule, lawmakers who object to several agency regulations draft a separate resolution for each one. Each of those resolutions then moves through Congress on its own and succeeds or fails on its own.

Timing is the second constraint. A member must introduce the resolution within 60 days of continuous session after Congress receives the rule. The count runs in days of continuous session, not calendar days.

That distinction means the deadline cannot be read off a calendar. The window depends on when Congress formally received the rule. It also depends on how many days Congress remains in continuous session afterward.

The Congressional Review Act gives the Senate a fast track for disapproving agency rules. A discharge petition signed by 30 senators can pull a resolution out of committee. A simple majority can then pass it, and it cannot be filibustered.

That combination sets the Senate path apart from the route most legislation travels. Ordinary bills usually depend on committee action to reach the floor. They can also be held up by extended debate on the floor.

The discharge petition addresses the first obstacle. A committee that does not act on a joint resolution of disapproval cannot keep it from moving. Once 30 senators sign the petition, the measure comes out of committee and heads toward a floor vote.

Floor debate on the resolution is limited under the statute. That limit addresses the second obstacle. Senators who oppose the resolution cannot use open-ended debate to prevent a final vote from taking place.

No supermajority is needed at the final vote. A party holding a narrow Senate majority can pass a disapproval resolution with its own members' votes. Support from the minority party is not required.

The expedited process is available for a limited time. It lasts for 60 Senate session days after the rule is received by Congress and published.

A session day counts only when the Senate actually meets. Recesses and adjournments do not add to the total. As a result, the window can run longer than 60 calendar days.

The count depends on two events, receipt and publication. A rule that has reached Congress but has not yet been published has not started the Senate window.

Once the 60 session days pass, the fast-track procedures no longer apply to that rule. Senators who want to use the discharge petition and the majority-vote threshold must act inside the window.

The Senate fast track governs only one chamber. A disapproval resolution passed this way still needs approval from the House and the president before it becomes law.

A Congressional Review Act resolution must pass the House and the Senate and then win the president's signature to cancel a federal rule. Unlike the Senate, the House gets no statutory fast track for these measures.

The difference between the chambers is written into the law itself. The statute builds an expedited path for the Senate. It provides no matching procedure for the House. A disapproval resolution introduced in the House gets no special protection from the Congressional Review Act.

House leadership fills that gap with its own tools. The usual route is a special rule reported by the Rules Committee. That special rule sets the terms for bringing the disapproval resolution to the floor. As a result, the timing of a House vote rests with the chamber's leadership rather than with a deadline in the statute.

Passage in one chamber is not enough. Both the House and the Senate must approve the same resolution before it reaches the president.

The president then has two choices: sign the resolution or veto it. A signature enacts the disapproval. A veto returns the measure to Congress.

Overriding a veto takes a two-thirds vote in each chamber. Both the House and the Senate must clear that threshold separately. A resolution that passed either chamber by a narrow margin will not reach two-thirds without added support. That math makes a veto difficult to overcome.

The presidential step shapes how the law works in practice. A president is unlikely to approve a resolution that cancels a rule from the president's own administration. Successful use of the Congressional Review Act has mostly come under one set of conditions.

The president and the congressional majorities belong to the same party. The targeted rule came from a predecessor's administration. Under that alignment, both chambers can pass a disapproval resolution and the president can sign it. That pattern links the law's real-world use to changes in party control of the White House.

Once a Congressional Review Act disapproval becomes law, the targeted rule is treated as though it never took effect. The agency that wrote it also loses the power to reissue a rule that is "substantially the same" unless a later law specifically authorizes it.

Those two consequences point in opposite directions in time. One erases the rule's past. The other limits what the agency can do next on the same subject.

The "never took effect" treatment reaches backward. The rule does not simply end on the date the resolution becomes law. For legal purposes, it is handled as if it had never been in force.

The reissue bar reaches forward. An agency cannot return months later with a near-copy of the disapproved rule. To write something substantially similar, it needs Congress to pass a new law that specifically allows it. Like any other legislation, that law must clear both chambers. It must then either win the president's signature or survive a veto.

The open question is the phrase itself. The statute does not define "substantially the same." It offers no test for how far a replacement must depart from the original before it falls outside the ban.

That gap leaves the scope of the restriction unsettled. An agency weighing a new rule on a subject Congress has already disapproved has no statutory definition to consult. It has to judge for itself how close is too close.

The Congressional Review Act adds a further limit. It states that actions and omissions under the law are not subject to judicial review. That language narrows the usual path of asking a court to decide what a statute requires.

The result pairs a firm prohibition with an undefined standard. A disapproved rule cannot come back on the agency's own authority. The boundary between a barred near-copy and a permissible new rule remains unsettled in the statute's text.

The Congressional Review Act's lookback period gives a new session of Congress a chance to review rules that arrived late in the previous one. It applies to rules submitted within 60 legislative days in the House, or 60 session days in the Senate, before sine die adjournment.

Sine die adjournment is the formal end of a session. A chamber adjourns without setting a date to return. The lookback window counts backward from that point.

Rules caught in the window are treated as though they were submitted on the 15th session day of the next session. That legal fiction resets the timing. The review clock starts over in the new session.

The restart reaches the introduction deadline too. Lawmakers have 60 days of continuous session after a rule is received to introduce a joint resolution of disapproval. Under the lookback, that period runs from the 15th session day of the new session.

The Senate's fast-track window restarts as well. Senators get a fresh 60 session days to use the expedited procedure against rules that reached Congress in the final stretch of the prior session.

The window is measured in days a chamber actually meets, not calendar days. The House count uses legislative days. The Senate count uses session days. Each chamber keeps its own calendar, so the two windows can open on different dates.

No one can pin down the start of the lookback window while a session is still under way. It depends on how many days each chamber meets before final adjournment. The start date is fixed only after the fact, once the calendar is complete.

That uncertainty matters for agencies finishing rules late in a session. A rule sent to Congress near the end of a session may or may not fall inside the window. The answer turns on a schedule that has not yet played out.

Rules that arrive earlier in a session do not get this second chance. They follow the ordinary review timeline instead.

Congress first used the Congressional Review Act successfully in 2001, overturning the Occupational Safety and Health Administration's ergonomics rule. Successful use then stayed rare until 2017, when a Republican Congress and President Donald Trump overturned many Obama-era rules.

The ergonomics rule came from OSHA, the workplace safety agency within the Department of Labor. Congress passed a joint resolution of disapproval against it. President George W. Bush signed the measure on March 20, 2001. With his signature, the resolution became law and the rule was canceled.

That first success arrived just under five years after President Bill Clinton signed the CRA on March 29, 1996. For that entire stretch, the statute had not canceled a single regulation.

The disapproval had lasting consequences for OSHA. Under the law, the ergonomics rule is treated as though it never took effect. The agency also cannot issue a rule that is "substantially the same" unless a later act of Congress specifically authorizes it.

For years after 2001, the ergonomics repeal stood as the only successful use of the CRA. Lawmakers kept the power to introduce disapproval resolutions throughout that period.

Use of the statute rose sharply in 2017, 16 years after the ergonomics vote. President Donald Trump took office that year alongside a Republican Congress. Those majorities used the CRA to overturn many rules finalized under the Obama administration.

The 2017 surge fit the conditions under which the CRA has mostly worked. The president belonged to the same party as the congressional majorities and was willing to sign their disapprovals. The rules in question had been issued by a predecessor rather than by the sitting administration.

That alignment matters because the president can veto a disapproval resolution like any other joint resolution. Overriding a veto requires two-thirds of each chamber. Without shared party control, a resolution that clears both chambers can still fail at the president's desk.
