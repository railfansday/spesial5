Artificial reefs vs. natural reefs, explained through the SS United States, the ocean liner set to be sunk off the Florida Panhandle as a dive site

The SS United States is scheduled to be sunk in the Gulf of Mexico off the Florida Panhandle. The ocean liner is 990 feet long, 108 feet longer than the Titanic. Once it settles on the seafloor, it is set to become the world's largest artificial reef. The project cost $13.5 million. Its organizers want the wreck to draw more divers and fishing enthusiasts and to give tuna, marlin and swordfish a new home.

The ship's first life played out above the waterline. More than 3,000 workers built it in Virginia in 1950 and 1951. It sailed on its maiden voyage in 1952 and went on to cross the Atlantic more than 800 times. Its passengers included Grace Kelly, Elizabeth Taylor, John F. Kennedy and Britain's former King Edward VIII. A young Bill Clinton sailed on it in 1968. The liner was decommissioned in 1969. Later plans by a hotel magnate and by Norwegian Cruise Line $NCLH to reuse it went nowhere. From 1996 until recently, it sat at a dock in Philadelphia.

Its next role will be underwater. The wreck is planned to rest about 25 miles southwest of Destin, in roughly 180 feet of water. Its top deck is set to sit about 60 feet below the surface, shallow enough for inexperienced divers. It will not be alone. About 500 artificial reefs already lie in nearby waters, according to Okaloosa County spokesperson Nick Tomecek. Tomecek said the previous largest was the USS Oriskany, an aircraft carrier scuttled off Pensacola in 2006.

The sinking puts a practical question in front of divers, anglers and coastal communities. How does a steel hull placed by people compare with a reef that grows on its own? Comparing artificial reefs vs. natural reefs means looking at ecology, marine life, cost, tourism and risk.
