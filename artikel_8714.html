He Ran 3,067 Miles Across America to Set a New Record, Averaging 73 Miles a Day

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

Cameron Balser broke the TransAmerica Run record, finishing in 41 days, 16 hours, and 45 minutes.

Forrest Gump has nothing on Cameron Balser.

Doing his best impersonation of the fictional movie protagonist who developed a passion for running, Balser, a 35-year-old red-bearded and straggly-haired ultrarunner from Indiana, spent the past six weeks spreading messages of love, joy, and kindness on an epic quest to run across the U.S. in record time.

Averaging about 73.6 miles per day, the Kokomo, Indiana, runner left San Francisco at 5 a.m. PT on October 10 and ran across parts of 11 states before crossing the George Washington Bridge and entering New York City at 11:02 p.m. ET on Friday night. After running a few brisk miles through Manhattan, he eventually met up with his mom, stepdad, and two sisters and slowed to a walk on the final few blocks on the way to his finishing point.

His intent was to conclude his journey on the steps of New York City Hall, just as Pete Kostelnick did while setting his TransAmerica Run record of 42 days, 6 hours, and 30 minutes back in 2016.

The only trouble was that when Balser arrived in Lower Manhattan with a gaggle of two dozen or so friends, family members, and random followers, they found all of the gates to City Hall Park closed and locked. So instead, he walked to a point adjacent to City Hall and, like Gump mythically did in Monument Valley, Utah, he just stopped.

Balser officially ended his relentless push across the country at 11:45 p.m., which means he covered the 3,067-mile journey in a record-breaking 41 days, 16 hours, and 45 minutes—roughly 13 hours and 45 minutes faster than Kostelnick.

“I know it was about the record, but mainly it was about us coming together and being together and loving one another,” Balser told the group around him after he finished. “We shared this experience, and I am so lucky and grateful to be able to do this, and I couldn’t have done it without so many people. I am grateful for this life and I’m going to keep living it like I do. So thank you so much, it means so much to me to have your support.”

Shortly thereafter, his crew and family members celebrated with some late-night New York pizza and, after devouring a slice or two, Balser almost immediately crashed into a deep sleep.

“And we didn’t set an alarm for 5 a.m.,” crew chief Sam Yeary said.

Balser caught the running bug in high school and continued running competitively as a middle-distance runner for Vincennes University. He went through some hard times in his early 20s and dealt with depression that took him to a dark place that had him pondering suicide. But with support from friends, a job as wellness and housekeeping director at the YMCA of Grant County in Marion, Indiana, and an immersion into ultrarunning races from 50K to 100 miles, Balser got a new lease on life and has been spreading joy ever since.

During parts of 2023 and 2024, Balser spent nearly 10 months running the entire perimeter of the U.S. self-supported, covering 11,170 miles at a much more forgiving pace of about 40 miles a day. He built a big social media following along the way, picked up a sponsorship from new footwear brand Mount to Coast, and started planning the TransAmerica run he says he'd been thinking about for seven or eight years.

He planned to start in the spring, but his training suffered after he got sick, so he postponed it and began looking for a place to work and train. That’s when Yeary, a friend he met at a few Indiana ultrarunning races organized by Meshingomesia Track Club, offered Balser the opportunity to live, work, and train at his 60-acre family-run regenerative farm near Bloomington.

Training regularly with Yeary, Balser earned some money to help fund his journey, wrangled up a crew of friends and began making plans for October. He knew Kostelnick, a two-time Badwater 135 champion, had averaged more than 72 miles a day in 2016 to shatter the previous record Frank Giannino Jr. set in 1980. Balser decided to commit to running roughly 75 miles a day, which meant 15 to 16 hours on his feet with a rotating crew in a van meeting him about every four miles.

His route closely mirrored Kostelnick’s, taking him across parts of California, Nevada, Utah, Colorado, Nebraska, Iowa, Illinois, Indiana, Ohio, Pennsylvania, New Jersey, and New York. He passed over the Sierra Nevada range, across Nevada and Utah’s Uinta Basin, over the Continental Divide at Colorado’s Cameron Pass, and down the Poudre River Canyon, across the Great Plains and through Northern Appalachia, ultimately enduring more than 140,000 feet of elevation gain.

Things didn’t go flawlessly the entire way. He had some stomach issues in late October and suffered from nasal irritation brought on by dry conditions. But for the most part, he was cruising along and following his catchphrase of “movin’ and groovin’” without any major setbacks.

“It was probably one of the toughest challenges any of us have ever done. It was absolutely insane,” Yeary told Runner’s World early Saturday morning. “We set a big goal this summer when he worked on my farm. We got to think big and dream big. He’s had this on his mind for a long time, but the stars aligned this fall, and we put together a plan to make it happen.”

Balser was ahead of Kostelnick’s record pace from the start as his progress was tracked on the Breaking 42 website and Instagram.

He was greeted by supporters at numerous points along the way and often joined by runners who ran alongside him for many miles. Yeary said Balser and the crew were thrilled, humbled, and grateful to have so many Americans cheering on a couple of Indiana boys.

“Even as a crew, we were blown away by him performing at that level,” Yeary said. “It was hard, but it was necessary for us to treat his consistency for 70-plus miles every day like it was something normal. But we had to in order to keep things going. 70 miles is no joke!”

Balser’s effort developed an inspirational following on his way across the country. Social media engagement spiked over the final days of his quest, and more than 420 people watched his final miles through New York City via Instagram Live. Among those following along and offering support and congratulations on the live stream were U.S. Backyard Ultra champion Mark Dowdle and two-time Badwater Ultramarathon champion Lisa Smith-Batchen.

“As an amateur recreational ultrarunner, I am so inspired,” said Weston Traub, a San Francisco runner who was following along on Instagram as Balser finished. “Seeing him push the limits of endurance reminds me how strong the human spirit is. I have all of these long Strava routes mapped out in my phone that I’ll do ‘someday.’ Nothing crazy like running across the country. Seeing Cam get it done makes me think, ‘Do it now!’ Sometimes next year doesn’t happen, and life gets in the way.”

Balser wore a YMCA of Grant County T-shirt while running through New Jersey and into New York City on Friday night. As part of his Breaking 42 campaign, Balser raised money through a website and plans to give 100 percent of donations directly to that YMCA branch.

“I know people really focus on the record, and, you know, that's a beautiful thing to go out there and set something,” Balser said. “But the main thing I wanted people to know is just that they're beautiful in the way they are. When we come together to do things, especially if it's something that speaks to our heart and what's possible with that, it's endless, you know?

“Keep your heart open and find the right people when your head's down to pick you up. I'm truly grateful. I have put my body through more than I probably would want to ever do, but, I know the reach of that, that love. Every step I take, every breath I get to take, it reaches more people, and that's a beautiful thing. And I think it'll ripple through my life and other lives. This record will be broken, but the feeling will last forever.”

Brian Metzler is a Boulder, Colorado, writer and editor whose work has appeared in Runner’s World, Sports Illustrated, ESPN, Outside, Trail Runner, The Chicago Tribune, and Red Bulletin. He’s a former walk-on college middle-distance runner who has transitioned to trail running and pack burro racing in Colorado.
