A faster algorithm for extracting hexadecimal digits of π

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

This post introduces a new algorithm for extracting hexadecimal digits of \(\pi\) without computing the preceding digits. It beats the standard term-by-term evaluations of Bailey–Borwein–Plouffe (BBP), Bellard, and Huvent on operation count. Even against Huvent’s eight-term formula, the savings are about 61% fewer divisions and 38% fewer full modular exponentiations.

The underlying series goes back to Euler, but has been rearranged to exploit more efficient evaluation. Collecting its terms by odd denominator lets us share the arithmetic in two places:

The common denominator \(M=2k+1\) means we can add four remainders and divide once. The surprise is that we don’t have to compute those remainders independently either. The gaps between the exponents are small multiples of \(k\) plus constants, so one starting power and the step \(2^k\bmod M\) let us reach the others by multiplication.

Two full exponentiations modulo \(3M\) serve both denominators \(M\) and \(3M\). That gives us up to eight powers of two, and we still only divide once.

Together, these two tricks save about a million full exponentiations per million hexadecimal places skipped compared with Huvent, before any machine-specific optimization. In practice, this algorithm leads to an implementation that runs in nearly 40% less wall-clock time.

To see where the savings come from, let’s recall the BBP trick. To skip \(n\) hex digits, shift \(\pi\) left by \(d=4n\) bits and take the fractional part. Shifting by \(d\) bits adds \(d\) to each exponent, so we’re left adding and subtracting terms of the form \(2^E/N\). When \(E\ge 0\), write \(2^E=qN+r\). Then \(2^E/N=q+r/N\), and we don’t care about \(q\): it’s an integer. Even at \(d=4{,}000{,}000\), we can compute \(r=2^E\bmod N\) without ever constructing a four-million-bit numerator.

The terms with \(E<0\) make up the tail. They decay geometrically, so we sum them directly, keeping enough guard bits to settle the last digit we want.

The above is essentially the BBP trick, and is the basis for three decades of digit extraction algorithms.

As such, when I was programmatically looking for efficient BBP-formula, I ranked them by how many fractions I’d have to evaluate to reach a given digit. Each fraction in the main loop costs me a modular exponentiation and a division. Bellard’s formula advances 10 bits for every seven fractions; Huvent’s formula advances 12 bits for every eight. After finding Huvent’s formula independently, I’ve tried feverishly to find one with a better ratio, without success. So I looked toward ways of optimizing existing series.

One clue tipped me off. There’s a choice hiding in the last step of Bellard’s terse derivation. He has one sum in powers of \(1/4\) and another in powers of \(1/1024\). Since \(4^5=1024\), splitting the first sum into five progressions lets him write both sums in powers of \(1/1024\). What if, instead of lining up the powers, we lined up the denominators?

Notice the denominators of the fractions. Since \(4m+2=2(2m+1)\), we can halve the numerator of the middle fraction and leave \(2m+1\) in the denominator. As \(m\) runs over the nonnegative integers, \(2m+1\) gives every positive odd integer. The other two progressions, \(4m+1\) and \(4m+3\), split that same list between them. So each odd denominator appears twice in each sum.

For example, consider the denominator 13. The first progression hits it at \(m=3\); the middle one hits it at \(m=6\), where the original denominator was 26. That’s two contributions from the sum in powers of \(1/64\) and two from the sum in powers of \(1/1024\). The powers are different, but all four numerators can be added before we divide by 13. We can collect terms this way for every odd denominator.

For an odd denominator \(N=2k+1\), the two contributions in each logarithm come from \(j=N\) and \(j=2N\). All further doublings give multiples of four, where \((1+i)^j\) is real and contributes nothing. At \(j=N\) the imaginary part of \((1+i)^N\) is \((-1)^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}2^k\); at \(j=2N\) we’re looking at \((2i)^N=(-1)^k 2^N i\). At \(j=N\), the first logarithm multiplies the imaginary part by \(8/2^{2(2k+1)}\), giving the numerator term \((-1)^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}2^{1-3k}\). Collect the other three contributions in the same way and we have the boxed formula.

Call a power active if its exponent is nonnegative. The slowest-decaying exponent is \(E_1\), which stays nonnegative up to \(k=\lfloor(d+1)/3\rfloor\). That’s about \(d/3\) odd denominators, and we divide just once for each one. Even the largest modulus is only \(2d/3+O(1)\), compared with about \(d\) for Bellard or Huvent, and \(2d\) for the original BBP formula.

As for the remainders \(r_j=2^{E_j}\bmod N\), notice that the middle two exponents differ by \(k\). If we already have \(r_3\) and \(x=2^k\bmod N\), then \(r_2\) is just \(xr_3\bmod N\). In fact, we can step from the smallest exponent up to the largest:

Start at the smallest active power. We pay for one full exponentiation to get it and one to get \(x\); the rest are a handful of multiplications. Past \(k\approx d/5\) only \(E_1\) is active, so don’t bother computing \(x\).2 That’s two exponentiations for the first \(d/5\) denominators and one for the remaining \(d/3-d/5\), or about \(8d/15\) altogether.

We only want the fractional part, so we can compute both numerators modulo 39, take the remainder of \(3A+B\), and divide by 39 just once.

Now consider the step powers. For 13 the index is 6; for 39 it’s 19. Since \(19=3\cdot6+1\), once we have \(x=2^6\bmod39\), we get the step for 39 by cubing and doubling: \(2^{19}\equiv2x^3\pmod{39}\).

In general, tripling a denominator \(M=2k+1\) changes its index from \(k\) to \(3k+1\). Substitute that index into the four exponents above to get the partner’s exponents. Now read the active exponents from both numerators in increasing order. The common \(d\) cancels in every gap, leaving a small multiple of \(k\) plus a constant. We need just two full exponentiations modulo \(3M\): one for the first power in the list and one for the step \(2^k\). Multiplication takes us up the rest of the list.

We never need to compute the step just for \(3M\). Its largest exponent, \(d-2-9k\), is smaller than the second exponent for \(M\), \(d-1-5k\). If \(3M\) has even one active power, \(M\) already has at least two.

We don’t want to pair 39 with 13 and then pair it again with 117. So we start pairs only at odd \(M\) not divisible by three. If \(3M\) has an active power, we take it with \(M\); otherwise we process \(M\) alone. Odd multiples of nine, like 117, stay unpaired.

The denominators that no longer need their own turn are precisely the odd multiples of three that aren’t multiples of nine. That’s two out of every nine odd denominators, both where we need one exponentiation and where we need two. Multiplying the counts above by \(7/9\) gives about \(7d/27\) divisions and \(56d/135\) full exponentiations, compared to \(2d/3\) of each for Huvent.

Returning to the pair 13 and 39, while 39 is active, I never reduce modulo 13: changing the numerator for 13 by a multiple of 13 changes its tripled contribution by a multiple of 39. So I do all the squaring and multiplying modulo 39. Since 39 is odd, I can compute its inverse modulo a power of two once and reuse it for every Montgomery multiplication in the pair. I triple the numerator for 13, add the one for 39, and convert back from Montgomery form just once for the final division.

There’s a nice trick inside each exponentiation, too. Suppose a Montgomery reduction leaves \(-14\) modulo 39. Normally I’d add 39 to get 25. But if I’m going to square it next, 14 is just as good: \(14^2\equiv25^2\pmod{39}\). Even if the current exponent bit calls for doubling first, the next square still erases the sign. So for all but the last bit, I use vector absolute values instead of correcting the sign. On the last bit I do the usual reduction, since the numerator needs the actual residue rather than its negative. That change made the exponentiation loop about 8% faster on Zen 5.

Each vector lane handles a different odd modulus. I batch moduli with the same set of active powers so that one lane doesn’t sit idle while another steps through three extra residues. The hardware multiplies 52-bit inputs, so the largest modulus matters: ours is about \(2d/3\), compared with \(d\) for Huvent. That lets us reach about 50% farther into \(\pi\) before we need wider arithmetic.

I originally went looking for a series with fewer fractions per bit. If you evaluate every fraction separately, Euler’s series actually needs more fractions per bit than Huvent’s, so it has been easy to pass over. Sharing the modular arithmetic across common and paired denominators is what makes it faster, and I suspect similar tricks apply to other BBP-type series.
