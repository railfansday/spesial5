CrowdStrike Makes AI Security Push With Blockbuster Zscaler Deal

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

News, news analysis, and commentary on the latest trends in cybersecurity technology.

CrowdStrike's $44 billion acquisition of Zscaler would unite endpoint protection and zero-trust network security to obtain greater control over AI agents.

CrowdStrike has agreed to acquire Zscaler for $43.9 billion in stock, combining endpoint protection and zero-trust network security to help enterprises control AI agents running across their systems.

The deal, announced Monday, aims to give chief information security officers (CISOs) a single supplier for security controls spanning endpoints where agents operate and the network connections used to reach applications and data.

The proposed transaction surpasses Google's $32 billion acquisition of Wiz, making it the largest announced cybersecurity acquisition on record. For customers, whether the companies can turn their existing integrations into unified enforcement while preserving support for competing products remains to be seen.

CrowdStrike co-founder and CEO George Kurtz said the proliferation of AI agents has changed the role of network security, making Zscaler's traffic inspection complementary to the Falcon platform's visibility into endpoints and cloud workloads.

"The network used to connect people to places," Kurtz said during an investor call announcing the deal and discussing CrowdStrike's fiscal third-quarter results. "Now it connects agents to data."

CrowdStrike expects the transaction to close in the second half of fiscal 2028, subject to customary closing conditions. The companies will operate independently until then, according to CrowdStrike CFO Burt Podbere.

Bringing network and endpoint security into a common control plane makes sense even without the growth of AI agents, according to cybersecurity analyst Frank Dickson, founder of Dickson Research. Their increasing use makes the combination more compelling, he tells Dark Reading.

CrowdStrike's Falcon Guardian discovers AI agents, governs their access, and protects them at runtime, Kurtz said. Zscaler's Zero Trust Exchange provides inline inspection and access controls for traffic moving to applications and services. Combining those capabilities would connect what an agent does on a device with where it sends data, according to the two companies.

Kurtz described the planned integration with an example of an agent on a laptop pulling sensitive information and sending it to an unapproved AI model. Falcon would see the agent's activity on the device, while Zscaler would see the destination on the network.

"Together it will be one detection, one decision, and one kill switch that stops the agent on the device and cuts its path on the network," Kurtz said.

The companies already share 16 integrations, and customers send Zscaler data into CrowdStrike's Next-Gen SIEM. The combination would bring their telemetry together in that platform and use the expanded dataset to support CrowdStrike's security AI models, Kurtz said.

Zscaler founder, chairman, and CEO Jay Chaudhry said combining the companies will allow them to go beyond what they can accomplish through existing partner integrations, including coordinating policy enforcement across endpoints, cloud environments, and the network.

"Imagine being able to have a unified policy that can do all this stuff," Chaudhry said during the conference call.

Zscaler's network-centric architecture should make integrating the endpoint software less burdensome than it might seem because much of Zscaler's processing happens in its distributed network rather than on the device, Dickson says.

"The integration is going to be much lighter because much of the heavy lifting done by Zscaler is done in the network," he says.

According to Kurtz, CrowdStrike's next-generation endpoint sensor already has network traffic inspection and proxy capabilities that should help with integration. Asked during the call by Deutsche Bank analyst Brad Zelnick how the deal would affect CrowdStrike's single-software agent architecture, Kurtz reiterated that commitment without providing a delivery date for the combined agent.

"The brand promise, single agent. That's not going to change," he said.

The CrowdStrike-Zscaler combination could "fundamentally change the competitiveness of the industry, 100%," Dickson says.

Microsoft has comparable breadth, he adds, but questions whether it could match the depth of the software giant's combined offerings. And it doesn't mean Palo Alto Networks, for example, needs another acquisition to respond. The vendor already has the technologies to compete, Dickson says, pointing particularly to its identity capabilities and its CyberArk deal.

Palo Alto Networks is also pursuing a different route into enterprise AI. Its Nov. 18 agreement to acquire Applied Compute for $4.5 billion in stock would extend its reach into training and deploying open-weight AI models. While CrowdStrike is emphasizing controls over where agents run and communicate, Palo Alto is moving further into the infrastructure used to build and operate enterprise AI.

"They're changing the terms of competition away from functionality to trust," Dickson says of Palo Alto's approach.

Dickson also points to Cisco's expansion in identity, underscoring the wider competition to connect identity with security enforcement.

For other security vendors, the Zscaler acquisition increases the importance of partnerships and differentiation by industry or customer size, Dickson says. Microsoft's broad security portfolio already required that approach; the CrowdStrike-Zscaler combination would add another large platform competitor, he notes.

"You need as many friends as you can, and then niche as hard as you can," Dickson says.

For CISOs, the competitive shift also raises the question about whether a broader platform can simplify security operations without limiting product choice.

After closing, CrowdStrike intends to bring Zscaler's offerings into Falcon Flex, allowing customers to expand across both portfolios through the same subscription model. CrowdStrike's Podbere said cross-selling and broader adoption would help generate approximately $1 billion in annualized revenue savings within 30 months after closing.

Deutsche Bank's Zelnick asked what the transaction would mean for customers that want another endpoint or secure access service edge (SASE) provider. Kurtz emphasized the single-agent strategy rather than detailing how those mixed-vendor deployments would work.

Separately, responding to a question about vendor lock-in, Kurtz said CrowdStrike would continue working with outside technologies and accepting data from other vendors.

Coexistence with competing network security products is already common, Dickson says, and he does not see the acquisition itself as a reason for those customers to change course. Deeper integration could make adding Zscaler more attractive to existing CrowdStrike customers, he says.

Despite his enthusiasm, Dickson says the CrowdStrike-Zscaler combination needs stronger identity capabilities to deliver on its AI-agent security ambitions. Discovering an agent and inspecting its traffic are not the same as establishing its identity and determining precisely what it is authorized to do.

Agents need a registry, authentication tied to their identities, and fine-grained authorization, Dickson says, though he questions whether either company's current portfolio fully provides that combination of capabilities.

CrowdStrike is working to address that area. During the investor Q&A, Kurtz said the company expects to introduce an agent identity provider around January, with controls intended to provide just-in-time access and zero standing privileges across human, machine, and agent identities.

The challenge is expanding as employees outside software development gain the ability to build agents, Dickson says. Security teams must contend not only with more agents but also with greater autonomy and creators who may lack experience building secure applications.
