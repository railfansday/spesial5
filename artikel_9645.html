Every week, a company terminates an engineer or a sales rep with access to sensitive production data. As part of the offboarding checklist, the IT administrator logs into Okta or Microsoft Entra ID, finds the employee's profile, and clicks Deactivate.

The IT admin closes their laptop, confident that the employee is locked out of the company’s entire SaaS stack.

Twenty minutes later, that terminated employee opens their personal laptop, refreshes an open browser tab for your B2B SaaS application, and exports the entire customer database. Or worse, a Python script on their local machine continues pulling records via a Personal Access Token (PAT) for the next six months.

Why did this happen? Because SAML and OIDC only govern the front door. Once a user is inside your application, your Identity Provider (IdP) is completely blind to what happens next.

If you are building a B2B SaaS product, relying on SSO login checks alone means your enterprise customers have a massive, silent offboarding vulnerability. Here is a technical breakdown of the four architectural gaps that keep terminated users active and how to engineer the fixes.

How the problem gets built: When a user logs in via SAML 2.0 or OIDC, the IdP hands your Assertion Consumer Service (ACS) endpoint a signed payload saying: "As of 9:00:00 AM, Alice is an active employee."

Why it fails during offboarding: If Alice is terminated at 9:15 AM and removed from Okta, Okta does not notify your application in a standard SAML setup. Your API gateway continues to validate Alice's stateless JWT strictly by checking its cryptographic signature and its exp timestamp. Because the signature is still valid, Alice has uninterrupted access for the remaining 23 hours and 45 minutes.

When the 15-minute JWT expires, the browser must hit your /auth/refresh endpoint. This forces a stateful lookup where your backend can verify that tenant_memberships.status == 'ACTIVE' before minting a new 15-minute JWT.

How the problem gets built: Many engineering teams build SAML/OIDC login + Just-In-Time (JIT) user creation and consider "Enterprise SSO" complete. They skip building a SCIM 2.0 (System for Cross-domain Identity Management) server because customers only ask about SAML during the initial trial.

Why it fails during offboarding: JIT provisioning is a one-way street. It creates users when they log in, but it can never tell you when a user leaves. Without SCIM, your application only discovers a user was deactivated in Okta when their session finally expires and they try to log in again—which could be days or weeks later if you use rolling refresh tokens.

Immediately Revoked. Any API call using this token must fail with 401 Unauthorized the moment the user's tenant_membership is deactivated.

Immediately Revoked. Refresh tokens issued to third-party apps on behalf of this user are purged.

Remains Active (With Audit Alert). Never let critical production CI/CD pipelines run on a human engineer's PAT. Provide dedicated Service Accounts owned by the Tenant, and fire a warning notification to workspace admins if the user who originally created the Service Account is deprovisioned so they can rotate the secret if needed.

How the problem gets built: Before Acme Corp upgraded to your Enterprise tier and configured SAML SSO, five of their engineers signed up for your product using a standard email + password or "Sign in with Google." Later, Acme's IT admin configures Okta SAML in your portal.

Why it fails during offboarding: When one of those original five engineers is fired and removed from Okta, they can no longer log in via the "Sign in with SSO" button. So they go back to your login page, click "Forgot Password" (or use their old cached password / linked Google account), and log right back into the workspace—completely bypassing the Okta SAML wall.
