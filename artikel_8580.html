Where Should I Live? – Weigh 14 Factors, Starting With What Your Pay Buys

Choosing where to live is a money decision first, so screen each place on its prices, its state and local taxes, and what your job pays there. Then rank the other factors, from commute to climate, by how much each matters to you, and spend a month in your top pick before you sign a lease or buy.

California’s prices ran about 27% above Arkansas’s in 2024 (110.7 against 86.9), the widest gap between any two states in the Bureau of Economic Analysis’s regional price parities, which score each state’s prices against a national level of 100. For rents the gap was wider still, with California’s nearly three times those of West Virginia, the cheapest state for rent (154.3 against 54.2).

So the same paycheck buys about 27% more in Arkansas than in California, before taxes and before any difference in what your job pays in each state. That’s why the three money factors below come first: affordability, taxes, and jobs. Screen every place you’re considering on those three, then use the other 11 to choose among the places that pass.

If you don’t have a list yet, start with the best U.S. cities to live in, places where remote workers get more for their money, college towns worth moving to, or, for a move abroad, inexpensive countries.

Start with each state’s price parity, then look closer at housing. Census QuickFacts lists the median rent and home value for cities and counties side by side, though its figures average several recent years, so check current listings for the home you’d need too.

Then add up what housing would cost each month, rent or a mortgage payment plus utilities, and divide it by your monthly income. By the Department of Housing and Urban Development’s standard, which the Census Bureau uses, a household that spends more than 30% of its income on housing is cost-burdened.

Compare the whole state and local tax bill, not just the income tax. A state with no income tax can still cost you more overall if its sales and property taxes run high. The Institute on Taxation and Economic Policy’s Who Pays? report estimates the share of income that families at each income level pay in state and local taxes, state by state, so find the group closest to your income.

If you’ll keep a remote job based in another state, check your employer’s location rules and the tax bill before you move.

Look up what your occupation pays in each metro area and how many people do that work there. The Bureau of Labor Statistics’ occupational employment and wage estimates give both for every metro area.

To compare pay between states, divide it by the state’s price parity and multiply by 100. In a state at 110, for example, a $70,000 salary buys about what $63,600 buys at the national price level, before taxes. A state’s parity averages its cities and rural areas, so treat it as a rough guide.

If you plan to buy, look at how home prices have moved, not just where they stand. The Federal Housing Finance Agency’s House Price Index tracks single-family home prices by state, metro area, county, and ZIP code, so you can compare five or 10 years of history across your list. How long homes sit unsold in current listings shows how easily you could sell later. Buy only if you expect to stay long enough for owning to beat renting.

QuickFacts lists the average travel time to work for each city and county, a quick way to compare commutes across your list. If you’d rather not drive, check the transit agency’s routes and schedules to see that buses or trains go where and when you’d need them.

If you fly often, check the nearest airport’s nonstop destinations on its website against the cities you visit most, and count the drive and parking in every trip’s cost. A bigger airport an hour farther away can still save time if it flies nonstop where the closer one connects.

Look up reported crimes for each city or county police department in the FBI’s Crime Data Explorer, using the same year for every place on your list. A citywide total can hide big differences between neighborhoods, so treat it as a first screen.

Climate sets your heating and cooling bills and your insurance costs, not just how comfortable you are. FEMA’s National Risk Index shows which communities are most at risk from 18 natural hazards, from floods to wildfires, so check it for every place on your list. Where flood risk runs high, price flood insurance along with a home or renters policy.

List what you do with a free weekend, from concerts and pro sports to hiking and worship, and check that each place offers it within an easy drive. The city’s events calendar and local news show what’s on in a typical month.

Price your usual grocery list at the stores in each place on their apps or websites, and check how far the nearest full-size supermarket is. If eating out is a big part of your week, see what’s within a short drive and what it costs.

QuickFacts also shows each place’s population and how much it has grown or shrunk since 2020. Size sets how many jobs, stores, and services you can choose from, and how much traffic comes with them. A shrinking population can mean fewer of all three later, so weigh the trend along with the size. Whether the city, a suburb, or the country costs you less depends on housing, car, and commute costs added together.

Count the trips you’d make each year to see family and friends, and price them, time off included. The help you’d leave behind has a price too. If relatives watch your kids now, the Labor Department’s National Database of Childcare Prices lists paid child care prices by county.

If you have kids or plan to, compare the schools in each place on your list. GreatSchools, a nonprofit, rates schools from 1 to 10 against other schools in the same state using state data, so compare ratings within a state, never across state lines.

Check that doctors who take your insurance practice nearby, along with a hospital you’d trust. Medicare’s Care Compare lets you compare the quality of care at Medicare-certified hospitals near any address. If you buy your own coverage, HealthCare.gov previews plans and estimated prices by ZIP code before you apply.

Before you sign a lease, buy a home, or book the move, live in your top pick for a month. Airbnb hosts can discount stays of 28 nights or more, and Furnished Finder lists only rentals of 30 days or more, with no booking fee for renters.

Live an ordinary month, not a vacation. Drive or ride your commute at rush hour, shop where you’d shop, and walk the neighborhoods you’d live in at night and on a weekend.

Book the month for the season you’d like least there, such as July in Phoenix or January in Minneapolis, because you’ll live through it every year.
