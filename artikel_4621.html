Mindfulness, Motivational Interviewing, and Exposure in IFS

Here’s a question in the holiday spirit: What do you get by mixing a nonalcoholic goblet of mindfulness (let’s say, mint water), a mug of motivational interviewing (lemon water), and a glass of exposure therapy (spring water)? My answer: an elixir called Internal Family Systems (IFS), an approach to therapy and life that's based on two big assumptions, each with important subcategories.

First, the mind is plural; we all have parts, and when one part gets hurt and banished from consciousness, other (often slightly older) parts come online to protect it. The first protectors, the proactive ones, take on the job of inhibiting genuine self-expression. Some warn the hurt part (called an exile in IFS), some criticize, and some perform socially—all with the aim of preventing more harm. As their inhibitions and shaming grow intense, a set of reactive protectors come online to offer relief with various disinhibitions and addictive processes that are highly effective at distracting from or soothing emotional pain in the short run.

While their radically different tactics naturally put them in conflict, both sides unintentionally aggravate the underlying emotional pain of exiled parts. When proactive protectors shame exiles, they feel even worse; when they feel worse, reactive protectors feel obliged to launch some kind of disinhibition, often in the form of an addictive process. In turn, disinhibitions inspire more shaming, and so matters escalate.

But this is not the end of the story. The second assumption made in IFS is that the mind has the capacity to receive (or produce, depending on your beliefs) a consciousness that is qualitatively different from parts. This different consciousness, called Self in IFS, is characterized by inviting mental states like curiosity, kindness, connectedness, clarity, courage, and compassion. Parts need, and indeed thrive under, the influence and guidance of Self. From the IFS perspective, we suffer when our parts are cut off from Self and revive when that connection heals.

The first step in IFS therapy is to engage protectors. Their willingness is an essential element of therapy for any client, even those who are mandated. That said, the IFS way of encouraging protectors to be willing to engage is paradoxical. After calling the client’s attention to their internal experience, we ask them to look around for unwillingness. For example, if a client comes to therapy eager to change a habit like drinking a lot at the end of the day, the therapist asks if any other part either doesn’t believe change is possible or has reason to hold onto the status quo. Eventually, respect and inclusion—all parts are welcome—encourage even the most reluctant protectors to engage.

While IFS treatment comes in unequal portions, one for protectors and the other for exiles, we invite attention and willingness (those ingredients of mindfulness) throughout. But what about the mug of motivational interviewing? This tart aperitif is for protectors: What do you do? What would happen if you stopped doing it? Who do you protect? Are you tired of all this responsibility? Are your tactics working? Are you willing to try something new? Are you willing to meet someone else inside who can help? Let’s call it multi-player motivational interviewing. It goes on until protectors are willing to allow the Self to have contact with an exile.

We can only proceed to exposure in the second portion of IFS therapy (which ironically often takes far less time) when protectors say yes, and they will only say yes when everyone feels sufficiently connected to Self. In this second portion of therapy, exiles revisit those moments when the worst possible news landed (I’m weak, I’m alone, I’m worthless). These are the conclusions protectors have always feared. Keep in mind that even as exiles stayed desperately hopeful, protectors have desperately feared their hope. For protectors, it’s axiomatic that reliving terrible moments will be excruciating, pointless, and possibly dangerous. While protectors always feel responsible for the system’s survival, they’ve never succeeded in challenging identity insults that took root in vulnerability.

Our job is to persuade them to risk finding out if Self has the power to falsify identity insults. While we can explain that Self is the guardian who will take care of everyone going forward and that Self can even rescue parts who are trapped in the past, we’re asking them to try something new, and it feels risky. We can’t force them. They have to be willing.

We can, however, promise that it won’t be a careless exercise. When the exile tells its story, other parts will not hear—and we’re not expecting them to learn to tolerate—a tale of defeat and humiliation. Accompanied by Self, in an environment of validation, the exile will have the opportunity to revise its bad experiences in real time and come to a new conclusion: I’m legitimate, my feelings are valid, I’m not alone. A bad thing happened to me, but I’m not bad.

IFS calls this witnessing. We can also call it exposure. As many IFS practitioners have written, the compassion and hand-holding of the IFS brand of exposure is tailor-made to calm uncertainty and falsify negative identity beliefs.

While IFS therapy is accumulating an evidence base, I keep in mind what it shares with various thoroughly researched mental health treatments, not least mindfulness, motivational interviewing, and exposure.
