The 2027 Toyota GRMN Corolla Is Almost Worth the Squeeze

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Revised suspension, new gear ratios, and more torque make the GRMN Corolla more of a blast on track.

Toyota’s GR Corolla has valiantly filled the gap left by the deceased Subaru WRX STI and Mitsubishi Lancer Evo as a rally-inspired compact that combines everyday usability with performance. Combining a torque-rich turbocharged three-cylinder with a manual transmission and all-wheel drive in a practical four-door shell still sounds like a winning formula to us.

But how does it fare if practicality is reduced to sharpen the driving experience? When Toyota announced a limited run of track-focused GR Corollas with no back seat and a $65,655 price tag, alongside some choice mechanical upgrades, the first reaction was skepticism. Why dilute the approachable, jack-of-all-trades appeal of the existing GR Corolla with a more expensive version? To answer that, Toyota took Road & Track to Eagles Canyon Raceway on the outskirts of Dallas, Texas, for the chance to give the GRMN Corolla a proper romp around the track. I’m happy to report that the special edition offered a compelling defense.

Limited to 730 units, the 2027 Toyota GRMN Corolla’s upgrades are primarily focused on improved dynamic response. The suspension, tuned at the Nürburgring and Fuji Speedway, gets shorter, stiffer bump stops up front and longer bump stops in the rear to better deal with circuit curbing and midcorner bumps. Toyota has also firmed up the steering weight, especially at high speeds, and revised front-end aerodynamics to better vent turbulent air through new flaps in the wheel wells and carbon-fiber hood. Wider Michelin Cup 2 tires are also standard, replacing the GR’s Pilot Sport 4s. Driven hard on the elevation-heavy road course, the GRMN felt much more confident and able.

Part of the GR Corolla’s handling charm has historically been its rally-car rowdiness—a tendency to roll and then set into a corner, plus some degree of rear-end playfulness under power and braking—and the revised suspension setup manages to maintain this character. Approaching Turn 12, a descending, off-camber sweeper where the correct line takes the front end over an elevated curb, the GRMN Corolla refused to be unsettled. Similarly, the tendency to swing out the lightened rear end under braking I remember well from the GR Corolla seemed largely negated and easier to correct when it did happen.

The front end of the GRMN Corolla was also sharper than ever, with a rapid turn-in and heavier, confidence-inspiring steering weight. Leaning into Turn 6 at wide-open throttle, the front end didn’t waver as I asked for more steering angle at nearly 100 mph. There was even steering feel, which is a treat on any modern performance car. However, rapid steering changes were sometimes accompanied by a binding feeling, a trait we’ve experienced in previous GR Corolla variants. It was hard to say whether the aerodynamic upgrades contributed to the solid front end, but the addition of the grippier tires no doubt played a significant part in the improved confidence. Back-to-back laps in a standard GR Corolla on Pilot Sport 4s tires proved the GRMN was more planted and offered much better communication of its position and limitations.

Toyota has only made small changes to the 1.6-liter turbocharged inline-three in the GRMN, adding just 7 lb-ft of torque to take the total to 302 lb-ft, accompanied by an unchanged 300 hp. Response felt improved off the line, and there was more top-end grunt too, although the biggest reason for the perception of additional surge was likely the new revised-ratio six-speed manual transmission, which is exclusive to the GRMN.

With a longer first gear and shorter third gear, the car found a good middle ground between the slightly-too-shortness of the STI and the excessive length of something like a Porsche 718. For reference, fourth gear runs out at 105 mph, a good compromise between the thrill of changing gears and having to do so too often. The new, slightly smaller shift knob also felt more ergonomic in my hand, and the updated shifter-cable mount offered more satisfying action.

Of course, a core part of the appeal of the regular GR Corolla is its tunable differential, and this is carried over unchanged on the GRMN. Track mode offered the most rear-biased power split, allowing 70 percent to be sent to the back axle, while Gravel mode split power evenly between both axles. Even in the rear-biased setting, the GRMN Corolla was more liable to push the front end on power than rotate the rear end around.

I actually preferred the GRMN Corolla in Gravel mode around Eagles Canyon Raceway, as the front-wheel-biased performance best practice of trail braking and on-throttle patience more easily led to a clean lap. Brake feel was also improved on the GRMN Corolla, with the GRMN exhibiting no fade over more than half a dozen laps, and there was always more power available through the pedal when asked.

Ergonomically, the GRMN Corolla remains a sneakily competent sports car trapped in a four-door hatchback shape. With new bucket-style seats in red and black upholstery, the GRMN Corolla offered more lateral support at the expense of comfort. For me, the new seats felt thinner and decidedly less comfortable than the set in the standard GR Corolla. Even so, the driving position is low enough and well placed for pedal and shifter access. The pedal box was also spaced for easy heel-toe downshifts, though the intelligent throttle-blipping downshift assistance system means a driver doesn’t need to do this. Similarly, the lack of rear seats didn’t add any obvious level of noise intrusion, although the 66-pound omission came at obvious cost to carrying capacity. All told, the GRMN Corolla improves on the standard GR Corolla formula in nearly every dynamic way, but does that make it worth the premium?

At $65,655, the GRMN Corolla is a hard sell for the average enthusiast buyer. Sure, it is the best-driving GR Corolla yet—they should all come from the factory in this configuration—but it doesn’t have particularly inspiring acceleration nor mind-numbing on-track performance. Its best feature is pure agility, but even rear-driven sports machines like the venerable $65,395 Cadillac CT4-V Blackwing or the $69,550 BMW M2 offer plenty of hustle these days. Sure, there are few modern performance road cars that could outrun the GRMN Corolla on a gravel track, but that’s not a strong enough use case to sway the typical enthusiast buyer away from more horsepower and bigger engines.

Regardless, the GRMN has already proved itself well priced considering the warmth of the response it has so far received from enthusiasts, many seeking to upgrade from the GR Corolla. They will likely snap up every unit, regardless of markup. And they will be happy.
