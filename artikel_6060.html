When the airliner is at or below 15,500 feet, its system can issue a traffic advisory for another airplane whose transponder replies without giving its altitude. The cockpit display leaves off the data tag. It seems at least as plausible (probably more so) than a UFO. So is a false alert.

That said, there were several flase collision warnings approaching Washington National last year, and they were all with an aircraft at exactly 2,300 feet – just like the controller reporting an aircraft at 2,300 feet here.

And American Airlines pilots have reported seeing things out the window. Over Arizona in 2018, a Learjet pilot reported something passing overhead. A controller asked American Airlines flight 1095 to keep an eye out. That crew saw something reflecting light several thousand feet above them, too.

In 2021, a pilot on American Airlines flight 2292 from Cincinnati to Phoenix described a fast-moving cylinder going right over the top of the jet in New Mexico. American initially told me they couldn’t tie the call to their crew. After a debrief, they confirmed it. The FBI closed its file because it lacked jurisdiction.

The day before the Baltimore exchange, the Pentagon released 40 more UFO documents and three videos. One 1952 CIA briefing revisits sightings around the airport this flight had just left. That July, Washington National and Andrews both picked up unexplained radar returns. The Air Force sent up interceptors but came back empty.

My favorite exhibit from Friday is a single radar slide from a 2023 case. Civilian radar got eight hits in 84 seconds. If one object made all eight, it averaged at least 1,650 miles per hour.

Five days before Thanksgiving, the American Eagle pilot already knew what he was going to do with his controller’s report. “It’s a good story to tell my family. I like that.”
