J.P. Morgan Says Tesla’s Robotaxi Fleet Could Match Waymo’s Miles by Late 2027 With 18,000 Cars. The Number That Matters Is How Much Each Car Earns.

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

The robotaxi battle will be won and lost over the total cost of ownership per mile.

By Lee Samaha – Nov 22, 2026 at 8:12AM EST

J.P. Morgan analyst Rajat Gupta believes that Tesla's (TSLA -7.64%) robotaxi fleet could match Alphabet's (GOOG -2.59%) (GOOGL -2.70%) Waymo fleet in cumulative autonomous miles by the end of 2027. Such an event would mark a milestone in Tesla's robotaxi development.

Still, it's not the key metric that really matters, because this is a war likely to be won by a different and even more critical metric. It's such a key number that it will arguably determine Tesla's overall value.

It's important to be clear about this number. Going back to the We, Robot event in 2024, Elon Musk outlined that the operating cost of a Cybercab is "probably going to be $0.20" per mile, with "The price of everything would be about $0.30 to $0.40 per mile," with a Cybercab cost of $30,000.

However, these are Tesla's costs, so any individual buying a Cybercab to operate it as a robotaxi needs to factor in the $299-per-month full self-driving (FSD) subscription fee. Assuming a Cybercab is moderately run for 300,000 miles, it could rack up four years of subscriptions worth $14,352 in FSD costs.

By "everything," Musk presumably means the fully loaded cost per mile, which includes Cybercab cost, energy cost, maintenance, cleaning, remote operations, and any other operational costs, in the TCO per-mile target of $0.30.

This is where the battle over robotaxis will be won, because Tesla is highly likely to face competition from rivals developing low-cost electric vehicles (EVs) equipped with autonomous driving software. While its vision-only approach, leadership in EVs, scaling of Cybercab, and lead in the operational learning that will reduce TCO per mile give it a massive initial edge, it does not mean its robotaxi margins can't be eroded over time.

The argument that bears often use against Tesla is that rivals could eat into its competitive advantage, but I think it's flawed.

First, Tesla's vision-only approach implies lower structural hardware costs than the light detection and ranging (LiDAR) systems used by Waymo and others. Second, autonomous driving software is not free, and robotaxis need to be purposely built for it, both of which add costs for Tesla's rivals.

Third, assuming that rivals could even get to $0.20-per-mile operating costs (or another figure close to where Tesla will get) assumes they can build scale and operational learning that Tesla is doing now. Finally, rivals could go for higher hardware costs (LiDAR plus licensed software) and more miles of robotaxi operation, but that would mean higher up-front costs, probably more maintenance costs, and robotaxis becoming obsolete more quickly.

The good news is that Tesla's miles under robotaxi expansion mean it's learning to reduce TCO per mile before others can. That's a massive edge.
