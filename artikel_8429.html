A deal website’s discount is only as real as the price it’s measured from, and a crossed-out price may be one the item rarely sold for. Use sites that sell their own deals and sites that gather other stores’ discounts, but check the item’s price history and the seller’s return terms before you buy.

Under the Federal Trade Commission’s Guides Against Deceptive Pricing, a seller’s former price is legitimate when the seller offered the item at that price, regularly and openly, for “a reasonably substantial period of time” before the markdown. The guides’ own example of a false bargain is a pen priced at $10 for a few days so the seller can advertise its usual $7.50 as a price cut.

A crossed-out price on a deal site isn’t always the seller’s own former price. It can be the manufacturer’s suggested retail price, a price taken from another store, or a list price the seller sets itself, and the sites below use all three.

So the percentage off says little on its own. Check the item’s price history, what it has actually sold for over recent months, before you trust a discount.

Sites that sell their own deals are best for browsing closeouts and one-day offers. Deal finders are best when you already know what you want and can set an alert for it.

These sites sell the items themselves, so their own shipping and return rules apply.

These sites post other stores’ deals and send you to the store to buy, so that store’s shipping and return rules apply. For the lowest price on one item across stores, use a price comparison site instead, and let a shopping browser extension test coupon codes at checkout.

Read the seller’s return policy before you pay, too. Several sellers above take no returns for a change of mind, and others make you pay the return shipping.

A deal saves money only on something you already meant to buy, at a price near its recent low. If the item wasn’t on your list before you saw the deal, wait 24 hours and see whether you still want it.
