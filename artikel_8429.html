Financing Your First Rental? Expect 15% to 25% Down and a Higher Rate

Finance a rental property with a conventional investment mortgage when your income qualifies, or a DSCR loan, which looks at the rent instead, when it doesn’t. Either way, plan on 15% to 25% down, a rate at least half a percentage point above a home loan’s, and several months of payments in savings. Get preapproved before you make offers.

Lenders charge more on a home you won’t live in because the loan is riskier for them.

If your income qualifies, start with a conventional investment mortgage. Under Freddie Mac rules, it takes 25% down on a building with two to four units and as little as 15% on a single-family rental. Below 20% down, you’ll also pay for mortgage insurance. A duplex you’ll live in counts as your home instead and can take 5% down.

A DSCR loan, short for debt service coverage ratio, qualifies you on how well the property’s rent covers its payment instead of on your income. It usually costs more than a conventional loan, so it’s the fallback when your income won’t qualify, and DSCR lenders often want a credit score of at least 680 and 20% to 25% down. Some banks also make portfolio loans, which they keep rather than sell, so they set their own terms.

On a conventional loan, Fannie Mae’s reserve rule asks for six months of the rental’s full payment, taxes and insurance included, in savings, investments, or vested retirement money. Its rental income rule counts 75% of the expected rent, and if you’ve managed rentals for less than a year, that rent can only cover the property’s own payment, never count as income.

Borrowing the down payment against your own house, with a home equity line of credit, puts the house at risk unless your paycheck can repay the line. Telling a lender you’ll live in a home you plan to rent is what the Federal Housing Finance Agency calls occupancy fraud.

Say you buy a $250,000 rental with 25% down and a 30-year fixed loan at 7.75%, against 7% on a home loan. You’d bring $62,500 plus closing costs and six months of payments in reserve, and pay about $1,343 a month in principal and interest, $96 more than the same loan’s $1,247 at 7%.

Get preapproved by two or three lenders, one of them a local bank or credit union, for the loan you’ll use before you make an offer.
