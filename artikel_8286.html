Idaho Wildlife Commissioner Finally Resigns as Plea Deal in Elk Poaching Scandal Draws Hunter Outrage

The seven-person panel that constitutes Idaho’s Fish and Game Commission is down to six after commissioner Brody Harshbarger resigned Wednesday amid pressure from sportsmen and at least one state lawmaker. Harshbarger took a plea deal on Nov. 9 to dismiss five of seven charges related to illegally killing two bull elk in the region he represents.

“Your service on the commission is concluded, effective immediately,” wrote Gov. Brad Little’s chief of staff, Zach Hauge, in response to Harshbarger’s resignation, obtained by the Idaho Capital Sun. “Maintaining the integrity, credibility and effective operation of the Idaho Fish and Game Commission remains a top priority for Gov. Little.”

Harshbarger was charged in March with seven wildlife violations from the 2025 hunting season, including poaching two bull elk in Fremont County, where he lives. The former commissioner stepped away from commission duties once he was charged, but retained his seat until this week while the case continued, effectively leaving the position vacant.

“I understand everyone deserves their day in court, but commissioners are charged with making and upholding our regulations,” Nick Fasciano, executive director of the Idaho Wildlife Federation, tells Outdoor Life. “And we do expect them to be held to a higher standard. The whole thing is disappointing.”

The commission sets statewide hunting, fishing, and trapping seasons plus bag limits and other regulations based on recommendations and research supplied by Idaho Department of Fish and Game scientists. Each member of the commission can serve two four-year terms, and no more than four commissioners can come from the same political party. Harshbarger is a farmer in Ashton, Idaho. It’s an area known for massive chunks of public land and substantial grain and potato operations.

“I love our wildlife and the community we live in,” Harshbarger’s profile read on the commission’s website before it was removed. “We have a lot of challenges, and I am excited to help continue to solve them. What an amazing place to live and work.”

Harshbarger pled guilty to hunting without a tag and shooting across a road. The remaining five charges, which are being dismissed in exchange for his guilty plea, are two illegal big-game kills, hunting from a vehicle, failure to retrieve game, and trespass (one bull was on private property).

When news of Harshbarger’s plea deal went public, hunters and anglers started contacting regional IDFG offices and the governor’s office to demand his removal. The plea deal means his penalties could be as low as $500 and a one-year hunting suspension when he’s sentenced Dec. 9.

Frustration has also boiled over on social media around Harshbarger’s case.

“Wake up Governor Little and do the right thing,” one Instagram user commented on the news of the plea deal, prior to Harshbarger’s resignation. “You and Senator Risch are already working to sell off our public lands and now you won’t step in to protect our wild game. Shame on you two.”

Harshbarger was appointed by Gov. Little in 2024.

“Public trust is difficult to earn and easily lost,” wrote Idaho state Rep. David Leavitt in an email sent to Little on Tuesday that called for Harshbarger’s dismissal. “Maintaining that trust requires us to uphold the same principles regardless of who is involved. Idaho citizens deserve nothing less.”

Little is also now in charge of appointing the director of IDFG. The commission did that until this year, when Little signed a publicly opposed bill into law changing the procedure from a seven-person decision to a one-person appointment by the governor, with confirmation by the state Senate.

“Commissioners and the director now serve at the pleasure of the governor,” Fasciano says. “The commission is not empowered to boot out one of their own members even if they were so inclined.”

Current IDFG director Jim Fredericks, a career fisheries biologist, was already leading the agency when the new law took effect July 1, making him the state’s last director selected by the commission.

“The actual department of Fish and Game is going to take some heat for this [Harshbarger incident], but they weren’t the decision makers here,” Fasciano says. “The commission and the director were not able to drive a resolution here and they are also not responsible for the sentencing. A lot of frustration is going to be directed toward them, that isn’t their fault.”
