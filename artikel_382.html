Chinas moon plan mirrors NASAs, except for one big difference

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

China's plan to land people on the moon by 2030 mirrors NASA's on paper but skips crewed lunar test flights, trusting robotic rehearsals for its first human landing instead.

Chinese space officials laid out their approach at the 7th Manned Space Symposium in Beijing on Oct. 31, according to Xinhua, the Chinese government's wire service. A slide reportedly from the presentation posted by CNSpaceflight, an independent outlet that tracks the Chinese space program, shows two of the initial missions with nobody aboard. A crew flies the Mengzhou, or "Dream Vessel," capsule to Earth orbit in 2027. Then the robots take over. The Lanyue, or "Embracing the Moon," lander touches down on its own in 2028, and an uncrewed mission rehearses the full landing sequence in 2029.

"For any crewed mission, we will try to fly it in an unmanned state first," said Wang Xiang, chief designer of the Chinese lunar exploration program, in the English captions of a CCTV report, China's state-run broadcaster.

The split cuts to the heart of a heated new space race and reveals two different institutional cultures. China's tight-lipped, military-run program trusts machines to prove its lunar hardware works. NASA's civilian program, working in public view to land astronauts first in 2028, leverages human test pilots. U.S. leaders say they're reviving the Apollo playbook that beat the Soviet Union to the moon: put crews on key test flights, launch often, and build on each mission.

"It's not entrepreneurial thinking or tech industry pixie dust or some new management philosophy," said NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman at a news conference on Oct. 23. "It's a playbook this agency knows really well — the same playbook that went from Apollo 7 splashdown to Apollo 11 in just nine months."

NASA has already flown one of those crewed test flights. Artemis II sent four astronauts around the moon in April. Next year, the four-person Artemis III crew will spend about two weeks in Earth orbit practicing maneuvers to dock the Orion spaceship with test versions of two commercial landers.

One lander is from Blue Origin, and the other is from SpaceX. Astronauts will climb into Blue Origin's lander to check its air and life-support systems, but will only briefly dock with Starship.

Earlier this year, NASA decided to do that test as a separate mission, shoehorned between Artemis II and the first crewed moon landing. If something goes wrong, "we can come home much more quickly," explained Jeremy Parsons, who manages NASA's Artemis program.

Apollo followed roughly the same script. Four crewed test flights came before Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin landed in July 1969. China's first crew to head for the moon will be the one that lands.

Despite this major difference between the plans, NASA will lean on some robotics, too. SpaceX aims to land an uncrewed Starship on the moon by the end of 2027, and Blue Origin is working on its own uncrewed demonstration after Artemis III. NASA will keep both landers in the running as long as possible before picking one for its first crew, while China is building a single lander.

In other ways, the two plans look alike. Two Long March-10 rockets will launch China's capsule and lander separately, and the pair will link up in orbit around the moon. Two of the three crew members will then ride the lander to the surface and back. NASA's Orion carries four astronauts but also sends just two down. Blue Origin's lander even follows China's route, flying to lunar orbit on its own to meet the crew. SpaceX breaks the pattern, docking its Starship with Orion near Earth and pushing the spaceship to the moon.

China says its uncrewed flights will make the first crewed landing safer. Its robotic lander also will gather some scientific data, measuring how hot the lunar surface gets and how the dust behaves. Its 2029 rehearsal will run every step, from docking in lunar orbit to landing, lifting off and docking again. The program has already completed early tests of its rocket, capsule, and lander.

The race, Isaacman warned, "will be much closer than it ever needed to be," emphasizing that China has the spacecraft, landers, and a "credible two-launch architecture" to put taikonauts, the Chinese term for astronauts, on the moon within three years.

NASA's own deadlines have slipped repeatedly. Artemis first aimed for a 2024 landing, and Isaacman recently acknowledged that Artemis III was running about three months behind schedule.

He conceded that urgency has its limits: "No amount of my enthusiasm or schedule pressure changes the laws of physics."
