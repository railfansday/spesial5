GPT-6 Astra and Opus 5.5 expose a blind spot in AI oversight

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

In a METR exercise, Opus 5.5 scored GPT-6 Astra’s proposed AI tests about 0.2 points higher on a 0–1 scale than a human scorer did. A gap that large could affect which tests get pursued.

In METR’s test of AI oversight, Opus 5.5 gave GPT-6 Astra’s proposed AI tests higher scores than the human evaluator did. METR said the gap could change whether its scoring rules recommended pursuing a test. In a separate exercise, leading AI agents placed ahead of most human entrants across 15 past programming contests.

Across Al Zimmermann’s programming contests, METR reported median placements from the 96th to the 98th percentile for Fable 5.1, Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra. About four people still beat the strongest models in the median contest.

The agents had 48 hours per contest on a 28-core machine. Each submission gave them a numerical score they could use to see whether their changes improved the result. Human contestants typically had about three calendar months, used their own hardware and could see the public standings. METR compared the agents’ scores with the original contest results.

In a separate exercise, METR asked agents to design tests that would help researchers assess what AI models could do. AI judges and a human scorer had access to project files, scores from trial runs and the agents’ case for why their tests were relevant and feasible. The human scorer assessed whether each proposed test was worth including in METR’s suite. METR said none of the designs was useful enough to include in its report.

The AI judges tended to agree more closely with the human scorer when a weaker model had designed the test. When the model that designed the test was as capable as the judge or stronger, the judges tended to rate the work more highly than the human did. Opus 5.5 agreed most closely with the human scorer, yet rated GPT-6 Astra’s proposals about 0.2 points higher on average on a scale from zero to one.

The comparison came from one exercise, and what counted as useful depended in part on METR’s research priorities. METR said models underused some resources in the judgment tests. It also reported strong model performance on some longer machine-learning tasks with automatic scoring. A separate AI peer review audit tested whether reviewers’ objections changed the findings by running code on the underlying data.
