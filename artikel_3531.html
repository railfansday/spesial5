Cheeky Malware Linked To Popular Gacha Game Cracks City Security

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

East Asia--and particularly Japan--has been suffering a rash of cybersecurity incidents as of late, affecting everything from major banks and cellphone service providers to city governments and small businesses. Gaming has been one of many areas targeted: between arcade games on Sega's network going offline, 11 million Capcom IDs being compromised, and a high-schooler hacking Bandai-Namco, some major publishers have been suffering a string of embarrassments.

One recent incident involved the PC launcher for the popular gacha action shooter NIKKE: Goddess of Victory, where malicious code would install a trojan called k2f that would compromise an individual's PC, taking screenshots, logging inputs, and installing/executing programs. Unfortunately for one Japanese municipality, someone's gacha habits may have lead to a much bigger incident.

Iwamizawa is a city of about 75,000 located in Hokkaido, Japan's northernmost island. It's not a place that makes a lot of news, but it recently went viral on the Japanese-speaking side of social media. As reported by Automaton, the city issued a notice that an IT sub-sub-contractor handling the city's fixed asset records suffered a security breach--specifically, a k2f hijack. Observers soon surmised that the most likely source of k2f would be someone playing a certain sexy gacha game on a work PC.

The subcontractor in question, IT services company GIS Hokkaido, would then confirm to Japanese tech news site ITMedia that, yes, NIKKE's compromised PC launcher was the source of the problem. When asked if an employee or subcontractor had launched the game on a work PC, GIS politely replied that "they would prefer not to comment."

Moral of the story? While there's nothing wrong with playing a suggestive gacha game on your own time, you probably shouldn't do it on the clock, and definitely not on a company PC, lest you become internet meme fodder.
