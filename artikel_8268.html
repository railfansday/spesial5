Space Mirrors Promise Daylight in the Dark, But Rescue Teams Aren’t Convinced

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

A floodlight operated by Reflect Orbital illuminates the night sky. (Photo: Kern County Sheriff’s Office)

In the middle of a dark night on the foothills of Mt. Whitney, onlookers were suddenly flooded by a cone of light 3 miles wide by 10 miles long.

The source was a high-powered floodlight, placed nearby as part of a test exercise by a startup called Reflect Orbital. The Los Angeles-based company’s mission is to “launch the future of sunlight” by placing a swarm of mirrored satellites in low Earth orbit built to redirect sunlight anywhere on Earth on demand. The ground-based floodlight was designed to mimic that effect.

Among a wide range of use cases including agriculture, entertainment, and defense, the company claims rescue as one potential use of its tech in the coming years.

On a country road outside Lone Pine, California, a handful of seasoned rescue volunteers and public safety officials would find out how much — if any — a light field roughly the size of Manhattan and twice as bright as a full moon would improve their chances.

Phillip Cash, operations leader for China Lake Mountain Rescue Group, said his team arrived at the site with optimism.

“My team is largely scientists, engineers, technicians,” he told DarkSky International. “We live for this sort of stuff.”

Group leaders reported to the San Francisco Chronicle and DarkSky International that they envisioned very limited uses for the technology in rescue. They struggled with glare, saw little promise of benefit for helicopter crews, and realized the light could also shut off unexpectedly.

In the test, China Lake MRG and Inyo County SAR rehearsed standard search-and-rescue tasks twice — once in the dark, once under the big light. Crews ran grid searches, stretcher carries, and rope rigging.

Cash first noticed the light made it easier to move around on the ground. His peripheral vision was improved, he told DarkSky, and he didn’t have to worry about stepping in a hole.

But from there, the efficacy mostly faded.

Glare was an issue. While that may or may not be the case with a satellite as the source, Cash noted most rescuers already wear hats at night for this very reason. Limiting any light from the sky or ambient sources preserves night vision.

Another limiting factor of the test was that the organizers placed white pylons in the field as rescue targets, instead of the usual manikins. This created artificial reflectivity that helped Cash’s team find their marks more easily. But, he told DarkSky, his team realized the light could have a problematic effect on actual human subjects — especially anyone lost.

“They’re more likely to go, ‘Oh, look at all this light. Maybe I can find my way back now,’” he said.

Cash was also doubtful it would help SAR helicopter crews locate or navigate at night, noting they already use night vision as standard practice.

His takeaway: For the most part, it’s better to leave the daylight in the daytime.

“It’s not going to make a huge difference to wilderness search and rescue except in a few cases,” Cash told the Chronicle. “We’re pretty good at working at night already.”

Officials’ minds remain open to the technology. A spokesperson for the Kern County Sheriff’s Office told Backpacker that they intend to let SAR groups lead the way on any future implementation. That’s particularly the case with a team like China Lake MRG, a nonprofit that supports its equipment bills mainly through fundraising.

“Given the circumstances, if we needed it, it would definitely be something we would utilize,” said Lori Meza, Kern County Public Information Officer. “But we have no idea what it would cost.”

That price tag could be a real issue. While Reflect Orbital has not officially published any pricing information for its prospective services, Sky & Telescope reported that the company plans to charge around $5,000 per hour for its services, with constellations of multiple satellites — necessary to recreate the kind of bright conditions the team experienced — costing far more. (By comparison, an Airbus H125 helicopter, like the Kern County Sheriff’s Office operates, costs about $2,500 an hour to run, according to charter operator Craft.)

“Most search and rescue teams, at least in California, will not be able to afford it,” Cash told DarkSky. “They won’t even think about affording it. We cannot pay for that sort of thing.”

Emphasizing that any cost was still unknown, Meza said Kern County officials only buy equipment for SAR teams in limited cases. She gave the example of a purchase of vehicles several years ago.

“We do provide certain resources, and there might be an isolated case here or there,” she said. “But a purchase like a contracted service would be a new thing.”

She added that the Sheriff’s Office isn’t taking a position on the technology itself, and that they were grateful to be part of the test.

Other observers are less neutral. DarkSky International has functioned as a sort of public watchdog on Reflect Orbital, lobbying for what it sees as responsible development. Its five principles of outdoor lighting have been widely adopted, and published by the U.S. Department of Energy.

DarkSky sees the same gap in the technology for rescue as China Lake MRG — usefulness, which is also principle one of five.

“The first question is ‘is it needed?’” said Drew Reagan, DarkSky senior marketing and communications manager. “And in this case, the rescue groups found the need was pretty minimal.”

Many of the potential impacts of Reflect’s plan are unknown, because the government has allowed it to move forward with no environmental review.

If its plans become reality, the change in the night environment could be massive. The Federal Communications Commission has given the green light for Reflect to launch its first functional satellite. Its launch plan would place 50,000 in orbit by 2035. Even if they weren’t de facto floodlights, Reagan pointed out, the reflectivity of that many satellites alone would noticeably brighten the night sky everywhere on Earth.

“This represents a fundamentally new technology that will have a direct impact. Not only for the dark sky community or astrotourists, but for migratory birds, for insects,” Reagan said. “It’s not DarkSky’s goal to plunge the world into medieval darkness. We want to be part of these conversations. We just want to make sure we look before we launch.”
