How This Year’s Movies From ‘Artificial’ to ‘I Play Rocky’ Turn Political Anxiety Into Compelling Stories

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

For years, Simon Rich wanted to sound the alarm about AI.Rich’s childhood friend Dan Selsam worked as a researcher at OpenAI; Selsam showed Rich, a writer known for humorous essays and for the film “An American Pickle,” an early version of the software, and the two friends discussed its ramifications.“It seemed to have real contempt for mankind,” Rich says. “It scared the hell out of me.” Rich published a book, “which was completely ignored,” and found himself striking out in his attempts to get attention with magazine articles. “So, in desperation,” he recalls, “I was like, ‘Maybe if I got a movie star…’”He got Andrew Garfield. In “Artificial,” Garfield plays OpenAI CEO Sam Altman as a blithely destructive sociopath who manipulates his way into the center of a potentially civilization-ending technological advance. Rich uses the juicy character to tell a story about the most consequential political issue of the present moment.“I’ve been living with knowledge of this danger for at least five years, due to my fluke friendship with this scientist,” he says. (Selsam issued a public warning about AI’s increasingly uncontrollable behavior in September.) “I wrote this film out of a desperation to communicate to the world this information.”That desperation may be unique to Rich. But the desire to use the tools of screenwriting to illuminate political concerns is certainly not. Had “Artificial” simply been an enumeration of AI’s dangers, it’d likely have attracted as much attention as Rich’s book; instead, it’s a well-told story with a sharp argument at its core. That makes it one of several movies this year that use character and incident — the stuff of quality storytelling — to illuminate their writers’ very real political concerns.That goes for the year’s most-discussed blockbusters: Writer-director Christopher Nolan has imbued “The Odyssey” with a rueful sense of what it means to lose one’s humanity in wartime, while, if it’s anything like its two predecessor films, “Dune: Part Three,” written by Denis Villeneuve and Brian K. Vaughan, promises to examine the uses, and misuses, of power.But it’s showing up across more human-scale dramas this fall, as well. Take “I Play Rocky.” The film depicts the writing and production of “Rocky,” which — before it was an Oscar-winning smash — was the Hail Mary attempt of unknown actor Sylvester Stallone (played by Anthony Ippolito) to write himself a place in the industry. For screenwriter Peter Gamble, the story has resonances well beyond Hollywood history.“‘Rocky’ came out right after the Vietnam War, and it was a time when people needed to believe,” Gamble says. Stallone, in the film’s telling, is the ultimate underdog; Gamble, who himself yearned for a screenwriting breakout, could relate. The message of the film, the writer says, is intended in part for his children: “They’re like, ‘We’re all going to die from global warming. If not, AI is going to kill us next week.’ What power do I have in the universe at all? We need to believe in ourselves again.”American history provides many stories that feel akin to the charged atmosphere of recent years: One, as Gamble notes, is the demoralization of the Vietnam War, and another is the cataclysm of the Great Depression. “The Weight,” written by Matthew Booi and Shelby Gaines, is a rip-roaring adventure film in which unjustly imprisoned convict Samuel Murphy (Ethan Hawke) has the chance to win his freedom by transporting gold bricks across an unruly wilderness. The viewer roots for Murphy in part because he’s compellingly played by a movie star, and in part because the boot under which he struggles looks, at a moment of increased attention to mass incarceration and wealth inequality, familiar.“You don’t want it to be didactic and message-y or preachy in any way,” Gaines says. “But the idea was to try and tell a good story on a personal, individual level and hope that it would echo sympathetically with things that happen on a larger social level — both during the Great Depression and today.” Murphy’s journey depicts the stakes of its historical moment in frankly personal terms — his loss of freedom means the loss of access to his daughter, a crisis the film treats with gravity and righteous anger.“Abstraction doesn’t help you as a writer much,” Gaines says. “If you want to convey something, the heart is the place to start.”Or you can go for the gut. “Ink,” which James Graham adapted from his own stage play, makes the case that Rupert Murdoch (Guy Pearce) and newspaper editor Larry Lamb (Jack O’Connell) created the venal media world we live in today. The dawning realization that Lamb, especially, is a force for evil hits particularly hard because Graham has, in the film’s early going, taken pains to make him so charming and his exploits so rootable.“You need these people to surprise you,” Graham says, “so that you want to be in that newsroom — and suddenly, weirdly, for a brief moment, you might even want them to get one over on the establishment.” How did tabloid culture gain such purchase? Because, as the film’s characters make clear, we’re willing to pay for it.“Being Heumann” makes us root for its band of rebels, too — but, in this case, they’re working to bring about a better world. The biopic, written by Sian Heder and Rebekah Taussig, tells the story of Judith Heumann (Ruth Madeley), the activist who led the 1977 demonstration over the government’s failure to enforce disability-rights legislation. By its nature, the film is explicitly political, but its screenplay is also the product of a thoughtful, careful approach to representation and inclusivity.Taussig, who, like the late Heumann, uses a wheelchair, saw a certain power in learning about — and then writing about — the many activists Heumann helped organize. “What was so exciting about the research,” she says, “was this gold mine of learning about these absolutely compelling people.”The first-time screenwriter had grown up seeing “very little disability representation — and [when there was], it always felt so flat. There is a richness to the experience of disability that gets missed if you don’t live it and you can’t portray it.”Taussig’s ability to mine her own insights enriches the film, one that Heder, an Oscar-winner for “CODA,” intended to inspire and delight audiences, as well as educate them. “It’s very important to me that this story reach the widest audience possible,” Heder says, “because the events of the story actually were about a triumph. In a time where a lot of us are feeling really helpless, it was so important to express disabled joy and humor. I wanted to take the audience on a really entertaining ride, where they could leave seeing the world differently.”“Fjord” has a similar reframing effect — though its true-life basis is substantially more downbeat than Judith Heumann’s story. Cristian Mungiu took inspiration from the story of the Bodnariu family, an immigrant couple whose five children were taken away by the Norwegian state amid accusations of child abuse. “Fjord” presents the story of Mihai and Lisbet Gheorghiu (Sebastian Stan and Renate Reinsve) in a who-do-you-believe social drama, one with painful repercussions.“What I’m looking into, in the stories that I pick up,” Mungiu says, “is this possibility of speaking about something bigger than just that story.” For its writer-director, “Fjord” addresses the painful consequences of globalization, as people from different traditions find that they cannot in fact understand one another. “It’s not a reenactment” of the Bodnariu story, he says: “That’s the starting point of a story in which everything is structured to speak about the relationship between the majority and the individual.”“Fjord” won the top prize at this year’s Cannes; the runner-up, “Minotaur,” also places a family drama in counterpoint with a story of major import. Here, a story of marital infidelity (based on Claude Chabrol’s 1969 film “The Unfaithful Wife”) plays out in 2022 as Russia is scaling up its military actions against Ukraine.Writer-director Andrey Zvyagintsev had been working to put the film together for years — and, as he wrote, the mobilization against Ukraine was all over the news. “It was a very logical thing,” he says through a translator. “I started to fill gaps in the script with the realities of what was happening.” Like Simon Rich trying to spread the word against AI, Zvyagintsev used his script to convey what he was hearing, like local mayors asking businesspeople to choose which of their employees will be conscripted. “This is not something I took from the air,” Zvyagintsev says. “This is real information that came by phone.
