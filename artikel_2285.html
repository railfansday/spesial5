How football fanzines gave supporters a voice and changed the game forever

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

As has been evident once again during the opening months of the new season, Scottish football fans are not exactly slow to voice their displeasure or shy about agitating for change.

If they are unhappy with how their team is performing on the park or the way their club is being run off it, then they tend to make their feelings known.

Such activism is very much a modern-day phenomenon. So why do rabble-rousers stage protests in the streets, gather in angry mobs outside of stadiums, hold up inflammatory banners in the stands and boycott matches and merchandise when they are aggrieved about a perceived injustice?

Daniel Gray, the English author, editor of Scottish football quarterly Nutmeg and presenter of the When Saturday Comes podcast, believes the fanzine movement which reached its zenith in the 1980s and 1990s altered the landscape of the sport forever and ultimately emboldened the present-day supporter.

Gray’s fascination with fanzines – which can be traced back to the discarded copy of Fly Me To The Moon that he retrieved from the Ayresome Park terraces at a Middlesbrough match as an eight-year-old boy – has led to him penning a fascinating history of the independently produced publications which have long held him in thrall.

Fanzine: The Story of Football’s Alternative Press drives home how much professional football has changed, both for good and for ill, in the past five decades and underlines the role played in that transformation by those who grew frustrated at their voices not being heard during what was unquestionably a dark period in the game.

“Fanzines have led directly to so much of what we see going on in the sport today,” he said. “They were the first time that fans had ever spoken up for themselves. Before that, where and when did you ever hear from supporters?

“Maybe you got the odd letter in the local newspaper from time to time. But that would have been about it. There was never any criticism in the matchday programme. But finding a collective voice paved the way for what came next. It empowered fans.

“There was a definite reaction to fanzines. Clubs, politicians and the police were all terrified about having their authority questioned for the first time. Nobody would think twice about questioning the way the game is run now. There is a lineage back to fanzines in that.

“I am completely in awe of the people who were behind the whole fanzine movement. It would have been very easy for them to sit and moan and do nothing about the state of football in the 1980s. But they did something else altogether.”

Daniel Gray's new book Fanzine (Image: Promotional)

The Shamrock is widely considered to be the first football fanzine in the United Kingdom. It was a short-lived publication which John Doogan, a member of the Shamrock Celtic Supporters Club in Edinburgh, launched in 1963 due to his growing frustration at how poorly the Parkhead club was faring.

It contained poisonous attacks on the board and scathing analysis of the team and players, was sold outside the stadium on matchdays and became, much to the annoyance of the besieged hierarchy, popular due to the widespread disenchantment among the support.

Gray has seen a copy of the first edition which is held in the extensive fanzine archives in the National Library of Scotland in the capital. He is firmly of the opinion it is as important a tome as its prized copy of the Gutenberg Bible. “It is quite something,” he said. “It is up there with all of their ancient relics for me.”

It was, though, a different era that he was most interested in. Fanzine focuses on a turbulent time of seismic change in British football.

“I honed in on the 10-year period between the mid-1980s, when the fanzine boom really started, and the mid-1990s, when modern football, in inverted commas, came into being,” he said.

“I came across research which claimed, and it was a conservative estimate, that a million fanzines were sold in the 1980s and 1990s. It became this mass movement. I wanted to tell the story of that movement, but also to tell the story of that time in football through the eyes of the fans.

“I wanted to show what fans were thinking about and feeling. I knew the conditions fans experienced when they went to a match were bad, but I didn't realise quite how bad until I started to go through those thousands of fanzines. There is a lot of stuff about how fans were treated by the police and how unsafe a lot of the grounds were.

“The public perception of football fans was just so bad. The perception was that all fans were hooligans. Fanzines came about to show that was untrue, to show that a lot of fans were funny and bright and talked about music and politics and books and other things. They really did reshape the way the fans were perceived.”

Gray added, “One of the greatest agonies of going through all of these old fanzines was seeing how prescient many of the writers were. A lot of them were saying grounds were inherently unsafe, a lot of them were warning that bad things were going to happen.

“Sure enough, there were then these big, horrible events, the disasters at Bradford, at Heysel and at Hillsborough. It was far, far worse than anyone could possibly have imagined.

Meadowbank Thistle fanzine AWOL (Image: Promotional)

“So their writing was incredibly perceptive. The quality of the writing, the campaigning and the solidarity between them all helped to change both the way fans were viewed and the conditions in which they watched matches.

“In the mid-1980s, you didn't admit you were a football fan in polite company. By the early 1990s, television networks and radio stations were desperate to have fan-based content. Celebrities, too, were trying to attach themselves to football. A lot changed in a short journey.”

But it was the edgy, often bizarre, frequently hilarious humour in the fanzines which Gray, and so many football supporters of his vintage, adored. He found himself in stitches as he perused the pages of beloved Scottish titles like AWOL, the fabled Meadowbank Thistle publication which is highly prized by collectors.

“I came across an Arbroath fanzine from the late 1980s called Tomato Soup and Lentils in the National Library archives," he said. "It had a bit of the Brechin hedge sellotaped to it. It was a free gift.

“I spoke to Archie MacGregor of the marvellous The Absolute Game and he felt that Scottish fanzines were different from English fanzines and perhaps had more humour in them because fans up here in Scotland weren't faced with as many obstacles as fans in England were. ID cards, for example, were being threatened down south at one point.

“So the tone was slightly different here in Scotland, there was probably a bit more room for sarcasm. They were very sardonic and quite surreal at times. They were far more earnest in England.

“Generally speaking, the lower down the leagues the club was, the better the fanzine it had. There was far more for their fans to moan about so it made for much better content. So the St Mirren and Dundee United fanzines would tend to have a far higher level of sarcasm.

“Meadowbank Thistle had a strangely large number of fanzines for such a small club. AWOL was utterly fantastic. It is loved by a lot of people. There was some football content, but on the whole they wrote about other things.

“They once had an article that was headlined 'The Shoplifter’s Guide to Edinburgh'. That was a bit too much for the club and those responsible got hauled in to answer some questions. But that was fairly typical of the strand of Scottish humour that ran through fanzines. There was definitely surrealism.”

The origin stories of the titles, which include Brian Moore's Head Looks Uncannily Like London Planetarium (Gillingham), The Deranged Ferret (Lincoln City), The Memoirs of Seth Bottomley (Port Vale) and 53 Miles West of Venus (Preston North End), were often as funny as the stories contained within.

A fanzine seller at Turf Moor (Image: Phil Oldham / Shutterstock)

Gray particularly liked Wendy Who?, which St Johnstone fans named after a song they regularly belted out during matches at their old Muirton Park home.

"Oh Wendy Saints, oh Wendy Saints, oh Wendy Saints go marching in . . . "

“I loved a Dundee United fanzine called Can I Bring My Dog?," said Gray. "It was a question a United fan asked the organiser of a supporters' bus before a trip to Denmark for a European game. I thought that was brilliant.

“There’s A Store Where the Creatures Meet was a St Mirren fanzine which the author Christopher Brookmyre wrote for. Those are the lyrics from The Doors’ song Love Street, which was obviously the name of their old ground.

“The crossover between music magazines and football fanzines was huge. Quite often when I was going through old fanzines a flexi-disc would fall out."

Gray continued, “Very creative people got involved during what was a really energising time in football. There was an awful lot of quality writing, including by people like Christopher Brookmyre, Irvine Welsh and Neil Forsyth.

“The first time Neil saw his words in print was in a Dundee United fanzine. Irvine wrote for Hibs fanzines under various pseudonyms. He probably cut his teeth writing for Hibs Monthly in the late 1980s. Anyone could send something in. There was a wonderful democracy to it.”

Internet message boards, social media websites and short-form video platforms are the outlets which football supporters use to vent their spleens in 2026. The digital age, though, has by no means brought about an end to fanzines. Indeed, Gray found they are enjoying something of a revival.

“There are around 70 printed fanzines still left,” he said. “A lot of them are old titles like Fly Me To The Moon at Middlesbrough, A Love Supreme at Sunderland and The City Gent at Bradford. But there are new fanzines which younger supporters are producing as well.

“I am sensing a real desire to get away from screens and to embrace this very old medium. I think football supporters love having something ephemeral. They love an object that marks them being at a match.

“Few clubs use physical tickets now and fewer clubs are producing matchday programmes now. So maybe fanzines are becoming your souvenir of the day, of the game.

“It has been a bit unexpected, but it is happening. I see a real interest in getting away from the anger and the hot air of YouTube channels, social media sites and radio phone-ins and a desire to just be a bit more reflective.

A Manchester United fanzine seller at Old Trafford (Image: Philip Oldham/BPI / Shutterstock)

“I don't think there's going to be a full-scale renaissance or a return to what the movement once was. It is similar to the rise in popularity of vinyl records among music lovers, which nobody saw coming. I do feel there is a space for them, a role for them.

“I certainly yearn for the fanzine, for the content that was in them and for the tone of them. So much football writing now is about tactics and transfers. I miss something that is barely relevant to the match I was at, that is full of humour and that reflects what fans actually talk about when they stand behind the goal.

“Yes, the 1980s was undoubtedly a horrific time to watch football. But there were elements to it, like the affordability that went out the window, in the Premier League anyway, when all-seater stadiums came in during the early 1990s, that I do miss.”

Fanzine was a real labour of love for Gray and it shows. It is a forensically detailed, beautifully crafted, utterly engrossing account of the glorious heyday of the often bonkers but invariably brilliant magazines which football fanatics who were determined to have their say produced in their bedrooms, in their garden sheds and on their kitchen tables.

There is, with Scottish magazines like Cappielow Bugle (Morton), Follow Follow (Rangers), Northern Light (Aberdeen), Not The View (Celtic), Railway Ender (Ayr United) and Walking Down The Halbeath Road (Dunfermline) covered, plenty of interest for fans from these shores.

“It was a privilege to write it,” he said. “The sort of person who started a fanzine wasn't really in it for an ego trip or to further their career. Sometimes it takes an outsider like me to say, ‘What you did was incredible’.”

Fanzine: The Story of Football’s Alternative Press by Daniel Gray is published by Bloomsbury Sport, costs £20, and is available to buy now.
