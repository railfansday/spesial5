Australian music industry rejects ‘divide and conquer’ proposal to give musicians exemption from AI scraping

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Reported proposal to give musicians special treatment is ‘unworkable’, Aria chief executive says

Get our daily political email, free app or daily news podcast

The music industry has declared it will not accept an AI carveout deal that leaves other creative sectors behind, rejecting a reported proposal to give musicians special treatment while allowing big tech to train on other copyrighted material – described by David Pocock as a “divide and conquer strategy”.

The trade union representing writers and creatives is “alarmed” at a proposal to let AI companies train on Australian data if they buy a permit, an idea also strongly rebuffed by the Coalition and Greens.

The Albanese government would not comment on the reported proposal but senior ministers have raised concerns about current copyright law in recent days, hinting reforms were needed.

Annabelle Herd, the chief executive of the Australian Recording Industry Association (Aria), and Claire Pullen, the CEO of the Australian Writers’ Guild, said the creative community – which this week launched a major AI campaign – would remain united in rejecting such changes.

“I can’t see any creative workers leaving their colleagues behind,” Pullen said.

The Australian Financial Review reported on Thursday the government was considering a copyright change under which AI companies would buy permits to train on Australian content but that music may be exempted. Such a proposal would be more in line with AI companies after Anthropic suggested a model of “conditional approval” to scrape content unless a rightsholder objected, and OpenAI told a parliamentary committee it would “potentially” support paying into a fund for creators.

Guardian Australia was not able to independently verify details of the proposal. One senior member of the creative community, involved in copyright discussions with government, said they had heard some details of such an idea – but others said they had not and doubted the Albanese government would seriously entertain it. One source in the AI industry, who declined to be named, said they would welcome such a plan.

Herd said she was unaware of the proposal before Thursday but the music industry would not desert other creatives.

“Had we been consulted, we would have advised this scheme is unnecessary, unworkable and designed to suit the commercial interests of AI companies, not protect creators,” she told Guardian Australia.

“Our position is unchanged: we stand with the rest of the creative and media industries.”

Herd and Pullen said such a scheme would be difficult to implement technically, questioning how content such as movies or TV shows – which combine copyrighted elements such as music, video and screenwriting – would be treated if just one element was exempted from AI scraping.

“This just seems like such a convoluted path, when AI companies have had an open invitation to sit down and negotiate for years,” Pullen said. “We have been working with government to come up with a model since 2024. We’ve come up with solutions and tech has steadfastly refused to even talk.”

While the prime minister, Anthony Albanese, has declared it would be “theft” if creatives weren’t paid for their work, and that artists “need to be compensated”, some AI companies have argued it is too difficult to make individual licensing deals under existing copyright law.

The Media, Entertainment and Arts Alliance said it would be “alarmed” at the reported proposal and demanded protections promised by Albanese “must apply to all creative sectors”.

While government MPs declined to comment on the report, senior Labor figures have recently begun more forcefully pointing to what they claim are shortfalls in copyright law in the AI age – despite major rightsholder groups rejecting the need for change. The industry minister, Tim Ayres, on Tuesday said “the status quo is no good” for creative workers, a line repeated by the acting prime minister, Richard Marles, on Thursday. Marles added on News24: “People are being ripped off”.

The attorney general, Michelle Rowland, also used newly strengthened language when contacted, promising “certainty and strong protections for Australian artists”.

“Right now, the work of Australian creatives is being used without their consent or payment,” a spokesperson for Rowland said.

“The government has long advocated for tech companies to strike commercial deals with Australian artists, and we will continue to do so.”

Potentially carving out musicians did not win support among parliamentarians, either.

The shadow attorney general, Michaelia Cash, claimed such a proposal would “divide the rightsholders”.

“This ham-fisted tactic should be strongly resisted. Everyone who owns property should have the same right for it not to be stolen.”

Pocock also described it as “some sort of divide and conquer strategy”.

“It’s hard to know what [Labor] are actually doing at the moment. The joint select committee hasn’t even given its report to the government, and we’re hearing all sorts of whispers about what the government is planning.”

The Greens senator Sarah Hanson-Young urged the government not to meet copyright demands requested by big tech.

“What they’re asking Australia to do actually is to undermine copyright the world over,” she told News 24. “Australia will become known as the Cayman Islands for AI if we allow this loophole to happen.

“I say, don’t be that stupid. Stand up to them.”
