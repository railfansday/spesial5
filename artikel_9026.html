Some games you remember as brilliant, and others you remember as brilliant because you were 12, sitting in front of an Amiga with a joystick in one hand and a pile of floppy disks beside you. Alien Breed is probably a bit of both.

Team17's sci-fi shooter celebrates its 35th anniversary with this new collection, bringing together six games from the series' original Amiga era (read my Apidya review for more Amiga gaming). That's quite a lot of history in one package, taking in the original top-down shooters and the later, more ambitious attempts to bring the series into the 3D era.

And there's a lot to like about the way this collection has been assembled. Alongside the games is an interactive archive filled with original artwork, developer interviews, soundtracks, design documents and even material from Alien Breed projects that never made it to release. It's a proper look back at a British studio finding its feet during one of gaming's most interesting periods.

But one question hangs over it all: are these games still fun to play?

The collection includes Alien Breed (1991), Alien Breed: Special Edition '92, Alien Breed II: The Horror Continues, Alien Breed: Tower Assault, Alien Breed 3D and Alien Breed 3D II: The Killing Grounds.

The first four are the games most people will remember, and I played more of the classic top-down 2D shooters, with their overhead perspective, maze-like levels, and hordes of aliens waiting around every corner. There's a little Gauntlet in the DNA, a bit of Smash TV, and more than a hint of James Cameron's Aliens. You explore abandoned space stations, collect ammunition, hunt for keys, and try not to get overwhelmed by the growing number of creatures closing in, often because of a brutal respawn system.

It's a simple formula, but Team17 – now a publisher of some of the best indie games – knew how to make it entertaining. There's a nice rhythm to working through a level, finding supplies and opening up new areas, although the difficulty can be pretty unforgiving.

Playing those early games now in the 35th Anniversary Collection showcases both the strengths and weaknesses of the series. These games are brutally fun when you take time to learn the maps, play and replay, remember where keys are located, and manage the alien 'breed' and its AI by keeping it locked behind corridor walls.

But it can also become overburdened by the horde; without the right weapons, life is short-lived, and it can be frustrating to waste keys and then not be able to unlock the doors you need, and restart beckons. The original is worse for this; the Special Edition improved, and by Alien Breed II the team had ironed out the kinks, and the game plays with a perfect rhythm. Back in the 90s, we had to play two games and three years to get perfection; on this collection, it's right there.

Some may disagree; I think Alien Breed II, with its visual detail and balanced map design and item placement, is the best of the series, but Tower Assault comes close. The environments offer more variety and a stronger sense of progression, but the second game is the real gem.

Then there's the move into 3D, something I envied back in the day because you needed an Amiga 1200 or Amiga CD32 to run it. Alien Breed 3D arrived in 1995, when everyone wanted to make a Doom-like shooter, and Team17 was keen to prove the Amiga could compete with the growing PC gaming setups and new consoles like PlayStation. It was an ambitious move, especially given the hardware limitations, and the sequel, The Killing Grounds, pushed that idea further but, interestingly, dropped much of the Alien Breed branding and imagery for a more Doom-like aesthetic.

Playing them now is a different experience because a) I can play them and b) they're important games in the story of the Amiga. But being historically interesting and being enjoyable shooters are two very different things.

Alien Breed 3D now feels like a tech demo, and it drops much of the series' identity: the 3D experience. I remember the impact this game had back in '95, especially for Amiga owners used to pushing the computer's graphics to the limit. But is it a good game? Not really. It's a decent Doom clone, but even in '95 it couldn't compete with games like Dark Forces.

Alien Breed 3D II is slightly different, though: it plays better today and has a cleaner, more focused Doom-like appeal, but much of Alien Breed's visual hooks and identity have been ironed out in favour of what feels like a solid, standalone Doom clone.

But playing all the games back to back in the 35th Anniversary Collection is more interesting than just replaying games I loved on my Amiga, because it shows how a series develops, how improvements are inched forward, how trends and pressures pull on a brand and force new directions. This is where the collection excels.

If you're buying this collection purely for the games, you might be missing its biggest selling point.

The Virtual Museum is an interactive archive that lets you explore the history of Alien Breed through original box art, advertising, concept work, soundtracks and interviews with the people who made the games. It also includes a timeline and material from projects that never saw the light of day, which I've spent far more time exploring than I imagined. It includes a cancelled PSP game that looks like it could have struck the right balance between 'modern' 3D and classic Alien Breed corridor shootouts, and an Alien Breed RTS that would have used randomised levels and X-Com-like gameplay.

I love seeing this sort of material included in retro collections. It's easy to throw a few old games into an emulator and call it a day, but preserving gaming history takes more than making sure an old title still runs on a modern console.

Alien Breed was part of Team17's early identity, and this archive gives you a better idea of how the studio developed the series, what it was trying to achieve and how its ambitions changed as gaming moved from 2D to 3D.

The collection includes interviews with the original artists and developers, plus art, models, design docs, and even videos showing concept art progressing from sketch to final painting. Those cancelled games offer a wealth of content rarely seen in retro game collections, including level maps, concept art, and even gameplay demos.

The original games also get welcome additions that make them more accessible. Twin-stick controls are supported across the 2D and 3D titles; you can choose from display formats, CRT filters, and screen bezels, and save states mean you don't have to commit to a full session every time you sit down.

The collection also includes local co-op in supporting games, online Rush Mode and a new solo Horde Mode. These additions are useful, particularly if you want to play with someone else or fancy a more immediate arcade challenge.

I've spent time digging into the setup menus and fiddling around; for example, there's a choice of CRT scanlines, but also scanline transparency levels. There's no remake-versus-original graphics mode, because these aren't remakes but excellent emulations. I find the new DualSense control layout feels right; it's modern without ruining the series' distinct feel, and you still get that Alien Breed thing of hugging corridor walls.

There are some compromises, though. The original games have been preserved in their original formats, and while that's important, it also means some of their limitations come along for the ride. The 3D games in particular will test your patience if you're used to modern shooters because you need to play within the game's little 3D window (it's not as modernised as the Star Wars Dark Forces Remake, for example), and the collection could have done more to make those games 'modern'. But then that's the knife's edge a collection like this runs: we want the games we had 35 years ago, but we expect today's features; we can't have it both ways.

As a fan back in the day, I love having all these games together on a modern console, partly because they're still good, but also because I never got to play the 3D entries. Alien Breed is an important part of Team17's history, and the collection does a good job of showing how the series evolved across six releases.

But I don't think the games themselves have aged equally well. The top-down shooters still have something to offer if you enjoy old-school arcade games, with Alien Breed II being the one I've been returning to more than the others. The 3D games are more difficult to recommend as anything other than historical curiosities.

But it's the archive that makes this collection worthwhile. The artwork, interviews and unreleased projects give Alien Breed a context that simply playing the games can't provide. It's a chance to look at a British studio's creative development over an exciting period for gaming, a time when Team17 could have been swallowed but it thrived, and that's worth celebrating.

If, like me, you grew up with an Amiga, there's plenty here to enjoy. If you've never played Alien Breed before, however, I'd approach it as a piece of gaming history rather than a newly discovered classic because not every game in the collection is a banger.
