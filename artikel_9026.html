Warhol’s Watches

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Piaget’s second collaboration with The Andy Warhol Foundation for the Visual Arts translates the pop artist’s 1986 Camouflage self-portraits into dials made of gemstone marquetry.

One brand, however, stood out. Warhol owned at least seven wristwatches by the Swiss maison, Piaget. Founded in 1874 in La Côte-aux-Fées, a village in the heart of Switzerland’s Jura mountain range, the house was renowned in the latter half of the 20th century for its creative selection of timepieces in unusual shapes, many featuring dials made of exotic ornamental stones. One of those watches, a quartz-powered reference 15102 model that Warhol purchased from the brand in 1973, now forms the foundation for a Piaget collection bound to appeal to lovers of art in general and fans of Warhol’s revolutionary oeuvre in particular.

Housed in an 18-karat gold, 45 mm cushion-shaped case designed by Jean-Claude Gueit, Piaget’s then artistic director, the striking piece, which was part of a limited series manufactured between 1972 and 1977, featured a black onyx dial. Fittingly, it was called the Black Tie. In 1988, Piaget purchased Warhol’s watch at Sotheby’s landmark sale.

The artist’s choice of Piaget was no accident. In the 1950s and ’60s, the house had built its reputation on extreme thinness, introducing ultra-thin movements that transformed the watches into slim, supple pieces of jewelry. By the 1960s, Piaget had paired that technical prowess with a bolder sensibility: gold bracelets, vivid hardstone dials, and silhouettes that owed more to the jeweler’s bench than to the watchmaker’s. The result was a crossover brand that suited the decade’s appetite for spectacle. For Warhol, who drew no line between commerce and art, a watch that doubled as a gem-set object was irresistible. The Black Tie’s onyx dial, as sleek and dark as a lacquered cigarette case, could not have been better suited to the man who documented the era’s elite so closely.

Twenty-six years after Piaget purchased the Black Tie, the brand used the occasion of its 140th anniversary in 2014 to create a revival collection called Black Tie Vintage. The line caused such a stir among Piaget’s clientele that in 2024, for its sesquicentennial, the maison decided to up the ante. Thanks to a collaboration with The Andy Warhol Foundation for the Visual Arts, Piaget was given the rights to rename the Black Tie Vintage watch after Warhol himself.

The first collaborative watch to emerge from Piaget’s work with the foundation was the 2025 Collage, a limited edition of 50 pieces featuring an artful composition of ornamental stones—yellow Namibian serpentine, pink opal, and green chrysoprase—inspired by Warhol’s Polaroids. This month, Piaget has introduced a second piece, which also uses ornamental stone marquetry, this time to pay tribute to the Camouflage self-portraits that Warhol created in 1986.

“When you buy it, you don’t only buy a watch, you buy a whole story and heritage,” Jean-Bernard Forot, Piaget’s head of patrimony, tells ARTnews. “You can, of course, talk about the material, what it does for you. But at the same time, you have to talk about Warhol, you have to talk about Piaget, you have to talk about art.”

Limited to 86 pieces, after the year in which Warhol created the eight-print Camouflage series, the watch, whose dial combines the ornamental stones sodalite and turquoise with satin-finished yellow gold and polished rose gold, faithfully interprets the artist’s work without blindly mimicking it.

“Camouflage is very difficult to work on, even in design, because we don’t just copy and paste the artwork—we have to find another way to express camouflage,” Forot says.

Among the final serial group of paintings Warhol completed before his untimely death in 1987, the works paid homage to the military-inspired camouflage pattern, originally intended to help soldiers blend in with their surroundings. In Warhol’s hands, they became a comment on Abstract Expressionism, not to mention a deeply personal statement on his lifelong effort to conceal his true self beneath wigs, makeup, and a carefully constructed facade.

Printed in acid brights across enormous canvases, the swirling forms feature in self-portraits overlayed with Warhol’s face, framed by his signature shock of hair. For an artist who spent his life hiding in plain sight, the Camouflage series is arguably his most meta work.

In order to capture the essence of the paintings on the face of a watch, Piaget’s artisans took some liberties with color. Rather than emphasizing Warhol’s use of yellow, for example, they highlighted the brand’s use of turquoise, Forot says.

“Once the team finished the drawing, which was validated by The Andy Warhol Foundation for the Visual Arts, then they started working with the gem material,” he says. “We have to try to remain true to both the design itself as well as the techniques.”

Study the dial of the watch and you’ll see something of an optical illusion: a sense of depth and texture that isn’t quite there. “When you look at the Piaget Camouflage, you have the feeling that you have four layers,” Forot says. “But in fact, it’s an illusion because they are all flat and on the same level. Even if you have the feeling that the turquoise is above the gold, in fact, it’s at the same level.”

The careful craftsmanship required to make the watch—each dial features 27 pieces of gold and ornamental stone marquetry and takes more than 100 hours of labor to design, research, and craft—is a testament to Piaget’s decades of expertise in the esoteric world of gem-setting. Since the maison introduced gold and color into its watches in the 1960s, it has worked with some 50 different ornamental stones, including lapis lazuli, jade, coral, onyx, and tiger’s eye.

The maison’s legitimacy in the realm of color is unassailable—and enduring. Piaget designated 2026 its “Year of Color,” based on its Colours of Extraleganza collection, representing the third and final chapter of a high jewelry trilogy that began with the maison’s 150th anniversary in 2024. “Piaget is really coming back to visibility in color and gold,” Forot says.

While the blend of hues on the dial of the Camouflage watch lends the piece its name and raison d’être, don’t overlook its iconic gold frame nor the movement that ticks within. The cushion-shaped case features three rows of textured grooves known as gadroons, a Piaget signature. It houses a self-winding manufacture movement, the 501 P1. And let’s not forget the caseback, where Andy Warhol’s signature appears alongside the number of timepieces in the limited series.

Best of all, the Camouflage limited edition, like the Collage that came before it, is steeped in the lore that swirls around Warhol’s personal history with Piaget.

“Warhol was a real collector—of everything,” Forot says. “But even if he owned many watches from many brands—including Patek Philippe, Cartier, Bulgari, and so on—there is only one picture with him and the founder of a watchmaking company. And it’s Yves Piaget.”

The legendary chairman of the maison, Yves Piaget is the fourth generation of his family to be involved in the business, which was founded by Georges-Édouard Piaget in 1874 and acquired by the Swiss luxury group Richemont in 1988. In the 1970s, at the height of the brand’s 20th-century popularity, Yves and Warhol became friends.

A photo of the men dated 1979 offers proof that they found common ground in their shared love of timepieces. At the time, Yves presided over the Piaget Society, a glamorous social circle centered on the brand and its community of jet-setting movers and shakers. And Warhol, of course, had the Factory, his studio in Manhattan, a hub of art, filmmaking, and creativity.

“They weren’t exactly the same, but they were both surrounded by artists, extravagant personalities—they were social long before The Social Network,” Forot says. “In a sense, they had the same philosophy.”

Yves’ recollections of Warhol during that time paint the artist as a quiet figure, whose later fame didn’t square with his penchant for careful observation.

Nevertheless, the two hit it off. Yves invited Warhol to Palm Beach to attend the world cup of polo, a sport that the maison made famous through its 1979 Polo timepiece, an emblem of the rich and famous. And he continued to send Warhol gifts, including boxes of chocolate, that the artist, a true magpie, kept as part of his time capsules. Stored in cardboard boxes, the objects, ephemera, letters, and gifts Warhol collected included personal relics from his multiyear friendship with Yves, although Forot jokes that the box of chocolate, while part of the collection housed by The Andy Warhol Museum in Pittsburgh, is, thankfully, empty.

Warhol’s attraction to the brand was hardly anomalous. Piaget’s clientele included more than its fair share of the rich and famous, including Jackie O, Elizabeth Taylor, and Halston. Even Salvador Dalí was a longtime collaborator. In 1967 the surrealist teamed with Piaget on a collaborative collection of gold watches and jewelry, long before such partnerships became commonplace. Featuring gold coins, dubbed Dali D’Or, emblazoned with profile portraits of himself and his wife, Gala, the watches and jewels that emerged from the partnership offer yet another testament to Piaget’s high-flying reputation as the watch for those in the know and a symbol of wealth, taste, and extravagance, all qualities that continue to define the brand today.

Michael Dayton Hermann, managing director of strategic initiatives at The Andy Warhol Foundation for the Visual Arts, says that the collaboration with Piaget “is an expression of how the artist’s vision remains as relevant and compelling today as ever.”

“This unique ongoing project is especially meaningful given Warhol’s personal friendship with Yves Piaget and his passion for the maison’s watches, which he collected during his lifetime,” Hermann tells ARTnews. “It also embodies Warhol’s belief in the creative possibilities of art and commerce, generating proceeds that support the foundation’s philanthropic mission, which has awarded more than $300 million in grants to advance contemporary visual art.”

Just as the collaboration celebrates Warhol’s singular take on the role art plays in commerce, it equally underscores Piaget’s decades-old legitimacy in the world of art. Watches may not be the first things that come to mind when recalling Warhol and his legacy, but he collected them, wore them, and, in the case of Piaget, befriended the man who made them. Think of the Camouflage limited edition less as a commercial arrangement and more as the next chapter of a decades-long conversation started by Warhol and carried on in gold and gems.
