What vegapunk-agents v0.3 fixed from my last post, and the one sentence I'd missed

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Leer en español: https://dev.to/ckomiya/lo-que-la-v03-de-vegapunk-agents-corrigio-de-mi-ultimo-post-y-la-frase-que-se-me-escapo-2he4

vegapunk-agents is an open-source multi-agent architecture pattern (and conceptual framework): six specialized agents that never talk to each other, but read and write a shared memory called Punk Records. In the first post I explained the idea. In the second I walked through one full mission and ended with a list of things the system still did badly.

This post covers what got fixed from that list, and corrects something I said back then: why one of the agents refused to work.

A caveat: these are few tests (one mission per setup, and tests of 5 or 15 attempts). Read them as clues, not firm conclusions.

Thanks to everyone who commented on the previous posts: several of these points got sharper because of those comments.

On memory poisoning (a bad piece of data from the web that slips into the shared memory and contaminates every agent): of the five planned defenses, four are implemented, but only two have been seen working in real missions, and none has been tested against an attack. It will get its own post.

Atlas and York are the only agents that work with files: Atlas writes them and York opens them to check them. In the three full missions that reached that phase, Atlas failed or refused on its first attempt every time. In two of them it wrote only after I added a note asking it to. In the third it refused even with a note, and the mission ended incomplete; I drafted that note with help from the AI assistant I use to plan the project. And in one mission, York didn't even open the files and declared the mission not ready to close.

Their explanations were telling. Atlas said its instructions forbade using tools after reading its context file (the file with the mission's goal and information). York, explaining why it hadn't opened the files, said it had been told to read only that context file. No instruction in the project says either thing.

In the last post I wrote that Atlas refused because it lacked a tool to browse the web. That's what Atlas said. Looking at all three missions together, I think the underlying cause was something else.

My first thought was that the new version had made Atlas worse. It hadn't: the instruction was the same in the previous version, and Atlas failed there too.

The most likely cause lay in how the agents work. None of them writes directly to the shared memory. Each one returns its result in a fixed format (JSON), and the system saves it. That's why their instructions ended like this:

For handing back the result, the sentence is correct. But Atlas and York applied it to everything: "only the JSON" became "don't write files" and "don't open anything". It's a case of what's known as over-refusal: a model refusing to do something legitimate because it reads a restriction more broadly than intended. The other agents don't work with files, or their instructions name their tools, and it didn't happen to them.

One thing I couldn't separate: Atlas and York are also the only ones running a smaller model (Haiku). That may play a part.

The change was small. The instruction now says, in essence: first do your task using your tools, then finish by handing back the JSON.

To check it, I repeated the same situation several times, with the old version and the new one, and counted how many attempts succeeded. Before starting, I fixed what would count as success and how much it had to improve for the fix to count as good. That matters: it means I can't move the goalposts after seeing the results.

Why didn't Atlas pass if it stopped refusing? Because my test required two things: writing the files and handing back the result in the right format. With the new version, Atlas wrote the files in all 15 attempts (the bottom part of the chart), but only 12 did both things (the top part). In the other 3, at the very end, it wrapped the JSON in text, the system didn't accept it, and the attempt counted as a failure. In other words, Atlas now does the work; the failure is now in how it hands it back. (I saw this by reviewing the attempts after the test, so it's an explanation, not something the test proves.)

I also found something else that complicated things. Claude Code, the tool the agents run in, adds to each of them, without my asking, a tool called SubagentHandback plus an instruction to use it to return their result. So each agent got two orders that clashed: "answer with only the JSON" and "hand back your result using this tool". In one mission that caused a delivery to get lost. With the new text, in the checks I ran, the agents delivered correctly through it.

A test in isolation isn't a real mission, so I ran the whole sample mission: validating a fictional business idea (renting tools between neighbors in Lima) with all six agents. Before starting I wrote down five conditions:

The first four were met. The fifth had a wording problem:

A football analogy explains it better. Imagine that before a match you bet: "my team wins by more than one goal". Your team wins 2–1. Only then do you realize the phrase can be read two ways: "more than one goal in total" (they scored two, you win the bet) or "more than one goal of difference" (they won by one, you lose). If you pick the reading that proves you right after seeing the score, you're cheating, even if you don't mean to.

The same happened to me. Since I noticed the double meaning after the result, I take the reading that doesn't favor me: the mission met four of the five conditions. Not because anything went wrong, but because I don't want to adjust the rules after seeing the result.

Even so, it's the first full mission that finished with both files delivered and no note from me. An earlier mission had finished without a note, but without files.

One idea I'm keeping. One word in the agents' instructions, "only", was read more broadly than I meant. And my own condition could be read two ways. Writing for agents and writing for yourself have the same problem.

The next phase is building a second engine with LangGraph, and this mission will be the reference to compare against.
