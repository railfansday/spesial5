Frozen meals can be the hero of the day when you’re short on time and energy—or when you don’t feel like spending a bunch of time in the kitchen to get a meal on the table.  Stocking your freezer with a few boxes can help you manage mealtime even during the most hectic times. Practical solutions for today’s busy man.

You can still find less healthy TV dinner classics like mac and cheese and Salisbury steak in the freezer aisle, but increasingly, you’ll see more options that are health-focused and revamped with fresher ingredients. That means you no longer have to sacrifice nutrition for convenience. And improvements in how they are made and the ingredients used can make some, dare-we-say, crave-worthy, instead of always feeling like a second-rate meal. Prison-like gruel, these are not.

To help you make the best choices possible, we’ve gathered some legit nutritious and protein-rich frozen meal options worth tossing in your cart.

Selecting your quick-fix meal requires some label reading to figure out which ones are nutritionally better performers. It is important to keep in mind that not all frozen meals are even close to being created equal. Here are some criteria you can use to evaluate the nutritional value of a deep freeze meal.

Whole Foods: Overall, you should be able to recognize the majority of ingredients listed on the back of the package. Check labels for what seems like a lot of flavorings, emulsifiers, and thickeners that you would struggle to use in your own cooking. Ask yourself how much of this stuff you can easily pronounce.

Protein: A good frozen meal should contain at least 20 grams of protein. Protein helps support training efforts to build muscle and keep you full and satisfied, reducing the likelihood of overeating.

Fiber: It can be a challenge to get the recommended amount of daily fiber. So it’s very helpful if a frozen meal contains decent amounts of this. A good target is at least 5 grams of fiber per meal. But if a meal has less, you can look for ways to beef up the numbers, such as adding additional vegetables or sprinkling on seeds. Or serve it up with a side-salad or steamed veggies.

Vegetables: It remains a trend that frozen meals are light in vegetables. But it’s still worth trying to find options that are made with two or more different veggies for extra nutrition. You can always toss in a handful of spinach or arugula, or add a secondary serving of cooked frozen vegetables like peppers, broccoli, or green peas.

Sodium: Manufacturers often make use of salt and other sodium-containing ingredients for preservation purposes and to enhance flavor and improve the texture in frozen meals. Ideally, a frozen meal would have 800 mg or less, but many still have more than this. If that is the case, just be sure to go easy on your sodium intake elsewhere in your diet.

Sugar: Look for frozen meals that are not wrapped up in high amounts of added sugars. Remember, there is a big difference between naturally occurring sugars found in items like vegetables and those added during manufacturing. Glazed proteins and sweet sauces such as barbecue and teriyaki can drive up the added sugar numbers. Ideally, look for meals that list no more than 5g of added sugar on the nutrition panel.

Grains: Still to this day, many frozen meals rely on refined grains like regular pasta and white rice rather than more nutrient-dense whole grains. It’s not a deal breaker if your subzero noodles are made with white flour, but it’s a smart move to try to find meals made with whole grains like brown rice or quinoa for a bigger nutrient and fiber windfall. A newly released study review found that consuming 60 to 100 grams of whole grains per day was most protective against heart disease.

Saturated Fat: For most of us, it’s still a good idea to keep saturated fat intake in check for improved long-term heart health. Look for meals with less than 6 grams of this type of fat.
