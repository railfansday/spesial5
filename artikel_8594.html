Your savings rate, the share of take-home pay you save, sets how long you’ll work before you can retire on savings alone. With nothing saved yet and a 5% yearly return above inflation, a 15% rate takes about 43 years, 30% about 28, and 50% about 17. Find yours and raise it to match your target date.

A pay raise alone won’t bring retirement any closer. Save the same share of a bigger paycheck, and you spend more too, so the nest egg you’ll need grows right along with what you save.

Raising the share you save is what brings retirement closer, because each dollar you shift from spending to saving does two things. It grows your nest egg, and it lowers the spending that nest egg will have to cover once you retire.

Assume you start with nothing saved, your savings earn 5% a year above inflation, and they must cover all your costs once you retire. That takes savings of 25 times your yearly spending, enough that withdrawing 4% each year covers a year of costs. Take a couple bringing home $100,000. If they spend $75,000, they save 25%, need about $1.9 million, and get there in about 32 years. If they spend $60,000, they save 40%, need $1.5 million, and get there in about 22.

On those assumptions, here’s how many years each savings rate takes to reach 25 times your spending, a point also called financial independence. Money you’ve already saved shortens the wait, and so would Social Security or a pension, since either would cover part of your spending.

To find your rate, divide last year’s long-term savings by your take-home pay, counting 401(k) contributions and any employer match in both numbers. If your row has you working past the age you’d like to stop, move down the table to the first row that doesn’t, and make its rate your target.

Raising a low rate cuts the most years. Going from 15% to 25% takes about 11 years off your working life, while going from 40% to 50% takes off about 5.
