Why You Should Never Plug an Unknown USB Drive Into Your PC

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Posted on Nov 18 • Originally published at simonebusatto.it

Safety Note: Educational content: the techniques, tools, and phenomena described are for informational and security purposes. Practical activities must be performed exclusively on your own or authorized systems under appropriate safety conditions.

Imagine the scene: you are walking down the street or enter an office and find a USB drive left on a table. The first instinct for many is to plug it into their computer to find out who it belongs to or out of curiosity about the files inside. It seems like a harmless gesture, but in the field of cybersecurity, this is one of the most serious mistakes you can make.

When we talk about USB-related dangers, we aren't just referring to classic viruses or trojans contained within a folder. While traditional malware is still a risk, there are devices that do not behave like mass storage at all. These tools are designed to deceive the computer at the hardware level, tricking the operating system into believing the connected device is something else entirely.

The primary risk is that the attack happens invisibly. Many users think that if they open the drive and see no suspicious files, the system is safe. In reality, malicious code can be executed the exact moment the device is powered on, without the user needing to click any files or manually launch a program.

BadUSB represents one of the most insidious threats because it exploits a fundamental vulnerability: the trust a computer places in input devices. A BadUSB is not a flash drive, but a microcontroller programmed to emulate a USB keyboard (HID - Human Interface Device).

Here is what happens technically when a BadUSB is inserted: the PC recognizes the device as a standard keyboard. Since keyboards are trusted devices, the computer does not require special permissions to accept their input. The microcontroller can then send keystroke sequences at superhuman speed, opening the terminal (CMD or PowerShell on Windows, Terminal on macOS/Linux) and executing a series of pre-programmed commands.

While BadUSB targets data, USB Killers target hardware. These devices do not contain malicious software, but an electronic circuit designed to physically damage the computer's motherboard.

The operation is based on energy accumulation. Inside the drive are capacitors that draw voltage from the USB port (usually 5V) and store it. Once a critical threshold is reached, the circuit abruptly reverses the polarity and releases the stored energy as a high-voltage discharge directly into the USB port's power pins.

Because the protection circuits of many motherboards are not designed to handle such violent and sudden voltage spikes, the discharge can fry voltage regulators, the chipset, and in many cases, the entire CPU. The result is a computer that no longer turns on, with permanent hardware damage that often requires a motherboard replacement.

Warning: this article is purely educational. It does not provide operational instructions for building such devices, as manipulating high-voltage circuits without professional skills and equipment is extremely dangerous.

If we analyzed a BadUSB or a USB Killer in a controlled environment, we would notice substantial differences compared to a common flash drive. Opening the case of a BadUSB, we would not find the classic NAND memory chip, but a small microcontroller (similar to those used in Arduino or ESP32, but in miniaturized formats) with specific firmware.

In the case of a USB Killer, the interior would be dominated by high-capacity capacitors and a switching system (such as a MOSFET or a transistor) that manages the charge release. If we monitored the event with an oscilloscope, we would see the USB port voltage rise gradually as the capacitors charge, followed by an extremely rapid and destructive voltage spike returning toward the PC.

We have seen that a simple USB port can become a critical point of vulnerability. Whether it is a logical attack like BadUSB, which can lead to account compromise by simulating a keyboard, or a physical attack like USB Killers, which damage hardware with high-voltage discharges, the risk is real and immediate.
