New CEO at Villa eyes manufactured housing, ADU opportunity

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Villa hired former Century Communities CFO David Messenger as CEO to scale its ADU and manufactured housing lines. Since 2019, Villa has delivered 425-plus homes and is building projects in Santa Rosa, CA, Altadena, CA and Westminster, CO.  AI Summary

Offsite construction, one of the housing affordability crisis’s hidden-in-plain-sight solutions, gained a blast of momentum this summer when the ROAD Act called for removing the permanent chassis requirement for manufactured homes. Now, some builders are preparing to capitalize on that growing buzz.

Villa, which operates in California and Colorado, is among them.

Since launching in 2019, the company has delivered more than 425 homes across three core verticals: manufactured single-family communities, ADUs on single-family lots, and ADUs within multifamily communities.

The builder originally focused exclusively on ADUs, but has since expanded into primary homes, delivering its first batch of manufactured single-family houses in Northern California earlier this year. The company is currently building 25 homes in Santa Rosa, California and 14 duplexes in Westminster, Colorado, while also helping rebuild housing for victims of the 2025 Eaton Fire in Altadena, California.

The goal now is to scale both the ADU and manufactured housing businesses, with plans for larger single-family communities of up to about 100 lots. To support that growth, Villa announced earlier this week that it hired David Messenger, former CFO of Century Communities, as its next CEO.

For Messenger, moving from one of the country’s largest production homebuilders to an offsite builder focused on ADUs and infill development is quite a pivot. But it also represents an opportunity to focus more on attainable housing in the high-cost areas where Villa operates.

For example, in Santa Rosa, a city of about 180,000 north of San Francisco, the average home price is about $705,000. Comparatively, homes in Villa’s new community, Acacia Village, start at $620,000

“I’ve been drawn to affordable, attainable housing. I think it’s something that the country needs,” Messenger said in an interview with HousingWire TBD.

Beyond the opportunity to deliver attainable housing where it’s badly needed, Messenger sees room for growth across all of Villa’s verticals.

“We’ve got three verticals already established. I think all three can continue to grow,” he said.

Villa’s ADU business has plenty of room to grow, Messenger said. Homeowners in California and Colorado are driving strong and growing demand, while California multifamily operators are increasingly building ADUs on open or underutilized land to generate extra rental income.

“More and more multifamily operators see [ADUs] as a way to increase their rental income…at a relatively low cost to their overall project,” Messenger said.

The other growth opportunity is in Villa’s new manufactured home communities. Messenger sees an opening to partner with land developers to build larger communities, potentially with up to around 100 lots. Instead of taking on the expense and risk of acquiring, entitling and developing land themselves, Villa plans to purchase fully entitled and developed land.

“There is an opportunity to work with land developers who, as they’re developing land, are looking for a takeout. The land developers don’t necessarily want to own the land long term. I think if we can provide them a takeout when it’s finished, that is an attractive option for both of us, as we can buy a finished lot. I don’t think that Villa is going to be in the game of developing land, and we’re not looking to take on entitlement risk,” Messenger explained.

Messenger also plans to draw on his experience at Century Communities to help Villa build a more scalable operation. Although the companies operate differently, he believes many of the same production homebuilding principles can apply.

“Having spent 10 years at a production homebuilder, you learn how to establish repeatable processes. Homebuilding involves unpredictable issues every day, so the goal is to make those issues more predictable. Offsite construction lends itself to that because a significant portion of the home is built in a factory, making the process more predictable. It’s about establishing standardized processes that can be repeated for every unit and every lot,” Messenger said.

“Once we get those processes working like a machine, that’s what makes the business scalable,” he added.

As Villa scales, Messenger doesn’t expect production capacity to be an issue. Instead of owning and operating its own factories, the company contracts with reputable, third-party production facilities to build its homes and ADUs.

“We’ve partnered with different factories all over the state of California. We partner with factories in Colorado, Nebraska and Albuquerque for dealing with those projects. We are developing an extensive array of relationships to make sure that we don’t get hamstrung by a capacity problem,” he said.

The 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act, which became law in July, included a provision that removed the decades-long requirement that manufactured homes be built with a permanent steel chassis. The change has received a lot of attention, leading The New York Times to name chassis as its word of the day on November 11.

Manufacturers cite two main benefits of this rule change, which the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development is now finalizing. First, removing the steel chassis gives manufactured homebuilders greater design flexibility, allowing them to build larger, multi-story and more aesthetically appealing homes that more closely resemble traditional site-built housing.

“If you actually walk through a manufactured home product today and look at the finishes being delivered, it’s a completely different product from what people actually think about. If we can do some marketing and outreach and get people in the door to see what these homes look like, I think they’ll be amazed at what they see,” Messenger said.

That added design flexibility could help the industry shed longstanding stigmas and gain acceptance in communities where zoning restrictions historically thwarted manufactured housing, which is typically a more affordable alternative to traditional stick-built homes.

Manufacturers also see an opportunity to expand into high-cost urban and suburban infill markets, where multi-story designs can make higher-density development more economically feasible. Villa, which operates in high-cost cities with limited developable land, is among the homebuilders positioned to benefit from this rule change.

“As we’re evaluating communities, not having a chassis requirement should open up more lots and communities where we can build on,” Messenger said. “I think it provides additional opportunities for us.”
