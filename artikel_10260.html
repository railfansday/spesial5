This is the least glamorous part of building with LLMs and it's where a lot of agent projects quietly fall over. The model is rarely the problem. The fetch is. In this article we'll look at why live web retrieval has become so unreliable for AI tools, what the numbers say about the shift, and how to build a small, well-behaved fetch layer in Python that fails loudly instead of feeding your model garbage.

A few years ago, a plain requests.get() worked on most of the public web. That's no longer true, and the reason is simple: there are now more bots than people.

According to the 2025 Imperva Bad Bot Report, automated traffic made up 51% of all web traffic in 2024. That's the first time in a decade it has passed human activity. Bad bots alone (scrapers, credential stuffers, scalpers) went from 32% to 37% of traffic in a single year.

Source: Imperva Bad Bot Report 2024 and 2025

From a site owner's point of view, your polite research agent and a credential-stuffing botnet look much the same at the network edge. Both are non-browser clients making fast requests from data-center IP ranges. So the defaults have tightened. In July 2025 Cloudflare began blocking known AI crawlers by default for new customers, and bot management products now look at TLS fingerprints, header order, IP reputation and JavaScript execution, not just the User-Agent string.

It helps to understand the publisher's side of the deal, because it explains why your agent is being treated with suspicion.

Cloudflare splits AI crawling into three purposes: training (collecting data to train models), search (building an index for AI search answers) and user action (fetching a page because a person just asked a question). Training dominates.

Training crawls take content and send nothing back. Cloudflare measures this with a crawl-to-referral ratio: how many pages a platform crawls for every visitor it sends to the site. In its crawl-to-click gap analysis, the ratios for some AI platforms ran into the tens of thousands, compared with single digits for traditional search.

If you ran a publication and saw those numbers, you'd block too. The catch is that blocking is usually done by user agent or IP range, so it hits user-action fetches as well. Those are the ones that happen when a person asks your agent to read a specific article, and Cloudflare's 2025 Year in Review found they grew more than 15x during the year. Your agent is caught in a fight it didn't start.

The lru_cache means you fetch robots.txt once per origin rather than once per page. In a long-running service you'll want a cache with a TTL instead, since robots.txt files do change.

The marker list is deliberately simple. It won't catch every interstitial, but it catches the common ones, and a false "blocked" is much cheaper than a confident summary of a CAPTCHA page. Log every blocked result with its first few hundred characters so you can grow the list from real data.

Retries should depend on the bucket. Throttled and broken responses get exponential backoff with jitter. Blocked and not-allowed responses get no retries at all.

Note the cap on wait. A site sending Retry-After: 3600 is telling you to go away for an hour. An interactive agent should report that to the user rather than block a request thread for that long.

The cheapest fetch is the one you don't make. Agents often re-read the same documentation page several times in one session. Store the ETag and Last-Modified headers and send them back as If-None-Match and If-Modified-Since. A 304 Not Modified response costs the server almost nothing and lowers the odds of being rate limited.

Wire these into fetch() by passing headers=conditional_headers(url) to client.get(). On a 304, return the cached text. On a 200, call remember().

The web your agent reads in 2026 isn't the open, fetch-anything web many of us learned on. More than half the traffic is automated, publishers have good reasons to be wary of AI crawlers, and the defaults now lean toward blocking. You can't change that. You can make your agent a good citizen, though: one that identifies itself, respects the rules, backs off when asked, and tells the truth when it can't read something.

Most of the reliability gains come from the unglamorous parts: classifying failures, caching and passing honest errors to the model. Get those right and your agent will feel much more dependable, even on the days when half the web won't let it in.

It depends on the jurisdiction, the site's terms of service and what you do with the content. Respecting robots.txt and site terms is the baseline. For commercial products, get proper legal advice.

Only for pages that render their main content with JavaScript. Try a plain HTTP fetch first. Headless browsers are slower, cost more to run and are easier to fingerprint.
