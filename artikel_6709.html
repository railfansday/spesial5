The Benefits of Sauna Therapy: What to Know Before You Sweat

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

Experts explain how sauna sessions may support circulation, recovery, skin health, and stress relief.

Although saunas may not be the most relaxing spa or high-end gym amenity, recent studies show that as little as 30 minutes in a hot box can significantly boost the immune system and reduce the risk of cardiovascular and mental health issues, including hypertension and dementia. A physician should monitor the practice, particularly if you have any preexisting conditions. Sauna sessions can also offer several beauty benefits. The research reveals that quick stints in high heat are just enough to jump-start stress reduction, help fight off a cold, and even give skin a nice little glow-up. Experts break down the perks of sauna therapy and how to do it safely.

According to board-certified surgeon, physician, and longevity specialist Dr. Darshan Shah, who is also the founder and CEO of Next Health, sauna therapy offers several potential benefits for both physical and mental well-being, including:

Muscle Recovery: Increased blood flow helps deliver oxygen and nutrients to muscle tissue, supporting recovery and relieving muscle tension.

Skin Rejuvenation: Increased circulation helps deliver oxygen and nutrients to the skin, supporting a healthy-looking complexion. Heat exposure may also support skin health and collagen production, which plays an important role in maintaining skin elasticity and firmness.

Immunity & Cardiovascular Health: Heat exposure raises core body temperature, supporting the body’s natural immune response, while promoting vasodilation and improved circulation. Regular sauna use may also help support healthy blood pressure and vascular function.

Weight Management: Heat exposure temporarily increases heart rate and energy expenditure—although he clarifies that sweating primarily results in water loss rather than fat loss.

Detoxification: Sweating helps the body eliminate small amounts of certain substances, while heat exposure may also support circulation and lymphatic function.

There’s a difference, but it’s worth noting that the aforementioned study and conclusions gathered from it focused on dry heat. However, Shah says the main difference is how the heat is delivered and how your body responds to each.

Brianna Olamiju, a board-certified dermatologist at Spring Street Dermatology in NYC, adds that the benefits of dry saunas and steam rooms are similar, including increased circulation and relaxation. “Dry saunas typically operate at higher temperatures with lower humidity, while steam rooms have lower temperatures and significantly higher humidity,” she explains. Steam rooms may temporarily relieve nasal congestion, but Olamiju shares that neither has been proven superior for skin health. “People with sensitive skin, eczema, or rosacea may find that excessive heat aggravates their symptoms,” she tells ELLE.

Shah’s take is that ultimately, the best option depends on personal preference, heat tolerance, and individual wellness goals. “Consistency and finding an approach that feels comfortable are more important than choosing one type of sauna over another.”

Traditional dry saunas typically operate between 150°F and 195°F, while steam rooms are generally around 100°F to 120°F. Olamiju explains that although many studies have examined sauna benefits at approximately 175°F, she says there is no universally established ideal temperature. “I recommend starting at a comfortable temperature and gradually increasing as tolerated. Higher temperatures don’t necessarily translate to greater benefits,” says Olamiju.

Temperature matters when determining the intensity of heat exposure and how your body responds. Shah concurs that hotter doesn’t necessarily mean better. “The goal is to find a temperature that allows you to experience the benefits of heat exposure without putting unnecessary stress on your body,” he adds.

Another option that Shah’s Next Health saunas utilize is infrared heat. These chambers operate between 110°F and 140°F. “Unlike traditional saunas, which can reach significantly higher temperatures, infrared technology uses light-based heat to warm the body directly. This creates a more tolerable experience for many individuals and can allow for longer sessions,” Shah explains.

Studies show that dry air at high temperatures (typically 70 to 100 °C) for 10 to 30 minutes per session was the sweet spot to reap some of the more significant benefits of sauna therapy, but Shah says there is no one-size-fits-all number since the frequency of infrared sauna use will vary depending on individual factors such as health goals, tolerance, and time constraints. “As a starting point, I generally recommend a client aim for two to three sessions per week for maximized benefits,” says Shah.

For beginners, Olamiju suggests starting with five to 10 minutes per session and gradually increasing to 15 to 20 minutes as tolerated. Two to three sessions per week are recommended as a reasonable starting point. “Some studies suggest that more frequent sauna use may be associated with greater cardiovascular benefits, but consistency and individual tolerance are most important,” Olamiju also notes.

Both experts agree that a wide range of individuals can benefit. For Olamiju, it’s anyone looking to reduce stress, improve relaxation, or relieve muscle soreness. “It may also offer cardiovascular benefits for some individuals. However, sauna therapy should complement, rather than replace, regular exercise and other healthy lifestyle habits,” she advises.

Shah says sauna therapy is particularly useful for those looking to support circulation, recovery, skin health, and overall well-being. He adds that if you’re experiencing chronic pain or muscle tension, a sauna session can help, as increased blood flow may help alleviate discomfort and support recovery. Lastly, he says sauna therapy may also support skin health by promoting collagen production, which can improve skin elasticity and contribute to a smoother, more radiant complexion. “Overall, sauna therapy may be particularly appealing to individuals looking to manage everyday aches and pains, support skin rejuvenation, improve circulation, or simply incorporate more relaxation and recovery into their wellness routines,” says Shah.

Like any form of heat therapy, sauna use isn’t appropriate for everyone, and it’s important to understand your individual health considerations. “Individuals with cardiovascular conditions, uncontrolled blood pressure, a history of fainting, or other underlying health concerns should consult their physician before starting sauna therapy. Pregnant individuals should also seek medical guidance before using a sauna,” Shah warns.

Some of the biggest risks of sauna therapy are dehydration, overheating, dizziness, and low blood pressure, so stay hydrated before, during, and after your sessions. From a dermatologic perspective, Olamiju also explains that excessive heat and sweating can exacerbate conditions like rosacea, eczema, and sensitive skin. “I recommend staying hydrated, rinsing off after sauna sessions, and applying moisturizer to support the skin barrier,” she says.

Can older individuals reap the benefits of sauna therapy?

Absolutely. But both Shah and Olamiju warn that age can bring changes in cardiovascular function, blood pressure regulation, and the body’s ability to manage heat. If you’re taking medications that affect blood pressure or hydration, consult your physician before beginning sauna therapy. Also, start with shorter sessions at lower temperatures and gradually adjust based on comfort and tolerance. “The goal isn’t to achieve a particular temperature or duration; it’s to find a safe, sustainable routine that supports long-term health and quality of life,” says Shah.
