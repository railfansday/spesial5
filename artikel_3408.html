All 7 Michael Mann Crime Movies, Ranked Worst To Best

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Michael Mann is widely regarded as one of the most influential directors in the crime genre. His movies have multigenerational appeal, and his earliest film, 1981's Thief, is still held in high regard more than 40 years later.

Many of Mann's movies have survived the test of time and have become iconic in the genre. Another one of his adored classics is 1995's Heat. His De Niro and Pacino masterpiece is arguably one of the greatest crime movies of the decade. It's a bold but defensible claim, even alongside other legendary 90s crime movies, such as Pulp Fiction, Goodfellas, and The Usual Suspects.

The main characters in Mann's movies are often masters of their craft, which is a huge part of their allure. In Collateral, for example, Tom Cruise stars as Vincent. He's well-dressed, cool, and calculating. He's also a brutally efficient killer, bringing Max (Jamie Foxx), a cab driver, into a busy evening on the job.

Mann's best crime movies are so strong that audiences can notice their influence in other films in the genre. We're specifically looking at those projects in this list. It's also worth mentioning that Michael Mann's filmography sets the bar unusually high, and several movies the list covers are widely regarded as classics. With this in mind, if the theme or cast resonates with you, you will likely even find something to love in the lower-rated movies on this list.

Blackhat is a stylish cybercrime thriller starring Chris Hemsworth as Nick Hathaway. He's serving time in prison, but he's temporarily freed to help Chen Dawai (Leehom Wang) with a high-level hacking case. The stakes are high, as whoever is pulling the strings has already sabotaged a power plant, and no one knows what they may do next.

Like several other movies on this list, Blackhat is quite long at 2 hours 13 minutes. The first act is also slow, and heavy on technical jargon. To the movie's credit, the hacking scenes feel grounded, and Mann fully explores the horrors that someone could theoretically trigger at the push of a button.

Hemsworth is a fun protagonist, though less grounded than McCauley in Heat, for example. He's an incredible hacker, but also such a formidable fighter that he can beat three thugs simultaneously without taking a single hit. Fans of cybercrime stories will find plenty to enjoy in Mann's 2015 thriller, but some audiences may take umbrage at the pacing and character development.

Public Enemies is another unusual movie in Mann's filmography as it's based on a real-life historical figure. The film is set during the Great Depression in the 1930s and stars Johnny Depp as John Dillinger, a ruthless gang leader and bank robber.

The FBI grows increasingly desperate to catch Dillinger and puts Melvin Purvis (Christian Bale) on the case. Like Dillinger, he's at the top of his game, and a busy cat-and-mouse chase ensues. Depp delivers a fascinating, charismatic portrayal of Dillinger. He's charming, but also threatening, and his performance stands out alongside Bale's more measured, serious Purvis.

Mann takes time to explore both main characters, though arguably not as much as in some of his other movies. Still, the 1930s setting, vehicles, and guns make Public Enemies feel quite different from the other crime movies on this list. The action scenes are intense, even though some others can feel drawn out over the movie's 2 hours and 20 minute runtime.

Miami Vice may look like a buddy-cop movie at first glance, but it's actually a gritty crime thriller. Jamie Foxx and Colin Farrell star as Ricardo Tubbs and Sonny Crockett. They are two experienced detectives who go deep undercover on a dangerous drug operation.

The authorities explain that several high-profile agencies are compromised, so the job is risky for the duo, with little support if something goes wrong. Nevertheless, they masquerade as criminals, hoping to get close to Montoya (Luis Tosar), a powerful crime boss.

Miami Vice is an intense movie, especially when Crockett becomes emotionally involved and struggles to keep his two lives apart. It's also a long film, at 2 hours 12 minutes. The pacing can feel inconsistent, and several scenes don't feel like they add much to the story, or could at least be shorter. Like several films on this list, Miami Vice appears grounded and realistic. It's also quite tonally different from the TV show, though that won't matter if you haven't watched the 80s series.

Manhunter is quite different from many of Michael Mann's other movies. It also deserves a special mention as it's the first movie adaptation of Thomas Harris' novel, Red Dragon. William Petersen stars as Will Graham, a skilled former FBI agent brought back into the fray to solve a chilling serial killer case.

The authorities dub that killer the Tooth Fairy, owing to bite marks left on the victims. Graham hopes to learn about the killer by talking to Brian Cox's Dr. Lecktor in prison. Lecktor is extremely intelligent, but he's also cold and arguably feels more grounded and less grandiose than Anthony Hopkins' portrayal.

Manhunter is so much more than Silence of the Lambs with a lick of paint on top. Mann's cinematography makes many scenes look stunning, and Graham's growing attachment to the case, even as the danger increases, is believable. It's a slow-burning movie, though the pacing works for a more psychological crime thriller.

Collateral is a brilliant, intense action thriller and crime story starring Tom Cruise as Vincent, one of his few villain roles. He's an ice-cold assassin with a list of names he must dispatch before the night is up. He decides to use Max (Jamie Foxx), a cab driver, to ferry him around the city to his targets.

It's a real treat to see Cruise as such a stylish antagonist. Vincent is calm and collected, but he also has presence and seems genuinely dangerous. His evolving relationship with Max is a massive highlight of the movie. Mann takes time to explore Max's flaws, which Vincent also picks up on and exploits.

The action scenes in Collateral feel genuine and are satisfyingly snappy and visceral. Cruise is believable as a professional assassin, just as Foxx passes for a civilian out of his depth. Collateral arguably demonstrates Mann at his most accessible. It's a two-hour movie, but an action-packed one, with an exceptional cast and an easy-to-follow story.

Thief is an important movie in Mann's crime filmography. The 1981 title marks his feature-film directorial debut, showcasing his iconic style and telling a story that still holds up remarkably well more than 40 years later. James Caan stars as Frank. He's a highly capable thief, especially when it comes to breaking into safes.

Despite his skills, Frank is also looking to leave the criminal life behind him. He starts working for Leo (Robert Prosky), a powerful crime boss, for more lucrative jobs, but soon realizes retiring may be harder than he thought.

Frank feels like the blueprint for many of Mann's crime leads, with similar motivations. Caan is exceptional as Frank, but the highlight of Thief is arguably the heists themselves. The scenes and equipment feel authentic. There's a believable rawness to the heists, from the preparation to the execution. The cinematography is also widely praised, with Mann showcasing beautiful, moody scenes and intense lighting in the dark, topped off by an incredible soundtrack.

Heat is widely regarded as a masterpiece and among the greatest crime movies ever made. The movie stars Robert De Niro as Neil McCauley, a professional thief. A messy job has attracted the attention of Vincent Hanna (Al Pacino), a veteran officer. McCauley and his crew decide to go ahead with a daring heist despite the heat, and a clash between the two seems inevitable.

As is typical of Mann's movies, McCauley and Hanna are both incredibly skilled and at the top of their games. McCauley reveals his famous 30-second mantra to Hanna during a gripping talk over coffee at a diner. In that moment, both men understand exactly where the other is coming from, and it's remarkable how Mann turns a simple conversation into something so intense.

Heat is a long movie at almost 3 hours, yet every scene has a purpose. We learn about the motivations of every key member of McCauley's crew, as well as Waingro (Kevin Gage), the newcomer who made that initial score so messy. There's a strong case to be made for Thief topping this list, as it's a stunning directorial debut. However, Heat tops the list for its stellar performances and jaw-dropping heist and shootout. It's also a surprisingly accessible Michael Mann movie despite its length, where it feels like not a second is wasted.
