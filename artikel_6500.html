I Learned How Baby Carrots Are Made &amp; Haven't Bought Them Since—Here's Why

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

I have some very strong feelings about a very small vegetable.

As a lover of all veggies, I love every type of carrot out there—except packaged baby carrots. That’s right: those bright orange cylinders with suspiciously perfect rounded corners that lurk in vending machines, school lunchboxes, and convenience store cold cases across our nation. They might be easy to eat on the go and better for you than a bag of Cheetos, but there’s a lot more going on behind the scenes of this seemingly simple healthy snack.

Here’s the truth about baby carrots—and why you might want to think twice about snagging a bag.

The bag might claim otherwise, but packaged “baby carrots” are typically larger carrots chopped into 2-inch pieces, then peeled and polished down to that perfect snackable size. Real baby carrots are immature carrots harvested before they reach full size. They’re sold in bunches—unpeeled, often with the tops still attached.

While a whole carrot has a delicate, nuanced sweetness, the taste of these so-called baby carrots is best described as... factory-fresh. Don’t believe me? Grab a bag of regular carrots and do a side-by-side taste test.

Baby carrots were originally “invented” in the 1980s to make misshapen or broken carrots sellable. This was a smart way to reduce food waste and save money, but it also helped reinforce the unrealistic expectation that produce must look perfect in order to taste good. Today, baby-cut carrots are grown and processed to meet specific standards for size and shape, all to produce those identical little orange cylinders.

Here’s the bottom line: When you buy a bag of baby carrots, you’re buying into a world that’s seemingly as obsessed with unnatural perfection as the modern-day beauty industry.

Energy conservation is top of mind for consumers these days, but making baby carrots look so uniform takes a lot of machinery. After they’re sorted, they chop the carrots into roughly 2-inch pieces and shave them—twice. Then, they go through a rough cutter, which gives them a general shape, before being polished to create those unrealistically perfect rounded edges. That’s a lot of extra work to make something that was completely edible to begin with look a certain way.

To prevent bacterial growth, baby carrots may be rinsed with a diluted chlorine solution before packaging. They’re then rinsed with water, and the chlorine treatment itself is an “acceptable” food-safety practice which doesn’t make baby carrots unsafe to eat. Still, do you really want your “fresh” snack to have undergone chlorine treatments of any kind? For me, that’s a hard no, and I’d rather buy whole carrots and wash and cut them myself.

Because baby carrots have been peeled and cut, they're more prone to drying out and developing something called “carrot blush,” a thin white film caused by dehydration. The film itself is harmless, but it certainly doesn’t make them look appetizing. Worse, they can turn slimy inside the bag, even before you open it. And that’s downright gross.

Prices vary depending on where you shop, but baby-cut carrots can cost more per pound than regular whole carrots. You're essentially paying extra for someone else to handle the peeling, cutting, and polishing. For a task that takes barely any time at home, I'd rather keep the difference in my wallet.

Seriously. Enjoy the taste and texture of whole carrots, potentially save some money, and skip the extra processing by simply buying a bag of whole carrots and cutting them yourself. It’ll take you less time than you spent reading this article.
