According to Notary, the two main sacrifices you make when buying cheaper running shoes are comfort and longevity. That doesn’t mean you’re stuck with choosing uncomfortable shoes when you’re searching for cheaper pairs. It just means you have to do the extra, er, footwork, when considering more affordable shoes.

Notary notes the materials are not as premium or as durable. “That being said, the industry does benefit from what I call the technology trickle-down; many of the materials on lower priced models were once used on a ‘fancier’ one.” For example, the Brooks Launch 12 has DNA Flash v2 foam which was used in its Hyperion Elite super shoes.

“A lower cost shoe will use older foams and fabrics, possibly less outsole, or no outsole, and less involved construction techniques. All of those realities typically lead to less overall comfort than what else is currently available,” says Notary. A cheaper shoe will be comfortable, it just won’t have the same max plush and support of a top-shelf trainer.

Notary says comfort is king. “If it feels heavy, stiff, and firm in the store, don’t expect that to change. Conversely, if it feels hyper-light, super flexible, and marshmallowy soft, there is a good chance it won’t be as durable.”

Perhaps you’re leaning toward one end of the spectrum of cushioning or somewhere in the middle. The best way to find if a cheap shoe—or any running shoe—is right for you is to try it on first and go for a test run in the store. Some shoe stores allow you to run on the sidewalk or on a treadmill to see if the shoe’s a good fit.

Cheap running shoes have a shorter shelf life. “Because the materials are less premium they typically break down faster even when they’re not being worn,” says Notary. “This isn’t always true and is somewhat of a generalization, but higher grade materials generally offer more durability. If we’re considering mileage, a lower-cost shoe typically yields fewer miles.”

Notary adds, however, that some of the most expensive running shoes on the market right now, i.e., super shoes or shoes with a carbon plate, also have a shorter mileage life, which is another thing to put in perspective.

We took pains not to include discounted models on this list but shouldn’t be overlooked when buying running shoes. In some cases, the differences between last season and the current season are unnoticeable.

Beware of dupes on sites like Amazon. A usually expensive model slapped with a too-good-to-be-true price tag is probably just that. The model in question likely isn’t the shoe promised, is poorly made, and could cause injury due to poor quality materials. Check the seller to see it’s from the actual brand (and make sure it’s not someone posing as a well-known brand).

For models by lesser known brands, Notary gives us some pointers when choosing running shoes. “Things to look for are a rubber outsole and a removable footbed/liner,” he says. “These are two quick check aspects of a viable running shoe. Also, look for fewer sewn overlays on the upper, more will only cause problems with flexibility and add excessive weight to the shoe.”

Will cheap running shoes make me run slower?

In general, a cheaper shoe will not have as much pop or energy return as a more expensive one which in theory should make it slower. Lower cost shoes will likely lack plating or supercritical foams found in today’s ‘fastest’ shoes. However, a lot depends on how comfortably the shoe moves with you. Some of the ‘fastest’ shoes on the market today don’t agree with my body’s mechanics, and I find them slower than more conventional shoes in my rotation.

Are cheap running shoes a good choice for new runners?

In my world, any shoe that gets someone running is a good choice. That said, a well-fitting, comfortable technical running shoe will have a much better chance of keeping a new runner in the sport.

It’s a shame that with so much great and relatively affordable product available, many people start the sport in the wrong shoe and quit, never knowing a different reality exists for them. Unfortunately the ‘running shoe’ category is a bit of a catch-all, and there are plenty of shoes categorized as ‘running’ that have no place being labeled as such.

How old can a past shoe model be for it to be ok to run in?

Unworn shoes will definitely lose some cushioning after about two to five years due to oxidization, which hardens the midsole foam. Glue also weakens over time, increasing the risk of delamination in shoes that have been sitting around a while, primarily between the upper and the midsole and the midsole and the outsole. They can be used, sure, but they won’t have the typical 300 to 500 mile range of usability nor the comfort of a ‘fresher’ pair.
