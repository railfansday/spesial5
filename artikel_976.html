A gut–brain sensory-motor circuit regulates sugar ingestion in <i>Drosophila</i>

Nature (2026) Cite this article

Bidirectional communication between the brain and digestive tract is critical for optimizing nutrient preference and food intake, yet the neural mechanisms that couple gut nutrient sensing with ingestive motor control remain poorly understood. Here we identify a gut–brain sensory-motor circuit that regulates sugar ingestion in flies. We show that the brain neurons that regulate food ingestion, IN1s1, receive excitatory input from enteric sensory neurons that penetrate the foregut epithelium and detect sugars in the gut lumen during ingestion. Functional imaging and connectome analyses reveal that IN1s integrate excitatory input from multiple sugar-sensing neurons, including enteric sensory neurons, and provide inhibitory output to enteric motor neurons that control the muscles at the entry of the crop—a stomach-like organ. During ingestion, IN1 activation suppresses these enteric motor neurons, relaxes the crop duct and promotes rapid transfer of food into the crop, thereby enabling sustained food intake. Together, our findings describe a gut–brain circuit that controls the motor activity of digestive tract muscles during feeding and suggest a circuit mechanism by which nutrient signals from the gut contribute to the dynamic regulation of food intake across metabolic states.

Supplementary Tables 1, 2 show full experimental genotypes and exact sample sizes. f, g, Schematic (generated in Adobe Illustrator) summarizing the proposed gut–brain circuit and its state-dependent regulation of crop filling. In both conditions, ingested sucrose passes through the foregut, and enteric Gr43a neurons (yellow) detect nutrient arrival in the gut and relay this signal to IN1s (green) in the brain (green arrow). IN1s integrate nutrient/taste input from multiple sources and inhibit CEM neurons (magenta; red lines), which in turn control the crop duct muscles to regulate the routing of ingested food into the crop. f, Under hungry, high-sugar-concentration conditions (~1 M sucrose), the IN1s drive sustained opening of the crop duct, resulting in high crop filling (orange dots indicate ingested sucrose). g, Under fed, low-sugar-concentration conditions (~100 mM sucrose), the same circuit produces only transient opening of the crop duct, resulting in low crop filling. Dashed arrows indicate the path of ingested food through the digestive tract. The differential crop-filling outcomes across conditions reflect the IN1 circuit’s integration of nutrient and metabolic-state information (VNC = ventral nerve cord).
