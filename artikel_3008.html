Why S&P 500 ETF Investors Don't Have to Worry (Much) About Nvidia Stock Plunging

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

CEO scandals drive home the value of diversification.

One of the biggest stories on Wall Street this week is the resignation of Jensen Huang, former CEO of Nvidia (NVDA -0.76%), caused by a shocking scandal involving extramarital affairs and a romantic relationship with a possible Chinese spy. Many investors are worried that Huang's departure could lead to weaker performance for his company, negative spillover effects for the entire semiconductor industry, or even a broader downturn in the artificial intelligence (AI) industry.

It's important to keep in mind that the stock market is much bigger than one company and one CEO. And Jensen Huang's departure is just one factor affecting Nvidia's stock price, which was already in significant decline before the scandal broke. As of Nov. 13, Nvidia shares have dropped by about 7% over the past week, by 13.5% in 2026, and by nearly 33% since reaching a record high on Oct. 6. Since Nov. 13, the stock has been turbulent, with a slight bounce in pre-market trading on Nov. 18 following its latest earnings release.

But if you own S&P 500 index (^GSPC -0.38%) exchange-traded funds (ETFs), there's no reason to panic about Nvidia's scandal and a possible further sell-off in the stock. Let's look at a few reasons why.

Jensen Huang. Image source: Getty Images.

If you own individual shares of Nvidia, and the stock makes up a significant percentage of your portfolio, you might want to think carefully about whether you're still bullish on the company's future without Jensen Huang. Even though Huang resigned, the Nvidia scandal might not be over yet. There are significant risks and uncertainties facing Nvidia, regardless of who the CEO is.

As of this writing, Huang and Nvidia have not been accused of criminal wrongdoing. But Huang and the company are likely to face further investigations. And more news about the scandal keeps coming out. Jensen Huang was romantically involved with a Chinese businesswoman who was also an alleged Chinese spy. And according to reporting from The Information, this alleged spy might have used her relationship with Huang to gain access to billions of dollars' worth of cutting-edge Nvidia semiconductors, which she then helped provide to companies in China.

But the U.S. government doesn't want Nvidia's best chips going to China, for national security reasons. Nvidia's chips have been subject to targeted export controls since 2022. Again, this is hypothetical -- no one has accused Jensen Huang or other Nvidia employees of violating export controls or other U.S. laws. The company has said that it has no reason to believe that Nvidia corporate secrets or U.S. national security were compromised and has denied any knowledge of Nvidia chips being smuggled into China.

But what happens if more bad news comes out, or an investigation finds that the company violated export control laws or SEC regulations? What if the U.S. tightens its export controls on advanced semiconductors, making it harder for Nvidia to sell its chips worldwide? That could be more bad news for Nvidia stock going forward.

This is a lot of complexity and uncertainty, and it might be more than Nvidia shareholders want to accept. Even after a solid earnings report, the stock could have further to fall.

Nvidia is one of the largest stocks in the S&P 500. As of Sept. 30, it was the largest holding in the Vanguard S&P 500 ETF (VOO -0.31%), making up about 8.4% of the fund's assets. As of Nov. 12, after its recent sell-off, Nvidia has fallen to second place in the S&P 500 index, making up about 6.15% of the index.

What if Nvidia stock falls further from here? Let's say the stock goes into another 10% decline by the end of 2026. That's bad news for individual shareholders. But if you own Nvidia through an S&P 500 ETF, that one stock makes up only about 6.15% of the fund's portfolio. If that one stock drops by 10%, the fund's value would only drop by about 0.62%.

In this example, even if Nvidia declines by another 20% from its Nov. 12 share price, an S&P 500 ETF's Nvidia holdings would only decline by about 1.24%. And that doesn't mean the ETF would automatically go down along with Nvidia. Nvidia is only one stock; the other 499 companies in the S&P 500 index might go up. Just because one stock goes down doesn't mean all the stocks or sectors will follow.

Other semiconductor stocks or AI stocks might go up even if Nvidia goes down. Investors might shift money into other companies that they believe will be more competitive in the future if Nvidia struggles to recover. The S&P 500 is constantly sorting and rebalancing itself as some companies gain or lose market cap.

The S&P 500 is not guaranteed to go up or be a "safe" investment. The entire stock market can still go down by 10%, 20%, or more in any given year. But the S&P 500 tends to be steadier than any individual stock, and the Vanguard S&P 500 ETF has delivered annualized returns of about 12% over the past five years.

The Nvidia scandal shows the value of diversification. It's easy to be confident about individual stocks when the economy is booming, and everything is going well. But when crisis hits, prominent CEOs resign in disgrace, and companies are suddenly thrust into uncertainty and face allegations of serious misconduct, that's when putting too many eggs in one stock's basket can hurt investors.
