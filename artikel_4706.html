"What Does This Mean For Me?" When Life Is All About Ourself

We don't interact with our life directly; we interact with what life means about us. What if the problem isn't that life isn't meeting our needs? What if the problem is that getting our personal needs met has become the single focus of our life?

In part 1 of this series, I discussed the many ways we’re conditioned and wired to be self-focused in today’s world, to constantly monitor and manage our own okay-ness and whether our needs are being met. And furthermore, how each moment is experienced as what we've decided it says about us. Does this situation, conversation, decision, opinion make me feel less than or better than? Does it make me feel valued, comfortable, seen? Does it validate my story of “who I am”? I posited the idea that viewing our lives through the lens of “does this serve me?” is, at its root, an attempt to be happy, shore ourselves up, and ultimately, survive. While, in fact, we’ve got it wrong: putting “me” at the center of the universe and the reason everything happens ends up making us suffer.

So what exactly is the suffering that comes with our relentless self-focus—what’s wrong with seeing everything through the lens of how it affects “me”?

When we experience every situation and everyone else's choices through the lens of “self,” as fundamentally a commentary on how we're doing and a referendum on our worth, then every moment carries the potential to wound, undermine, humiliate, and destroy us. Every interaction can threaten who we think we are. We have to remain hyper-vigilant, therefore, on high alert for whether we’re okay or not okay, and any experience that feels like it doesn’t support our story of ourselves, which can also include a simple difference of opinion.

We live in a constant state of “not-enoughness.” We can never get enough approval, recognition, validation, reassurance, success, or reflection. The self that needs to be continually affirmed is never permanently satisfied; whatever we “get” only lasts as long as this particular experience. We imagine that if we win this argument or get this situation to “go our way,” and be “good for us,” we’ll finally get enough affirmation, enough validation to trust our worth and put down our “me” sword. But it never happens; there is no “enough” point, and so we live in a constant state of lack, always trying to get what we need from the outside so we can be okay on the inside.

Our life becomes an endless project dedicated to self-management.

We feel like we have to be constantly improving the project of "me"—making sure we're doing enough and getting enough. Our life becomes a full-time job of managing the person we're trying to be and be seen as. The result is utter exhaustion and burnout.

Because we experience other people as threats to our self-story, and see others through the lens of whether their behavior serves or doesn’t serve us, we can’t meet people for real, as they are. We’ve got too much skin in the game. Who they are becomes about who they make us feel we are. And so, we can’t really relax fully and get to know others and give them the space to be who they are as whole beings, independent from us, with their own motivations, desires, wants, and needs that are not about us. All roads lead back to us in some way, which clouds our view of the other. We can’t really listen because we have to constantly monitor and scan for potential threats to who we are. We can’t allow others to have a different opinion or experience from us because it “means” our opinion is wrong and consequently, there’s something wrong with us. Our “me” agenda requires everyone else’s thoughts, opinions, and truth to confirm and align with our “me” project, which is a lot of work since that’s never the case. That said, only some parts of other people are allowed into our relationships, the parts that work for us and give us what we need. But we’re not available to meet or know the whole of them.

Our relationships are, therefore, built on filling our own holes and reassuring our own insecurities—getting what we need to feel good or at least okay. We end up using other people to satisfy and validate ourselves. We give in order to feel appreciated and be valued as helpful. We listen to others in order to be perceived as a good listener and so that we can be listened to. How we relate is driven by how it relates to and reflects on us. Our relationships, therefore, can’t be truly open, honest, and emotionally intimate; we can’t be vulnerable and undefended, and can’t really know others as people in their own right, who they really are separate from us. We remain disconnected, relating to others as merely supporting characters in our personal movie—the movie playing in our me-focused mind.

Because we’re always telling ourselves a story about our life, what it means for us, whether we’re getting what we need, and whether our life is serving the project of “me,” we’re not available to life as it’s actually happening. We’re not in the present moment; we're trapped inside a story we’ve told ourselves about our life and how our “me” is faring in it. At the same time, we’re not truly connected to other people. Other people are characters in our personal movie, and are experienced through the lens of whether they’re filling our needs and making us feel good about ourselves. Other people are not subjects in their own right, but objects who can help support who we think we are.

Our self-focused approach to life is an attempt to keep us happy and secure, but it ends up backfiring, leaving us feeling disconnected from life and other people, enslaved to how we're being perceived. It leaves us defensive and emotionally fragile, vulnerable to other people’s different opinions and experiences, and having to constantly manage the project of “me” and make sure we’re enough.

In part 3, I’ll dive into the profound benefits of dropping “me” from the center of our universe, and the relief that comes when we learn to show up for life without our “me” in the way, and when we can see life as something happening, but without turning it into something happening to, for, and because of us. What if it’s not about us?
