How to Survive the Year’s Final Six Weeks

It’s that time of year. Whether you like it or not, the holidays are coming up. Turkey Day, Hanukkah, Christmas, and Kwanzaa— the end of the year can mean at least one family gathering. And while such get-togethers can be fun, they can also be stressful and anxiety-producing. Here are four self-care reminders to help you survive the final six weeks of the year.

Reaching for a hug is a common greeting in many family households, but fist bumps are entirely appropriate. They are less invasive and more hygienic. And for children and teens: hugs are not required. For people with histories of unwanted touch, this can be a valuable reminder. Of course, if you are a hugger, then feel free to hug as long as your hugging partner agrees by stretching their arms.

Holiday gatherings can be very stressful events. You can always take a break. Mediate. Go outside. Take a nap. Read a book. Enjoy a family photo album. We often feel we have to absorb every second with our loved ones during a holiday celebration. This is especially true if we don’t see one another often and family members have traveled distances. However, we are at our best when we are not overwhelmed. If you start to feel overextended, take a deep breath and restore yourself. You might even practice a simple meditation to check in with yourself and make sure you are enjoying the experience. If you are not, get curious, ask yourself why, and rejoin the group when your heart feels more open.

Holidays can stir your emotions. They often remind us of loved ones who have passed, treasured childhood memories, and more. You may wonder why you are suddenly feeling a wave of sadness or guilt at a seemingly joyful, celebratory event. It's okay to get emotional. Our brains link holidays to a myriad of emotional states that we may not always feel prepared for or even understand. If you start feeling emotional, remind yourself that it is perfectly normal and that others in the room are also likely feeling emotional, too. If this happens, consider sharing your feelings with a loved one whom you feel safe around.

Participating in a structured activity can also help decrease stress. You can play a game, pull out a deck of cards, or even piece together a puzzle. If you prefer something more active, go outside and toss a ball around. When we focus on a fun activity together, we can often alleviate stress with light-heartedness. If games aren't your thing, being creative might be. Try a craft together, a coloring book, or bake a cake. Connect with loved ones through play.

Gatherings can feel like an obligation, but they are really a gift we give one another. When we take time to see each other in person, share a meal, engage in a holiday tradition, exchange gifts, and check in with one another, we are opening ourselves up to connection. We are choosing to bond. But that decision isn’t a requirement, and if you find yourself feeling that it is, these self-care practices can help you get through the anxiety or discomfort.
