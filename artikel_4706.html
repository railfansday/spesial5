The Need for Cognitive Closure and Why You Assume the Worst

Some people find uncertainty much more distressing than others do. An ambiguous, uncertain situation, like whether someone will reply to an email, whether a friend is upset with you, or why a partner has gone quiet, is harder to live with than hearing a clear piece of bad news. Conscious or not, your brain may then assume the worst outcome, just so you no longer have to put up with the grueling ambiguity and anxiety.

The social psychologist Arie Kruglanski and his colleagues coined the concept of the need for cognitive closure, describing it as someone's "desire for a firm answer to a question and an aversion toward ambiguity." The answer one craves doesn't need to be pleasant, as long as it ends the ambiguity. People with a high need for closure tend to show two tendencies. The first is urgency, the wish to reach an answer as soon as possible, and the second is permanence, the wish to keep that answer for as long as possible once you have it (Kruglanski and Webster, 1996). With a high need for cognitive closure, once you have an answer, you may stop seeking more information and find it hard to take in anything that might change your mind.

The need for closure is a personality trait, a persistent part of how you think and feel, and not only a temporary mood. It is not a symptom or a sign of illness, and it has both costs and benefits. If your need for closure is high, you may make important decisions quickly and confidently, and you feel calmer when your life has order and routine. If your need for closure is very low, you may put off important decisions until the last possible moment and find it hard to settle on one direction. The cost of a high need for closure: You may reach conclusions before you have enough information, and miss opportunities.

The questionnaire that researchers use to measure the need for closure asks things like how much you like order, how much you like predictability, how quickly you like to make decisions, how uncomfortable you feel with ambiguity, and how reluctant you are to reconsider a view once you have formed it (Webster and Kruglanski, 1994). Some statements people agree or disagree with are: "I dislike unpredictable situations." "I feel uncomfortable when someone's meaning or intention is unclear to me." "I'd rather know bad news than stay in a state of uncertainty." In everyday life, you might notice a high need for closure in small ways. You may feel uneasy when plans remain undecided; you may want a meeting to end with a decision even when no decision is needed yet, and you may find it hard to leave a disagreement unresolved.

People with a high need for closure do tend to report more anxiety. In one study of 400 college students during the COVID-19 pandemic, the students with a higher need for closure reported more anxiety and more stress than the students with a lower need for closure (White, 2022). The pandemic was a long period when nobody knew what would happen next; it makes sense that people who find uncertainty distressing were likely to find that period harder. A related trait, called intolerance of uncertainty, has been studied much more in connection with anxiety. It describes how well a person copes when they cannot predict what will happen, and researchers have found that it is indeed related to the need for closure (Berenbaum and colleagues, 2008).

A high need for cognitive closure may also make you more sensitive with people. When a friend replies to a message more briefly than usual, or a colleague seems less friendly than usual in a meeting, you may not be able to leave the question unanswered, and decide they are annoyed with you. If you have a high need for closure, you may also do many things to get an answer, even though they could backfire. You may send someone several messages in a row to get a reply, even though you know that more messages are likely to annoy them. You may ask a partner again and again whether they are angry with you, until the asking itself irritates them. You may even end a friendship yourself; you no longer have to wait to find out whether the other person wanted to end it. In each case, you get an answer sooner, and you may also bring the very undesirable outcome you fear, paradoxically.

Mistaking the Need for Closure for Pessimism

You may have been told for years that you take things too personally or that you think too much. Other people may not understand how hard it is for you to sit in uncertainty, and they may never understand that certainty—even when painful—is easier than the suspense. They may assume that you are simply a pessimist. Even a therapist may not understand what is happening, and they may try to help you stop catastrophizing by asking you to look at the evidence and find a more realistic explanation.

While others may not understand what is happening in you, the first step is to understand yourself. If you know that your need for closure is high, you can at least understand that you are not simply irrational. Most of the time, you are trying to end a feeling that is hard for you to live with, and that is completely human. Plus, a high need for closure has uses as well as costs. You may make decisions more easily than many people, and you may be good at organizing plans and work, so you can leverage those strengths in places where a quick decision helps, such as planning your week or leading a project.

Wanting a clear answer is an ordinary human wish, and there is nothing wrong with you for wanting one. You may have spent years treating this part of yourself as a flaw, and you may have tried to hide it or to argue yourself out of it. But actually, it is simply a trait, a part of who you are. If you want to moderate the trait, you don't have to become someone else, someone comfortable not knowing. You may only need to stay unsure a little longer than you used to, long enough for more information to emerge.
