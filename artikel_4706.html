Co-Regulation: We Don’t Outgrow the Rhyme and the Rhythm

Somewhere right now, a parent is humming the same four notes over and over. The baby in their arms doesn’t understand the words of the song, and there is no need. The rhythm is the message: you are held, you are safe.

Psychologists call this co-regulation, one nervous system lending calm or strength to another. Some of the earliest communication occurs through rhythm.

The research reveals the power of early, embodied communication. When mothers and infants interact face to face, their heart rhythms begin to coordinate (Feldman et al., 2011). Infants stay calm about twice as long when sung to as when spoken to, even when the song is in a language they’ve never heard (Corbeil et al., 2016). In one study, infants relaxed to lullabies from distant cultures; their heart rates slowed, and their pupils narrowed to songs whose words they could not possibly recognize (Bainbridge et al., 2021).

Something in the sweet repetition of a lullaby speaks directly to the body. Nursery rhymes do the same, while also teaching language.

But embodied communication is not just for babies. We don’t outgrow the rhyme and the rhythm. We don't outgrow the need for deep connection. We just give them grown-up names: poetry, song, prayer.

We call it poetry. Something in metered verse reaches the body before the mind catches up. We listen to poetry and get goosebumps. It activates the brain’s reward regions (Wassiliwizky et al., 2017). In an experiment, when people recited verse in a steady meter (hexameter verse used by Homer in the Iliad and Odyssey), their heart rate began to synchronize with the rhythm of their breath — more so than with breathing exercises (Cysarz et al., 2004). The ancient bards reciting epics by the fire weren’t just telling stories. They were tuning up the listeners’ bodies. Poetry is co-regulation.

We call it song. Strangers who sing together bond faster (Pearce et al., 2015), and when choir members sing in unison, their heart rates synchronize (Vickhoff et al., 2013). Some songs unite entire nations. In 1792, an officer wrote a marching song for French troops near the Rhine. As it spread, volunteers from Marseille sang it, and the city’s name became associated with it. The Marseillaise turned crowds of strangers into a people who breathed as one. It has been sung ever since in moments of defiance, wherever people needed to borrow courage from each other and from prior generations.

We call it a work song. For centuries, sailors tied their knots to shanties, field workers kept pace with call-and-response, and laborers raised walls to a shared beat. The songs coordinated muscle movements while also carrying humor, hope, grief, and protest. They made hard work more bearable by stressing the human connection.

And we call it prayer. Reciting rhythmic prayers regulates breathing and the heart’s rhythms (Bernardi et al., 2001). But the type of prayer seems to matter: God-focused prayers such as gratitude and adoration are linked to greater well-being than more obligatory or self-focused forms (Whittington & Scher, 2010).

Emotions are contagious. One irritated person can shift the mood of a whole team, and that mood shapes cooperation and conflict (Barsade, 2002). Dysregulation threatens the quality of decision-making, performance, and talent retention.

Yet most workplaces treat regulation as an individual’s job, to be handled silently, with sheer willpower and perhaps an app. But humans have always steadied each other.

Rituals help reduce anxiety (Hobson et al., 2016), strengthen coworker bonds, and increase helping (Yim et al., 2025). A team doesn’t need a nursery rhyme or a sea shanty, but a poem, a meaningful ritual, or sharing in authentic gratitude can help a group of adults find common ground and a source of calm.

Many autistic adults describe using stimming, rhythmic movement, or vocalization to help self-regulate and cope with the unfriendly world (Kapp et al., 2019). These behaviors are often treated as symptoms to be extinguished or are stigmatized as "unprofessional." Yet they use the same neural self-regulation mechanism as the ancient work song, the chant, and the lullaby.

When neurotypical people regulate through rhythm, it is called tradition, worship, or team spirit. When neurodivergent people do it, it is often called a problem. A neuroinclusive culture recognizes the tool as universal and lets people use it in their own way. And perhaps learn from their deeper connection to timeless human wisdom that modern societies too often pretend to have outgrown. Neurodivergent people might, once again, offer a canary-in-the-coal-mine insight into something that benefits everyone.

Rhythm is powerful, and power can be misused. La Marseillaise calls for an enemy’s “impure blood” to water the fields. The same rhythm that binds a people together can also bind them against someone. Rituals forced on people become a source of stress rather than comfort. Co-regulation is helpful when it is offered, not imposed or manipulative (Yim et al., 2025).

A parent humming a lullaby doesn’t demand that the baby calm down or attempt to squeeze performance. A bard by the fire shares wisdom without imposing one's own will. Workplaces could learn from that: care works better than coercion, and companionship works better than control.
