Scott Eastwood Is Still Seeking a Rush, Just at a Slower Pace

He still surfs. He still trains jiujitsu and Muay Thai. And the actor still lifts and still enjoys putting himself in situations that demand something from him physically.

What has changed at 40 is the way he prepares for all of it.

There was a time when Eastwood could train hard, wake up sore, and trust that his body would bounce back. Now, he is far more interested in staying mobile, pain-free, and ready for whatever he wants to do outside the gym.

Over the last five years, Eastwood says that shift has become increasingly intentional.

“Recovery is probably the biggest difference,” Eastwood told M&F. “I think I’d probably put it number one. I listen to my body more and take more care in my recovery than I ever did in my 20s or 30s.”

This doesn’t mean that he’s less serious about training, it just means Eastwood’s objective has evolved. Looking like a capable athlete has given way to a much more important goal: staying physically capable.

“Obviously I want to be strong, but I also want to be really flexible and limber and be able to react to whatever the world throws at you, where you’re not going to pull something or you’re not going to hurt yourself.”

Eastwood ,who’s latest film, Lucky Strike, is now streaming on Hulu, used to organize his gym work around the familiar bodybuilding split—chest, back, and different muscle groups on different days.

Now he prefers training that puts his entire body to work.

“I think [now I’m] just a little bit more focused on holistic workouts,” Eastwood said. “I’m far more focused now on my core, just because of body pain and how your body feels, and all the things that feel better, which kind of stem out from your core.”

When he isn’t working on a film, Eastwood says he typically lifts three or four times per week, splitting those sessions between gym and home workouts. He also tries to fit in Muay Thai or jiujitsu. Yoga has become a constant.

Eastwood says there isn’t a day that passes where he doesn’t do some version of it, often half an hour in the morning and another later in the day.

When he is filming, the expectations change.

A 6 a.m. call time might require a 5 a.m. gym session. Long production days can already be physically taxing, so those workouts become less about progress. It’s an evolution in the thinking that every training session needs to prove something. Eastwood still trains hard, but there isn’t much value now in forcing his body through movements that no longer feel worth the risk.

“You’re spending a lot of time on your feet, and it’s typically physical,” Eastwood said. “So you’re kind of just doing a little more maintenance when you’re working. You’re not getting the gains that you might get if you’re not working.”

The gym has never been the only testing grounds for Eastwood.

Surfing, martial arts, and hiking are still a part of his life, and each demands something slightly different from his body.

It’s a large part why strength alone no longer satisfies him.

“If I want to continue to do jiujitsu, and I want to do Muay Thai, and I want to continue to surf, I’ve got to take care of my body,” Eastwood says. “Which means strength, flexibility, and mobility. I’ve got to kind of do all these things, or my body is going to get trashed doing these sports.”

Those activities also provide him with feedback that a mirror will miss. A stiff body doesn’t perform well on a surfboard. Poor mobility isn’t less obvious when someone is trying to control you on a mat. Carrying unnecessary soreness into either activity can turn something meant to be enjoyable into a problem quickly.

Eastwood hasn’t lost his appetite for these passions that come with risk. However, age has made him more selective about which risks within those passions are worth taking.

That means foregoing chasing a massive surf or testing himself on the mat against whoever wants to go the hardest. When he was younger, these thoughts might have not even entered into his thinking.

Now, it’s more thoughtful. He still wants the challenge, to compete with himself, and to feel capable. These activities are just no longer a barometer for his toughness.

Ask him what physical quality matters more now than it did at 25, and the answer is an understandable one.

“Flexibility,” Eastwood says. “Flexibility and just not being in pain. If you can take care of your body where you’re not in pain all the time, that’s really valuable.”

One of the biggest shifts for Eastwood is the attention to recovery.

Yoga, sauna sessions, and Epsom salt baths have all been part of his routine for years, but sleep has become especially important as both his training and work demands have changed.

“Just getting a good night’s sleep, I value it so much more now,” Eastwood says.

10 to 15-hour set days require focus, energy, and often physical effort. Arriving not fully rested often produces immediate consequences.

“I can’t do the job that I do well and focus if I haven’t had a great night’s sleep,” he said.

His approach to nutrition is also uncomplicated. Eastwood says he follows a Mediterranean-style diet built around whole foods, including fish, red meat, grains, and plenty of vegetables. He also refuses to turn an occasional bag of chips or dessert into a moral crisis.

“I don’t make it some hard and fast rule that you have to do,” he says. “I just think those just get a little too rigid.”

His preference for simplicity also played a role in why Eastwood became a co-owner of North Performance.

Eastwood says longtime friend Mike Lucas shared the frustration of trying to travel while managing individual supplements. Both men wanted something easier to maintain without pretending a supplement could replace the fundamentals.

“You still have to work out. You have to get good sleep and have good recovery,” he says. “Having good supplements is just going to help those things. The basics are the basics. They don’t change regardless.”

When he is filming, Eastwood says North is part of his morning routine, but the rest of his priorities remain the same.

He still has to eat well. He still has to move. If the day is packed and there are only 25 minutes for himself, he’ll find somewhere to fit in exercise, yoga, or mobility work. Everything beyond that is what he calls “extra credit.”

It’s a simplicity that defines fitness at 40 for him.

Looking strong doesn’t mean anything if his back is aching or his body is too tight to do the things all of his training was meant to prepare him for.

The goal going into the next decade is simple: keep enough strength to be useful, maintain mobility to move well, and have enough durability to keep saying yes to the things he enjoys.

“I think fit is more how you feel and less aesthetic,” Eastwood said. “[Looking good] is great, but if you’re in pain because you’re not doing it correctly, then who gives a shit about what you look like?”
