The Witcher 4 Is Not a PS5 Exclusive, CDPR Clarifies After Sony Deal

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Did anyone really think The Witcher 4 was going to be a PS5 exclusive?

For those who missed it, CDPR confirmed yesterday that it has signed a marketing deal with Sony on its upcoming RPG.

While the details are light, it’s expected this will take the same shape as PlayStation’s arrangement with GTA 6, where the title’s promoted “like” an exclusive – even though it’s technically not.

But it seems there was some confusion online about the nature of Sony’s deal, which CEO Michal Nowakowski has now clarified on social media.

“We’re looking forward to our partnership with PlayStation! And just to be clear: The Witcher 4 and our other upcoming titles will remain multiplatform.”

It’s as expected really, but Nowakowski still faced follow-up questions about whether Ciri’s story may launch first on PS5, before other platforms.

“All the platforms release at the same time: PS5, Xbox Series X|S, and PC. No one gets left behind.”

Of course, this is all entirely as expected – the original comments mentioned a marketing deal only, and I don’t think Sony could afford to keep a game as big as The Witcher 4 off other platforms, to be brutally honest.

It does hopefully mean the game will be optimised for the PS5, though, taking advantage of unique features like the DualSense et al.

I’m sure we’ll learn a lot more about how Sony and CDPR are collaborating once we get closer to the game’s release.

@TrollOfWar As a developer, I'm not only very familiar with Unreal Engine, I'm tremendously aware of how terrible it has been of late to work with effectively and efficiently.

I don't know of a developer or development team who is happy with the state of Unreal Engine right now. At this point I would assume those that are, are legally obliged to say so, or are receiving some financial benefit as a team for being aligned with it.

There is no such thing as a PC first engine, all game development engines run on PC first, and are built and incorporated around the implementation of console runtime environments using either SDK tools, Xbox/Nintendo/Sony DevTools or a combination of all or next to none.

As some terribly low budget Dev's will not have the SDK, select a drop down in Unity/Unreal Engine, export to the console and then have assistance from a platform suggested studio that assists, or an internal onboarding team.

So, a PC first engine simply means the developers are focused heavily on PC development as their primary bread and butter, it has nothing to do with the engine capabilities.

Unreal Engine itself is one of the most famous examples of PC first, as its engine literally was and is built around PC first ideology within development.

It's just marketed differently depending on which page of their site you click, which rep you speak to and so forth.

By going from REDengine, over to Unreal Engine, CDPR have not done so due to the ease at which they can deliver on console environments, it's due to the fact they clearly don't have the in-house technical staff to assist with incorporating all that is needed in a real-time environment into their engine. At least in an ongoing fashion during development, especially due to the dawn of at least 2-4 new consoles arriving in a year or two.

The issue is that Unreal Engine can be modified somewhat, it can be altered ad-hoc, yet ultimately all substantial feature sets and abilities are governed by their internal team of "specialists".

Lumen, Nanite, both wonderful, developers didn't get to implement that ahead of time, they didn't get to pick the time frame, they didn't get to choose how it speaks to the rest of the engine.

You lose a lot, a hell of a lot going from a very established, very capable engine runtime environment that your entire studio have spent years tailoring, to then go to an off the shelf solution with little to no say on the next major features and how they work alongside other tools.

There's a reason Pixar use Marionette and Render Man, it's not because it's easier, it's because every step of the way they get to not have to ask another company if they can "Please Sir have some more".

CDPR going from in house to off the shelf does not show they have learned anything, it shows their team quite literally don't know how to develop and maintain an in house engine during development of a cross-generational release.

@ButteredCrumpet Interesting post. But I think there's a few things worth highlighting.

The main reason CDPR switched to Unreal Engine was stated in their debrief about why CyberPunk 2077 was delayed three times, and why even then it STILL came out in a poor state that required years of development to get it up to scratch. Their primary conclusions were related to the RED Engine, staff turnover and the issues these caused. (CDPR are very open and this was all in their investor calls)

That's not to say the RED Engine was bad, it isn't, but new staff would not be familiar with it, nor their custom tools, and many of their best devs would have to spend time getting the new staff up to speed, rather than actually working on the game. This slowed the whole process down considerably and reportedly caused some top staff to leave, which also didn't help. But it was made even worse because they also had a high staff turnover - for a variety of reasons - and so this process would repeat again and again, slowing progress to a crawl.

Interestingly 343 Industries came to exactly the same conclusion in their debrief as to why Halo Infinite was delayed a year, why it launched in an incomplete state, and again took years to bring many features expected at launch. Same conclusion: Bespoke engine and the issues this caused with a high staff turnover, hence they also decided to go UE5 before Halo was moved to Activision.

TLDR: Ultimately the decision for both studios was that for all the potential downsides of Unreal Engine the pros outweighed the cons.

What's particularly interesting is it isn't really about the technical capability of the engines, while that would have been a consideration, it's more about the people making it and the actual management issues of that when scaling up for these massive projects.

As the old saying goes, the best tool is the one you actually use.

The issue is that Unreal Engine can be modified somewhat

UE5 is almost entirely modular. You don't like the physics model, you don't like the lighting, the rendering or even major features like Lumen or Nanite you can replace them all.

Obviously at some point it makes no sense to, you might be better off building your own engine.

It will be interesting to see how CDPR will adapt UE5 for their uses. Like you I was slightly sceptical about using UE5 but will wait to see before judging. But I think it's fair to say most studios that release on UE5 don't have their own Engine Programmers who built something as complex as RED Engine, CDPR do, so they should be able to make the engine run better than most.
