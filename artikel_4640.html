Delusions: A Willful and Dangerous Psychotic Symptom

I had interviewed James multiple times over a four-month period, at the request of his attorney. I initially examined him two days after he was arrested for stabbing one of his roommates. After arriving home following an evening of drinking and cavorting together, James inexplicably grabbed a knife from the kitchen and viciously attacked one of his roommates before being subdued. Fortunately, the victim survived.

When interviewed by the police, all of James’ roommates described him as “acting weird” for some time. He made cryptic, hostile comments about how he was being “left out” of their plans, which they all said wasn’t true. Still, they emphasized, he was never violent toward them or anyone else.

They all confirmed that, on the night of the attack, large amounts of alcohol were consumed. A blood test completed on the night of his arrest confirmed that James had also used methamphetamines, which he later admitted to using regularly for months.

During my interviews with James, he revealed how he became increasingly convinced that his roommates were not merely socializing without him but were plotting against him. Over time, his suspicion morphed into a fixed delusion that organized his perceptions and the meanings he attributed to their behavior. If they were all out, he assumed that they planned the activity to avoid having to be with him. When they laughed, he believed they were laughing at him. He wondered if they were “plotting something” against him.

Over time, James’ paranoia became increasingly acute. On the day of the stabbing, he was convinced that the victim-roommate arranged for a helicopter to follow his every movement. “I couldn’t even go out in the fresh air… clear my head… I was being followed…”

James suffers from a drug-induced delusional psychosis. Unlike hallucinations, which are sensory-perceptual experiences (e.g., hearing voices that are not there), delusions are false beliefs that have ossified into convictions that are unsupported by objective reality. Psychotic delusions are self-referential, meaning that the individual is at the center of the psychotic belief. For instance, a character on television is believed to be sending messages to oneself, or, in James’ case, the helicopter was following him. This is different from having beliefs that are accepted by certain cultures or religions that seem strange to outside observers.

After months of treatment with psychotropic medication, James began to express some doubts about his paranoid beliefs regarding his roommates, and his agitation lessened. He admitted that it seemed far-fetched that they would have him followed by a helicopter, but still, he was adamant that “something’s going on” with them, and that they harbored ill intent towards him.

With the passage of time and proper treatment, it is not uncommon for delusional beliefs to soften yet continue to have currency in a mind that had been floridly psychotic. This was certainly the case with James, and was the case with other delusional individuals whom I have examined, including those whose delusional beliefs were the result of chronic drug use. Why is this so?

Individuals with chronic schizophrenia frequently suffer from both delusions and hallucinations. They tend to have recurrent episodes of acute psychosis. Their psychotic symptoms are fierce and disruptive, which prompts immediate and intense therapeutic interventions. Symptoms then lessen, and the patient stabilizes, albeit frequently at a lower level of psychosocial functioning. Some patients with schizophrenia will be capable of insight into their distorted thinking during those periods of psychotic remission.

The clinical presentation of those with delusional disorder is quite different. Hallucinations are not a salient feature of the condition or are not present at all. The delusions fester for long periods, while the patient seemingly functions relatively normally. Frequently, the delusions are not as bizarre as James’ belief that his roommates were having him followed by a helicopter. The delusional content may initially appear quite normal, such as a radical political belief. Internally, though, it is much more extreme and reality-distorting. For instance, one might express the belief that a national politician is corrupt, for which there is convincing evidence. Such a rational thought based on objective evidence then becomes reality-distorting and taken to the extreme, such as a conviction that the politician is hypnotizing constituents with his intense stare during his television interviews.

For a formal diagnosis of delusional disorder, the psychotic belief must be present for at least one month. It’s a condition that is relatively rare; delusions that result from long-term drug use are not so uncommon and can cause a major disruption in one’s life, even after a period of sobriety.

Typically, the delusions become increasingly intractable over time. As its longevity as a belief exists in the patient’s mind, so does its conviction as a reality, and its resistance to logic, persuasion, and psychotropic medications.

Why are delusions so enduring and resistant to change? As belief systems, delusions are not sensory-perceptual experiences like hallucinations, which are distinct from a person’s sense of self. An auditory hallucination is frequently experienced as coming from the outside surroundings, or, if internal, as a foreign voice. An enduring delusion takes on the form of a value or a guiding principle. In other words, delusions become part of one’s identity. As such, it is accepted as important to one’s sense of place in the world, and functions to provide meaning in a confusing and ambiguous world.

As I interviewed James over time, I noticed that his rational thinking and reality testing improved, though he resisted an outright rejection of his psychotic beliefs about his roommates. At one point, I commented to him, “You are not ready to give it up,” referring to his beliefs about his roommates. He quietly responded, “Yeah, yeah…yeah.”

Delusions had become part of James’ identity and a way of understanding his world. As with all dimensions of one’s identity and sense of self, change is gradual and takes time.
