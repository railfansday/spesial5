Research shows Aimbient can automatically capture the reliable OR data health systems need to improve efficiency at scale

NEW YORK, Nov. 18, 2026 /PRNewswire/ -- The first large-scale, multispecialty validation of automated operating room documentation, led by Stanford Medicine researchers and published in JAMA Surgery, found that ambient surgical and procedural AI from Aimbient (formerly Surgical Safety Technologies) matches human accuracy on key OR timestamps.

The findings show Aimbient can automatically generate reliable OR documentation at scale, a foundation for health systems looking to improve OR utilization and efficiency.

Running continuously across 14 operating rooms and one procedure room at Stanford Health Care during the eight-month study, Aimbient used real-time computer vision to automatically detect when patients entered and exited the operating room. Researchers compared those automated timestamps with manually entered timestamps, using blinded human video review as the ground truth. They found no statistically significant difference in accuracy between Aimbient's automated timestamps and manual documentation across seven surgical service types.

And while human accuracy degraded during overnight shifts and long transplant cases, Aimbient remained resilient to documentation fatigue.

"Every OR metric a health system cares about, from first-case on-time starts to turnover time, is based on the accuracy of the timestamp underneath it," said Teodor Grantcharov, MD, PhD, founder and CEO of Aimbient. "Stanford Medicine researchers showed Aimbient can capture those timestamps automatically, around the clock, with the same accuracy as a human at the keyboard. Our AI systems are resistant to fatigue, stress, and furthermore free up cognitive bandwidth for the operative team to focus on patient care. Logging accurate timestamps, 24/7-365, gives health systems reliable data to see how their ORs are actually running and get more capacity from the ORs they already have."

Previous validations of computer-vision OR timestamps were limited to a single procedure type. The Stanford Medicine study in JAMA Surgery was conducted independently and included every surgery performed in the study rooms, spanning general, vascular, transplant, urologic, gynecologic and orthopedic surgery.

Manually entered timestamps lag the actual event by an average of five to six minutes, as the circulator is busy caring for the patient the minute they roll in.

In use, Aimbient documents these timestamps as events occur, giving perioperative teams a more current view of room activity without waiting for manual entry. More reliable, timely timestamps could also strengthen the data used for scheduling, operational reporting and capacity decisions.

"To our knowledge, this is the first study of its kind that examines the accuracy of automated OR documentation at scale, across multiple surgery types in a major academic hospital," said Chloe Nobuhara, MD, general surgery resident at Stanford Medicine and lead author of the study. "These timestamps are a relatively small milestone in the entire patient's journey, but now there is the ability for computer vision to parse data out of a highly chaotic OR environment."

The stakes are significant. According to published estimates, operating rooms generate up to 70% of hospital revenue and can cost as much as $100 per minute to run. Improving operational efficiency by even a few minutes can yield substantial annual operating margin, and health systems are increasingly turning to data-driven technologies to improve performance.

Automated timestamps are one application of Aimbient's broader intelligence platform, which is designed to work before, during and after every surgical case or procedure. Aimbient runs across the day: forecasting the schedule before the first case, tracking and documenting in real time during it, and turning every completed procedure into data that improves the next. By showing what is actually happening in the room, Aimbient gives health systems a reliable basis for scheduling and capacity decisions, moving procedural AI beyond documenting what happened and toward improving what happens next.
