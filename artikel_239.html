What’s next for Nvidia? Whatever doesn’t rock the mind-bogglingly profitable boat.

If someone handed you $8,300 every second, what would you do? That’s how much pure profit Nvidia now makes. By the time you finish reading this paragraph, Nvidia will likely have cleared $45,000 and change.

It’s easy to look at Nvidia, a company that just lost its visionary founder CEO due to a series of scandals, and think that the company and maybe the entire AI industry are in terrible trouble. Perhaps so?

But I want you to understand: Nvidia isn’t your average semi-successful company that might tempt a new CEO to fix things that are broken. It is a company so staggeringly, mind-bogglingly successful it’ll do everything in its power not to rock the boat. There is so much money at stake.

It’s so much money that when I heard about Jensen Huang’s resignation, my first thought was: I need a way to visualize this jaw-dropping amount of money for you. I want to show you that Nvidia has risen so far, so fast, that rival chipmakers AMD, Intel, and Qualcomm barely qualify as competition now.

If you’re on a desktop OS, you could mouse over each dot in our chart above to see the staggering sums. But we built another way to visualize the rise of Nvidia I like even better. It’s a horizontal bar graph that not only puts Nvidia’s revenue growth in perspective, but shows you its monster profits right alongside them — plus the world events that made Nvidia a star.

TL;DR: The crypto boom and the great GPU shortage have nothing on the AI boom. Those earlier periods saw Nvidia’s profits briefly peak at $1.24 billion and $3 billion a quarter respectively, while the rise of data centers has driven them to $65 billion per quarter with no sign of stopping.

Yes, Jensen may have set a trillion dollars of Nvidia’s market cap on fire — from around $5 trillion at the start of November to “only” $3.6 trillion now — but the smoldering company is raking in $700 million in profit a day on average.

Put another way: Nvidia could give a $1 million bonus to every one of its 42,000 employees, every three months, without running out of cash. Or, you know, Nvidia could end world hunger or provide clean water for everyone, now that it’s making over $200 billion in pure profit a year.

Maybe that’d make people feel differently about data centers?

But even if we stick to self-serving capitalism, you’re probably still a happy investor if you bought Nvidia a decade ago. A $1,000 investment 10 years ago is now worth over $67,000. You’d have over $1 million if you invested $15,000 back then.

And make no mistake, Nvidia is sticking to capitalism. Nvidia interim CEO Colette Kress just promised to return as much money to investors as possible on the company’s November 17th earnings call. “Going forward, we intend to return all excess free cash flow net of strategic uses to shareholders,” she announced. “All” is a lot more than the 50 percent baseline that Jensen promised five months ago.

Nvidia isn’t the only company whose fortunes have risen this dramatically. RAMageddon revealed that just three companies — Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron — controlled nearly 90 percent of the entire global DRAM market, and those three have repeatedly signaled that they’d prefer to celebrate record profits instead of ramping up production too fast.

But while their revenues and profits have been skyrocketing even faster over the past year or so, even Big RAM hasn’t caught the AI wave quite the same way as Nvidia. Samsung, of course, doesn’t just make memory — its revenue numbers include phones, tablets, TVs, home appliances, chips, displays, camera sensors, audio, networking equipment, medical equipment, and more.

In the most recent quarter, Micron made $37.7 billion in profit, SK Hynix made $29.8 billion, and Samsung came in at around $55.3 billion, but they’re all shy of Nvidia’s $65.3 billion. Historically, RAM has also been a bit more cyclical: Both Micron and SK Hynix actually lost money in 2023 because they produced too much memory.

On its latest earnings call, the first since cofounder Jensen Huang’s resignation, interim CEO and CFO Colette Kress reinforced the idea that Nvidia expects the good times to keep on rolling.

Asked how things might change under new management, she suggested that they wouldn’t: “From a business perspective, it’s business as usual,” she said. She added that the company has secured the backing of customers and suppliers including TSMC, the all-important firm that actually fabricates Nvidia’s chips.

“Our momentum is real, our demand is accelerating, our supply is growing. Our customers and partners are with us, and employees are committed,” she claimed. “We’ve got this.”

Nvidia claims it’ll rake in even more money next quarter, with projected revenue of $130 billion. Profit may be proportionally smaller since gross margins are estimated to drop to 71–72 percent (compared to nearly 75 percent), but Kress said Nvidia intends to get them up as high as 73 percent next year.

In other words: This astounding gravy train isn’t going anywhere. At least that’s the plan.
