20 Ways to Save Money as a Family – Child Care, Food & Kids’ Costs

For parents who pay for child care, it’s usually the biggest cost that comes with kids, so that’s where your family should start saving. Check for free public pre-K and state child care assistance before you pay full price, use tax breaks on what’s left, then cut food and kids’ costs.

Child Care Aware of America puts the national average price of child care at $13,184 a year in 2025, about 10% of a two-parent household’s median income and 33% of a single parent’s. Feeding more people adds up as well, and so do the clothes, gear, activities, and parties kids outgrow or ask for again every year.

Each move below cuts one of those costs and works on its own, so you can take them one bill at a time. For rent, insurance, and other bills every household pays, the ways to save money on a tight budget still apply.

Unlike a tax break, which only trims part of the bill, a free seat can take a paid year of preschool off your costs entirely.

Before you pay for preschool, check whether your state or city offers free public pre-K, and apply as soon as applications open, often months ahead. If you still need a paid program, compare co-op and part-day preschools, then pay with pretax money from a dependent care flexible spending account or claim the child and dependent care credit.

Assistance helps pay the daycare bill itself, which suits a family short on cash better than a tax credit that pays back only once you file.

If you work or attend school or job training, apply to your state’s child care assistance program even if you think you earn too much, and do it early, since many states keep waiting lists. For a child under 5, apply to free Head Start or Early Head Start as well.

Read more: You May Qualify for Child Care Assistance - Some States Help Middle-Class Families

Up to a yearly limit, what you still pay for care after the free and subsidized options can come out of a dependent care account at work.

If you pay for daycare, preschool, or day camp so you can work, enroll in your employer’s dependent care flexible spending account (FSA) at open enrollment. Money you put in skips federal income and payroll taxes, so in the 22% bracket each dollar of care costs you about 70 cents. Elect only what you’re certain to spend.

Read more: Dependent Care FSA - Pay for Daycare Before Income & Payroll Taxes

After-school care and summer day camp can count toward the credit for kids under 13, so it keeps paying long after the pre-K years end.

If you pay for daycare, preschool, a nanny, or day camp so you can work, claim the child and dependent care credit when you file. It’s worth 20% to 50% of up to $3,000 in care costs for one child under 13, or $6,000 for two or more, minus any tax-free dependent care benefits from work.

Parents who want care at home rather than at a center get the most from it, and it runs most smoothly with a family a few minutes away.

Hire one nanny together with another family, and each family usually pays about two-thirds of a nanny’s going rate while the nanny earns a third more. Put the nanny share’s terms in writing first, including how much notice a family gives before leaving, and pay the payroll taxes on your own share.

Read more: Share a Nanny With Another Family - Each Pays Less, the Nanny Earns More

Evenings out and weekend appointments need care too, and trading hours pays for that care with your time instead of a sitter’s fee.

A babysitting co-op lets you trade hours of watching other families’ kids for hours of free sitting when you need it, for date nights or appointments. Join one near you or start one with five to 15 families you trust, track the hours in points, and cap how many points any family can owe.

Read more: Babysitting Co-op - Trade Hours With Other Families Instead of Paying a Sitter

Planning dinners ahead decides what your family buys before you reach the store, which puts every grocery trip on a budget from the start.

Before you shop, spend 20 minutes planning the week’s dinners from what’s on sale and what’s already in your kitchen. Pick five dinners that share ingredients, save a night for leftovers, keep a backup meal on hand, and write your grocery list from that plan. You’ll make fewer trips and order less takeout.

Read more: Family Meal Planning on a Budget - Let the Week's Sales Set the Menu

In weeks when grocery money runs short before payday, this move matters most, and it works alongside free school meals and other food help rather than in place of them.

When food money is tight, build your family’s meals around a dozen cheap staples, such as rice, dried beans, eggs, and potatoes, and cook them from scratch instead of buying boxed or frozen dinners. Make enough at dinner to pack the next day’s lunches.

Read more: Feed a Family on a Tight Budget - Cook From a Dozen Cheap Staples

As a family grows, the biggest package looks like the obvious buy, and a glance at the shelf tag on each grocery run shows when it isn’t.

Before you grab the family size or the sale item, check the unit price on the shelf tag, which gives the cost per ounce, count, or sheet. It’s the only fair way to compare packages of different sizes, and when both tags use the same unit, it shows in seconds whether the bigger or discounted package costs less.

Read more: Unit Pricing - The Family Size Isn't Always the Cheaper Buy

Once kids reach school age, a free or reduced-price school lunch cuts the cost of one meal every school day, right through high school.

Before you pay for or pack another school lunch, find out whether your kids can get free or reduced-price meals. Your school or state may already feed every student free, and kids in families on SNAP food benefits get free meals automatically. Otherwise, fill out the school’s meal application each school year, or whenever your income drops.

Read more: School Lunch Can Cost 40 Cents or Nothing - Check Whether Your Kids Qualify

Formula and baby food are costs only a young family carries, and WIC helps pay for them in the years before school starts.

If you’re pregnant, recently pregnant, or raising a child under 5, WIC provides healthy food, infant formula, and breastfeeding help. In most states the income limit for a household of three is $50,542 a year before taxes, and Medicaid or SNAP enrollment qualifies you on income automatically. Apply through your local WIC office.

Read more: Pregnant or Raising a Baby? Medicaid or SNAP Already Meets WIC's Income Rule

Dinners out don’t have to disappear from a family budget, and this move keeps them in it while your kids are young enough to qualify.

Several family chains let kids eat free one or two days a week, usually with one free kids’ meal for each paying adult entrée, and a couple let young kids eat free any day. Plan your family dinners out around those days, and confirm your location runs the deal before you go.

Read more: Kids Eat Free at 10 Chain Restaurants - Each Deal's Day, Age Cutoff & Catch

Every growth spurt brings the clothing bill back, and secondhand prices keep that repeat cost small without asking your kids to go without.

Buy most of your kids’ clothes secondhand, since children outgrow clothes long before wearing them out. Start with a kids’ consignment sale, a pop-up event usually held each spring and fall where local families sell what their children outgrew at a fraction of the new price. Then fill gaps at resale shops and by swapping with other families.

Read more: Buy Kids' Clothes Secondhand - Shop the Spring & Fall Consignment Sales First

Clearance covers the pieces you’d rather buy new, such as a winter coat, and works alongside secondhand shopping instead of replacing it.

New kids’ clothes cost the least at end-of-season clearance, but your child’s current size likely won’t fit when that season comes back. Shop for next year instead, usually one size up, and bring a list of what each child still needs so the markdowns fill real gaps.

Read more: Kids' Clothes on Clearance - Buy Next Year's Size, Not This Year's

Gear is the first big bill a new baby brings, and you face it before the baby even arrives.

Most baby gear lasts longer than your baby needs it, so borrow it or buy it used after checking for recalls and missing parts. Buy the car seat and crib mattress new, and take a used crib only if it was made after June 28, 2011. Use your registry’s completion discount on what you buy new.

Read more: Borrow or Buy Used Baby Gear - but Get the Car Seat & Mattress New

Borrowing toys saves money and the shelf space they’d fill, and it lets your kids try new ones as often as their interests change.

Toys for babies and toddlers fit a stage your child soon outgrows, so borrow them instead of buying. Start with your public library or a toy library, then try swap groups and rental services, and rotate the few favorites you keep so they feel new again.

Read more: Borrow the Toys Your Kids Will Outgrow - Libraries Lend Them for Little or Nothing

Each child in school brings a new supply list every year, so the savings multiply in a family with more than one student.

Before you buy school supplies, gather last year’s leftovers, from backpacks and binders to calculators and pens, and cross everything that still works off the teacher’s list. Then buy only what’s still missing, and get the basics during July’s back-to-school sales, when folders, notebooks, and crayons can cost under a dollar each.

Read more: Shop Your House Before Buying School Supplies - What You Reuse Beats Any Sale

Activity costs can climb every season, and this move keeps the yearly total down while your kids keep playing.

For the average family, most of what a child’s sport costs goes to travel, lessons, camps, and gear, not registration. Start your child in a rec league or community program, buy sports gear used, rent musical instruments, ask about financial aid, and keep paid activities to one or two a season.

Read more: Kids' Activities Cost Less in Rec Leagues - Skip the Travel, Buy Gear Used

Birthdays come once a year for each child, so a family with several kids throws several parties, and the savings from keeping each one simple add up.

Host your child’s birthday party at home or in a park instead of booking a trampoline park or play place. Keep the guest list to your child’s age plus one, serve pizza and a homemade or grocery-store cake, and trade rented entertainment and goody bags for a game the kids play together.

Read more: Throw a Kids' Birthday Party at Home - Invite Fewer Guests and Skip Goody Bags

Summer break gives kids the most free time of the year, and planning those weeks ahead keeps most days from costing anything.

Keep your kids busy until school starts without a big bill by repeating a few cheap outings every week, such as a discounted summer movie, a free library program, and a trip to the splash pad or pool. Sign up early for free programs that fill up, and let the kids help spend a small fun budget.

Read more: 20 Cheap Summer Activities for Kids - Most Are Free With a Little Planning

Together, these moves trim the costs that come with kids, a little on each bill and the most on child care. Start this week by looking up your state’s free pre-K program and child care assistance, then apply to whichever your family qualifies for.
