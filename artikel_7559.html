Quebec politicians decry court-ordered oath of allegiance to King Charles III

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Quebec politicians will have to pledge an oath to King Charles III or risk legal battles over their laws.

MONTREAL (CN) â The Superior Court of Quebec ruled Monday that a 2022 law allowing members of the province’s National Assembly to pledge allegiance only to Quebecers, and not to the British king, is unconstitutional.

The decision sparked waves of protests from Quebec politicians, putting the upcoming legislative session in limbo.

Superior Court Justice Ian Demers found the provincial government overstepped its powers by abolishing the king’s oath without the approval of Ottawa and the other provinces. He ruled in favor of Lawrence David, a University of Ottawa law professor who challenged the legislation in August.

The judge agreed with David that suspending the law is necessary to avoid chaos in the National Assembly. The professor and founder of the Public Interest Litigation Institute argued that laws adopted by members of the National Assembly who had not pledged their allegiance to King Charles III would be invalid and that the law violated Quebecers’ right to proper representation.

David also claimed the oath could be abolished only with the unanimous consent of the provinces and the federal Parliament, because the change encroached on the role of the lieutenant governor. Canada is a constitutional monarchy, and lieutenant governors represent the Crown.

Quebec politicians appear to agree with the law. None of the 127 members of the National Assembly elected in October has sworn allegiance to the king. The court nonetheless found the law unconstitutional.

“If the stay were not ordered, all laws passed by the National Assembly would be unconstitutional and would have to be reenacted,” he added, predicting the process would only “paralyze” the government and harm Quebecers.

“The government of Quebec strongly condemns a ruling that clearly goes against the democratic will of Quebecers, who have expressed their desire to put an end to the disloyal act of swearing an oath to the British monarch and to the straitjacket of the monarchy in general,” the premier said in a statement released on social media in French.

In a press conference in Montreal on Monday, St-Pierre Plamondon called the ruling a “disgrace” and said he “cannot take an oath to the king of England.” He vowed to take all necessary measures to oppose the judgment.

The premier noted Quebec never signed the 1982 Canadian Constitution, and that the Constitutional Act of 1867 was imposed on the French-Canadian people after the British conquest of the French territory. He insists Quebecers should be allowed to follow the democratic process rather than be forced to follow rules imposed on them.

The legislative assembly set for Dec. 1 is now up in the air. Lawmakers must decide between swearing allegiance to the king and risking court challenges to every decision they make.

All the opposition parties also criticized the ruling and welcomed the government’s appeal, deploring that the decision could push back further the start of the National Assembly.

The Parti Quebecois has long been fighting the presence of the monarchy in Quebec. St-Pierre Plamondon promised earlier this year to abolish the Office of the Lieutenant Governor of Quebec, or at least divert its resources to fight issues such as the homelessness crisis.
