Once you retire and accumulate enough to support the life you want, it’s likely your priorities will change. You won’t need to squeeze every last dollar of growth from your portfolio. You might be more focused on making what you’ve built last.

“The only purpose of money is to build the life you want.” What does it say about you if some of your happiest memories are those during which you didn’t have much money?

Happiness starts out higher when you’re young, dips in middle age and then goes back up in your older years. The thing about the happiness curve is you overestimate your future life satisfaction when you’re young and underestimate your future happiness when you’re in middle age. Both feelings are probably wrong but impossible to ignore.

Everybody wants to argue about what AI is going to do tomorrow. Which jobs will disappear? Which companies will lose? What happens to the people who get left behind? Those are fair questions. But what about the jobs that don’t exist yet? What about the industries nobody has invented? What about the opportunities we can’t even imagine today?

Renting comes with uncertainty. You might have to move, you’re subject to someone else’s rules, and you have less autonomy over your living space. Homeownership, meanwhile, is presented as stability, security, and a permanent place for your children to grow up. Sounds perfect. But where are the property taxes, maintenance bills, unexpected repairs, and time spent dealing with contractors? What about the opportunity cost of tying up a substantial portion of your net worth in a single, illiquid asset?

Retirees sometimes tap savings for one-off expenses. Withdrawing money from a traditional 401(k) or individual retirement account is not as simple as taking the amount you need, advisors say. There are tax implications to consider that go beyond owing taxes on the money you withdraw.

Many adult children are ignoring the old adage “don’t count your chickens before they hatch,” and conducting their finances based on an inheritance they may not receive. Baby boomers are sitting on a pile of wealth, and some researchers estimate they’ll have to pass about $124 trillion of that in what’s dubbed the “Great Wealth Transfer” to next generations through 2048. Others, though, warn potential heirs not to get their hopes up.

Financial silence creates immense, unnecessary suffering. The administrative scramble piled on top of deep grief is enough to break a family apart. So why do we wait until a crisis to have these conversations with our parents?

The holidays are just around the corner. A new inflation report just dropped. That means one thing: the Cost of Thanksgiving Discourse. The most expensive part of Thanksgiving is now less about the table and more about how you get there.

Even physicians who often have a 403(b), 457(b), or defined benefit plan available too, usually max out their 401(k) first. The 401(k) is the best retirement account most workers can get, and that it’s still worth it for doctors.

That habit got expensive in 2026. Itemizers now lose the deduction on the first 0.5% of AGI they give away, and top-bracket donors see the tax benefit capped at 35%. The write-off shrinks unless you change how the money leaves your account before December 31.

Give appreciated stock in place of cash and the capital gain never shows up on your return. Bunch two or three years of donations into a donor-advised fund and you clear the floor once. Past 70½? Send the gift straight from your IRA.

Which move fits depends on whether you’ll itemize this year.
