Bitcoin's long-term trend value, based on various models like "Plan B", has been set ridiculously high, so chasing targets set by those models might not be the best decision out there, considering modern market conditions.

A revised Bitcoin Power Law model sets BTC's long-term price at $99,000, which is almost $50,000 below the $146,725 estimate that has been used by the traditional Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) model.

The creator of the newer, accordingly more accurate model believes the Bitcoin Power Law is still valid, but the fair value is certainly nowhere near the relevant market conditions.

One way or another, the newer models still set the current price threshold at one of the lowest historical levels in the history of the asset—approximately the 1st percentile of its distribution, which means that it stands among 60 of Bitcoin's daily closes that fell below this threshold.

The new model uses a completely different method for calculation: Quantile Regression instead of Ordinary Least Squares (OLS). The main difference is that OLS guesses Bitcoin's price trend based on its historical averages, while QR uses specific points in the price distribution, not an average. Essentially, the newer calculation model smoothens out extremes that made OLS less accurate.

Despite the newer calculation and estimation approach, the improved calculation algorithm and everything else, models are estimating, not making solid predictions.

There are thousands of other factors to consider when determining Bitcoin's next move, so any kind of model is useful only for evaluating the momentum and historical significance of the most recent movements.

The model also places its 0.1 percentile at $55,185, a level below which Bitcoin has closed only six times. The creator argues that only the lower part of Bitcoin's price distribution, between the 0.1 and 5th percentiles, may continue following the Power Law, while higher levels gradually move down towards this lower boundary.

The model also uses Protocol Time instead of Calendar Time. Still, its creator maintains that the Power Law is not broken. The problem, according to this view, is how previous models measured it and how much they allowed market bubbles to influence the results.
