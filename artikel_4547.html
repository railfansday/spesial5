Two-Thirds of Dementia Content On TikTok Is Misleading, Researchers Say

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

A new survey suggests that about two-thirds of dementia advice videos on TikTok are misleading

Of the videos examined, the analysis shows that nearly half contained inaccurate and potentially damaging information.

The researchers say that approximately 44% of TikTok creators who share dementia content lack credibility and appropriate qualifications.

Although misleading dementia content may appear on other social media platforms, this research examined only TikTok content, warranting further investigation.

The survey was commissioned by Homewatch Caregivers and has not been published in a peer-reviewed scientific journal.

A spokesperson for TikTok told Healthline that if health-related content is potentially harmful, it is flagged for removal. The spokesperson added that 99.6% of misinformation deemed violative was proactively removed from the platform in 2026.

The spokesperson added that TikTok relies on fact-checking and guidance from public health authorities to help assess the accuracy of content. The platform may also apply warning labels to content that cannot be verified as accurate.

Looking to social media for health information is not uncommon. A recent poll reports that 31% of adults in the United States use social media at least once a month to look for health information and advice.

Experts advise seeking information about dementia from trusted sources, such as a primary care doctor and organizations like the National Institute on Aging (NIA) and the Alzheimer’s Association, rather than social media, where content creators who share advice often lack expertise.

“It’s normal to want answers quickly when you’re caring for someone living with dementia, and a short video from someone who seems to know what they’re talking about can be really convincing,” said lead researcher Jennifer Winarski, senior manager of care operations and quality for Homewatch CareGivers.

“Despite there being some genuinely helpful information out there, you don’t always know whether it’s backed by medical evidence, so we wanted to bring our own expertise to uncover whether popular TikToks can be trusted,” she told Healthline. “We hope our report will encourage people to be a bit more careful about what they act on.”

For the survey, researchers selected a sample of 500 of the most-watched dementia-related videos on TikTok with hashtags such as:

Researchers then transcribed the video data to assess its accuracy.

The researchers found that two-thirds (66.4%) of the popular TikTok videos contained misleading information. Of those videos, about half (47.6%) contained potentially damaging misleading dementia information.

“It’s clear that many of the creators mean well, but much of what we saw was personal opinion rather than proven information, and in a few cases, general good health tips were presented as if they were specifically helpful for individuals living with dementia,” Winarski said.

“If families act on that, they could end up spending time and money on things that won’t help — and in the worst of cases, it could delay getting the support their loved one really needs,” she added.

Dung Trinh, MD, an internist for MemorialCare Medical Group and chief medical officer of the Healthy Brain Clinic in Irvine, CA, said it’s important to be aware of the volume of misleading dementia information on social media. Trinh was not involved in this survey.

Some people may be making decisions about their health based on incomplete, misleading, or incorrect information, he said.

“For example, someone might see a video claiming that a particular supplement can reverse Alzheimer’s disease or that certain memory problems automatically mean someone has dementia,” Trinh said.

“These messages can lead to unnecessary fear, false reassurance, wasted money, or delays in getting a proper diagnosis.”

“We are entering an exciting era in Alzheimer’s care, with advances in blood-based biomarkers, earlier detection, and treatments that may slow disease progression in appropriately selected patients,” Trinh continued.

“Getting an accurate diagnosis and receiving timely medical guidance are becoming increasingly important.”

The researchers examined the content creators behind the TikTok videos included in their analysis. They found that 44% of video creators behind the misleading videos had no relevant qualifications.

Rehan Aziz, MD, vice chair of education in the Department of Psychiatry at Hackensack Meridian Jersey Shore University Medical Center and associate professor of psychiatry and neurology at the Hackensack Meridian School of Medicine in New Jersey, said that content creators offering dementia advice should have relevant qualifications. Aziz wasn’t involved in the survey.

“Start by looking at the creator’s profile for a full name and a specific credential, such as MD, DO, PhD, or RN, along with a specialty,” Aziz told Healthline.

“For dementia, the most relevant experts include neurologists, geriatric psychiatrists, geriatricians, neuropsychologists, and nurses, including advanced practice nurses, or social workers who specialize in dementia care.”

“You can confirm a doctor’s license through your state’s licensing board website and check board certification at certificationmatters.org,” Aziz said.

As of 2020, more than 55 million people worldwide were living with dementia — a neurological condition that negatively affects the brain. Researchers predict this number will hit 78 million by 2030.

The increase in the number of people seeking dementia content online is a reflection of this trend.

When looking for accurate dementia information and guidance, Nestor Beltre, MD, a neurologist with Baptist Health Kenneth C. Griffin Neuroscience Institute, part of Baptist Health South Florida, said a great first step is to speak with their primary care physician. Beltre wasn’t involved in the survey.

“Memory changes can occur for many different reasons, and some causes, including vitamin deficiencies, depression, and certain medical conditions, may be treatable,” he told Healthline.

“Early evaluation can help identify what may be contributing to these changes and determine the most appropriate next steps. Depending on the individual’s symptoms, a neurologist, psychiatrist, or neuropsychologist can provide further evaluation and guidance,” Belter continued.

Beltre advised turning to trusted resources such as the Alzheimer’s Association and the National Institute on Aging, which can increase public understanding of memory-related conditions and available treatment options.

“The most important message is that patients and families have resources available to help them better understand and manage cognitive health,” he said.
