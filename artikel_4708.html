Why We Work So Hard… and What It Costs Us

Posted November 24, 2026 | Reviewed by Michelle Quirk

I was home recently, in the house I grew up in, for the first time since Mom died a few months earlier. I could feel her absence in my remembered expectations: I'd anticipate her coming down the hallway any moment now, her whole face lighting up as she spotted me around the corner; I'd open the fridge, prepared for it to be overflowing with my favorite foods, bought in anticipation of my homecoming; as dinner approached, I'd brace myself for her questions about what she could cook for me, or what she should order from Jerry's Pizzeria, a hometown staple and our family's favorite for over 50 years, and, of course, I'd look at the backgammon board sitting near the dining room table, waiting for her eager arrival and her excited Ready to play?, followed by her endless, playful attempts to cheat as the game wore on. (Mom, you can't cover the dice after you roll them!)

But other parts of her absence were more subtle, more internal…

With Mom, there were always two things alive at once. The first was her deep longing for connection and closeness, expressed through food and play and hug after hug after hug. (Mom called them fasses, her own made-up word; Come, let me fass you! she'd say, and then she’d squeeze you with all her might.) This part of her was born in the family village she grew up in, parents, uncles, aunts, and cousins all sharing one apartment building, filling her childhood with people who belonged to each other.

The second part awake in her was her fierce determination to see that I had a secure future, safe and stable, with the financial freedom to enjoy my life. Mom believed that kind of future would be earned through effort and hard work.

I took in both, and now, with her gone, I was giving myself the chance to sort through what I'd inherited: What did I want to keep, and what might I, at long last, set down? It's a reckoning many of us share…

Most parents, like Mom, love their children dearly and want to see them build a secure future. In a Pew Research Center survey (Minkin & Horowitz, 2023), 88 percent of American parents said it was extremely or very important that their children be financially independent as adults. No wonder so many of us grow up hearing some version of the same message: Build a secure life, and work hard to get there.

As young children, we comply with our parents’ priorities, believing that if we do what they ask, if we work hard, we’ll keep them near (Haines & Schutte, 2023).

That’s what I did. Early on, I put my faith in hard work as the way to ensure Mom stayed close… but not long after, I began noticing its limits. Sure, a new achievement brought a burst of applause, and a bigger accomplishment came with a trip to Classics Bookstore to pick out any book I wanted (I usually chose a Marvel graphic novel), but the celebration, and the closeness that came with it, never lasted long enough.

Still, I kept going, certain that the next achievement would bring what the last one hadn't, caught in what Tal Ben-Shahar (2007) calls the arrival fallacy: the belief that one day I’d accomplish a goal that would finally bring lasting closeness with Mom.

I’m still working hard today, and, if I'm being honest, I'm still caught in a version of that same arrival fallacy, a part of me believing hard work will accomplish some magical goal that will draw loving people near me (…and research suggests I’m far from the only one [Ben-Shahar, 2007]).

Now, with Mom gone, I find myself thinking about work. It's more than just ruminating on Too much work is bad, or wishing Mom hadn't taught me to work so darn hard. It's a deeper look back: Which part of my longing to do meaningful work was mine all along, and which part of my work ethic was handed to me at an early age and swallowed whole? Which parts of this life of hard work do I really want to keep, because it genuinely gives me something, and which parts do I want to change, because it takes more than it could ever give back?

I know work can be good for us. As social psychologist Marie Jahoda (1982) explained, beyond a paycheck, work gives us structure to our days, a sense of purpose, connection with other people, an identity, and regular activity, all important contributors to our well-being (Paul et al., 2023). I’ve felt every one of them, and I want to hold on to these parts.

But there comes a point where work stops giving and starts taking. For me, the real cost was that I had raised work above nearly everything else, even above time with the people I love… missing out on the very thing I’d hoped work would bring me in the first place. Writer Derek Thompson (2019) calls this workism, the transformation of work into the centerpiece of identity and purpose, often coming at the cost of relationship.

But my hard work wasn't only costing me relationship. It was also masking the loneliness I felt in its absence. In all the busyness and commotion and commitments that work brought, there was no time to feel the isolation I'd carried since childhood: I grew up with a Mom who loved me deeply, but who was stretched so thin by the tasks of everyday life that I was alone far more than I wanted to be. I grew up lonely, and from an early age, the busyness created by hard work spared me from feeling it.

So, what will I keep? I’ll keep working, but I want my work to be less rigid and more organic, trusting that when someone walks into my room, or my life, I can set the work down and turn toward them… rather than keep them waiting because “Work matters most of all.” Deep down, I know the research is spot on: Belonging is a fundamental human need (Baumeister & Leary, 1995), and two million years of living in small, close-knit bands have wired us to want nothing less. It’s time I move further toward it.

I think Mom wants that for me too, sharing myself with the world, not just through what I do, but through how I show up for others in this life.
