Football Daily | England’s Nations League triumph in Spain sparks familiar rush to glory

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Glory. Imminent and near-enough-guaranteed glory. That, surely, is what we’re looking at from the giddy heights England reached in crushing Spain 3-2 on Sunday. “If we can find that first hour consistently, we are a genuine favourites for the next tournament,” roared Steven Gerrard, a statement he believed in so much he then said it again using different words: “The first 60 minutes told me, from watching it, that we’re capable of winning the next tournament. That’s the big thing to take away.” Thomas Tuchel reckoned England had reached the “highest level football-wise in all aspects of the game”. This is the kind of positivity engendered in visiting international teams when they romp to victory in Madrid. Just listen to what the England captain said on the flight home: “You play like that and you’ve got a chance against anyone, anywhere. You get 11 fellows who can play a bit – and let’s face it, they’re not going to be there in the first place if they can’t – and you get them running themselves off their feet, working themselves into the ground for an hour and a half, and you’re odds on before you start, aren’t you.”

You’ll have worked out from the use of the word “fellows”, which dropped out of common parlance long before Harry Kane was born, that the person who said that wasn’t England’s megastar marksman. In fact it was a chap called Bobby Moore, after England had prevailed 2-0 in Madrid in December 1965. “If we got to the World Cup final and we went out and played like that, I’d fancy myself. No trouble about that,” Moore concluded. Seven months later England got to the final and went out and played like that, with glorious results. And what’s happening seven months from now? Only the actual Nations League final. Get those red and white ribbons ready, Monsieur Ceferin, it’s coming home.

Sure, winning in Madrid in the 1960s wasn’t exactly a guarantee of success – before England, the last team to win there had been Scotland, who on the back of a 6-2 victory in 1963 proceeded not to qualify for any major championships for another decade – but, as time passed, the achievement became rarer and the Spanish themselves stronger. So much so that before Sunday night no team had done it for 39 years, eight months and 28 days. And that was England too. In fact England have won on four of their nine visits to the city, a 44.44% rate, while everyone else in the world has emerged victorious seven times in 63 attempts, a meagre 11.11% ratio. Since that Scotland game in 1963 Spain have lost there just five times: to England (in 1965), England (in 1968), West Germany (in 1982), England (in 1987) and England (on Sunday). Madrid, it’s fair to say, is ours (sadly this has not been an exchange arrangement, Spain having won three and lost one of their last six visits to Wembley).

So, how much of our life savings should we all be putting on England to win the next [insert tournament of choice]? Well, hold your horses. In 1987, the last time they (or anyone) won in Madrid, everyone went a little over the top. The Express declared England were ready to win the World Cup. According to Gary Lineker, scorer of all four goals, “after the game Andoni Zubizaretta, the Spain goalkeeper, came into the England dressing room, walked over to me and said in a perfect cockney accent: ‘[Effing] hell.’” The following summer England headed to the Euros as second favourites. “England are quite capable of reaching their first final in a major tournament since 1966,” declared David Lacey in Big Paper’s big preview. Turned out they were even more capable of losing every game and being knocked out inside a week. The lesson: winning in Madrid might not mean anything, but it’s still good fun when it happens.
