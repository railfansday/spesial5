COP deadlock prompts calls for a reset of climate diplomacy

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

COP31 has been branded a failure after countries couldn't reach a final agreement, divided over a reference to "fossil fuels." But was the decision to walk away from a watered-down text actually a show of strength?

The longest-running climate talks in history came to a disappointing conclusion on Sunday as negotiators failed to come to an agreement on a shared political direction on climate change.

After two days of extended talks, representatives from country after country, tiredness etched into each face, voiced their opposition to the so-called "Antalya Pact."

"I understand that there is no agreement on this text. For that reason, these decisions have not been adopted," said Murat Kurum, Turkish president of the COP31 climate summit.

One of the main sticking points: the words "fossil fuels." While some nations, including petrostates like Saudi Arabia and Russia, wanted them omitted from the text, others saw it as a "step backwards" not to include them.

Around 130 parties — among them vulnerable island nations, developing countries and richer EU members — called for the reintroduction of the two words into the text, to no avail. And so many refused to support the adoption of the final COP agreement.

The deadlock signals a failure for the UN process. But the number of countries willing to walk away from what they saw as a weak text was, arguably, a show of strength, and one that had been long coming.

The phrase "fossil fuels" first appeared three years ago in the closing decision of COP28 in Dubai with the promise of "transitioning away from fossil fuels in energy systems, in just, orderly and equitable manner." But it has been omitted from every text since.

Yet this year's climate summit was the first to acknowledge that the world will breach the critical 1.5-degree Celsius (2.7-degree Fahrenheit) threshold that scientists said would safeguard the world from the worst impacts of planetary heating.

And so, the pressure was on for progress; to turn pledges from previous years into concrete action. But that progress didn't come.

"We don't celebrate the deep divisions exposed or the lack of a political signal from COP31 capable of driving climate action in line with science, equity and the law," said Nikki Reisch, director of the climate and energy program at the Center for International Environmental Law.

"But the rejected text would have done more harm than good."

The lack of agreement was not solely about fossil fuels; money also played a part.

Developing nations have long argued that they need additional financing, both as a means for them to move away from coal, oil and gas, but also to help them cope with the consequences of rising temperatures.

Countries all over the world are being battered by extreme weather events. Catastrophes like flooding, drought and wildfires have been fueled and worsened by rising global temperatures. Often the worst hit are poorer countries with low rates of greenhouse gas emissions.

And yet, richer states behind much of the historical emissions are increasingly unwilling to foot the bill. Dissatisfaction at home due to higher food and fuel prices has left many governments loathe to pay more to finance climate action abroad.

With the United States  — the world's biggest historical emitter  — now absent from negotiations and on the verge of leaving the United Nation's Framework Convention on Climate Change, the European Union was portrayed as this year's villain with some observers accusing the EU of hypocrisy.

"You can't demand action and then block the finance that makes this possible," Fanny Petitbon, policy analyst at 350.org, said in a statement.

As the world's largest provider of climate finance, the EU reportedly refused to negotiate on providing additional finance and has repeatedly called for other nations to step up.

The failure to find an agreement is a particularly bitter turn of events for countries that face ever worsening impacts of climate change, like the island states that are being confronted by a reality that could see their countries wiped off the map as sea levels rise.

Although the Turkey-led electrification agenda calling for an increase in global electricity consumption to 35% by 2035 was not formally adopted — it made up part of the rejected final agreement — more than 90 countries pledged to move forward regardless.

It's not a silver bullet. There was no agreement on ensuring the increase was forged only through clean, renewable energy. But it would still signal forward momentum, driven by economic forces and a need for home-grown energy security.

And there are other hopes. The second conference on the Just Transition Away from Fossil Fuels will take place in Tuvalu in July 2027. Initiated at the COP30 conference last year in Belem, Brazil, it has seen increasing support from countries putting together concrete roadmaps for abandoning coal, oil and gas.

There has been much talk of blame and who is responsible for the breakdown and collapse of talks. What is clear is that the UN climate summits need reform. Progress, when it does happen, is slow. The present model, based on consensus, requires all countries to agree on every measure. There have been many calls for majority-rule decision making, which might have forced the inclusion of language like "fossil fuels" into the text.

But ultimately, this is not the time to focus on fault. As the climate crisis accelerates, governments need to reflect — and quickly — on what can be done outside of these UN processes, within their nations, to speed up climate action.

"The take-home message from COP31 is clear: the world must do much more to confront the climate crisis — and it must do it now," said UN Secretary-General Antonio Guterres in a statement.
