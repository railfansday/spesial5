The energy strategy most companies don’t have

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

For trucking fleets, manufacturers, and other energy-intensive businesses, energy has been little more than a line item for the past two decades. Today it’s a competitive variable. Whether a business can secure the energy it needs—and afford it—increasingly determines whether it can grow.

Global energy use keeps growing and electricity demand is rising more than twice as fast as energy use overall, according to the International Energy Agency.

The problem is that war and geopolitical conflict, along with tariffs reshaping the cost of equipment, are affecting supply and cost. Policies are also shifting with election cycles. This year alone, oil swung from $118 a barrel to under $72 and back above $100, while Washington rewrote the nation’s fuel-economy rules. No single shock is decisive—the shocks compound.

As a result, nearly every fleet and fuel-retail operator I speak with is wrestling with whether to use electric, diesel, or natural gas. However, energy markets are moving too fast and too unevenly for anyone to call it with confidence. The operators pulling ahead have stopped trying to choose one. Instead, they’re building the capability of running on more than one energy source—and deciding in real time which to use, where, and when.

FedEx is a case in point. It now runs nearly 9,500 electric vehicles while testing renewable diesel for the long-haul trucks where, as the company puts it, “full electrification is not yet feasible at scale.” And it isn’t alone: Roughly 19% of North American fleets now operate EVs, and 30% run hybrid or alternative-fuel vehicles.

PAY FOR FLEXIBILITY WHERE IT COUNTS

Optionality isn’t free. A second energy source means added infrastructure, more complex procurement and, often, some loss of efficiency. No business can hedge everything. The discipline is deciding where flexibility is worth paying for.

The most resilient operators I see ask two questions about every site, route, and asset: If this energy source were disrupted tomorrow, how much revenue would stop? And how exposed is it to a single fuel, supplier, or grid connection?

Where both answers are high, redundancy is insurance worth buying now. A delivery depot that charges its entire fleet from one utility feed can’t run routes when that feed goes down. Where impact is high but exposure is modest, flexible supply contracts, rather than long-term agreements locking in a single source, may be enough. Where the impact is low, optimize for cost and move on.

THE EASY PART IS THE HARDWARE

Adding EV chargers to a forecourt or alternative-fuel trucks to a fleet is now easy. Running them together is not.

A mixed-energy operation creates decisions a single-fuel business never faced. Which vehicle takes which route, given its charge level and the day’s energy prices? When should a depot charge its equipment to avoid peak demand costs? How do you compare cost per mile across diesel, natural gas, and electric when the data lives in separate systems?

The advantage goes to businesses that can see their entire energy picture in one place and act on it. In a volatile market, the ability to switch is only as valuable as the ability to know when to switch.

3 MOVES WORTH MAKING NOW

For leaders of fleet, fuel-retail, and other energy-intensive businesses, building a multi-energy operation starts with understanding where you’re vulnerable and what flexibility is worth. These three moves lay that groundwork.

1. Map your 24-hour exposure. Identify the sites, routes, and assets that would stop generating revenue within a day of losing their primary energy source.

2. Put a price on flexibility. Capital cases that compare only cost per gallon or per kilowatt-hour miss the value of being able to switch. Make the cost of downtime an explicit line in every energy investment.

3. Instrument before you diversify. You can’t coordinate what you can’t see. Bring fuel, charging, payments, and fleet data into a single view before adding the next energy source.

    Energy isn’t going to get more predictable. Leaders who build for adaptability will succeed whichever way it goes. As an executive, ask yourself: If your primary energy source were disrupted tomorrow, how quickly could your business respond?

    Mark Morelli is president and CEO of Vontier.
