The Quorum Clause Case

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

The consequences are serious, but the merits are strong.

In 2020, during the early days of the pandemic, the House of Representatives adopted a rule that would allow absent members to cast a vote by proxy.  At the time, there were ample warnings that this process had constitutional infirmities, but the House pressed ahead. Indeed, the House of Representatives continued to use the proxy rule through the end of 2022. One such bill was the Consolidation Appropriations Act of 2023. At this time, the justification for social distancing had passed. The new House assembled in January 2023. I see no masks. Members of Congress are walking around with their families.

At the 2023 State of the Union Address in March 2023, every every seat was filled.  Look at this picture. I don't see many members wearing masks.

Don't tell me there was some actual need to bypass the quorum rule at this point.

The pandemic was only a few years ago, but it is important to remember how many brazen violations of the Constitution there were in short order. As Justice Gorsuch wrote in Roman Catholic Diocese v. Brooklyn v. Cuomo, "Even if the Constitution has taken a holiday during this pandemic, it cannot become a sabbatical." The proxy rule was one such sabbatical.

In 2023, Texas challenged the constitutionality of one provision of the appropriations bill, the Pregnant Workers Fairness Act. In 2024, Judge Hendrix of the Northern District of Texas wrote a magisterial opinion that stretched more than one-hundred pages. He explained in depth why the enrolled bill doctrine does not block review and why the proxy rule violates the quorum clause. At the time, I read the opinion and thought Judge Hendrix put forward an extremely justification. The House tried to take a shortcut and got caught. A panel of the Fifth Circuit reversed, but now the en banc Fifth Circuit has affirmed Judge Hendrix's decision.

I'll admit, the immediate reaction to this decision is "it can't be right!" This intuition, I submit, is based more on the consequences than the merits. If every bill passed during the COVID period is now subject to a constitutional challenge, the consequences could be serious. But I encourage you to back off the consequentialism. Far too many judges and lawyers start with the consequences and work their way backwards. If the House of Representatives decided to take a shortcut and ignored a constitutional requirement, then the members of that body should bear the consequences of their decision.

The en banc Fifth Circuit split (as if often does) by a 9-8 vote. Thankfully, there were no senior judges on the three-judge panel. Judge Wilson wrote the majority opinion and Judge Graves wrote the dissent. Judge Haynes continues her unusual practice where she doesn't join any opinion, reducing her thoughts to a single footnote.

* Judge Haynes states that she does not think it is appropriate to throw out the Federal House of Representatives in this arena, so she dissents. She also appreciates Judge Graves's dissent.

Judge Haynes might think she is being clever, but I doubt anyone else agrees. Indeed, in a number of recent cases, Judge Haynes simply writes that she affirms for the reasons stated by the district court--as if nothing that her colleagues wrote made a difference. I think it shows a lack of respect to her colleagues who spend considerable time preparing opinions and she doesn't even bother explaining where she disagrees. She is capable of writing reasoned opinions, but just shrugs at the en banc stage.

Judges Wilson and Judge Graves agree that Texas has standing. They further agree that the enrolled bill rule does not preclude review. The disagreement is purely a legal question about whether the proxy rule violates the Quorum Clause. Having read both the majority and dissenting opinions, I don't think the issue is even close. The strongest argument for the dissent is the "Unanimous Consent" rule, and the majority demolishes that argument.

I am fairly certain that the new Attorney General opposes this case and will do everything in his power to scuttle the suit, as well as the Fifth Circuit's en banc precedent. In recent days, the Texas Attorney General has been dismissing and settling many cases. The reason, I suspect, is to avoid giving the new Attorney General a vehicle to enter into binding consent decrees. I suppose it is conceivable that Texas will try to enter into some kind of settlement with the United States. Or maybe Texas just moves to dismiss the case with prejudice before January 1. That would end the case, but leave the Fifth Circuit's precedent in place.

I suppose another state with a similar Article III injury could intervene. I am not certain about the timing as there is a final judgment in place--although a judgment is not really final until a cert petition is disposed of.

If this case stays alive past January, the United States will almost certainly file a cert petition. The new Texas Attorney General will not oppose certiorari and may ask for a summary reversal. I suspect the new AG could even argue that since there is no adversity, there is no longer a live Article III case, and the Fifth Circuit decision should be vacated under Musingwear. I am not entirely sure how Munsingwear applies when the Attorney General switches sides, but we may soon find out. Then again, the Supreme Court routinely decides cases in which there is no adversity, so the case might continue.

Indeed, the Supreme Court might also think it prudent to settle this matter. These challenges can arise in other contexts, and the longer this issue festers, the worse it will be. If so, the Court could appoint an amicus to defend the Fifth Circuit's judgment.

At that point, what will happen? Again, all members of the Fifth Circuit agreed that Texas has standing and the enrolled bill rule doesn't bar review. I suppose the Supreme Court could reject those holdings, but that would be rather transparent.

Once you get to the merits, the Court will have to do some work. I've seen some commentators lead of with a discussion of how many laws might be at risk. One of those statutes is the Electoral Count Reform Act of 2022. I'll close where I started. The meaning of the Constitution should not change based on the consequences. Either the House had a quorum or it didn't.

Do I have faith that a majority of the Supreme Court will follow that original meaning of the Constitution? No, I don't. Maybe three votes, at the most--which is often how the Fifth Circuit fares. But don't pretend the Supreme Court is making its ruling based on law.
