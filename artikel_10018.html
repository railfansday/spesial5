Rounding was never the issue. Sure, a store could be sued for rounding in the customer's favor, and they can continue to be sued for rounding after this law goes into effect. In the US you can sue anyone for anything. The question is will you win or not, and how much it'll cost the defendant. An individual suit over rounding would never happen if a merchant rounded in the customer's favor, the plaintiff couldn't show any damages and no lawyer would take their case on contingency so they'd have to pay up front if they wanted to have a go knowing they'll lose. What merchants were worried about is the class-action suit where even if the defendant's guaranteed to win (again, because the plaintiff can't show any damages) the cost of winning would be prohibitive and the settlement offer including attorney's fees would be less than the cost of winning. Which isn't a problem with rounding to the nearest nickel or anything real, it's about the relative costs of defending against a baseless lawsuit vs. settling.

IMO the new law is good anyway, the rounding error will even out so at the end of the year customers will be left same as they were if they'd got exact change. Besides which, the majority of people use cards for payment so they won't be affected at all.

A better solution would be one like European VAT uses where the tax is included in the purchase price. Then merchants could just adjust the price to be an even multiple of a nickel and no changes are needed in the system. It'd also allow true comparison of prices since the price would be what you'd actually pay.
