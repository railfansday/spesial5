Why El Clasico Is the Best Grudge Match in All of Sports

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Last Saturday, before the biggest grudge match in international soccer, Spanish police officers decked out in riot gear surrounded my Barcelona hotel. Blue lights bounced from the sun-stained stone of the alley as kids scrambled away in the distance. In the skirmish, one had lost a single ASICS sneaker that sat idly in the road until, sometime between night and the light of day, someone snatched it up for themselves. You often hear of the way heated football rivalries and cup finals bring out a kind of mob mentality among fans. The match, however, wasn’t set to kick off for another 24 hours.

Three days prior I’d landed in Barcelona to attend FC Barcelona versus Real Madrid, better known as El Clásico, for which Tyler the Creator’s GOLF brand had partnered with Spotify on Barcelona’s gameday kits. If you care about football in the slightest you know about El Clásico, though it’s worth knowing about even if you don’t.

Since 1902—the year of Real Madrid’s founding, and three years after that of FC Barcelona—the two biggest football clubs in Spain have clashed in a rivalry match dubbed El Clásico (the face-off now happens twice a season, plus various tournament play that varies in schedule year to year). Before last week’s installment, the match had been played 264 times over 122 years. In most high-stakes football rivalries, one club holds a historical edge: Arsenal has a handy 20 to 30 wins over Tottenham Hotspur in the North London Derby, depending on when you start keeping track, while Manchester United edges out Liverpool in total wins. But heading into the first El Clásico of the 2026-27 La Liga season, Barcelona and Real Madrid stood deadlocked with 106 wins each.

American sports rivalries are often the result of proximity, with most pitting divisional neighbors and crosstown rivals against one another, with the exception of teams that share a bitter history of championship bouts (like the Lakers and the Celtics). But if we’re being honest, not even Yankees-Red Sox holds a candle to El Clásico. Apart from both competing in the upper echelon of La Liga’s clubs, FC Barcelona and Real Madrid’s rivalry has stewed in over a century of political turmoil between the Catalan capital and the city that houses the Spanish throne. During the 20th century as Spain looked to enforce a uniform identity across the nation, Catalonia sought to maintain its independence from the crown and, crucially, its right to speak Catalan rather than Spanish. Francisco Franco’s dictatorship brought more enmity, as Madrid became the proxy club for a centralized Spain while Barcelona came to represent Catalonia’s Republican Left. During the Franco years, when both the language and the flag of Catalan were banned, Barcelona’s Camp Nou stadium became a safe haven for the free expression of Catalan identity. Meanwhile, Franco’s foreign minister Fernando Maria de Castiella declared Real Madrid “the best embassy we’ve ever had.” The wounds of that era have hardly subsided: while the war may be over, as recently as 2017 the Catalan people voted on whether or not they’d remain a part of Spain (the vote was declared illegal by the Spanish government, and there was police intervention at polling stations).

Today, the clubs are recognized as polar opposites in every sense, from politics to style of play: Real Madrid stands for conservatism and a unified Spain, while Barcelona remains the club of the rebellious Left. Madrid takes an Evil Empire approach to roster construction, buying up the most exciting free agents on the market for record-breaking fees. (This century alone they’ve rostered Cristiano Ronaldo, David Beckham, and Zinedine Zidane, and the club currently boasts a striker line that includes Jude Bellingham, Kylian Mbappé, and Vinicius Jr.) Barcelona, on the other hand, has the most acclaimed and successful youth academy in all of football. The school, La Masia, has promoted a singular theory of football for decades, prioritizing position play and a dominant possession game perfected by FC Barcelona veterans like Lionel Messi, Xavi, Pep Guardiola, and Andrés Iniesta. When Spain won the World Cup this summer, they did so with eight former students of La Masia on the pitch (one of whom, Marc Cucarella, currently plays for Real Madrid).

To this day, the rivalry is characterized by an unmistakable hostility. In my four days in Barcelona, I didn’t see one person wearing Real Madrid’s crest, though the souvenir shops lining La Rambla were well-stocked with the club’s bootleg gear. At one point, I walked past a wall scrawled with graffiti: “SPANISH GO HOME.” When we finally got to the match, most Madrid fans were sequestered to a supporter section in the upper deck surrounded by protective plexiglass.

“This is the biggest match in club football,” explained Joel Jaggar, a British transplant who’s lived in Barcelona for a decade. Jaggar runs Real Vintage Football, a rare jersey boutique in El Raval (just outside of the city’s Gothic Quarter) that was unsurprisingly overwhelmed with visitors all weekend. He’d stocked up on at least a hundred Messi jerseys, all of which were gone by the time they closed on Friday. “A lot of people come to Barcelona specifically for this game,” he shared. “Big fans, sure, but also people who want to experience the game for the sake of the experience.” Globally, he argued, the word “Barcelona” is as closely associated with the club as it is the city. “You can’t say the same for Madrid,” he quipped.

Jaggar and I met the morning after a swell of student-organized protests regarding the city’s cost-of-living crisis boiled over into vandalism and looting near the neighborhood that houses his store. There was even suspicion El Clásico would have to be postponed or cancelled altogether. Come morning though, those anxieties had faded–as is so often the case, football continued uninterrupted.

Even with capacity capped at around 62,000 during ongoing renovations, Camp Nou is something to behold. Ahead of kickoff, supporters lifted color-coordinated pieces of poster nestled into each seat,forming a massive, stadium-wide tifo in the colors of the club and, in our section, the Catalonian flag. Fireworks exploded, and El Clásico began.

But much of the match’s first half was mired in tension, as Barcelona dominated possession but failed to score. The crowd finally began to pop when Madrid nabbed their first goal, then properly exploded when Barca tied it up in the second half. When Mbappé scored his second goal of the evening at ‘87 minutes after a beautiful assist from Bellingham, he vaulted over to the gate of the Barcelona supporters’ section, taunting them as trash thrown from the crowd rained over him. Madrid held on, gaining the tiniest of advantages in the El Clásico win count: 107 to their rivals’ 106.

As we walked out of Camp Nou, which will hold a whopping 105,000 spectators when its renovation is complete, I thought about the sort of sporting events that tend to grace bucket lists: Super Bowls and World Cup Finals and World Series games. So often the point is the spectacle of a season or tournament ending—the big trophy, the championship ring, the confetti and fireworks. El Clásico is different. There’s no trophy at the end, no ultimate resolution. Like the ideological divide for which it’s become a proxy, El Clásico is in a constant state of ebb and flow. Each time Barcelona and Real Madrid take the pitch, there’s no decisive victory so much as another brick laid in the foundation of football’s greatest rivalry. These clubs have come to blows 265 times. But as long as there are football stadiums in Barcelona and Madrid, El Clásico will endure as a conduit for the enduring rancor between the country's two biggest cities.
