Humor in the ICU

I was likely the oldest fellow in the nation. I had switched from a long, happy career in headache medicine, largely a calm, outpatient practice, to the challenging world of neurocritical care.

Here I was, entering a field I knew nothing about, critical care medicine, at age 60. My attendings, mostly emergency room doctors, were often half my age!

Basically, I was terrified daily. Life Flight would arrive with a crashing subarachnoid patient, and I stood at the foot of the bed wondering what I should do next, what have I missed, how do I dodge the inevitable.

When I was faced with the chaos of treating hemorrhagic shock or sky-high intracranial pressures, my sense of humor evolved. As a headache doctor, my days had been relatively predictable. I listened to migraine stories and occasionally shared a laugh about the frustrations of missing keen life events “just because I had a headache.” But my sense of humor was gentle then, rarely requiring an explanation.

Now, faced with patients and families confronting unimaginable loss from catastrophic brain injuries, somewhere along the way, my sense of humor changed. I suddenly saw a chance remark, or lighthearted musing from a person facing death squarely in the face, as strikingly funny. Because patients are funny, and at times, unbelievably brave.

We had an older man with Tourette’s and a cervical spine fracture, waiting for hemodynamic stabilization before our neurosurgeons would take him to the OR for decompression. We poked him with a pin every hour on the hour, watching carefully for an ascending sensory level that would indicate his respirations were threatened. We placed a Foley and used digital stimulation when he developed an ileus. He tolerated this quietly for two days, but then, as he exited the ICU on a gurney under the watchful eye of the anesthesia team, he blurted out with some glee:

“Tell Doctor Tucker, the mother-f***r, goodbye and good riddance!!” We burst out laughing!

Swearing seems to relieve the tension. One of our older general surgeons, technically without peer, looked at us incredulously when we asked him to obtain consent for a tracheostomy from a locked-in patient who could only blink his eyes to assent. That evening, gray with fatigue from a long abdominal obstruction surgical case, he took one look at our patient, for whom he would need to painstakingly get consent one blink at a time, and said to us…

"How many blinks to f***-off?" But, of course, he stayed late and got consent.

Sometimes a timely, irreverent comment brings a smile in our work room late at night. It’s not that humor indicates a loss of our sensitivity to suffering; it’s that we develop humor to live and work beside suffering.

I often tell a story to our residents and fellows of a young woman who was called to see her younger brother, who had been struck by a car and lay quietly, but his face deeply bruised and swollen. She walked into his ICU room and immediately lost consciousness. She had Takotsubo’s stress cardiomyopathy (the broken heart syndrome), which caused cardiac arrest the moment she saw him. She was quickly resuscitated and recovered completely.

But I ask my team, tongue in cheek, if they have ever had a relative they weren’t fond of…and might like to do away with. I say, “Consider “happy heart syndrome…” Give her a surprise birthday party! Have all the guests hide in the dark at her house, balloons and toy horns ready; she opens the door, the lights suddenly go on, the balloons burst, and she keels over…dead.

No forensic pathologist could ever implicate you in her death. No toxins were given, no stab wounds to the heart, no malicious intent. The perfect crime.

Humor is more resilience than indifference. We do have to be careful not to laugh too loud in our workroom, as families nearby hold the hands of their fading, dying loved ones.

But humor reminds us that in the fragile world of critical care medicine, it is our humanity that steels us to come back to work the next night, and the next. If we can smile sometimes, in the face of sadness, we see that smile reflected in the faces of our patients and those who love them.
