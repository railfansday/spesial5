How Many Flight Attendants Do Airlines Typically Hire In A Year?

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

Cabin crew or flight attendant recruitment has always been extremely competitive, with thousands of people applying for a relatively small number of jobs. Only a very small percentage make it through the complicated hiring process, and it's well known that it is easier to get into Harvard than to get a flight attendant job. Only between one and four percent of all applicants get the job.

This guide will look into the facts and figures around airlines and recruitment numbers and what we can expect in the coming years. Will there be a shortage of cabin crew, and will the recruitment process have to change? How many cabin crew do airlines typically hire in a year?

Across the aviation industry in the US, airlines generate around 18,500 flight attendant job openings per year on average. Each airline's hiring varies depending on fleet size, route expansion, and annual attrition rates. Major carriers such as Delta Air Lines, American Airlines, United Airlines, and Emirates routinely recruit between 1,000 and 5,000 new cabin crew members each year. This can rise up to 8,000 during major post-downturn or surges in fleet delivery.

In contrast, smaller regional and low-cost airlines generally hire anywhere from a few hundred to around 1,000 cabin crew annually. This covers base growth and typical turnover rates of around 4% to 8%. Major carriers often receive upwards of 100,000 applications for just a few thousand spots, so hiring occurs in competitive, periodic windows that match aircraft deliveries and seasonal travel demand.

The US Bureau of Labor Statistics states that there were 133,700 flight attendant jobs in 2025, and the employment of flight attendants is projected to grow 9 percent from 2025 to 2035, much faster than the average occupation. Many of those openings are expected to result from the need to replace workers who transfer to different occupations or exit the labor force, for example, when retiring.

Despite the growing need for flight attendants, acceptance rates are extremely low, with 90% of applications being filtered out at the very first stage. Only 1% to 4% of applicants make the grade, and even then, not everyone completes training. It is a highly competitive career with many hoops to jump through. Emirates reportedly receives over 15,000 cabin crew applicationsevery month, according to Vertas Management. Just one percent of the more than 100,000 applicants get the job, according to Delta's hiring records obtained by FOX News. Major carriers like Delta frequently receive over 100,000 applications for roughly 1,000 to 1,200 roles.

The multi-stage screening process can take months, including screening, interviews, assessment days, online assessments, security checks, and medical checks, and often takes between three and six months. Even after that, there is a six- to eight-week training course that is heavily focused on safety and emergency procedures and aircraft equipment. Exam pass marks are very high, and there are only two chances to pass. Each batch usually loses a quarter of trainees during training.

Airlines are very picky about their crew members and expect high standards of safety and customer service. Personal presentation is extremely important too, as cabin crew are the face of the airline and represent the brand as a whole. Airlines can afford to be extremely selective about who they hire because they know there will always be plenty of candidates available. Some people don't understand the true nature of the job and may leave during training or probation because it's not what they thought it would be.

The same job but very different circumstances.

In January 2024, Emirates announced it was recruiting 5,000 cabin crew for 2024 to coincide with the arrival of the new A350 fleet. In 2023, they hired 8,000 cabin crew in 353 cities worldwide, creating a large and diverse workforce of 21,500 cabin crew as of August 2023. They are now estimated to have over 27,000 cabin crew. Emirates has reportedly changed its reapplication process from three months to six months, so a candidate can now apply only twice a year instead of four times a year.

American Airlines now employs just over 30,000 flight attendants, making it one of the largest cabin crew workforces of any airline in the world. The figure puts American ahead of rivals Delta and United, which each employ around 28,000 to 30,000 flight attendants. Southwest Airlines has around 20,000 flight attendants.

In 2025, Lufthansa recruited more than 2,000 flight attendants, according to Aeronews. Ryanair has over 16,000 flight attendants and is still recruiting for cabin crew. Qantas plans to recruit 600 flight attendants within five years to support its growing international network, according to Aerviva. The airline has also opened up a new base in Singapore. British Airways currently has around 15,000 cabin crew, with 12,000 being based at Heathrow, covering both short-haul and long-haul routes.

According to Aerviva, forecasts predict the need for over 1 million new cabin crew members through 2045, yet acceptance rates at major airlines remain under 5%. The International Air Transport Association (IATA) reports that international passenger travel demand is up 7.1% year-on-year and that it will double in the next two decades.

Boeing has also forecasted a demand for 1.02 million cabin crew members through to 2045, with around 40,000 openings every year to support fleet growth and replace retired crew or those who move to a different occupation. The global commercial aircraft fleet is projected to grow 2.4% annually through 2035, with widebody deliveries surging, particularly for US and Middle Eastern airlines.

Under EASA and FAA regulations, one flight attendant per 50 passenger seats is required legally for safety in commercial operations. Taking that into consideration, and with expected aircraft deliveries, India and South Asia alone will need around 51,000 new cabin crew by 2044 as their fleets treble.

This is fewer than the 13,000 hires it brought in last year.

Delta describes a seven-week onsite training program in Atlanta for successful candidates. The airline's hiring process includes an application, assessment, virtual job tryout, on-demand interview, event-day interview, and a conditional job offer before training. Delta also states that its application window opens and closes according to operational needs. Candidates who are not selected should follow the reapplication period shown in the current requisition. Most airlines ask candidates to wait at least three to six months before reapplying.

United has a 6.5-week training program in Houston after the selection stages. United says trainees become employees after graduating from training. The airline outlines the required qualifications and directs applicants to the posted role for current details. Requirements may change, for example, if they need speakers of a particular language or crew at a particular base. Applicants should read flight attendant postings thoroughly and check all working conditions before applying.

Most candidates do not do the research to find out what the job really is, and that is one reason so many people fail to get a position. They see the uniform and glamorous destinations but do not see the long hours, relatively low salary, and complicated lifestyle. They also do not always understand that the primary role of the job is to evacuate passengers in an emergency or deal with medical emergencies and fire onboard.

Global cabin crew openings are estimated at roughly 50,000 per year, driven by fleet growth and crew turnover or retirement. Airline recruitment is very dynamic and can change rapidly depending on fleet expansion. Regarding flight attendant recruitment, surges in hiring are also due to changes in airlines’ operational trends, expanding markets and networks, and shifts in candidate trends.

Global cabin crew recruitment is experiencing a sustained hiring boom, with CAE's Aviation Talent Forecast projecting a need for 678,000 new cabin crew members globally by 2034. Over 60% of global demand (roughly 415,000 roles) is driven by replacing retiring or departing staff rather than pure fleet expansion, the report says. Asia-Pacific leads global demand (38% of projected hires), followed by Europe (142,000), North America (129,000), and the Middle East (95,000).

Airlines are recruiting well ahead of the peak travel season; for example, airlines hiring in late 2026 are actively filling January/February training courses to staff summer 2027 schedules. Some airlines are closing their recruitment drives early, sometimes within 24 or 48 hours, because they have received so many applicants.

Shifts in airline recruitment include attracting cabin crew over 50 in the UK. EasyJet has seen an increase in over-50s hired, double that of 2022, and a fourfold increase in over-60s hired. Some airlines have started funding their training courses, or paying a salary during training instead of it being unpaid as was previously. As airlines mostly expand premium cabins, recruiters are also looking to prioritize hospitality experience and multilingual skills. Some of the rules around gender-specific uniforms and visible tattoos and piercings have also changed in recent years.
