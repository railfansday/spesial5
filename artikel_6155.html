The Hotel Restaurant Is Becoming the Main Event Again

Hotel restaurants are becoming destinations again. The smartest owners are giving chefs and operators the freedom to create places with their own character and following, rather than trying to serve every guest at every hour.

When César Ritz and Auguste Escoffier took over the Savoy hotel in London around 1890, they made its dining room the place to be seen. High-society women rarely ate in public then, but Ritz set out to make it irresistible. He fussed over lampshades until the light turned a flattering apricot, hired an orchestra for weekend nights, and insisted on evening dress.

On the food front, Escoffier supplied the reasons to come back, creating Peach Melba for Nellie Melba and getting frogs' legs past the Prince of Wales by calling them nymphs' thighs.

New money soon rubbed elbows with dukes, actors and the occasional prince. For eight years, the dining room was the reason the hotel stood out. Success went to their heads. The Savoy fired them in 1898 over missing wine and supplier kickbacks, with its owner, Richard D'Oyly Carte, complaining that they had "forgot they were servants."

Hotel restaurants are being rebuilt as destinations with their own identity rather than all-purpose outlets serving every guest need. The article traces the idea to César Ritz and Auguste Escoffier at the Savoy in the 1890s, when the dining room was the reason the hotel stood out, and argues that hotel kitchens later became Swiss Army knife utilities covering early breakfast, business lunch, late dinner, and midnight room service — a menu written to offend nobody. The current shift separates the headline restaurant from daily guest feeding: Baldi at the Waldorf Astoria Beverly Hills serves neither breakfast nor lunch, NUNA at the Four Seasons Miami focuses on dinner while Edge handles breakfast and lunch, and Kwame Onwuachi's Dōgon at the Salamander in Washington operates five evenings a week. Owners are also choosing operators with existing local followings over celebrity-chef prestige alone — the Waldorf replaced Jean-Georges's first West Coast restaurant with a local restaurant dynasty. The payoff, per the article, is social gravity: repeat local visits, cultural relevance, and business value that has little to do with occupancy upstairs. The cost is giving restaurateurs freedom to operate on their own terms.
