5 Things to Do if You're Forced to Retire Sooner Than Expected

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

It's not an ideal situation, but don't panic.

When you build a retirement plan around ending your career at a certain age, and you're forced to exit the labor force sooner, it can constitute a pretty big blow. But it's not totally uncommon to be forced into an earlier retirement due to a layoff, health challenges, or the need to care for a loved one.

The good news is that being pushed out of the workforce ahead of schedule doesn't have to completely upend your long-term plans. Here's how to pivot and salvage the situation.

If you're forced into early retirement due to a layoff, the first thing to do is review your exit package. You may be entitled to severance pay, plus compensation for accrued vacation and/or sick time.

Take the time to review the details of your severance agreement so you know what to expect. Also keep in mind that your agreement may include certain restrictions, so don't just gloss over the fine print.

Being forced into retirement sooner than planned could mean losing your health insurance. If you're not yet 65, you're typically too young for Medicare, which means you'll need to secure coverage on your own.

Take the time to review your options, which may include COBRA if you can afford the premiums or an Affordable Care Act marketplace plan. If your spouse is still working and has insurance through their job, joining that health plan may be the most affordable bet. While you should make sure to compare your choices thoroughly, don't wait, because going without health insurance is inherently risky.

If you're at least 62 when you're forced to retire, it means you're old enough to start collecting Social Security. Whether you should start benefits is a different question.

You won't get your Social Security benefits in full until age 67 if you were born in 1960 or later. And for each year you delay your claim past that point, until age 70, your benefits get a permanent 8% boost.

Filing for Social Security ahead of schedule could reduce a critical income stream for life, so before you do that, assess your retirement savings. You may be able to comfortably withdraw additional money from your IRA or 401(k) for a period of time so you can hold off on taking benefits right away.

Retiring sooner than planned could mean that your savings have to last longer, and that you need to reduce portfolio risk sooner. Take the time to assess your portfolio and make sure you have at least a few years' worth of safe investments, like bonds.

It's also wise to set yourself up with enough cash to cover one to two years of expenses. That way, if you need money when your portfolio is down, you won't have to lock in permanent losses by liquidating assets at a bad time.

If the reason for your forced retirement boils down to being downsized out of a job and you can't find a new one in your field, it doesn't mean your days of working have to end. You may be able to find flexible part-time work that lets you earn some income and stay busy.

Let's say you were earning $8,000 a month at your full-time job. If you're able to find gig work or a part-time role that lets you earn $2,000 a month, you're looking at a $6,000 monthly income shortfall instead of $8,000. Those part-time wages could take some pressure off your savings if you're tapping your IRA or 401(k) sooner than planned.

Just as importantly, having a job to go to could be good for your mental health. Even if you don't necessarily need the money, it could be an option worth pursuing.

Being forced to end your career before you're ready is not an easy situation to cope with. Taking the steps above could help protect your finances as you navigate your new reality.
