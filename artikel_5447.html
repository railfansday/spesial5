From NDAs to exit interviews: 10 ways companies protect trade secrets

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Trade secret protection against insider threats depends on layered controls, and these 10 measures, from access limits and NDAs to exit interviews, show how companies keep secrets in-house

In court, the value of a formula or a customer list is only half the test of a trade secret. The other half is whether its owner worked to keep it secret. Under U.S. law, information keeps trade secret status only if its owner takes reasonable measures to protect that secrecy. The Defend Trade Secrets Act of 2016 and the Uniform Trade Secrets Act, adopted in some form by most states, both contain that requirement. Source code or a pricing model left open to anyone can lose that protection.

Insiders are the hardest case for trade secret protection. Employees, contractors and vendors already hold legitimate access to the information a company most wants to keep. They do not need to break into anything. They need only copy a file, forward an email or carry knowledge out the door when they leave.

A 2018 federal case shows how that risk can surface. Prosecutors charged former Apple $AAPL engineer Xiaolang Zhang with stealing trade secrets related to the company's self-driving car work. The case involved an employee, not an outside intruder.

No single control closes the gap. Companies tend to layer legal, technical and human measures so that a failure in one can be caught by another. Contracts set expectations and give an employer a route to court. Software limits what a person can reach and records what they do. Training and offboarding practices shape whether people understand the rules and treat them as binding.

Those layers also serve a second purpose. If a dispute reaches court, a record of classification policies, access reviews and signed agreements helps show that the company took reasonable measures. Without that record, the legal claim itself can weaken.

This list covers 10 measures companies commonly use, from the first day of employment to the last and in some cases beyond it. It is a selection, not a complete catalog, and the order is not a ranking. Each slide explains one method, how it works in practice and where its limits lie. Security, legal and human resources teams can use it as a starting framework for an insider threat program. Other readers can use it to understand how organizations approach the risk of someone with a badge and a login walking away with what they know.

Companies start guarding trade secrets by deciding, in writing, which information qualifies. A firm cannot easily show a court that it protected a secret it never identified. It also cannot direct staff or software to protect information that no one has labeled.

The legal definition explains why this step comes first. Under the Defend Trade Secrets Act of 2016, information must draw independent economic value from not being generally known. A written inventory shows which information the company believes meets that test. It also gives the company a dated record to point to if it must show that it took reasonable measures.

An inventory typically begins with the categories that give a business its competitive edge. These can include product formulas, manufacturing processes, source code, algorithms, pricing models, supplier terms and customer lists. People from engineering, sales and research usually take part. They know which details would help a rival most. The inventory records where each item is stored, who is responsible for it and which teams use it.

Classification comes next. Many companies use tiered labels, such as public, internal, confidential and restricted. The tier determines how the information is stored, shared and eventually destroyed. A formula for a flagship product might sit in the highest tier. A general employee handbook might sit in the lowest.

Confidentiality markings put those labels into practice. A header or watermark on a document, a banner on a database login screen or a tag in file metadata tells a reader the material is protected. Markings also matter in litigation. A departing employee has a harder time claiming ignorance about a file stamped "confidential."

The approach has a known weakness. If a company marks nearly everything as confidential, the label stops signaling anything. Employees may learn to ignore it. In a dispute, an opposing lawyer can argue that the company never separated real secrets from routine paperwork.

Inventories also go stale. New products, acquisitions and reorganizations change what a company holds and what it values. For that reason, many firms review their lists on a regular schedule. The goal is a record that matches what the business actually guards.

Least privilege means each employee, contractor or system account gets only the access its job requires, and nothing more. Need-to-know applies the same logic to the information itself: a person sees a specific secret only when their work depends on it.

The idea has deep roots in computer security. Jerome Saltzer and Michael Schroeder listed least privilege among their design principles in a 1975 paper, "The Protection of Information in Computer Systems." Need-to-know comes from government handling of classified material. In that setting, a security clearance alone does not open every file.

Companies usually put these rules into practice through role-based access control, often shortened to RBAC. Administrators attach permissions to job roles, such as sales engineer or formulation chemist, rather than granting them person by person. A new hire inherits the rights of the assigned role. Researchers at the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology helped formalize the RBAC model in the 1990s.

Segmentation adds a second layer. A manufacturer might keep its process recipes in a repository separate from general engineering files. A software company might split its source code so that most developers can reach only the modules they maintain. An employee who copies everything within reach then leaves with a fragment rather than the whole.

Permissions tend to pile up over time. A worker who moves from research to marketing may keep the old research access unless someone removes it. Security teams call this privilege creep. Periodic access reviews, run on a fixed schedule or triggered by a role change, ask managers to confirm that each permission still matches the person's current duties.

Orphaned accounts create a related gap. Logins left behind by finished projects or reassigned staff give an insider a path that no one is watching.

The cost is friction. Tight controls can slow collaboration, and engineers locked out of a needed file may look for workarounds. Many firms answer this with a documented request process. Temporary access is granted, logged and set to expire on a fixed date.

A nondisclosure agreement signed at hiring defines what an employee must keep confidential. It also gives the company a contractual basis to sue if that information leaves.

Most employee confidentiality agreements follow a similar structure. They define confidential information, often by category, such as source code, manufacturing processes, pricing data and customer lists. They limit how the employee may use that information, usually to work performed for the employer. They also list exclusions, such as information that is already public or that the employee knew before joining.

Timing matters. Companies typically present the agreement before a new hire receives system credentials or sees sensitive material. A signature collected after months of access leaves a gap in the record for that period.

Duration is a separate drafting choice. Many agreements keep obligations for trade secrets in force for as long as the information stays secret. Less sensitive business information often carries a fixed term instead.

U.S. federal law adds a specific requirement. The Defend Trade Secrets Act of 2016 grants immunity to employees who disclose trade secrets in confidence to a federal, state or local government official, or to an attorney. That protection applies when the disclosure is made solely to report or investigate a suspected violation of law. It also covers disclosures filed under seal in a lawsuit.

Employers must give notice of that immunity in any agreement governing the use of trade secrets or confidential information. The rule applies to contracts entered into or updated after the law took effect in May 2016. The notice can appear in the agreement itself or through a cross-reference to a company policy document.

Leaving the notice out has a defined cost. The employer cannot recover exemplary damages or attorney fees from that employee in a later action under the federal statute. The company can still sue, but it gives up part of the available remedy.

An agreement has limits. A signature does not block a file transfer or a printout. Its value comes after a breach, when it supports a claim for an injunction or damages.

Noncompete and nonsolicitation clauses limit what a former employee can do after leaving, not just what they can disclose. Companies use them as a second layer behind confidentiality agreements. Whether a court will enforce them depends heavily on where the employee works.

A noncompete agreement bars a worker from joining a competitor or starting a rival business for a set period after departure. Its value for trade secret protection is indirect. If an engineer cannot work for a direct competitor for a year, a formula or product roadmap is less likely to travel with them. A nondisclosure agreement works differently. It lets the person work anywhere but forbids sharing protected information.

Enforceability is the central constraint. California voids most employee noncompete agreements under Business and Professions Code section 16600. Employers there cannot rely on them to keep departing staff away from rivals. A handful of other states also restrict them sharply.

Where noncompetes are allowed, courts commonly ask whether the restriction is reasonable. They look at its length, its geographic reach and the type of work it covers. A clause written too broadly risks being narrowed or thrown out entirely.

Rules also differ across borders. A multinational employer may face one standard in Texas, another in Germany and another in the U.K. Legal teams often draft jurisdiction-specific versions rather than relying on a single global template.

Nonsolicitation clauses are narrower. They typically bar a former employee from recruiting ex-colleagues or pursuing the company's clients for a defined period. They do not block someone from taking a new job. For that reason, courts in many jurisdictions treat them more favorably than noncompetes. California courts have limited many of these clauses as well.

Garden leave is another option, common in the U.K. financial sector. The departing employee stays on the payroll during a notice period but stops working and loses access to company systems. Their knowledge of current projects ages while they remain bound by their employment contract.

Federal trade secret law draws its own line. The Defend Trade Secrets Act bars courts from issuing an injunction that prevents a person from entering an employment relationship. Any conditions placed on new employment must rest on evidence of threatened misappropriation. What the person merely knows is not enough. The law also says such injunctions cannot conflict with state laws that prohibit restraints on a lawful profession, trade or business.

Data loss prevention software, usually called DLP, tracks where sensitive files go and can stop a transfer before the data leaves the company. It checks email, web uploads, USB drives and print jobs against rules that define what information must stay inside.

DLP tools generally operate in three places. Endpoint agents run on laptops and desktops. There they can see files copied to removable drives, pasted into other apps or sent to a printer. Network tools inspect traffic leaving the corporate network, including outgoing email and web uploads. Cloud tools connect to file-sharing and collaboration services, where much company data is stored.

The software decides what counts as sensitive by inspecting content. Simple rules match fixed patterns, such as the format of a credit card or Social Security number. Trade secrets rarely follow a fixed pattern. Companies therefore rely on other detection methods. Document fingerprinting lets a tool recognize a protected file, or a large excerpt from it, even after someone renames it. Exact data matching compares outgoing content against a registered set of records, such as a customer database.

Classification labels make the job easier. When a file carries a tag such as "confidential" or "restricted," a DLP policy can act on that tag instead of guessing from the text. Microsoft $MSFT Purview, for example, lets companies tie data loss prevention policies to sensitivity labels applied to documents and email.

Policies can respond in several ways. A tool may block a transfer outright. It may warn the user and ask for a business justification. It may encrypt the file or log the event for later review. Companies often start in monitoring mode to learn how data normally moves before they turn on blocking.

Some channels sit outside the software's view entirely. An employee can photograph a screen with a personal phone, copy details by hand or remember a process. DLP cannot stop what a person knows. Companies treat it as one layer that narrows the easiest exit routes, not as a guarantee that nothing leaves.

User and entity behavior analytics, or UEBA, spots insider risk by learning what normal activity looks like for each person and system. It then alerts security teams when behavior departs from that baseline, catching patterns that fixed rules can miss.

The approach starts with data. UEBA platforms take in logs from identity systems, file servers, cloud applications, VPNs and endpoint devices. Over time, they model typical behavior for each individual and for peers in the same role. An engineer who regularly opens design files has a different baseline from a sales manager who works mostly in a customer database.

Alerts come from deviations. One common trigger is a sudden spike in downloads. Others include access to repositories outside a person's usual projects and logins at odd hours or from new locations. Bulk activity shortly before a resignation is another pattern security teams watch for.

Many systems combine several weak signals into a single risk score. A late-night login on its own means little. A late-night login followed by a large export to a personal cloud account carries more weight.

Logging and audit trails sit underneath all of this. A record of who opened which file, when and from where serves two purposes. It feeds the analytics as events happen. It also becomes evidence if the company later needs to show what an insider took. Retention policies matter here. Logs deleted after a short window cannot support an investigation that begins months later.

The tools have limits. Baselines take time to form, so new hires and people changing roles can generate noise. An anomaly is not proof of wrongdoing. A flagged spike may reflect a legitimate deadline or a new assignment. Human analysts still need to review alerts before anyone acts on them.

Law shapes how far monitoring can go, and the rules differ by region. In the EU, the General Data Protection Regulation requires a lawful basis and transparency when employers process employee data. In Germany, works councils have co-determination rights over technical systems capable of monitoring staff. In the U.S., federal law sets fewer limits on workplace monitoring, but some states add their own. New York and Connecticut require employers to notify workers about electronic monitoring.

Companies using UEBA commonly set out their monitoring rules in a written policy that employees receive before the monitoring applies to them. Access to the behavioral data is usually limited to a small group of analysts. Those steps address the transparency duties in Europe and the notice laws in U.S. states such as New York and Connecticut.

Employee training turns a company's secrecy rules into daily habits. Staff who know what counts as confidential, how it is labeled and how to respond to suspicious requests are better placed to avoid leaking information by accident. They also cannot easily claim they never knew the rules.

Programs typically start at onboarding. New hires learn which categories of information the company treats as confidential, such as product formulas, manufacturing processes, unreleased designs, source code, pricing models and customer lists. They also learn where that material may be stored and who may see it.

Recurring sessions keep the message from fading after the first week. Companies often pair annual refreshers with shorter reminders tied to specific events. Examples include a product launch, a merger or a move to new collaboration software.

Much of the content is practical. Employees learn what a marking like "Confidential" or "Restricted" requires of them. That can mean not forwarding a document to a personal email account and not saving files to unapproved cloud services. It can also mean not discussing a project in public places such as airport lounges or trade show floors.

Training also covers approaches from outside the company. Social engineering attempts, including phishing emails and phone calls from people posing as colleagues or IT staff, aim to trick employees into handing over access or documents. Engineers and researchers may also hear from recruiters, consultants or expert network firms seeking details about current work. Training explains how to decline those requests and where to report them.

Another topic is what new employees bring with them. Companies commonly instruct hires not to bring files or confidential information from former employers. That instruction reduces the risk that the new employer gets drawn into another company's trade secret dispute.

Training matters in court as well as in the office. U.S. trade secret law protects information only when its owner takes reasonable measures to keep it secret. Records showing who attended which session, and when, can help a company show a court that it took those measures.

Training has limits. It cannot stop someone who has already decided to take information. It is aimed mainly at preventing careless mistakes and at making clear, in writing, what the rules required.

Companies extend trade secret protection beyond their own payroll in two ways. They bind contractors, vendors and partners to written confidentiality terms, and they share only the information each outside party needs. Third parties often hold access similar to what employees have. The controls have to follow the data outside the company.

Outside parties sit in a gap that internal policies can miss. A software contractor may work inside a company's code repository. A contract manufacturer may hold full production specifications. A marketing agency may receive customer lists or pricing plans. None of these people signed the employee handbook, and many work for firms that also serve competitors.

The legal stakes are direct. Handing confidential material to an outside firm with no confidentiality obligation can undercut a company's claim that it kept the information secret. A nondisclosure agreement, or NDA, signed before any disclosure gives the company evidence that it treated the information as confidential.

Vendor contracts often go further than a basic NDA. They can define what counts as confidential information and limit its use to a specific project. They can require the vendor to bind its own staff and subcontractors to the same terms. Many also require the return or destruction of materials when the work ends, with written certification.

Audit rights add a check on those promises. A contract can allow the company to inspect how the vendor stores and handles its data, or to request security documentation. Without such a clause, a company may have little visibility into what happens after data leaves its systems.

Technical controls mirror the contract terms. Contractor accounts can be set to expire on the contract's end date. That removes the need for someone to remember to close them. Access can also be confined to a single project folder, a separate environment or a virtual desktop that keeps files off the contractor's own machines.

Joint ventures and research partnerships raise a different problem. Each side wants to collaborate without handing over everything it knows. Partners often disclose information in stages, starting with nonconfidential summaries before sharing detailed specifications. Some set up clean teams — small groups walled off from the rest of the business — to review sensitive material. Clean teams are common in merger due diligence between competitors, where antitrust rules also limit what can be shared.

Records carry weight throughout the relationship. A log of what was disclosed, to whom and when lets a company show which secrets a partner actually received. That record can be central to a misappropriation claim if the relationship ends in a dispute.

The weeks around an employee's departure are a high-risk window for trade secret theft. Companies reduce that risk with a structured offboarding process. It covers recent account activity, an exit interview, returned devices, signed acknowledgments and same-day credential cutoff.

The review often starts before the person walks out. Once someone gives notice, security and legal teams may examine that employee's recent account activity. They look for large downloads, files sent to personal email addresses or cloud accounts, and access to projects outside the person's current role. A finding at this stage gives the company time to act before the employee starts a new job.

The exit interview serves a legal purpose as well as a human resources one. A manager or HR representative reminds the departing worker which categories of information remain confidential after the job ends. The employee is asked to confirm that company documents, code and customer lists have been returned or deleted from personal devices. Many companies ask for that confirmation in writing. A signed certification restates obligations the employee already accepted. It also creates a record if a dispute follows.

Physical and digital property comes back at the same time. Laptops, phones, security badges, hardware tokens and external drives are collected and logged. Returned devices are often preserved rather than wiped right away. That way, their contents can still be examined if questions arise later.

Access revocation belongs on the last day of employment. In tense departures, it can happen at the moment the person is notified. The cutoff covers network logins, email, VPN connections, cloud applications, shared drives and building entry. Accounts in third-party tools, such as code repositories or customer relationship management systems, are easy to miss. A single forgotten account can leave a door open after the person is gone.

The case of Anthony Levandowski shows how much scrutiny one departure can draw. Levandowski was a Google $GOOGL engineer who left to found a self-driving truck startup that Uber $UBER acquired. Waymo, Alphabet's self-driving car company, then sued Uber over trade secrets tied to his departure. The companies settled in February 2018. Levandowski later pleaded guilty to one count of trade secret theft. He received a presidential pardon in January 2021.

An insider threat program gives a company one team and one plan for spotting, investigating and answering suspected trade secret theft. Pairing it with a prepared legal response lets the company act quickly once evidence points to a specific person.

These programs commonly bring together three groups. Security staff understand the systems and the logs. Lawyers know what evidence a court will accept and what employment law allows. Human resources sees performance disputes, resignations and complaints that may give context to a technical alert. When these groups work separately, warning signs can sit in three different places without anyone connecting them.

The CERT National Insider Threat Center at Carnegie Mellon University's Software Engineering Institute publishes guidance that companies can use as a reference when building such a program. Its material treats insider risk as an organizational problem, not only a technical one. It addresses governance, policy and coordination between departments.

The response plan covers what happens after a credible alert. The first step is usually evidence preservation. That means securing laptops, phones, email accounts and access logs before anything is wiped or overwritten. Forensic examiners can then reconstruct which files were opened, copied or sent, and when. A documented chain of custody helps that evidence hold up later.

The legal options depend on what the review finds. The Defend Trade Secrets Act of 2016 lets a trade secret owner sue in federal civil court. In extraordinary circumstances, a court can order an ex parte seizure of property to keep a secret from spreading. Ex parte means the order is issued without advance notice to the other side. The law also allows courts to grant injunctions and award damages.

Some cases move beyond civil court. The Economic Espionage Act of 1996 makes trade secret theft a federal crime. Companies can refer matters to federal prosecutors. A referral puts the criminal case in the government's hands. Legal teams weigh that step against the company's own goals, such as speed or confidentiality.

Teams test the plan through tabletop exercises, which walk members through a simulated incident step by step. They also keep contact lists current and revise the plan when systems or laws change. Those exercises show whether the team can secure devices and logs fast enough to support a request for emergency court relief.
