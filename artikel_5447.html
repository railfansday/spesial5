How Senate confirmations work, from nomination to final vote

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Free daily briefing on global business news.

The Senate confirmation process runs from nomination to final vote, and these 10 rules and stages explain how presidential nominees win or lose approval

A three-word phrase in the Constitution — "advice and consent" — gives the Senate a say over who runs federal departments and who sits on federal courts. It also covers who represents the U.S. abroad. The President picks the nominee. The Senate decides whether that pick takes office. That division of power gives two branches of government a role in every appointment that requires confirmation.

The Senate confirmation process is not a single vote. It is a chain of formal rules, committee customs and floor procedures. Each link can move a nominee forward or hold one back. Some steps are written into the Constitution. Others exist only in the standing rules of the Senate. A few are traditions that a committee chair can honor or set aside.

Those rules have not stayed fixed. The threshold for ending debate on nominees has dropped from a supermajority to a simple majority. The time allowed for debate after that vote has been cut for many lower-level posts. The 2013 and 2017 changes were each led by the Senate majority leader of the day.

This guide follows the path a nominee takes. It starts with the constitutional text and the kinds of jobs that need Senate approval. It then covers the questionnaires, financial disclosures and background checks that come before any hearing. Next come the committee hearing and committee vote. After that is the blue slip custom, which gives home-state senators a voice on some judicial and law enforcement picks.

From there the guide moves to the floor. It explains holds, cloture votes and the final tally, including what happens when the Senate splits evenly. The ninth section looks at nominations that fail, stall or get sent back to the White House. The 10th covers recess appointments and the bulk approval of military promotions. Both work outside the usual one-by-one vote.

Each section stands on its own. A reader who wants to know what a hold means, or why a vice president might cast the deciding vote, can go straight to that section. Together the sections show how a presidential choice becomes a confirmed official — and every point where that path can end.

Article II, Section 2 of the U.S. Constitution divides the power to fill top federal posts between two branches. The President nominates, and the Senate gives its advice and consent before an appointment is made.

The clause names specific categories of officials. It lists ambassadors, other public ministers and consuls, and justices of the Supreme Court. It then adds a broader category: all other officers of the U.S. whose appointments the Constitution does not otherwise provide for.

The order of the verbs matters. The President acts first by choosing a person and sending that name forward. The appointment comes only after the Senate has consented. A nomination on its own does not place anyone in office.

The phrase "advice and consent" is the constitutional footing for the Senate's role. Committee hearings, floor debate and confirmation votes on presidential nominees all rest on these words.

The same clause also limits how far Senate review reaches. It lets Congress decide that certain positions, which the text calls inferior officers, can be filled without the Senate's consent.

For those posts, Congress may vest the appointment power in one of three places. It can give the power to the President alone. It can give it to the courts. It can also give it to the heads of departments.

The decision rests with Congress rather than the President. Lawmakers determine which inferior offices skip Senate review and which of those three authorities makes the selection.

The result is a two-track system built into the same clause. Ambassadors, public ministers, consuls, Supreme Court justices and other officers not covered elsewhere go through both the President and the Senate. Inferior officers can go through a single appointing authority if Congress chooses that route.

Beyond those four named categories, the text does not list individual job titles. Which posts require the Senate's consent depends on the general category of officers and on the inferior-officer choices Congress makes.

Cabinet secretaries, ambassadors, federal judges, U.S. attorneys, U.S. marshals and senior military officers must all win Senate approval before taking office. These posts sit on the confirmed side of a line the Constitution draws between officers who need the Senate's consent and lesser officers who may not.

The roster spans the executive and judicial branches. Cabinet secretaries run the executive departments. Ambassadors represent the U.S. to foreign governments. Senior military officers lead the armed forces. Federal judges sit at three levels: district courts, circuit courts and the Supreme Court. Judges at all three levels need the Senate's consent.

U.S. attorneys and U.S. marshals belong on the list as well. Both posts are tied to individual federal judicial districts. A U.S. attorney leads federal prosecutions in a district. A U.S. marshal handles law enforcement work for the federal courts in a district. The Senate must sign off on both, just as it does for a Cabinet secretary or a judge.

Senate confirmation does not cover every federal job. The Constitution gives Congress a choice when it comes to inferior officers. Congress must make that choice by law. Lawmakers can place the power to appoint them with the President alone. They can also give it to the federal courts or to the heads of departments. When Congress takes one of those routes, the appointment does not go to the Senate.

Without a law vesting an inferior officer's appointment elsewhere, the default under the Constitution is nomination by the President with the Senate's advice and consent.

The result is a two-tier system. A nominee for a senior post, such as a Cabinet secretary or a federal judge, faces the Senate's full process before taking the job. That process runs from vetting through a committee review to a floor vote. An inferior officer whose appointment Congress has assigned to the President alone, a court or a department head does not.

A presidential nomination becomes official when the White House sends a formal nomination to the Senate. The Senate then refers it to the committee with jurisdiction over the post, where questionnaires, financial disclosure and background checks begin.

The formal nomination turns a president's choice into business the Senate can act on. Until that document arrives, senators have no pending nomination to consider. Once it does, the nomination does not go straight to the full chamber. It goes to a committee first.

Referral depends on the type of job. The Senate Judiciary Committee handles nominations for federal judges. The Senate Foreign Relations Committee handles ambassadors. The Senate Armed Services Committee handles military officers. The position, not the person nominated, decides which committee reviews the pick.

Vetting begins with paperwork. Nominees complete questionnaires for the committee reviewing them. Those documents put a written record of the nominee in front of senators before any questioning takes place.

Executive-branch nominees face an added financial step. People picked for posts in federal departments and agencies file financial disclosure. That disclosure is reviewed through the Office of Government Ethics. A nominee's finances are therefore on file before the Senate weighs the nomination.

Judicial nominees undergo an FBI background check on top of their questionnaires. That check covers people nominated to the federal bench, where judges are appointed to the courts rather than to an executive agency.

These requirements produce different paths for different posts. An ambassador nominee goes to Foreign Relations and, as an executive-branch pick, files financial disclosure reviewed through the Office of Government Ethics. A judicial nominee goes to Judiciary and undergoes an FBI background check. A nominee for a senior military post goes to Armed Services.

All of this happens before the committee holds a hearing or takes a vote. The questionnaires, disclosure filings and background checks form the record that committee members review once the nomination moves to that next stage.

A Senate committee reviews a nominee in two stages: it holds a hearing, and then it votes on how to report the nomination. That vote shapes what comes next, but it does not settle the outcome.

The hearing comes first. The nominee appears before the committee with jurisdiction over the post. Senators on that panel question the nominee directly before any vote takes place.

The two stages do different work. The hearing lets panel members put their questions to the nominee in person. The vote then asks those same members to record a position on the nomination.

The committee votes after the hearing ends. The question on the table is not whether the nominee gets the job. It is how the nomination will be reported to the rest of the chamber.

A committee has three options. It can report a nomination favorably. It can report it unfavorably. It can also report it without recommendation. Whichever option the panel picks, the nomination leaves the committee and heads to the floor.

Each option tells senators outside the panel something different. A favorable report signals that the committee backs the nominee. An unfavorable report signals that the committee opposes the pick. A report without recommendation moves the nomination along while the committee takes no position.

Only committee members take part in that vote. Its result reflects the judgment of one panel, not the view of the Senate as a whole. The floor is where the full chamber first weighs in.

None of these reports binds the chamber. A favorable report does not guarantee confirmation. An unfavorable one does not end a nomination. On the floor, any senator can vote, whether or not he or she sits on the committee.

The committee stage informs the decision rather than making it. The full Senate decides whether the nominee takes office.

The blue slip is a Senate Judiciary Committee custom that lets the two senators from a nominee's home state weigh in before the committee acts. It is a committee practice, not a rule of the full Senate.

The tradition works through consultation. When a nomination tied to a particular state reaches the Judiciary Committee, the senators who represent that state are asked for their view. Their answer signals whether they are willing to see the nomination move forward in committee.

The practice reaches beyond the courts. Under Chairman Chuck Grassley, the committee honored blue slips for district judges and for U.S. attorneys. For those posts, the views of home-state senators carried real weight in whether a nominee advanced. A senator who declined to return a favorable slip could keep that nomination from moving through the committee.

Circuit judges were treated differently. Starting in 2017, the committee did not apply the blue slip to circuit court nominees. A home-state senator who withheld support could not use the custom to hold up a circuit nomination in committee.

The split shows how a custom differs from a rule. The full Senate does not vote on the blue slip, and its standing rules do not require it. The committee chair decides how much weight the slips carry and for which kinds of nominees. That is why district and circuit nominees could be handled differently by the same committee.

A rule of the full Senate applies the same way no matter who leads a committee. A committee custom carries no such guarantee. A later chair can draw the lines in a different place, widening or narrowing the set of posts where home-state objections count.

The blue slip operates only inside the committee stage. It can shape whether a nomination advances in Judiciary. It does not replace the committee hearing, the committee vote or the final decision on the Senate floor.

A Senate hold is an informal notice from a senator that he or she intends to object if leaders try to move a nomination by unanimous consent. A hold can slow a nominee down, but it does not block a nomination outright.

The hold works through unanimous consent, the shortcut the Senate uses to skip formal procedural steps. Under unanimous consent, a matter moves forward only if no senator objects. A single objection is enough to stop the shortcut.

That power belongs to every member of the chamber. The objecting senator does not need to sit on the committee that reviewed the nominee. Any senator can refuse consent on the floor, regardless of party or seniority.

A hold announces that objection in advance. The senator does not have to wait for the nomination to reach the floor. Leaders learn ahead of time that consent will be refused. They can then decide whether to take the formal route instead.

The hold is not a vote, and it does not formally reject anyone. It is a statement of intent. Its force comes entirely from the right every senator already has to deny unanimous consent.

The majority still has a formal route around an objection. It can file for cloture, the procedure that brings debate on a nomination to a close. Once the majority takes that route, the nomination can advance without the objecting senator's agreement.

The practical effect of a hold is to force the slower path. A nomination that could have cleared the chamber by consent must go through formal procedure. That procedure includes a separate cloture vote before the final vote on confirmation. Every extra step uses floor time that unanimous consent would have saved. The decision shifts from a single senator's objection back to the majority.

A hold gives one senator influence over timing, not a veto over the nominee.

Cloture is the Senate procedure that ends debate on a nomination. For every type of nominee, it now takes a simple majority rather than 60 votes. Senate Rule XXII governs the process. Rule changes in 2013 and 2017 lowered the threshold, and a 2019 change shortened how long debate can run once cloture passes.

The Senate does not cut off discussion of a nominee by default. Until debate on a nomination formally ends, the chamber cannot move to a confirmation vote. A successful cloture vote closes that discussion and puts the nominee on a path to the final tally.

Before 2013, ending debate on a nomination required 60 votes. Under that threshold, a nominee with the backing of a majority could still stall if supporters could not reach 60. The minority party therefore held real leverage over which nominees reached a vote at all.

In 2013, Majority Leader Harry Reid led a change that set a simple-majority threshold for cloture on executive-branch nominations and lower-court judges. Supreme Court nominees were left out of that change. They still faced the 60-vote bar.

That exception did not last. In 2017, Majority Leader Mitch McConnell extended the simple-majority threshold to Supreme Court nominees. The Senate then confirmed Neil Gorsuch to the Supreme Court on April 7, 2017, by a vote of 54-45.

Passing cloture does not trigger an immediate confirmation vote. Senate rules allow a period of post-cloture debate first. In 2019, the Senate cut that window to two hours for most sub-Cabinet executive nominees and district judges.

Higher-level posts kept a longer runway. Up to 30 hours of post-cloture debate remains available for Cabinet-level nominees, circuit judges, Supreme Court justices and certain other positions. The gap shapes the floor schedule. The two-hour limit lets the chamber process lower-level nominees faster than the 30-hour maximum allowed for top posts.

A nominee is confirmed when a simple majority of the senators voting approves the nomination. If those senators split evenly, the vice president can break the tie. That power comes from Article I, Section 3 of the Constitution.

The phrase "senators voting" carries weight. The threshold is measured against the votes actually cast on the floor, not against the full membership of the chamber. A senator who is absent or does not vote is left out of the count. As a result, the number of yes votes needed to win can change from one roll call to the next.

A tie means neither side has a majority, and the nomination has not been approved. Article I, Section 3 gives the vice president a way to resolve that deadlock. The vice president's vote is then added to the final tally. That is why a tiebreak confirmation shows up as a one-vote margin.

Vice President Mike Pence used that power on Feb. 7, 2017. He broke a tie to confirm Betsy DeVos as Education Secretary. The recorded count was 51-50. Senators had divided 50-50 on the nomination, and Pence cast the deciding yes vote.

Two recent Supreme Court confirmations show the simple-majority standard working without a tiebreaker. Amy Coney Barrett was confirmed on Oct. 26, 2020, by a vote of 52-48. Ketanji Brown Jackson was confirmed on April 7, 2022, by a vote of 53-47.

In each case, more senators voted yes than voted no. That is all the rule requires. Neither count was tied. The vice president had no role in either outcome. Barrett won by four votes and Jackson by six.

The final vote is the Senate's actual decision on a nomination. Committee action, holds and cloture all come earlier in the process. None of them settles whether a nominee is confirmed. Only the floor vote does that.

A nomination can fail in three ways. The Senate can vote it down, decline to act on it, or let it go back to the President under the chamber's own rules. Each path ends a nomination without a confirmation, and each works through a different mechanism.

An outright rejection happens on the Senate floor. The full chamber takes a recorded vote, and more senators vote no than yes. Robert Bork's Supreme Court nomination ended this way in 1987, when the Senate voted 58-42 against him. The roll call put every senator's position on the record.

Cabinet nominees can meet the same end. In 1989, the Senate rejected John Tower as Defense Secretary by a vote of 53-47. A floor defeat is not limited to judicial nominees. A nominee to lead an executive department faces the same final test as a nominee to the high court.

A stalled nomination fails without any vote at all. Merrick Garland was nominated to the Supreme Court in March 2016. The Senate never held a hearing on Garland, and it never held a vote. His nomination produced no committee record and no floor tally. The Senate's role in appointments includes the option not to act.

The third path is procedural. Senate Rule XXXI governs nominations that are still pending when the chamber stops meeting for an extended stretch. If the Senate adjourns or recesses for more than 30 days, those nominations go back to the President. The Senate can prevent that by agreeing, through unanimous consent, to keep them in place.

A returned nomination is no longer before the Senate. No further committee or floor action can take place on it.

The unanimous consent requirement gives each senator leverage at this stage. One objection is enough to stop the Senate from holding pending nominations through a long break. That leaves the fate of those nominations outside the majority's sole control.

These outcomes separate a nomination that is defeated from one that runs out of time. A defeat requires more no votes than yes votes on the floor. A stall or a return requires only that the process stop before any final vote.

Two routes let a nominee take office without a separate, individual Senate roll call. Recess appointments, authorized by Article II, Section 2, Clause 3 of the Constitution, let the President fill vacancies while the Senate is away. Military promotions, in most cases, clear the Senate in bulk by unanimous consent.

The recess appointment power works differently from the standard nomination path. Under the ordinary process, a nominee moves through committee and then the floor before taking office. A recess appointment skips those steps while the Senate is not in session. The President acts alone, and the appointee can begin serving without a confirmation vote.

The central legal question is what counts as a recess. The Supreme Court addressed it in NLRB v. Noel Canning, decided in 2014. The justices held that a Senate recess lasting fewer than 10 days is presumptively too short for the recess appointment power. Under that standard, a break of a few days generally does not qualify.

The ruling put the Senate's calendar at the center of the issue. If the chamber never stays away long enough, the President has no qualifying recess to use. The Senate has used pro forma sessions to avoid long recesses. These are brief sessions held for form's sake during a break. They divide what would otherwise be one long absence into shorter stretches.

Officer promotions follow their own track on the Senate floor. Rather than hold a separate vote for each officer, the Senate groups promotions together. A single unanimous consent request can then clear the whole list.

That shortcut depends on every senator going along. The bulk method covers most military promotions, not every one. A nomination left out of the routine package has to move through the Senate's regular procedures instead.

The two routes rest on different foundations. Recess appointments turn on the calendar and the 10-day line drawn in Noel Canning. Bulk military approvals turn on routine agreement among senators.
