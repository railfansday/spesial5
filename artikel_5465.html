Early data points to Vulcan’s first stage in launch failure that cost Amazon 40 Leo satellites

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

by Todd Bishop on Nov 17, 2026 at 8:52 amNovember 18, 2026 at 10:12 am

Update, Wednesday Nov. 18: ULA said Wednesday that preliminary findings indicate the first stage’s low thrust came from an inadequate fuel supply to its BE-4 engines, not the engines’ own performance. Read our latest story.

Early data suggests the first stage of United Launch Alliance’s Vulcan rocket underperformed just after liftoff Sunday, leaving it unable to put 40 of Amazon’s Leo internet satellites into orbit, ULA said Tuesday.

In its first detailed account of the failure, ULA cited “low booster performance,” using its term for Vulcan’s core first stage. As a result, the stage separated when the rocket was too low and moving too slowly to reach orbit.

That stage is powered by two BE-4 engines from Jeff Bezos’ Blue Origin. However, ULA didn’t say what caused the shortfall or whether the engines played a role. ULA said it’s very early in the process and that it wants to avoid speculation until the investigation is complete.

The Centaur upper stage appears to have started normally, and its two L3Harris RL10 engines operated as planned but couldn’t make up for the shortfall, ULA said. The six solid rocket boosters made by Northrop Grumman, the source of problems on two earlier Vulcan flights, appear to have performed normally.

The Federal Aviation Administration will oversee the ULA-led investigation and approve its final report, including any fixes.

What changed since Sunday: ULA’s account changes the picture from Sunday, when the failure appeared to come about 10 minutes into the flight, during the Centaur’s burn.

ULA said Tuesday that it appears to have lost telemetry and the mission at about that point, but that the trouble began just after liftoff. Spaceflight Now reported Sunday that the first stage appeared to burn longer than planned before separating.

Blue Origin CEO Dave Limp, asked on X why the Kent, Wash.-based company hadn’t commented, replied Tuesday morning.

“It is ULA’s mission so they should provide the updates,” Limp wrote. “We are supporting them with data and analysis from the BE-4s. Team Blue continues to ready the pad and New Glenn for our return to flight.”

Impact on Amazon Leo: Sunday’s launch was the first of 38 Vulcan missions Amazon bought for its planned rival to SpaceX’s Starlink. Vulcan is slated to carry more than 1,500 of Amazon’s first-generation satellites, more than any other rocket in the program.

Amazon also has 24 launches reserved on New Glenn, which uses seven BE-4s on its first stage. New Glenn has been grounded since an oxygen valve problem caused one to explode during a ground test in May. Blue Origin retrofitted the BE-4s on Sunday’s Vulcan with a fix for that problem, ULA CEO Mark Peller said before the flight.

Before Sunday, Amazon had launched 396 satellites for its Redmond-based Leo program on 14 successful missions since April 2025, most recently on an Atlas 5 in July. The Federal Communications Commission let Amazon keep deploying satellites after it fell short of a July 30 deadline to have 1,616 in service. Amazon still has until July 30, 2029, to deploy all 3,232 of its first-generation satellites.

Amazon has said it plans to start commercial internet service this year through Leo, formerly known as Project Kuiper. The next scheduled Leo launch is an Ariane 6 mission carrying 36 satellites, targeted for no earlier than Nov. 23 from French Guiana.
