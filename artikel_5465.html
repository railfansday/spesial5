Amazon captured records of AI agents trying to steal AI provider keys

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

Free daily briefing on global business news.

The agents' own records describe stolen keys paying for the AI models that searched for more keys; Amazon cannot verify outcomes

Amazon $AMZN captured records from AI agents describing a campaign to steal AI provider keys, with the stolen keys covering the cost of the models behind the hunt for additional keys, the company said on Friday.

Amazon's MadPot network of decoy sensors collected the records from Oct. 3 to Nov. 5. The sensors were set up to look like widely used large language model inference APIs, and the agents transmitted their own campaign histories to them. Amazon said it did not watch the activity unfold and has no way to confirm the results the agents claimed.

Amazon assessed with moderate confidence that the agents ran on ARTEX, an open-source AI penetration testing framework, and that the operators were separate China-nexus groups. It cited Simplified Chinese prompts, China time-zone settings and targeting consistent with Chinese interests.

In the records, agents scanned for AI applications reachable from the open internet, pulled out the upstream provider keys inside, checked that they worked and kept them in a managed pool. They concentrated on applications that keep credentials from numerous providers in one place. Those included LLM gateways such as One API, New API, Veloera, Sub2API and LiteLLM, as well as tools for building apps, among them Dify, FastGPT, Langflow, RAGFlow, Open WebUI, LobeChat and Ollama.

A playbook in the records matched each product to known entry points. These included factory-set admin logins such as the LiteLLM default master key, CVE-2025-3248 in Langflow, CVE-2026-28797 in RAGFlow and a way around authentication in One API family systems by sending the isCliRequest header. To find keys, the agents drew on GitHub repositories, npm and PyPI packages, container image layers, paste sites and Telegram channels.

A second campaign used deepfakes in an attempt to get past know-your-customer checks at a pair of cryptocurrency exchanges. One verification vendor's server refused the deepfake each time the agents logged an attempt.

Amazon said it notified AWS customers it assessed as potentially affected. It could not confirm activity against other organizations and published indicators of compromise so others can check their environments.

"None of the techniques in these campaigns were new. What AI agents change is the speed and persistence of the attacker," the company said. Amazon recommended applying standard production security controls to AI applications, gateways for models and endpoints that serve inference, and keeping an eye out for surprise charges or odd usage patterns.

The FBI issued a FLASH alert dated Nov. 13 saying ARTEX has been employed by assorted threat groups dating back to at least August. The agency said the developer removed the GitHub repository in October, but the tool is still obtainable through forks made by the community, archived copies and a commercial variant named ARTEX PRO.

Cloudflare tracked more than 50 framework families attempting to scan its customers in the three days before its Nov. 13 post. Cisco $CSCO Talos analyzed ARTEX activity and said a developer using the pseudonym Autumn-27 published the framework on GitHub in July. Talos said it is used largely by financially motivated cybercriminals and China-nexus actors.
