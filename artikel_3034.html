Also, the mobility startup that spun out of Rivian last year, has run into another speed bump. The company was already struggling to get its first e-bikes to customers due to a litany of supply chain headaches and the complexity of building a futuristic bike powered almost entirely by software. Now there is a new wrinkle, the company said in an email sent this week to customers, informing them that their wait just got a little longer.

Also said it identified “a few software-based issues, such as charging behavior,” affecting some of the Launch Edition versions of its TM-B bikes. As a result, it was pausing customer deliveries until it could work out a solution.

“Our team is hard at work on a firmware update to address these issues so that every TM-B owner can enjoy the full product experience we aspire to, beginning with your very first ride,” the email reads.

The issues were already popping up on the Also subreddit, where customers were reporting batteries draining overnight for no apparent reason, in some cases dropping from 70 percent to 40 percent or lower over a couple of days. Others noted that the parasitic drain would trigger a “bike issue detected” or “install battery to ride” error on the bike’s display. Trying to plug the battery in would often fail because the bike wouldn’t recognize the battery installed, they said.

Also has paused customer deliveries until it can fix the problem. The company said it had almost shipped out all of its Launch Edition bikes, after which it planned on transitioning to its Performance Edition. But now that timeline will be delayed beyond what Also previously communicated to its customers who are still waiting on their deliveries, Michele Mehl, senior PR manager at Also, says in an email to The Verge.

“The team recognizes this means a delay for customers who have already waited longer than expected, but believes it’s the right decision to ensure every TM-B delivers the full product experience we aspire to,” Mehl added. The company plans on sending another update on the delay on November 24th.

It’s been clear from the beginning that Also took on an enormous challenge when it set out to build one of the most high-tech, logistically complex e-bikes ever made. The USB-C chargeable batteries, which are available in either 538Wh or 808Wh packs, are supposed to offer up 100 miles of range. But the 240W power delivery exceeds current regulatory guidelines, forcing Also to work with standards groups to establish new safety and testing protocols.

In addition, the bike is powered by a pedal-by-wire system, which means there’s no mechanical connection between the pedaling and the movement of the bike. Instead, the rider pedals a generator motor on the crank, which replenishes the battery; a separate traction motor at the rear wheel is software-driven via a Gates Carbon belt drive.

Last month, when I asked Also President Chris Yu whether the company bit off more than it can chew with this bike, he pushed back:

I don’t look at it that way at all. If we were to revisit the design, I would do the exact same thing. The largest untapped opportunity isn’t cyclists who already tolerate the current bike experience. It’s the massive demographic of people intrigued by leaving the car behind who haven’t opted in yet. Their hesitations are consistent: fear of theft, avoiding maintenance every two months, wanting seamless navigation and audio, and needing to carry a child without having to buy three different bikes.

Solving those pain points requires a baseline of technology. Just like in automotive EVs, once you use software to personalize the ride and eliminate friction, people opt in. The majority of people attending our test rides haven’t ridden a bicycle in 10 or 15 years. Our goal wasn’t to go overboard; it was determining the minimum amount of innovation needed to create an experience as frictionless as stepping into a Tesla Model Y or Rivian R2.
