How to Prepare for RMDs Starting at 75 — Even If You're Only 60 Right Now

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

RMDs may be years away, but the smartest moves to shrink them start now.

By Keith Speights – Nov 26, 2026 at 4:44AM EST

Fifteen years might seem like a long way in the future. However, time goes by quickly.

Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs) may feel like a problem to deal with later if you're 60 years old. After all, for anyone born in 1960 or later, the tax obligation doesn't start until age 75. But that instinct is precisely what makes RMDs the most expensive retirement-planning mistake people make. The decisions available at 60 are worth far more than any RMD strategy available at 74 -- and by the time most people start thinking about this, the best options have already closed.

Here are four specific actions a 60-year-old can take right now to meaningfully reduce the RMD burden at 75.

The first step is understanding what your RMD will actually be. The IRS calculates RMDs by dividing the prior year-end account balance by a life expectancy factor from the Uniform Lifetime Table -- approximately 24.6 for a 75-year-old. A 60-year-old with $500,000 in a traditional IRA today, growing at 7% annually, will have around $1.29 million by the end of the year they turn 74, implying a first-year RMD of roughly $52,400. Running that projection now creates the awareness that motivates everything else on this list.

2. Begin Roth conversions in the low-tax window

The years between 60 and 74 -- particularly before Social Security begins and before RMDs start -- are typically the lowest-tax period of a retiree's financial life. Converting traditional IRA funds to Roth IRAs during this window pays today's tax rate on the converted amount in exchange for permanently removing that balance from future RMD calculations. Even modest annual conversions of $20,000 to $50,000 over 15 years can meaningfully shrink the RMD burden at 75. Importantly, Roth IRAs have no RMDs at all during the original owner's lifetime.

3. Coordinate conversions with claiming Social Security

The interaction between Roth conversions and Social Security taxation is the most underappreciated element of pre-RMD planning. Converting aggressively before Social Security begins keeps provisional income below the thresholds that trigger taxation on up to 85% of benefits. This can save thousands of dollars annually throughout retirement. The window for this strategy is finite and closes the moment both Social Security and RMDs begin arriving simultaneously.

4. Consider a qualified longevity annuity contract

The SECURE 2.0 Act allows retirees to use up to $210,000 (in 2026) of IRA funds to purchase a qualified longevity annuity contract (QLAC) -- a deferred income annuity that begins payments at a future date, typically 80 or 85. The limit is adjusted for inflation over time. Funds used for a QLAC are excluded from RMD calculations until the annuity begins paying, effectively reducing the RMD base and deferring both the income and the tax.

The most expensive RMD is the one that arrives as a surprise at 75 from an IRA that was never touched between 60 and 74. The least expensive one is the result of 15 years of quiet, deliberate preparation -- Roth conversions in low-tax years, careful Social Security coordination, and a QLAC that removes a portion of the IRA balance from the RMD calculation entirely.

The problem doesn't feel urgent at 60. That's exactly why starting now produces such dramatically better outcomes than waiting until it does.
