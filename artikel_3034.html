Apple, the F1 broadcast provider in the US, is supplementing coverage with its own iPhone cameras.

When you watch a Formula 1 race on TV, the screen is packed with lap times, driver stats and real-time video from inside the cars and around the track. During the Las Vegas Grand Prix race, some of those views were captured with iPhone Pro phones and transmitted in real time over T-Mobile’s 5G network.

Using iPhone cameras for big events is no longer a novelty. Apple has been shooting its event videos and ad spots with iPhone Pro models outfitted with cinema-grade lenses, SSD storage and broadcasting gear for several years now. In May, Apple broadcast a Major League Soccer game shot with iPhones strapped to professional lenses, following it up with a Major League Baseball game in September using its own camera hardware,

But compared to a ballpark, F1 ratchets up the complexity, especially in Las Vegas. At the Las Vegas Grand Prix, the race track snakes 3.9 miles through the city’s downtown core. Its route includes the famed Strip (South Las Vegas Boulevard), where most of the big hotels are.

“We control everything on baseball, so we can do whatever we want there,” said Royce Dickerson, executive producer of live sports at Apple TV. “We did 14 iPhones on top of our normal camera complement” during the baseball broadcast. He noted that 10 of those were connected via T-Mobile’s 5G wireless network.

But integrating iPhone cameras into an F1 broadcast, even for the sport’s US broadcaster, is more of a challenge. “I’ve been doing TV production for 20-plus years,” said Dickerson, “and the scale of their operation is unlike anything I’ve seen before.”

Because F1 has agreements with other camera partners, Dickerson did not specify how many iPhones were set up around the track. Neither Apple nor T-Mobile explained where the iPhones were placed in order to capture the speedy F1 vehicles racing around the Vegas Strip. I suspect they were likely deployed in more conventional environments for smartphone cameras, like the team garages and roving to catch celebrities in the paddock area and in the stands, though the iPhone setups wirelessly broadcasting footage at earlier sporting events means they could be easily placed in many locations on and above the streets.

“You really have to work hard to earn the fans’ attention [and] that viewing experience,” said Dickerson. “How are you getting your fans closer to their favorite athlete, to their favorite driver, to their team, to the personalities that surround the sport?”

In the T-Mobile zone at the Sphere – an area sponsored by the carrier and open to T-Mobile customers – at least 10 iPhone cameras were used to cover a stage where concerts played between events. That number is based on a diagram shared during a tour of T-Mobile’s Tech Trailer, where the company’s event coordinators stay on top of things like crowd movements and line lengths.

Dean Locke, executive director of broadcast, media and race operations at Formula 1, sees the combination of multiple cameras streaming wirelessly over 5G as an essential component to the Las Vegas race.

“If we just did cameras at a low level, the fences and track, we haven’t done a very good job,” said Locke. “How can we open up stages, concerts and all of the activities? Each race has a real kind of personality to it. You can only [capture that personality] with huge amounts of cameras, huge amounts of technology.”

If Apple is using iPhones to record live sports, are they recording any of that footage using spatial video for viewing in the Vision Pro? Imagine getting a track-level view of a group of Formula 1 cars speeding past at 200 miles per hour with Apple’s depth effect. “[There’s] nothing at this time I can say about it,” said Dickerson.
