Protecting cities from floods can come with a hidden cost for renters

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Flood protection projects in Copenhagen may raise rents and water bills unevenly, placing a heavier burden on lower-income tenants.

A city can become better protected against flooding while becoming more expensive for its poorest residents.

New research shows how drainage projects can leave tenants paying disproportionately through higher rents and water bills.

The study, led by researchers at the University of Copenhagen, examined how flood protection costs reach households across the Danish capital.

Lead author Natalie Marie Gulsrud and her colleagues combined geographical analysis with a detailed look at the Hørgården housing development.

Their findings show why a policy applied equally on paper can feel very different depending on where someone lives.

At Hørgården, roughly 60 percent of residents have incomes below the Danish average. The development also appears on the Ministry of Social Affairs and Housing’s Prevention Areas list.

Its aging sewers need repairs, and a planned cloudburst route brings another obligation. Rainwater draining from roofs must be separated from wastewater and connected to the new system.

The paper projects rent increases of 17%–29% for the combined work. For a 75-square-meter apartment, that would add 967–1,292 Danish kroner to the monthly bill.

Stormwater separation accounts for about 22% of that increase, or 213–284 kroner each month. Most of the increase comes from sewer repairs.

The loans will be repaid over 30 years. Residents moving into Hørgården years after the work is finished will therefore help pay for it.

Copenhagen is investing in cloudburst tunnels and drainage improvements to keep heavy rain from overwhelming its sewers. Separating stormwater from wastewater is part of that effort.

The work protects the city, but apartment residents and not-for-profit tenants can bear a disproportionate share of its financing.

“Climate adaptation is not only about flooding and sewer capacity. It is also about rising rents,” Gulsrud said.

“Your type of housing determines how much of the bill you pay. Our case study shows that one-size-fits-all climate adaptation policies worsen urban injustice.”

“Stormwater costs fall disproportionately on not-for-profit housing residents, despite Copenhagen’s reputation for climate leadership.”

The researchers compared adaptation plans with data on housing types and tenure. They found that not-for-profit housing often lies closer to priority areas for flood management.

That proximity matters because properties along a cloudburst route may have to change their drainage systems at their own expense.

“We can see that not-for-profit housing is often located where cloudburst routes are typically established,” Gulsrud explained.

“The problem arises when properties along these routes are required to separate stormwater from wastewater on their own land to connect it to the cloudburst system. This makes sense from a water-management perspective, but it can also impose substantial costs.”

For an older development such as Hørgården, connecting to the new infrastructure involves extensive work. Those expenses ultimately reach households through their rents.

Water bills introduce a separate problem. Stormwater charges are based on drinking water consumption in each dwelling, rather than the rainwater leaving the property.

Imagine a detached house beside a four-storey block containing four apartments. Both buildings have a 100-square-meter roof.

If every household uses the same amount of drinking water, the apartment block pays four times the stormwater charge. Its roof is no larger.

This arrangement disadvantages buildings where many households share a roof. Not-for-profit developments commonly have four to six storeys.

“It would be fair if payment reflected the amount of stormwater each property actually discharges into the sewer system,” Gulsrud noted.

“At present, charges are tied to drinking water consumption instead. There is a fundamental inequity in the way water bills are allocated and in how residents pay for climate adaptation and cloudburst management.”

“As water charges continue to rise, the burden falls most heavily on the most marginalised members of society.”

The researchers also examined how these decisions are made. They found a divide between departments responsible for technical infrastructure and those dealing with social policy.

That leaves household finances poorly accounted for when drainage requirements are enforced. A project can satisfy engineering standards while putting residents under greater financial strain.

“In this paper we hope to introduce a human aspect to a highly technical topic involving stormwater pipes, housing tenure, and climate financing,” Gulsrud said.

In Hørgården, public records showed that officials knew housing costs would rise. Those costs nevertheless did not determine whether the separation requirement would go ahead.

The authors argue that closer cooperation between social and technical departments could help distribute costs more fairly. Housing circumstances and household resources would then become part of planning the work.

For residents, that would mean considering the rent bill alongside the drainage plan.

“Who gets protected as the climate changes, and who carries the cost? A just transition needs social and technical governance working together, not on separate tracks.”

“If we don’t fix this, we risk deepening socio ecological inequalities in the green transition. We hope this research moves us closer to a transition that is genuinely just and caring,” Gulsrud concluded.

The study is published in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.
