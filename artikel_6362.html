When it’s really hot or humid out, opening a window or turning on a fan only gets you so far, especially when your house feels like it’s holding onto heat long after the sun goes down. If you’ve ever tried to sleep in a hot, airless room, you know how quickly feeling “a little warm” turns into being completely miserable. For homeowners and renters without central air conditioning, the standard solutions are typically ductless air conditioners, freestanding units, or window air conditioners.

Window air conditioning units work by pulling in warm and humid air, cooling and drying it, and sending it back into the room while pushing heat outside. Most units double as fans, and many now include dehumidifier modes, which is something you’ll appreciate on those sticky, midsummer days when the air feels truly soupy. And, unlike central air, a window air conditioner can run on a generator during a blackout. That’s a major plus in extreme climates.

The tricky part is finding a window air conditioner that actually fits your space, is easy to install, and doesn’t drive up your energy bill. Air conditioners are rated in British thermal units (BTUs), which indicate how much space they can effectively cool. A unit that’s too small will run constantly and never quite get there, but one that’s too large will cool too quickly without removing enough humidity. This leaves the room feeling clammy and uncomfortable.

That’s why several of our superlatives are organized around room size and use case, rather than just standout features. A 5,000-BTU unit may be a smart pick for a small bedroom, dorm room, or compact home office, while an 8,000-BTU model is better suited to a medium-size room where faster cooling and stronger air movement matter. Larger living rooms, open layouts, or rooms with high ceilings may need 10,000 BTUs or more. As a loose rule of thumb, small bedrooms may need around 5,000 to 8,000 BTUs, while larger living areas can require 10,000 BTUs or more. Lincoln says the most common issue is choosing a unit that is too small.

“A window unit may be able to knock down the temperature right near the unit, but that doesn’t mean it’s doing a good job across the full area,” says Lincoln. “In oversized rooms or open floor plans, I see units run nearly nonstop while still leaving parts of the space warm and damp.” She says that’s when customers note a room feels cool but clammy. “The system may be lowering temperature near the unit, but it isn’t really winning the humidity battle across the space.”

This also explains why a powerful fan, efficient compressor, or smart app only matters if it supports the way you’ll actually use the A/C. A stronger fan is most helpful when it moves cool air across a medium or large room, while an efficient inverter unit is especially useful in bedrooms or offices where the AC may run for long stretches. Smart controls are most valuable when they make daily use easier, whether that means scheduling, remote adjustments, energy tracking, or filter reminders.

Before you buy, measure your window—twice. Window units are designed for specific opening widths and heights, and some, like casement or sliding windows, require specialized models. Even with careful planning, you may strike out at first.

“Not every window unit fits every window well, even when the box says it should,” says Lincoln. “Consumers need to look at more than width. The window style, the sash opening, the weight of the unit, and the need for support all matter.” A window unit should be level side-to-side and pitched slightly outward so condensed water drains out of the house—not into your room.

It’s important to consider the unit’s weight, too. Larger units may require a bracket or homemade support to avoid strain and damage to the window and air conditioner.

CEER stands for Combined Energy Efficiency Ratio, and it tells you how efficiently a unit uses electricity over time, not just when it’s actively cooling. Older ratings like EER measure efficiency only while the compressor is running. CEER goes a step further by also factoring in energy used when the unit is idle, like when it’s on standby or cycling on and off throughout the day.

Efficient units use less electricity to deliver the same cooling power, which matters if you’re running the A/C daily through peak summer. Features like programmable timers, eco modes, and inverter technology can further reduce energy use.

When it comes to energy efficiency, Lincoln says it’s just as much about the rating as it is about how you install the unit—specifically how well-sealed the gaps are.

“The best sealing materials are simple, cheap, and worth the effort,” says Lincoln. Foam weatherstripping, rope caulk, and other similar materials can go a long way. “I’ve seen homeowners blame the A/C when the real issue was a half-inch gap letting hot, wet air pour in all afternoon. Sealing is not just about stopping heat. It’s about stopping humidity. And in summer comfort, that distinction matters.”

If you’re used to old-school window units, you’re familiar with the buzz and crackle as they cool. Thankfully, advancements in technology have resolved that issue a bit. Not all A/Cs are created equal when it comes to sound, though. “Traditional window units tend to be louder because they cycle on and off more abruptly,” shares Lincoln. She says inverter models are often better for bedrooms and offices. An inverter unit works more like a dimmer switch than an on/off switch. Instead of turning the AC off, it slows down once it reaches your desired temperature and maintains it by running at a lower, steady speed. “The difference between a hard compressor kick and a steadier low hum is noticeable when you live with it every night.” We’ve noted this feature on the units we reviewed when applicable.

Check the decibel (dB) rating, too. Units in the low 40s are considered quiet, while anything pushing 60 dB or higher can be disruptive, especially at night.

Modern air conditioners do more than just cool. Remote controls, WiFi connectivity, and app integration let you adjust settings from anywhere. Some units include dehumidifier modes, sleep settings, or directional airflow controls that improve comfort without cranking the temperature.

Lincoln says that the “extras” worth paying for don’t often top the specs list, though. “The features worth paying for aren’t always the flashy ones. A lot of people get distracted by phone apps and forget the basics,” she says of most consumers. “The extra features I’d actually pay attention to are variable-speed operation, a true dry mode if you live in a humid climate, auto-restart after a power outage, and a thermostat or sensor that’s actually useful for where people sit or sleep.”

“Window units can be efficient, but only if the install is right,” says our expert Kim Lincoln. “A poorly sealed window AC is basically paying to cool the air around the leak.”

With the right setup, though, window air conditioners can be surprisingly efficient — especially compared to cooling an entire home with central air. Window units give you the option of cooling only the rooms you need at that time.

The key is choosing the right size and looking for models with strong CEER ratings or Energy Star certification. Features like eco mode, programmable timers, and inverter compressors can further reduce energy use by preventing the unit from constantly running at full power.

BTUs measure cooling power, and getting this right is critical. As a baseline, you’ll need about 20 BTUs per square foot of living space. For example, a 150-square-foot room typically calls for a 5,000–6,000 BTU unit. You should also factor in real-world conditions: increase capacity for sunny rooms, kitchens, or higher ceilings, and scale down slightly for shaded spaces.

“When people ask how to calculate the right BTU for a room, the honest answer is that manufacturer charts are a starting point, not a finish line,” says Lincoln. “For a window unit, those charts can get you close.” Be careful about over-purchasing power, though. That doesn’t mean your room will be cooler. Oversizing can lead to quick cooling without proper dehumidification, while undersizing can leave the unit running constantly.

“This is the least glamorous advice and still one of the most important,” says Lincoln. “Clean filters are still one of the biggest performance factors. For window units, the filter should be checked regularly and cleaned about once a month during heavy cooling season, sometimes more often if the home has pets, dust, smoke, renovation debris, or high traffic.”

Most manufacturer specs recommend cleaning the filter every two to four weeks during periods of heavy use, as well. A clogged filter restricts airflow, which can reduce efficiency and force the unit to work harder. The good news is that most window AC filters are washable—just remove, rinse, let dry, and reinstall. Keeping the filter clean helps maintain strong airflow, improves cooling performance, and can extend the lifespan of the unit. If your AC does not have a washable filter, Lincoln recommends keeping a spare on hand. If your AC does not have smart features or a filter reminder, it’s clutch to add this task to your personal calendar so it is not neglected.
