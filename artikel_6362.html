Standing desks may change how people work, even while sitting

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Workers with sit-stand desks logged more active computer time, but researchers could not determine whether standing explained the difference.

Sit-stand desks are meant to get office workers out of their chairs. A large study has now linked the desks to roughly 43 extra minutes of active computer use each day.

The comparison was with coworkers who used fixed-height desks.

More than 10,000 employees of one company were included in the analysis. The company never recorded whether any of them actually stood up.

The analysis was led by Tricia Salzar, a regional ergonomist with the U.S. Army Public Health Command. Mark Benden, a professor at the Texas A&M University School of Public Health, co-led the work.

Benden also directs the school’s Center for Worker Health.

The records were drawn from a workplace wellness program. They cover workers who joined that program between 2012 and 2014.

Desks were not assigned by the researchers. The study can therefore show that the desks and the extra computer time went together.

The company is a California energy firm that employs about 24,000 people. Under its wellness program, keypresses and mouse clicks were logged by software on each worker’s computer.

A worker was counted as active only while the keyboard or mouse was in use. If 30 seconds passed without a keypress or a click, the clock stopped.

Workers were also asked in a survey whether they used a height-adjustable desk. That is another name for a sit-stand desk.

Salzar’s team identified 3,404 workers who answered yes on two surveys taken a year apart. They were compared with 6,741 workers who answered no both times. Anyone who switched desks during the year was left out.

Over the year, workers at fixed desks averaged 2.95 hours of active computer use a day. An average of 3.66 hours was recorded for workers with sit-stand desks.

The difference between the two groups did not take a year to appear. A gap of about 40 minutes a day in active computer time was already present in the first month.

Across a work year, the authors calculated, the daily difference comes to about 171 hours. That is more than four full-time weeks.

The sit-stand group also typed more. They averaged about 860 more keypresses a day than the fixed-desk group. They also typed roughly 80 more words.

Sit-stand users also made slightly more typos. This remained true after the team adjusted for the number of words typed. The authors wrote that this difference in typos may be too small to matter.

Long hours of uninterrupted sitting have been linked to health risks and to muscle and joint pain.

The survey therefore also asked how often workers felt physical discomfort at work. Soreness, tired muscles and eye strain were given as examples.

More discomfort was reported by sit-stand users, not less. In the first month, 30.1% of them said they never felt discomfort. The figure for workers at fixed desks was 35.7%.

The two groups then moved closer together. By the end of the year, 32.5% of sit-stand users said they never felt discomfort. Among fixed-desk workers, the share was 34.3%.

The change was described by the authors as negligible. In a group this large, they wrote, a difference can pass a statistical test. It can still be too small to matter in practice.

Why would workers with sit-stand desks report more discomfort in the first place? Some may have requested the desks because they were already in pain, the authors wrote.

The records do not show why any worker had one.

Salzar’s team converted the extra time into money. The federal Office of Personnel Management standard for a work year was used. The average wage was set at $52.04 an hour.

The authors put the value of the added computer time at about $8,900 per worker per year. A fully adjustable sit-stand desk costs about $2,200 delivered and set up, they estimated. It could therefore pay for itself in under three months.

The estimate assumes that computer activity can stand in for productivity. It also assumes that giving a worker the desk would produce the extra minutes.

Benden said the findings suggest that the desks “can directly influence overall work output and economic organizational productivity.”

The paper he co-wrote is more cautious. The authors wrote that “the only thing that we can truly determine” is an association. Workers who had the desks also used their computers more.

Benden has worked on standing desks as an inventor as well as a researcher. He founded Stand2Learn, a company that made standing desks for classrooms. It was acquired by the desk maker Varidesk in 2018.

He is also the named inventor on patents for adjustable-height desk accessories. Those patents are held by the Texas A&M University System. No outside funding and no conflicts of interest are listed in the paper.

The two groups were not matched. Sit-stand users were younger, with an average age of about 43.

The average for the fixed-desk group was 49. A larger share of the sit-stand users were women.

Salzar’s team also had no record of any worker’s job.

An administrative worker might spend more time typing, the authors wrote. An engineer might instead spend time at the computer reading reports and specifications.

Reading without typing or clicking does not register as active time.

Suppose sit-stand desks went mostly to workers in keyboard-heavy jobs. The job, not the desk, could then account for the gap.

The company’s records are also more than a decade old, and they come from its offices. They cannot show what the desks do for people working from home.

How often a desk was raised was not recorded in the survey. Neither was how long a worker stood or how many times a day the worker changed posture.

Standing may not explain the 43 minutes. The authors cited an earlier study from Benden’s group on this point. They wrote that fewer than 10% of people with these desks choose to stand.

People who do stand may spend only 10% to 20% of the day on their feet.

The authors offered another explanation. A standard desk is fixed at a height of 29 to 30 inches (74 to 76 cm). A sit-stand desk can be set to fit the person sitting at it. The benefit may come from that fit.

Benden said his group is also interested in how AI tools could change the physical workspace.

Future studies should record how often workers raise their desks and how long they stand, the authors wrote. Until one does, the extra 43 minutes cannot be credited to standing.

The full study was published in the journal Theoretical and Applied Ergonomics.
