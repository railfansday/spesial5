Tiny ocean buoys could transform how we predict powerful storms

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

Scientists found that drifting weather buoys in the Mediterranean Sea helped improve forecasts during a powerful storm that battered Sicily.

Modern weather forecasting relies heavily on satellites watching the planet around the clock.

Yet, when storm Harry drowned parts of Sicily in rain this January, a handful of barometers bobbing in the Ionian Sea helped the forecast.

Until the previous fall, that patch of sea had almost no weather instruments at the surface. Scientists dropped the barometer buoys there on purpose.

A project called the Mediterranean Extreme Events Experiment, or M3E, began releasing them in September 2025 to fill a long-standing observation gap. Storm Harry was its first big test.

A team led by David Lavers of the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts found that the buoys’ pressure readings reduced forecast error during the storm.

“The Mediterranean is a relatively small sea, but it can generate very powerful storms, and the countries around it pay a high price,” said Emanuela Clementi, a senior scientist at the CMCC Foundation in Bologna and a co-author of the study.

“With M3E we are filling a gap in the observations precisely where these storms form. Storm Harry gave us the first evidence that these data help forecasts,” she added.

Every forecast starts from a snapshot of the atmosphere. That snapshot comes from millions of measurements a day, from satellites, aircraft, balloons and ground stations.

But thick clouds and heavy rain blur what a satellite sees in the lower atmosphere. So a barometer at the surface in the right place still carries unusual weight.

Yet surface weather instruments have long been scarce across the Ionian Sea and central Mediterranean.

The region has seen extreme storms since biblical times, the authors wrote, and persistent cyclones tend to form there.

Three recent ones, storm Apollo in 2021, storm Daniel in 2023 and now Harry, did heavy damage around the basin.

The barometer buoys that filled the gap drift with the current. An underwater drogue hanging 49 feet (15 meters) below each one keeps it from washing ashore too quickly.

Fifteen came from the Global Drifter Program at the Scripps Institution of Oceanography, and five more were paid for by Italy’s OGS oceanography institute.

Each buoy measures air pressure and sea surface temperature, relays the numbers by satellite every hour, and feeds them into the global weather data stream within minutes.

The first seven went into the Ionian Sea in early September 2025, five from the research vessel Laura Bassi and two from a passing container ship.

More followed through February 2026. By late March, 10 of the 20 were still working; the rest had run aground in southern Italy or Greece, or been picked up by sailors.

One buoy drifted out of the Ionian Sea into the Adriatic and ended up along its eastern shore, tracing the regional current as it went.

Storm Harry formed as a small low over Morocco and deepened over Algeria a day later. Then it stalled over Tunisia and the central Mediterranean for days.

A steady southeasterly wind built waves of about 26 feet (8 meters) and drove moist air into the mountains of Sardinia, Sicily and Calabria.

Between January 19 and 21, 23 weather stations recorded more than 11.8 inches (300 millimeters) of rain. Eight of them passed 15.7 inches (400 millimeters), and eastern Sicily recorded the highest total, 19 inches (482.5 millimeters).

The toll was heavy. Losses in Sicily alone were estimated at more than €1 billion, a landslide hit the town of Niscemi, and people died in North Africa.

The European forecast model saw the Sicilian deluge coming about seven days ahead, helped by mountains like Mount Etna, which force moist air upward in the same place every time.

Northern Tunisia fared worse. One station there recorded 8.1 inches (207 millimeters), while the forecast issued at the start of January 19 had a median of just 1.6 inches (41.4 millimeters).

The authors suspect four small low-pressure systems between the Balearic Islands and North Africa contributed to the forecasting difficulties. Such systems are difficult to predict far in advance.

The team used two tests. The first scores every observation on whether it made the next day’s forecast better or worse.

Throughout the winter, the buoys’ scores were small but mostly positive, as expected when forecasts are already accurate.

On January 19, as Harry crossed the Ionian Sea, pressure at one buoy dropped sharply. Its readings proved particularly helpful in improving the forecast.

In the second test, the team reran the forecast for the storm’s two peak days with every drifting barometer buoy in the Mediterranean removed.

Near the Balearic Islands, where those four small lows sat, the two versions differed by about 2 hectopascals of pressure.

That pressure difference may seem small, but it can matter when forecasting compact weather systems.

The small lows were sensitive to pressure readings taken hundreds of miles to the east.

“These systems highlight the importance of collecting observations directly at sea,” Clementi said.

“The Ionian Sea and central Mediterranean have historically had limited coverage of in-situ atmospheric measurements, despite being areas where intense storms and persistent cyclones can develop.”

The authors call this a preliminary assessment. It rests on a single storm and a two-day rerun.

Four buoys received slightly negative scores after being deployed during relatively calm weather in late February, when their readings offered little opportunity to improve the forecasts.

The buoys are also a tiny fraction of everything the model takes in.

The team expects each one to count for more than a reading from a well-covered area, because it fills a gap.

Whether more buoys could help prevent forecasting errors like those seen in Tunisia remains uncertain.

The researchers now plan to focus on the small low-pressure systems that contributed to those errors.

Another 20 to 30 buoys are due to go into the Ionian and Tyrrhenian Seas starting this fall. The next Mediterranean storm will meet far more eyes at the surface.

The full study was published in the journal Quarterly Journal of the Royal Meteorological Society.
