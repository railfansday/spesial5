People are trusting AI advice, even when it’s really bad

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

AI advisors with hidden goals steered people toward worse choices in a new study, yet most participants still rated the advice as helpful.

Ask a chatbot which fitness tracker to buy and it feels like asking a knowledgeable friend with nothing to sell. In a new experiment, that friend had a hidden goal, and people followed it.

An AI advisor instructed to favor an inferior option increased the odds that people would prefer it over the best one to roughly five to eight times.

Most participants still rated the advisor as helpful afterward.

June Liu and Tatia Lee of the Department of Psychology at the University of Hong Kong ran the study with Sahand Sabour and Minlie Huang at Tsinghua University.

All 233 participants chose among hypothetical options in scripted scenarios and were told so. The authors flagged that as a limit on what the results can say.

The team built three advisors from one AI model, GPT-4o, changing only its instructions. One was told to help.

One was told to push a particular inferior option without ever saying so. Its prompt scored it on points: 100 for landing the target option, 50 for any other poor option, zero for the best one.

The third got the same goal plus a menu of influence tactics from psychology research.

Each person got one advisor and one kind of decision. Financial scenarios asked which fitness tracker, weight-loss medication, or clothing site to choose on a budget.

Emotional scenarios asked how to handle low self-esteem, a fight with a friend, or criticism at work.

Every scenario offered four options, and one fit the person’s stated needs.

In the money scenarios, the other three were a scam-like deal, an overbuilt version beyond the budget, and a subscription with long-term costs.

In the emotional ones, they were ways of coping that psychologists link to worse outcomes, such as withdrawing.

People rated each option from 1 to 10, talked with the advisor for at least ten exchanges, and rated the options again. The advisors also knew each person’s personality scores.

Before anyone talked to an advisor, every group rated the best option highest. Talking with a misaligned advisor shifted preferences.

Among people given the misaligned advisor on financial decisions, the share preferring the hidden target over the best option rose by 29 percentage points relative to the neutral advisor’s group.

With the tactics-equipped advisor it rose by 38 points. On emotional decisions the rises were 20 and 18 points.

Handing the advisor a toolkit of influence tactics didn’t reliably add to that.

The advisor given influence tactics was somewhat more effective at shifting financial preferences but less effective on emotional questions.

Even without that toolkit, an advisor with a hidden goal could steer people toward worse choices.

The researchers logged which tactics the equipped advisor chose. It leaned on pleasant ones, such as flattery and making the conversation enjoyable, rather than guilt trips.

On money questions, it used diversion, justification, urgency, and fabricated information more often, a mix the authors compared to predatory marketing.

On emotional questions, it leaned on pleasure and, at times, guilt, with a wider range of reassuring replies.

After the sessions, 86.8% of people in the financial group and 86.5% in the emotional group rated the misaligned advisor as helpful.

For the tactics-equipped advisor, the figures were 78.9% and 75.6%. The neutral advisor scored 97.5% and 87.2%.

Nobody was asked directly whether they felt manipulated, but some said so on their own.

On financial questions, 13.2% of people with the misaligned advisor and 28.9% with the tactics version mentioned a possible ulterior motive, and none of the neutral advisor’s group did.

On emotional questions, fewer than one in ten did in any group.

The authors’ reading is that people applied the instincts they use to judge whom to trust among other people to a system built around a different motive.

Perceived helpfulness didn’t explain the shift, either.

Participants considered hypothetical choices without making purchases or acting on the emotional advice.

Whether these preference shifts carry into real purchases or real conflicts remains unknown.

The sessions lasted at least ten exchanges, and the advisors knew each participant’s personality scores. The authors noted that these conditions went beyond a casual conversation with a general AI chatbot.

They described the result as what is possible under favorable conditions, not an estimate of everyday risk.

The study also tested only one AI model using a limited set of prompts.

Participants came from two pools, English speakers on Prolific and Chinese speakers on WeChat, and the pattern held in both.

Whether a human advisor with the same secret goal would move people as far is a question the study did not test. The authors named it as important work still to do.

The full study was published in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.
