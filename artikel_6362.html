The 7 Best Camping Chairs You’ll Be Happy to Sink Your Butt Into

Roughing it doesn't mean you need a rough landing. We got our hands on models from Yeti, Coleman, REI Co-op, and more.

A camping chair is the way to go if you want a comfortable place to sit around the campfire on your next camping trip. A quality camping chair balances portability with comfort, and some have features like thick, cushioned seats, footrests, or rocking bases. There are even camping chairs that pack down so small that you can fit them in your backpack—ideal for park concerts or to take backcountry camping.

Generally speaking, the most important things to look for are how easily the chair opens and collapses and how heavy or cumbersome it is to carry, along with the durability of the frame and seat material. Other considerations include weight capacity and the amount of support you get.

For this story, we stuck mainly to the classic collapsible camping chairs that work just as well for a week in the woods as they do sitting on your back patio. To help you narrow your search, our testers and I researched and then tested a selection of camping chairs, from the ultralight to the plush.

The newest camping chair from Yeti offers all the same quality of the Trailhead in a smaller, lighter, and more affordable package. The Trailhead Field is built with portability in mind, but doesn’t sacrifice comfort to do it.

The proprietary polyester blend Twilite fabric is flexible and smooth, while the aluminum frame sheds precious ounces without compromising too much durability. I actually enjoyed the Twilite fabric more than the the FlexGrid used in the original Trailhead. It has a bit more give and molded a bit better to my body, especially as I settled in.

It’s worth noting that the camping chair is much smaller than the original Trailhead, making it a lot easier to bring with you in the trunk of a small sedan without taking up too much space. That said, if you want a cup holder, you’ll have to pay an additional 20 bucks for the luxury.

Thanks to this affordable camping chair’s built-in insulated, four-can cooler, you can leave the kombuchas in the heavy cooler in the shade while you stash a few beer cans and a handful of ice in your chair.

Cooler sleeve aside, I also like this Coleman chair’s side pocket and cushioned backrest. The side pocket is big enough for books or tablets and has two mesh sections on the front for smaller items like bottle caps, keys, and a phone.

It has an above-average 325-pound weight limit. And while it isn’t the lightest camping chair on this list, I found its 8.5 pounds reasonable to transport, especially considering the attachments.

Make no mistake, this camping chair doesn’t lend itself well to extreme portability. But if you’re mostly car camping out of your truck or driving to a friend’s BBQ, you’ll love the extra size and support it offers.

Yeti’s Trailhead camping chair immediately stood out for its comfort. It’s made of a flexible, durable fabric that’s spread tight against the frame, with a weight limit of 500 pounds—the highest of any chair we tested. Despite its strength, the material wasn’t overly stiff either. There was enough give to cradle my legs and back, balancing out the support with softness. In fact, despite its lack of cushioning, I still found this to be one of the most comfortable.

Beyond the seat comfort, I was impressed by the sturdiness of the frame. It’s made of alloy steel and held up against scratches, heavier body weights, and exposure to sun and water. I also appreciated its locking mechanism; it’s essentially a handle on the back of the chair that snaps the frame in place and keeps the material taut. While it takes some effort to put into place, it allows you to shift around and even set it up on uneven ground without risk of it tipping while you lounge.

At more than 13 pounds, the Trailhead may be too heavy for some if there’s a long trek to camp. But its carrying case has backpack straps that are comfortable to wear, plus it keeps your arms free. Even on a walk of just around 10 to 15 minutes, I did feel its weight. It also isn’t cheap—it’s the most expensive camping chair we tested—and we wish it came in more than a couple colors, like Yeti’s impressive selection of coolers.

GCI has staked its claim as the king of camp comfort with its Rocker line of chairs. And the Kickback is Director of Product Content Will Egensteiner’s longtime favorite, keeping its spot on this list for close to six years now. Pistons on the back legs help with the rocking motion, and he found the resistance stiff enough that he wouldn’t accidentally tip the chair over backward—but not so aggressive that it felt like the Kickback was pitching him forward.

Both Egensteiner, who’s 6-foot-2, and another 5-foot-5 tester liked the seat height and had no problems sitting down and standing up. Egensteiner also liked that it has the usual cup holder and phone pocket on the armrests.

Despite that the chair is cumbersome to carry over a shoulder, the strap didn’t dig in. The Kickback is best for light activities such as car camping, backyard hangs, and picnics where you don’t have to lug it too far.

Some padding in the seat and backrest would be nice, but the fabric has some slack that makes the Kickback comfortable to sit in. Plus, the rocker design provides a nice recline that takes you off your knees and feet.

What sets the Helinox Chair One (re) apart from others is how compactly it packs down. Like earlier generations, the newest version of this ultralight chair has tubing that disassembles similar to tent poles, so it fits inside a mid-size backpack with room to spare. Along with its economical size, it’s also the lightest chair we recommend by several pounds. That makes it a good choice for hikes and backpacking trips.

During testing, I hauled the chair in its carrying pouch for close to half a mile on mixed terrain. Unlike some other options I tested, the Chair One (re) caused no strain at all.

That said, most other chairs were also ready to go the second I got to my destination. This camping chair must be set up. I had to lock the frame poles in place and stretch the cover over four different holding points. This took less than five minutes, which isn't much time, but is certainly more than other models.

Despite the small size, I actually found the chair incredibly comfortable. The design naturally provides a bit of recline and I was able to relax in it with ease. The material also offered a bit of give on the sides, which molded to my body.

If a little extra comfort is a major priority, the REI Co-Op Westward padded camping chair is a great choice. The foldable chair has foam padding sewn into the polyester material that provides a slightly more forgiving feel as you sit down. This made a major difference for me as the seated hours began to add up. Instead of readjusting every 20 minutes or so, I was able to sink in comfortably and let the padding carry the extra weight.

That said, my absolute favorite feature on the chair is the cup holder. The cup holder is built underneath the seat and swivels out when needed. That means I get the full arm rest for my arms and my drink is still inches away. The cup holder itself is also quite large, wide enough to fit my 32-ounce water bottle.

If portability is your biggest concern, the Crazy Creek AirCliner is the only camping chair we’ve tested and deflates, rolls up, and fits in a backpack with ease. The inflatable chair is also deceptively comfortable. In fact, once you find the sweet spot (which admittedly takes a minute), it genuinely rivals any other chair on this list.

Setup is also a breeze. After unclipping the latches and rolling out the chair, it takes just five full breaths to fully inflate it. One of the biggest concerns was that it would leak air over time, but after more than an hour of sitting, there was no noticeable softness or leakage.

The obvious downside is that there’s no frame, so you’ll be sitting right on the ground with your legs in front of you. While this isn’t a dealbreaker, it’s certainly something to consider

A camping chair’s durability is evaluated based on the frame, the material of the seat and back, and overall weather-resistance. Most of the camping chairs we write about are made with steel tubing that’s powder-coated or treated to resist rust and corrosion. This style of frame holds several hundred pounds without any bowing or bending and is still light enough to carry in your hands.

However, each chair on this list has a much different weight capacity so make sure to double check before you make your final purchase.

Similarly, seat durability relies on sturdy materials. Woven polyester is one of the most common options. Camping chair materials are often combined with a water-resistant coating so any spilled drinks or rain drops don't damage the fabric. This also protects any interior cushioning from absorbing water.

Great camping chairs are also treated to resist UV light. This helps prevent the fabric from fading over time. Similar to the metal frame, the fabric should also be strong enough to offer a weight capacity of 300 pounds at minimum.

While the most durable options (like the Yeti Trailhead) tend to be more expensive, think about upfront cost in relation to long-term use. If the chair lasts for several years, the initial cost is well worth it in comparison to a cheaper camping chair that might tear or break after just a couple of seasons.

Camping chairs are specifically designed to be portable. That said, the relative portability between them can range drastically.

Small, lightweight camping chairs pack down for easy transport and work well for backpackers or anyone traveling by foot. These typically come with carrying straps or collapse small enough to latch onto or pack into a large backpack. Ideally, they should weigh less than eight or nine pounds.

Larger chairs can weigh more than 10 pounds, which makes them far more cumbersome to carry and better suited to car camping and backyard lounging. What you get with that added size and weight, though, significantly more comfort afforded by a wider seat with more cushioning and a potentially higher weight limit.

Regardless of size, many camping chairs come with shoulder straps or carrying cases to make travel easier.

Camping chairs aren’t exclusively for camping, nor is all camping the same. If you simply want a chair for watching your kid’s soccer game or relaxing in the backyard, prioritize comfort above portability.

The more of an emphasis you put on comfort, the more added features are available to you. Many camping chairs come with foldout side tables, integrated cupholders, storage pouches, and even zip-up cooler bags.

For hikers and backpackers, smaller, lighter chairs are the obvious choices. If you’re using the chair for the beach, weather- and UV-resistance generally increase its longevity. It's also worth considering chairs with shorter legs so you can stretch out your own in the sand.

We carried the camping chairs through a park, taking note of how heavy they felt and whether they were cumbersome to transport up small hills and over boulders. After setting them up on grass and dirt patches to see how they balanced on uneven terrain, we took a seat, judging each by its relative comfort. Our testers spanned a range of heights and body weights so we could get a variety of impressions.
