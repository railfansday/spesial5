People with early-onset Alzheimer’s often face depression and dizziness before their diagnosis, but not high blood pressure

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Before early-onset Alzheimer's was diagnosed, depression, dizziness and brain injuries became more common, but high blood pressure did not.

High blood pressure and high cholesterol are two of the best-known risk factors for Alzheimer’s disease in older people.

In people diagnosed at 65 or younger, researchers in Finland found these two were no more common than in people of the same age without the disease.

What did rise was a scattered set of other problems.

In the five years before diagnosis, people who went on to develop early-onset Alzheimer’s were more often diagnosed with dizziness, depression, headaches, delirium, epilepsy, and brain injuries.

“Our findings support the view that early-onset Alzheimer’s disease is not simply a younger-age version of late-onset Alzheimer’s disease,” said Johanna Krüger, an adjunct professor at the University of Oulu and the study’s lead investigator.

“Rather than the familiar cardiovascular risk factors, we observed a range of other health problems becoming more common as diagnosis approached.”

Early-onset Alzheimer’s accounts for up to 10% of all Alzheimer’s cases. It tends to move faster than the late-onset kind, and it more often starts in unusual ways.

The early-onset disease is also slower to diagnose.

Earlier studies cited by the team put the average time to diagnosis at about 42 months, compared to about 31 months for the late-onset disease.

Diagnosis is often delayed by several years after the first symptoms appear.

The researchers wanted to know whether the years before diagnosis leave a trail in medical records.

The team came from the University of Oulu and the University of Eastern Finland. It started with every patient sent to the memory clinics at Oulu and Kuopio university hospitals from 2010 to 2021.

In Finland, suspected early-onset cases in those regions are sent to those two hospitals.

The team re-checked each record against standard criteria for Alzheimer’s. That left 407 people diagnosed at age 65 or younger, with an average age at diagnosis of about 61.

For comparison, a national agency randomly picked 4,035 people matched to the patients by age, sex, and region. None had a neurodegenerative disease.

Next, the team pulled every diagnosis for both groups from Finland’s national health records. Finnish doctors must record a diagnosis code after every visit, public or private.

The team compared the groups at three stretches of time before diagnosis: one to five years, six to 10, and 11 to 15.

Eleven to 15 years before diagnosis, none of the conditions the team examined differed between the groups. Six to 10 years out, one did.

Tinnitus and hearing loss were recorded in 4.7% of future patients compared to 2.1% of the comparison group.

The bigger change came in the last five years. Dizziness was recorded in 5.9% of future patients compared to 2.1% of the comparison group.

Depression appeared in 5.7% compared to 2.3%, and headache in 4.9% compared to 1.6%.

Brain injuries, strokes, epilepsy, thyroid disorders, alcohol-related disorders, and delirium were all more common too.

Delirium is a sudden state of confusion, and it was recorded in 1.5% of future patients compared to 0.1% of the others.

Each of these conditions was still uncommon, recorded in no more than about 6% of future patients.

High blood pressure or high cholesterol was recorded in 10.6% of future patients in the last five years, compared to 9.3% of the comparison group.

That gap was too small to rule out chance, and the two groups were close at every earlier stretch as well.

In late-onset Alzheimer’s, those heart and blood vessel risks have been linked to the disease in earlier research.

The authors wrote that only one earlier, smaller study had reported a similar result for the early-onset disease. They suggested that the causes of the two forms may differ, at least in part.

Health care visits rose only near the end. In the year before diagnosis, future patients had an average of 3.3 recorded care episodes, compared to 1.9 for the comparison group.

In the year of diagnosis it was 6.3 compared to 2.0. Two or more years out, the groups did not differ.

“Of course, no single disease or symptom predicts Alzheimer’s disease,” said Laura Leppänen, a doctoral researcher at the University of Oulu and the study’s first author.

“Our results suggest that, in the years before diagnosis, patients may accumulate a variety of seemingly unrelated symptoms and healthcare encounters. Recognizing this overall pattern could help raise suspicion of a neurodegenerative disease earlier than is currently the case.”

The study compares records, so it can’t say whether any of these conditions helps cause the disease.

Depression, delirium, or alcohol-related disorders may be early signs of brain changes already under way, and the authors wrote that the design cannot tell those possibilities apart.

The registers held no information on social and economic background, lifestyle, or genes, and diagnoses in them could not be checked independently.

Only people who lived through each stretch were counted. People in the comparison group could also have had early brain changes that had not been diagnosed.

Early onset was defined by age at diagnosis, not at first symptoms, which may have left out people whose symptoms began just before 65.

The results come from one country’s health system, and the authors wrote that they need to be checked in other datasets.

Researchers at the same universities have reported that early-onset dementia is more common than earlier international studies suggested.

Other teams are working on blood tests that might flag the disease years before symptoms.

The full study was published in the journal Alzheimer’s & Dementia.
