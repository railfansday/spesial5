Noah Kahan Deserved a Lot More Grammy Love Than Just One Minor Nomination. Here’s Why He Didn’t Get It

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

Fans and professional forecasters alike were shocked when the superstar nearly got shut out, after predictions across the board that his 'Great Divide' blockbuster would earn major award recognition. Some of the reasons the Grammys didn't ultimately go for him may be in plain sight.

Senior Music Writer and Chief Music Critic

In previous eras of less pervasive social media, we usually could only guess at just how disappointed music and film personalities were when they didn’t fare well with Grammy or Oscar nominations. But lately, more and more major and minor figures have been up-front and honest in sharing with their fans that, hey, yes, they’re feeling the apparent lack of industry love, too. As long as these reflections come off as more bummed out and philosophical than bitterly entitled or peevish — here’s looking at you, John Summit — they can make for a moment of connection between artists and their fan base. And so it is that, this past week, I followed one of our most likably candid superstars through what I came to refer to as the Five Stages of Noah Kahan Grammy Grief.

Kahan himself skipped over the anger phase, though his fans certainly felt that one, when it turned out that their hero got passed over for everything but one relatively modest nom, in defiance of all expectation. There were other stages they went through together. First came uneasy anticipation: The day before nominations came out, Kahan issued a whatever-will-be-will-be-type tweet, almost as if he’d gotten a hint that things were not going to go well for him. (“Whatever happens tomorrow I love you all and I have done everything I’ve ever needed to do in my career because of YOUR support.”) Then came resignation and premature acceptance: “Life goes on.” Then came gallows humor, as he took a fake phone call on stage from the Grammys and quipped, “Wrong number.” And finally, there was real acceptance, as he admitted in an affecting stage monologue that he was having a teachable moment as, without mentioning the awards, Kahan said he was assessing why he had hoped too much for something and put the value of his worth in the wrong place.

Undoubtedly, some would say he should have just put himself above it all and not even referenced the seeming snub, but my admiration is always going to go more toward people who address elephants in rooms. So maybe now he could move forward with his usual good humor… while, sure, having the fact that he just sold out his next 24 U.S. stadium shows to keep him warm at night.

But maybe us Grammy prognosticators aren’t yet ready to move on, because like pollsters in 2024, we want to figure out how we got Kahan’s chances wrong. And really, he was the only significant thing we forecasted incorrectly, since virtually everything else in the top three categories went as predicted, for once. Kahan seemed to have almost everything going for him, being the only artist who consistently had not just one but two albums in the top 10 all year long, on top of having recently become one of the biggest ticket-sellers in the business… and, lest the predictions be based only on popular appeal, he’d also gotten mostly great reviews lately, although he hadn’t started out his career as a critical favorite.

Professional prognosticators nearly all had Kahan’s “The Great Divide” album landing one of the eight open slots for album of the year, and most entertainment news outlets predicted the same for the title track in the fields of record and/or song of the year. The forecasters lining up to say he was close to a sure thing included those at Billboard, Rolling Stone, the Hollywood Reporter, Hits and, yes, Variety. Over at the awards site Gold Derby, “Great Divide” was seen as all but locked in for an album nomination no matter which of their columns you looked down — industry experts, their editors, or the site’s active followers. Instead, he wound up with a lone nod for… best contemporary folk recording, which was rather like the Recording Academy setting a bull down in a china shop instead of at the rodeo. There was one obvious stumbling block on the path to considering him a shoo-in for one of more big nods: the fact that he’d only gotten two nominations before, and only one as the primary person nominated. Kahan was nominated as best new artist for the 2024 Grammys (and lost to the empirically less popular Victoria Monet); his only other prior nom was in a country category as the featured duet partner on a Kelsea Ballerini single. But surely we were past the point now — we thought — where anyone still had to dude-splain Noah Kahan to the Grammy elite.

Of course it shouldn’t matter if “The Great Divide” spent most of 2026 in the top 10 hovering just above the continued presence of the four-year-old (!) “Stick Season.” if these albums blew chunks… which is the kind of thing that’s been known to sink a Grammy nomination. But “The Great Divide” demonstrably does not fall into the realm of suckage. If anything, it represents the kind of perfect storm the Grammys have traditionally loved to reward, where somehow, some way, songwriting that is idiosyncratic and qualitatively strong and unique captures the imagination of the masses who might usually be stereotyped as tending toward less distinctly personal fare. Kahan could have been seen as having it all…

Except for having a natural constituency among Recording Academy members.

Therein lies a rub. It did nag at me, even as I was making my own forecast that he’d get into the top categories, that I couldn’t picture in my mind’s eye which subset of voters would be putting their support behind him. In terms of either genre or geography, he did not and does not have the wind at his back.

The Recording Academy has 12 chapters spread across the U.S. This may come as a shock, but none of them are in Burlington. There’s a Pacific Northwest chapter, but good luck looking for a corresponding Atlantic Northeast chapter. Surely Kahan would be the first to laugh at the idea of his New England industry homies colluding as a voting bloc to ensure vindication for Vermont.

The genre thing looms as an even bigger problem. Leaving aside the general field at the top of the ballot, where does a Kahan rightfully belong among the 90+ stylistically divided categories that follow? You could consider him rock. Billboard certainly does; he has two songs in the top 10 of the rock airplay chart right now (“Orbiter” and “Draw You Out”). But are the voters who choose to vote in the rock categories — and as you probably know, they don’t get to vote in all 100 — really going there to vote for a guy like Kahan, as opposed to the harder stuff? He would seem like a closer fit for the Americana and American roots categories. But the Americana community hasn’t really fully embraced him, even though he has shown up to their events. He didn’t come up through their farm system, and in a genre that deliberately favors underdogs, he might’ve simply gotten too popular too fast. So, “contemporary folk recording” it is, even if that is one oddball catch-all category. (But as has been pointed out, there are people nominated in that every year who have the nom as a highlight of their professional careers, so there should be no shame in it for Kahan, and he’d undoubtedly agree.)

If you really want to know why we all should have seen Kahan’s near-shutout coming, anyway, consider the ongoing case of Zach Bryan, who also falls between genres, despite also being one of the biggest recording and touring artists in the universe at the moment. I thought of Bryan as I was wondering whether Kahan would face the same problem with the Grammys, but at the time, I thought Bryan might have been snubbed most of all because of his surlier personality, whereas Kahan being an overtly personable guy would make the difference. Turns out they’re probably just in the same ballpark after all.

And then there is the issue of white guys with guitars, and how they’re doing, generally, in the culture, or with the Grammys specifically. The demographics the Recording Academy has been courting in expanding and diversifying its membership do not suggest they’re bringing in vast amounts of people who are going to put a Kahan at the top of their ballot, any more than they’re going to vote for Paul McCartney (whose critically hailed “Boys of Dungeon Lane” would have been a shoo-in for top noms with the Grammy voters of the 2000s, but not the 2020s). When the Grammys invited 3,600 pros to join as voting members in the class of 2025, 56% were people of color and 60% were under 40. You don’t look at those stats and imagine that any of the various waning subgenres of rock are suddenly going to be overrepresented among the biggest Grammy nominees.

But here’s the other thing to take into account: all those new voters, along with the older ones the Academy has not aged it out, are getting it right more often than not. As much as I like “The Great Divide,” when I look at the album of the year lineup to see which nominee I would personally knock out to make room for it, there are only a couple of the eight that seem even remotely expendable. Most forecasters would probably say that Phoebe Bridgers’ “Lost Weekend” took a slot that was expected to go to Kahan, and maybe she knocked him down to ninth place, although we’ll never know for sure where he ended up. There’s absolutely nothing regrettable about her making it in, or the utterly deserving Rosalía or Raye or most of the others… and obviously no shame in women earning seven out of eight noms in some of the top categories, after the “step up, girls” era, and on merit. Of all the years for any sense of overriding Grammy outrage to take hold, this is not one of them.

But I can think of other artists besides Kahan I think got “robbed” in the top categories, like Sienna Spiro, who should have been up for all four top general-field trophies but is only in for best new artist. A year this solid and action-packed makes the case that the Academy should considering reupping the number of finalists in the top four categories from eight to 10, as was the case in 2022 and 2023 before tjeu dropped it back down again. If the Oscars can stick with 10 nominees at the top of the ballot, so could the Grammys.

In the meantime, as Kahan fans lick wounds, they ought to be able to take some comfort in the hundred factors that make even a worthy contender get shut out, or nearly ignored. It’s not him, it’s them — in all sorts of ways — and there are real reasons to believe that, not just the delusional ones hardcore stans come up with to justify more negligible artists being slighted. And if the reasons for being left out have to do with being the kind of artist who snuck up completely between the cracks, and who’s thriving beyond belief, even as he continues to be a little bit stepped over, that’s a triumph that might finally be even better than a Grammy.
