From Source to Site: How Recovered and Engineered Materials Shape Design

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Conventional construction often begins with a specification: a particular brick, concrete mix, manufactured panel or finish selected for a proposed building. What changes when the design process begins with a material that has already been recovered, produced or made available? Today, projects approach material resources through different processes: recovery, recycling and the engineering of locally sourced bamboo. Taken together, the varied approaches will show how material sourcing and processing can influence architectural decisions before a product reaches the building site.

Working with recovered or locally available materials introduces a different set of conditions into the design process. Recovered components rarely arrive in standardized dimensions or predictable quantities. Architects need to establish how many usable components are available, what condition they are in and whether their dimensions suit the proposed application. The available stock can influence the building's dimensions, the distribution of materials and the sequence of construction.

The tiles were collected from four locations and reused across the building's pathways, walls and roof. Tiles conventionally used for roofing are also incorporated into pathways and walls. The available quantity and condition of the tiles influence where they can be used across the house.

RAD+ar's Lattice Creative Garden in Jakarta uses recycled plastic to construct an external shading system. The project description states that 4,800 kilograms of plastic collected from the SCBD business district's waste stream were used to manufacture 16,800 lattice plates. The plates are suspended from a tensioned cable system, forming an undulating external screen designed to provide solar shading.

RAD+ar's Lattice Creative Garden in Jakarta uses recycled plastic to construct an external shading system. The project description states that 4,800 kilograms of plastic collected from the SCBD business district's waste stream were used to manufacture 16,800 lattice plates. The plates are suspended from a tensioned cable system, forming an undulating external screen designed to provide solar shading.

The architects relate the screen to traditional Indonesian latticework, adapting the use of patterned openings and filtered light through a system of recycled-plastic plates and tensioned cables. The repeated plates create the screen's pattern and determine how it is distributed across the façade. The recycled plates are therefore part of the building's shading system and visual composition, rather than a material used only for its recycled content.

The environmental performance of the resulting component depends on more than the fact that it contains recycled plastic. Its service life will depend on how the plastic responds to prolonged exposure, how often the plates need maintenance or replacement, and whether they can be recovered at the end of use. An assessment should therefore account for the material diverted from disposal, the component's service life and its end-of-life options. The source of a material and the processes used to manufacture it can influence its structural properties, availability and relationship to local construction practices.

Located at Mertasari Beach in Bali, the 84-square-meters pavilion by Cave Urban reinterprets the Bale Banjar, a traditional Balinese community meeting space. The pavilion's laminated bamboo structure is supplied through Indobamboo, which links smallholder forestry cooperatives in Flores with manufacturing in Bali. The pavilion also incorporates other materials, including reclaimed roof tiles and Ulin wood. The curved laminated bamboo roof rises to approximately 8.5 metres, spanning an open pavilion beneath it.

In a 2020 systematic review published in Waste Management & Research, Kambiz Rakhshan and colleagues classify barriers to building-component reuse as economic, technical, social, regulatory and organisational. Concerns about component quality, regulatory compliance, liability and cost can make architects and contractors reluctant to specify recovered materials. Wider use will depend on reliable systems for assessing components, documenting their condition, establishing liability and coordinating procurement.

Life-cycle assessment can help compare the environmental impacts of material systems, provided the scope and assumptions of each assessment are clear. Eco-Mantra's life-cycle assessment of BaleBio reports a 110 percent reduction in Stage A emissions against its stated conventional baseline. The result is attributed primarily to the bamboo system, including the carbon stored in the material and the impacts associated with its production and transport. The result applies to BaleBio's stated assessment boundary and baseline; it cannot be generalized to other bamboo products, reclaimed materials or locally sourced components. Any wider comparison would also need to account for service life, maintenance, transport and end-of-life treatment.
