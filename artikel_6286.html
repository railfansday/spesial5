Scientists reported that an AI-designed protein can activate broken insulin receptors in lab mice, hinting at a potential new treatment for some forms of severe insulin resistance.

A protein designed with the help of artificial intelligence (AI) has successfully switched on faulty insulin receptors in lab mice even when insulin itself couldn't.

The findings hint at a new way to treat certain rare, life-threatening genetic disorders that cause insulin resistance, in which the body's cells don't respond well to the blood-sugar-controlling hormone.

In a study published Wednesday (Nov. 25) in the journal Science Translational Medicine, researchers found that the AI-designed protein, called RF-409, lowered blood sugar and reduced damage to the pancreas in mice with a genetic form of severe insulin resistance. This, in turn, prevented dangerous weight loss that typically comes with the condition.

When blood sugar rises, the pancreas releases insulin, a hormone that binds to proteins called insulin receptors on the surface of cells. This changes the receptors' shape, triggering a series of chemical signals that ultimately help bring blood sugar levels back down.

But mutations in the gene that encodes the insulin receptor can disrupt this process. More than 150 such mutations have been linked to inherited forms of insulin resistance. In one of the most severe forms, called Donohue syndrome, children can experience dangerously high blood sugar after eating, and unusually low blood sugar between meals, as well as poor growth. Most children with the condition die before their first birthday.

"Some [of these] patients produce extremely high levels of insulin, but their receptors cannot respond properly," study co-author Eunhee Choi, a cell biologist at Columbia University, told Live Science. "Simply giving more insulin may not solve the problem."

That's why Choi and her colleagues explored whether they could activate the receptor in another way.

In 2025, the researchers used computational tools, including AlphaFold2, an AI program that predicts protein structures and how they fit together, to develop proteins that activate insulin receptors in a different way than insulin does. In that previous study, one of those proteins, RF-409, lowered blood sugar in both healthy mice and mice with obesity. In the new research, the team wanted to see whether RF-409 could also activate defective insulin receptors.

First, they ran experiments with laboratory-grown mouse and rat cells and with healthy mice, both with working insulin receptors. This confirmed that RF-409 largely mimicked insulin's effects on cellular signaling and metabolism. In the mice, RF-409 had a nearly 60-fold longer half-life in the bloodstream than insulin, meaning the protein stays in circulation much longer before breaking down.

"Both these features make [the finding] more than just an elegant trick," said Dr. Rajiv Agarwal, a nephrologist at Indiana University School of Medicine who was not involved in the study. Unlike a protein that mimics only some of insulin's effects, RF-409 reproduces most of its normal signaling, "which matters in children who need growth as well as glucose control," he added.

Insulin (orange) plugs into the insulin receptor (blue) to change its shape and trigger signals inside cells. The new protein activates the receptor in a different way.

To test how RF-409 works with defective receptors, the researchers focused on a mutation previously identified in patients with Donohue syndrome; this genetic mutation alters a key part of the receptor's structure, preventing insulin from binding to it. Computer models suggested that RF-409 could bypass the affected region and hold the receptor in the shape needed to transmit signals inside cells.

The researchers genetically engineered mice to carry this mutation. Compared with mice that retained a functioning copy of the insulin receptor gene, these mice had, on average, roughly 2.5 times higher blood sugar and 10 times higher fasting insulin levels (the amount of insulin in blood after a period without food).

When injected with insulin, mice with defective receptors showed no substantial drop in blood sugar. Treatment with RF-409, however, lowered mice's blood sugar by roughly 60% within two hours.

"It is like designing a new key to open a defective lock," said Kalaiarasan Ponnusamy, a computational biologist at SRM Institute of Science and Technology in India who was not involved in the study. "From a computational protein-design perspective, this is a major advance because it demonstrates that entirely new proteins can be engineered to restore receptor function without correcting the underlying genetic mutation."

After 14 days of continuous treatment, five mice given RF-409 had lower blood sugar, maintained their body weight and retained more abdominal fat than five mice given a saline solution, for comparison. The treated mice also showed less pancreatic damage.

"Extending this approach to other disease-causing receptor mutations is a realistic possibility, provided the receptor retains its ability to transmit signals," Ponnusamy said. Some mutations, for instance, prevent insulin receptors from reaching the cell surface or transmitting signals inside cells at all. That means the technique demonstrated in the study is unlikely to work against such mutations.

Identifying which mutations can be treated in this way is now a priority for Choi. The team also plans to investigate how RF-409 affects different tissues in the body and whether it remains safe and effective over longer periods of treatment, she said.

Although Agarwal foresees several hurdles in bringing RF-409 into clinical use; for instance, the immune system's reaction to the protein will have to be carefully studied, and in general, it's challenging to run trials with infants with rare disorders. That said, he remains hopeful that it could help patients whose diseases have no treatments to treat the underlying cause.

"This is the first approach I have seen that addresses the actual defect rather than working around it," he said.
