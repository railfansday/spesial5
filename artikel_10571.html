Beyond The 10x Developer Myth: What To Look For When Hiring Engineers

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Damian is the cofounder of BEON.tech, a company that connects LATAM’s top-rated remote software developers with companies from the U.S.

One of the best problem solvers I’ve known was a college classmate. He could troubleshoot almost anything and produced more output than anyone. On paper, he was a 10x developer. But he struggled to communicate his results and coordinate with others.

That experience stayed with me when I moved from software development into tech hiring in 2018. Early on, my team and I screened candidates with algorithmic challenges. Over time, we learned that proactivity, flexibility and communication mattered as much as solving a hard problem.​

Speed has defined the 10x developer since 1968, when Harold Sackman, W. J. Erikson and E. E. Grant found gaps of 16 to 1 or more in coding time and over 25 to 1 in debugging among 12 experienced programmers. Today, it also covers judgment, AI fluency and team impact.​

The traditional definition focuses on individual output. A 10x engineer writes more code, solves problems faster, or delivers features with less supervision than an average developer.

The modern interpretation is broader. A 10x engineer isn’t necessarily someone who writes 10 times more code. It’s someone whose technical judgment, problem-solving ability and execution can have an outsized effect on the team’s results. That distinction matters because individual output doesn’t tell the whole story.

Consider a team that cuts development time from two weeks to three days but still spends two weeks testing, reviewing security and resolving integration issues. The coding improvement is real, but overall delivery has barely changed.

An engineer who produces twice as much code can also create more work for reviewers, introduce maintenance problems, or become the only person who understands a critical system. Their output may look exceptional while the team’s delivery remains unchanged. Therefore, the relevant measure is how an engineer’s work affects the team’s ability to deliver reliable software.

Commits, tickets closed and coding speed measure activity, not necessarily outcomes. They don’t show whether the software solves the right problem or creates additional work downstream.

AI coding tools make this distinction more important. A 2025 METR randomized controlled trial involving 16 experienced open-source developers found that participants took 19% longer to complete tasks using AI tools in familiar codebases. Despite this, they believed the tools had made them faster.

The study covered a specific group and set of tasks, so its findings shouldn’t be generalized to every workflow. It does illustrate how perceived speed can diverge from actual performance.

Hiring teams face the same risk when they focus on how quickly candidates solve interview problems without examining the quality and maintainability of their decisions.

Some engineers work efficiently alone but struggle to share knowledge, document decisions or incorporate feedback. They may deliver quickly while creating dependencies that slow the team down later.

This is particularly costly in senior roles, where engineers influence architecture, code quality and the development of others. If every difficult problem has to go through one person, the team struggles to scale.

The question is whether an engineer makes others more effective or simply handles more work themselves.

Traditional technical interviews emphasize algorithms, data structures and problem-solving under time pressure. These skills matter, but they don’t cover the decisions engineers make in production.

A candidate may solve a coding challenge quickly and still struggle with an unfamiliar codebase, changing requirements or architectural trade-offs.​

A better hiring framework looks at how engineers approach problems, deliver work and affect their teams.

• Technical judgment means making sound decisions with incomplete information. Strong engineers clarify requirements, weigh trade-offs and know when a simpler solution is better.

• Delivery means getting reliable work into production. Candidates should explain how they handle changing priorities and unexpected problems without sacrificing what matters most.

• Systems thinking means anticipating the consequences of technical decisions, including their effects on security, reliability, integration and maintenance.

• Communication helps teams make decisions and resolve problems. Engineers need to explain trade-offs, document important choices and raise risks early.

• Mentorship and knowledge sharing determine whether an engineer’s experience benefits the wider team. Useful code reviews, documentation and support for colleagues reduce dependencies on individual experts.

• Sustainable performance means delivering consistently without relying on constant overtime or personal heroics. Shared knowledge and maintainable code help teams avoid depending on one person.

The balance depends on the role. A staff engineer may need stronger architectural judgment and mentoring skills, while a senior individual contributor may be assessed more heavily on delivery and technical execution.

• Structured behavioral interviews. Ask candidates to describe a technical decision that went wrong, how they responded and what they changed afterward. Specific examples reveal more than broad claims about their strengths.

• Work samples. Ask candidates to explain their design choices, identify potential failure points and describe how they’d maintain the system. Keep exercises relevant to the role and reasonably scoped.

• Reference checks help establish whether those behaviors carry over into a real team. Ask former managers whether the engineer improved the team’s capabilities and former colleagues how they handled code reviews, shared ownership and difficult technical decisions.

Combining these methods gives hiring teams more evidence than relying on a single interview or coding score.

The 10x developer concept started with measurable differences in individual performance. The mistake is treating those differences as a complete hiring strategy.

AI makes that mistake more expensive. Solving complex problems is becoming a commodity, and engineers are turning into orchestrators of agents. We now look at how candidates design AI workflows and test what the AI produces, and whether they’re curious, proactive and close enough to the business to own a feature from requirements to production.

Dedication is indispensable, but it doesn’t make an impact on its own. In the end, great results are built by teams.
