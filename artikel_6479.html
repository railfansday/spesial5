Grand Canyon hotels reopen 81 days after a deadly flood, echoing a 1966 disaster

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

Grand Canyon's South Rim hotels reopen November 20 after an August flash flood cut off their water. In 1966, a flood wrecked 40% of the same pipeline project.

For the first time since the end of August, visitors can book a hotel room on the South Rim of the Grand Canyon again.

On Friday, November 20, the South Rim’s hotels reopened to overnight guests, including El Tovar, which has stood on the edge of the canyon since 1905.

They had been closed for 81 days. A flash flood on August 29 tore through Bright Angel Canyon, killed two hikers and badly damaged the pipeline that carries water to the South Rim.

It was not the first time. In 1966, while that same pipeline was being built, a flood destroyed or carried off about 40% of the project, according to the National Park Service.

The park announced on November 18 that six South Rim lodges would reopen on November 20: El Tovar Hotel, Thunderbird Lodge, Kachina Lodge, Bright Angel Lodge and Maswik Lodge, along with Yavapai Lodge, which is operated by Delaware North.

On the same day, the park moved from Stage 4 to Stage 3 water conservation measures, which it called an important milestone in the restoration of South Rim water services.

Food and drink services will resume gradually, and the park is asking visitors to check its website for dining hours.

Conserving water is still important. Guests, visitors and residents are asked to take shorter showers, turn off faucets when they are not in use and avoid unnecessary water use.

The flash flood hit Bright Angel Canyon and the Phantom Ranch area at about 2:30 p.m. on August 29. By the next morning, 62 people had been evacuated from Phantom Ranch and the lower North Kaibab Trail.

Nearly all of the footbridges over Bright Angel Creek were destroyed.

Metal structures and debris washed into the Colorado River, which was closed to river traffic. In steep terrain, heavy rain can trigger flash floods in mountain rivers faster than expected.

Two hikers from Granbury, Texas, John Giusti, 46, and Brent Rosenkranz, 42, died in the flood.

A member of their hiking group was reported missing, and the park said in its September 8 update that one person remained unaccounted for.

The flood badly damaged the Transcanyon Waterline, a 12½-mile pipeline built in the 1960s.

It carries water from Roaring Springs, on the North Rim side, down through the canyon and up to the South Rim, where it supplies drinking water and fire protection for every facility, including more than 800 historic buildings.

The park’s drinking water comes from a single spring connected to more than 6 miles of caves.

About 40% of the old pipeline between the Phantom Ranch area and the North Rim was damaged, and the water intakes at Bright Angel Creek and Roaring Springs were hit hard.

On August 31, the park stopped all overnight stays at its hotels, including Phantom Ranch, and the South Rim began living on stored water.

The park stayed open for day visits. When it announced the closure, it said South Rim food and drink services, the Grand Canyon Clinic and the post office would remain open, some with reduced hours, while campers could only dry camp.

On September 16, a second flash flood damaged a temporary water intake that crews were building at Bright Angel Creek.

No one was hurt, but crews had to work out what repairs were needed and how the damage would affect the timeline.

On September 19, the park said it expected to start pumping water from the creek to a new treatment plant by November 16, and it was working on an agreement to haul about 200,000 gallons of water a day to keep its tanks from running low.

At the time, the South Rim was using about 137,000 gallons a day.

By November 18, the park said water services to the South Rim were being restored, which allowed the hotels to reopen two days later.

The pipeline itself was born out of a water problem. From 1901 to 1932, the Santa Fe railroad brought drinking water to the South Rim by train every day, and the trains came back as needed until the late 1960s.

In the summer of 1961, a water train of 20 cars carried about 204,000 gallons.

Construction of the Transcanyon Waterline began in January 1965, using 6-inch and 8-inch aluminum pipe. Most of the equipment was designed specifically for the project, and the rest of the work was done by hand.

A year later, the project was nearly finished when what the park calls a 1,000-year flood struck.

Fifteen inches of rain fell on the inner canyon, and the water carried away trees, boulders and sections of new pipe.

A survey after the flood found that about 40% of the project was destroyed or missing. The cost rose from $2 million to $5 million, and construction resumed in late 1967.

The flood’s high-water mark is still recorded on the walls of the Phantom Ranch Ranger Station.

The old line was built to last about 30 years and has been in service for more than 50. Since 2010, it has suffered over 85 major breaks.

A single repair often costs more than $25,000, and crews can reach the inner canyon only by trail or helicopter.

Breaks had closed the hotels before. In December 2025, after three significant breaks since late October, the park suspended overnight stays on December 6 and reopened the lodges on December 17.

In 2023, the park began a $208 million project to replace the pipeline and modernize the water system.

The new pipeline on the south side of the canyon was not damaged by either flood this year, and before August 29 the park was just over a month away from starting to use it.

Some inner canyon trails reopened in late October, including the South Kaibab Trail below the Tipoff and the River Trail.

But the Black Bridge, the Silver Bridge, Phantom Ranch and the entire North Kaibab Trail remained closed, and the park warned that water would stay off at Mile-and-a-Half and Three-Mile Resthouses and at Havasupai Gardens for the rest of the winter.

Hikers must carry all their water and a way to filter or treat it.

El Tovar, a National Historic Landmark since 1987, was built from local limestone and Oregon pine for nearly $250,000. The hotel, which has 78 rooms, is once again taking guests.

The park says water conservation remains important as operations resume, and that the patience of residents, visitors and businesses during the emergency helped it keep essential services running.
