11 Standout Projects from Dutch Design Week 2026

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

In its 26th edition, Dutch Design Week brought together more than 2,500 designers, from established names to emerging talent, alongside creative collaborations with major brands. This year’s theme, Rebel Authentic Worth (or R.A.W.), highlighted the social, cultural, and economic value of “design that creates space for experimentation, material research, and radical imagination.”

The theme placed particular emphasis on the unpolished and experimental at a time when technology and AI increasingly prioritize optimization and visual perfection. It also reflected a growing sense of uncertainty around technological advancement, exploring how design might challenge these developments, offer alternative perspectives, or help us navigate an increasingly complex world.

Here, we highlight 11 projects that stood out at Dutch Design Week 2026.

Design Academy Eindhoven’s annual Graduation Show offered a wide-ranging look at the next generation of design talent, with projects spanning objects, installations, and experimental research. Themes ran the gamut, including how change can bring about both gains and unintended consequences. Tradition can sustain a sense of belonging while carrying colonial or nationalist histories, for example, while emerging professions create new possibilities without necessarily guaranteeing a livelihood.

‘Safe House’ graduation project by Dirk Duif. Photography by Ronald Smits.

“This year’s graduation projects can be read as maps of how graduates have navigated contradictions and established their bearings: what to attend to, what to carry forward, what to stand for and which relationships might sustain their work,” says Nadine Botha, head of the Social Design Master’s program at DAE.

‘Excess Cornice’ graduate project by Yann Thiel. Photography by Femke Reijerman.

The exhibition’s examination of institutional systems was particularly timely. Graduating DAE students recently protested conditions at the school, citing inadequate studio space and limited facilities for making their work.

Large-scale installations in public spaces allow visitors to encounter and interact with design as they move through Eindhoven during Dutch Design Week. One of them, called Blind Spots, asks, “Who is safety built for?”

An AI illustration of ‘Creatives Blind Spots’ by Fairspace × Shruthi Venkat × Sagitta. Rendering courtesy of Fairspace.

Created by Fairspace, Shruthi Venkat, and Sagitta Creatives, the installation used familiar symbols of security—including surveillance cameras, screens, and construction netting—to explore the relationship between protection, surveillance, and control. Participants could wander through overlapping layers of mesh that obscured sightlines, while recorded voices offered different perspectives on what it means to feel safe.

Rendering and photography courtesy of Fairspace

Visitors were also invited to write down their experiences and tie them to the nets for others to read. As the contributions accumulated, the installation gradually became a collective record of how differently people experience safety in public spaces.

In Woven Landscapes, artist and material researcher Ori Orisun Merhav presented what she called a dialogue between humans, land, and other-than-human life, with an installation of sculptural forms combining woven palm fibers and insect-produced resin. The organic structures explored how materials shaped by living organisms might influence the way objects are designed and made.

Merhav is the founder of Made by Insects, an ongoing research practice investigating lac, a natural resin secreted by certain insects, and its potential as a material for design.

Photography courtesy of Ori Orisun Merhav

“The main practice of the studio is research,” Merhav says. “The sculptures are the leftovers, segments of the time and journey of our research.” Through weaving, binding, and coating, the resulting vessels connect traditional craft techniques with biological processes, suggesting a more collaborative relationship between human-made objects and the natural world.

Yinka Ilori is a British-Nigerian multidisciplinary artist and designer whose practice spans architecture, sculpture, furniture, and interior and graphic design. Known for his exuberant use of color and pattern, he brought that same approach to everyday household objects in a collaboration with Dutch housewares brand Brabantia.

For the limited-edition There Is Magic in Joy collection, Ilori sought to make daily chores more pleasurable by transforming practical items, including ironing board covers and waste bins, into playful design objects. The palette is sherbet-like orange, lemon yellow, and raspberry, with aqua accents and bold graphic patterns that give otherwise mundane products an unexpected personality.

Yinka Ilori photographed with the ‘There Is Magic in Joy’ collection. Photography courtesy of Brabantia.

“At the heart of my work is a belief that art and design should be accessible to all, with projects that bring communities together and create a positive social impact,” Ilori says.

Prefabricated housing is often associated with standardized designs, but Eindhoven-based TAM Industries is exploring how 3D printing could offer greater architectural freedom. Its House of Lights installation demonstrated how automated construction can accommodate more expressive forms without abandoning the efficiencies of industrial production.

Printed from recycled plastic using machines developed by TAM, the structure incorporated color directly into the material, eliminating the need for paint. Integrated lighting illuminated the printed facade after dark, transforming the house’s appearance.

The project also explored the potential for reusing its materials at the end of the structure’s life. Rather than treating automation as a constraint on creativity, TAM sees it as a way to make more varied architectural designs possible.

The idea that we live in uncertain times—and how design can provide spaces for reflection and reassurance—was pervasive at the festival. Beacons of Hope by the Amsterdam-based design duo Rive Roshan (Ruben de la Rive Box and Golnar Roshan) responded to this climate with an immersive installation combining textured glass, reflective surfaces, and kinetic light.

The duo treats light as a primary material, using glass and metal surfaces to shape its movement and create shifting patterns of reflection. The installation was designed to offer visitors a moment of pause, connection, and optimism.

“We believe design has a responsibility to foster connection and offer sanctuary,” the designers say. “By choreographing kinetic light, we tap into something universal and deeply emotional, offering a counter-narrative to the heaviness of the daily news cycle and the weight on our emotional well-being.”

“Ever since I can remember, I have been curious about how things work,” artist Gijs Schalkx says. That curiosity has led him to build an unconventional collection of vehicles, including a motorcycle powered by swamp gas, a car that runs on waste plastic, and an electric flying bicycle.

Presented as Onderweg naar een toekomst (On the Way to a Future), the functioning vehicles question our assumptions about efficiency, progress, and sustainability. “Technology is often presented as infallible, fast and efficient. I feel that most people take it in as passive consumers, without any awareness of the processes and infrastructures behind it,” he says.

Rather than rejecting technology, Schalkx uses these unusual inventions to expose the systems and resources behind it. “I try not to reject it, but to rearrange it,” he says. “The vehicles really work, but they fall just outside the logic of usefulness. They show our dependence on technology and infrastructure in our daily lives while also showing possibilities we have not yet imagined.”

Why do certain objects become irreplaceable, even when they have little monetary value? The DIRT’s exhibition The Ashtray, the Cupboard, and the Leather Boots emerged from conversations with three women about memories connected to favorite possessions—ordinary items transformed by personal histories into something treasured. The Dutch Institute of Royal Taste, aka The DIRT, was founded by creative duo Florine van Rees and Jeroen Dijkstra, who produce curated exhibitions and scenography.

“We form deep attachments to things and come to treasure them, often for reasons that have little to do with their material, value, or purpose,” the two designers say. At a time when so many products are designed to be replaced, the exhibition considered what makes certain objects worth keeping.

Recordings of the original conversations accompany the installation, inviting visitors to listen to the voices behind the selected objects. Three designers then interpreted those stories through new works exploring the relationship between everyday objects, memory, and identity.

‘Ritual Remains’ by Stina Henriksson. Photography courtesy of the artist.

In Ritual Remains, Stina Henriksson explored the rituals associated with fire, drawing connections between lighting a cigarette and lighting a candle. Both acts can evoke gathering, celebration, or contemplation, while leaving physical traces of what has burned. Her work considers how an ordinary object can become a vessel for memory and ritual.

‘Slaapzak kast (Sleeping bag cupboard)’ by Lotte Raida. Photography courtesy of the artist.

Lotte Raida’s Slaapzak Kast (Sleeping Bag Cupboard) draws inspiration from memories of camping holidays, reimagining a familiar piece of storage furniture through the form of a sleeping bag. The piece explores how furniture can carry personal associations beyond its practical function, connecting everyday storage with memories of comfort and time spent outdoors.

‘Make Me Fit’ by Astrid Vagner. Photography courtesy of the artist.

Astrid Vagner’s Make Me Fit examines the relationship between footwear, femininity, and the expectations placed on women’s bodies. “Growing up with body parts that didn’t fit into anything girly, particularly reflected in the limited selection of feminine shoes. I started to patch myself up in feminine attributes,” Vagner explains.

Through the familiar form of the leather boot, the work questions how clothing and accessories can shape our understanding of identity and belonging.

‘Weaving Life Stories of Resilient Women’ by Phlox van Oppen. Photography courtesy of the Swedish School of Textiles.

Students from the Swedish School of Textiles pushed beyond conventional ideas of fabric with Exit26, an exhibition exploring the intersection of craft, technology, color, and materiality. The works blurred the boundaries between textiles, sculpture, and wearable design, challenging distinctions between the physical and digital, the sensual and practical.

‘The Jeweld Hand’ by Viola Schmidt. Photography courtesy of the Swedish School of Textiles.

Highlights included Viola Schmidt’s The Jeweld Hand, which featured threads trailing from an elbow-length suede glove, obscuring the line between accessory and textile sculpture. Phlox van Oppen’s Weaving Life Stories of Resilient Women used multilayered jacquard weaving to explore women’s experiences of resilience and transformation.

“My work is rooted in an ongoing fascination with how textiles can carry memory, biography, and transformation,” van Oppen says.

With its rounded, pill-shaped drawers and playful proportions, Picche brings an unexpected sense of personality to a familiar piece of furniture. Handmade in India, the chest of drawers by Arunika Sarkar of Atelier Arunika draws on Postmodern design and the carefully composed visual world of filmmaker Wes Anderson.

“At a time when design is increasingly shaped by efficiency and standardization, Picche explores the value of individuality, play and emotional connection,” Sarkar says.

Challenging the idea that functional objects need to be purely utilitarian, the piece balances sculptural expression with everyday storage. “I’m interested in creating furniture that feels almost like a character—an object with its own personality, presence and story, rather than simply something that occupies a space,” she says.

As digital technologies become increasingly integrated into our personal lives, the boundaries between connection, convenience, and surveillance are becoming harder to define. Code 2026: Terms of Intimacy, presented by Impakt – Centre for Media Culture, explored these tensions through seven works examining how technology collects and uses our voices, attention, and bodily data.

‘The Affirmation Machine’ by Simon Pillaud was part of ‘Code 2026: Terms of Intimacy.’ Photography courtesy of Impakt.

Together, the works asked whether technologies that promise greater intimacy and connection might also be quietly reshaping the people who use them.
