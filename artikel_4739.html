Did Darwin Kill the Meaning of Life?

Posted November 20, 2026 | Reviewed by Tyler Woods

When asked in school what the meaning of life could possibly be, my friends and I answered "reproduction," seeing this as the natural conclusion of the truth of evolution. As faulty as this view of me and my friends was, it was based on the view that the evolutionary "purpose" of life is essentially reproduction. This, however, is usually not what is asked when faced with the question of what the meaning of life is, or rather what makes life meaningful. What is asked for, though, is some kind of higher, eternal purpose for humans. This is why I've recently published an academic article on the topic.

The strongest argument for existential nihilism has been provided by the naturalist philosopher Daniel Dennett in Darwin’s Dangerous Idea: Evolution and the Meanings of Life. Evolution as a random and blind mechanism involving nothing beyond reproduction, variation, and differential reproductive success is the fundamental law of biology. Natural selection explains the origin of life and man, without any reference to purpose.

However, and from here existentialists get their name, evolution denies these kinds of essences that have "plagued" philosophy since Plato. Everything is in constant change under the blind and random mechanism of evolution. There is no essence, such as rationality or moral sense, that we could attribute to human nature. When Sartre famously remarked that “existence precedes essence,” he meant that it is left for humans themselves to decide who they are.

To sum up: Evolution destroys all notions of teleology in the biological realm. Sommers and Rosenberg, in their review paper of Dennett with the fitting title “Darwin’s nihilistic idea: evolution and the meaninglessness of life,” phrase the conclusions of the Darwinian argument for existential nihilism in the following way:

Darwinism thus puts the capstone on a process which since Newton’s time has driven teleology to the explanatory side-lines. In short, it has made Darwinians into metaphysical Nihilists denying that there is any meaning or purpose to the universe, its contents and its cosmic history.

The theory of evolution dissolves all purposes in, as Dennett puts it, “Darwin's universal acid”: science, therefore, renders God oblivious and takes away the illusion of purpose from our lives. Dennett even goes so far as to attribute the origin of existentialism to Darwin:

Friedrich Nietzsche saw—through the mists of his contempt for all things English—an even more cosmic message in Darwin: God is dead. If Nietzsche is the father of existentialism, then perhaps Darwin deserves the title of grandfather.

When Nietzsche proclaimed that “God is dead,” we are supposed to view science, if not as the murderer then at least as the tool that was used to kill God. While Nietzsche is often taken as a deterrent to where Darwinian philosophy leads, I argue that Nietzsche’s Darwinism is better associated with his ideas about the death of God than with the Übermensch. Even though Nietzsche is often given as an example of a nihilist, most of his work is directed against the destructive consequences of nihilism, once God has been replaced by science, or more accurately, where science left a hole after getting rid of God.

Especially after the horrors of WW2, it is only reasonable to ask how anyone could continue to believe that there is a God or purpose to our lives. Much of existential thinking boils down to the fact, that there is no God and hence, no one to give the universe purpose and meaning to our lives.

Thomas Nagel offers a thought experiment in which humanity realizes that the human race and our whole planet were created by some creatures to farm and eat human flesh. Clearly, he argues, this doesn’t give us the purpose we ask for when asking for the meaning of life. Would the analogy change if it was God who created us for some purpose rather than a powerful alien race? Camus suggests that this may not work either: “I know that I do not know that meaning and that it is impossible for me just now to know it. What can a meaning outside my condition mean to me? I can understand only in human terms.” Even if God exists and has some purpose for us in mind, we cannot grasp it.
