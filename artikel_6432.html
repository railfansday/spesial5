Deep inside a Spanish cave, archaeologists found at least 120 Latin inscriptions, and five of them honor a goddess called ‘the Lady’

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

At least 120 Latin inscriptions in Cova Dones, Spain, name a goddess called Domina, the Lady, and point to women taking an active part in her cult.

One chamber of Cova Dones, a cave in Millares in Valencia province, now holds at least 120 Latin inscriptions. Teams from the universities of Alicante and Zaragoza recorded them during fieldwork in May and June 2026.

When the room was first explored in 2024, about 15 had been logged.

Five of the texts are vows to Domina, “the Lady,” a goddess the visitors addressed with reverence. Several of the names read so far are women’s. The team calls the results preliminary, and many texts are still being read.

The researchers call the room the Roman Sanctuary. It sits more than 650 feet (200 meters) from the entrance, according to the University of Alicante.

Most of the texts are believed to date to the 1st and 2nd centuries AD, and they cover the walls and the ceiling. Small natural pools on the floor, known as gours, catch water from the galleries, and visitors dropped offerings into them.

That first campaign also yielded a coin from the reign of Emperor Claudius. Someone had wedged it as an offering between a fissure and a stalactite in the ceiling, and in a team photo Xabier Garín Artázcoz points at the crack. Trinidad Pasíes Oviedo of the Valencia Museum of Prehistory recovered and conserved the coin.

The January 2025 announcement from the University of Alicante placed the room among the deepest known Roman inscription sites in Iberia, second only to Cueva del Puente in Burgos and possibly Cueva de Román de Clunia.

This year’s readings were done by two epigraphy specialists. Silvia Alfayé teaches ancient history at the University of Zaragoza, and Sergio García-Dils lectures at UNED in Seville. García-Dils also directs the Spanish School of Speleology.

According to Alfayé, five votive texts show that women “played an active role in her cult.” Three of them carry the word MALACI. In a release dated July 27, the University of Alicante called it an unpublished epithet for a goddess not known until now.

García-Dils noted that the texts are packed into one marked-off part of the cave, well away from the entrance. To him that “strongly suggests the existence of a well-organised and structured cult” with a named deity, not scattered visits.

The University of Southampton, where Aitor Ruiz-Redondo is also based, put out the English release on October 20. It says early readings suggest some women wrote their vows themselves, without a go-between.

Until now, the Roman cave sanctuary with the largest known set of inscriptions was La Griega, with 107. One of them names a woman.

The 2026 team logged objects on the chamber floor, among them two bronze rings found under the inscribed rock. There were two bronze rings, a bracelet, chalice-shaped cups, spindle whorls, bits of pottery and animal bones.

The whorls came up in numbers. Virginia Barciela co-leads the project with Ruiz-Redondo and teaches prehistory at Alicante. She said the whorls probably point to offerings of spindles, a textile tool tied to women in antiquity.

She also brought up the first written name of the site, Cova de les Dones, meaning Cave of the Women. That name may hold “the fossilised memory of a place long associated with women,” she said, or with female spirits.

Alfayé said the goddess’s title may have to do with water. Water shrines existed elsewhere in the ancient world, such as a circular water shrine at Pelusium in northern Sinai.

The inscriptions are extremely difficult to see with the naked eye. So the team photographed the surfaces for high-resolution photogrammetric models and read the texts on those.

That work turned up inscriptions no one had seen. It also split apart lines that earlier readers had taken as one text.

Jorge Angás and Carlos Valladares, from the IPH institute in Zaragoza and the DigHER project, ran the geomatics side. Javier Velaza Frías, a Latin professor in Barcelona, leads the study of the Roman inscriptions. Víctor Sabaté Vidal of the University of Valencia has also contributed to the project.

Latin wishes turn up on other Roman objects too, for instance openwork glass cups carved with drinking wishes in Greek or Latin.

In a Roman underground sanctuary, finding texts and offerings together in place is exceptionally rare, says the Southampton release.

Ruiz-Redondo, Barciela and archaeologist Ximo Martorell published the first results in 2023. In the journal Antiquity they described over 100 Paleolithic paintings and engravings. Some are older than 24,000 years.

Pottery from the Iron Age had already led archaeologists in the 1960s to class it as an Iberian sanctuary. In 2025 the team logged more than 100 stalagmites that people had modified on purpose.

For that kind of find, the University of Alicante ranks the cave second in the world, after Saint-Marcel in France.

Ruiz-Redondo said people may have kept returning for “its permanent water supply,” its landscape or its easy access.

At La Pasiega, Maltravieso and Ardales, red marks have been dated to more than 64,000 years ago.

Alfayé and her colleagues are still working on the goddess’s title. For now the team has not said who Domina was or what MALACI means. Many inscriptions are still being read, and much of the cave is unstudied, so 120 is a minimum.

The DONARQ project started in 2023 with money from the Generalitat Valenciana and the British Academy. In September 2026 the sanctuary was shortlisted for a National Geographic +Historia readers’ prize. Public voting closed on October 12.

This year the regional Pla Restaura program and the Millares town council are paying for more work. The money goes to the cave’s access and entrance, safety measures, a digital twin and a catalog of the Paleolithic images. The company Arquivolta Patrimoni i Societat is coordinating it, and virtual visits should follow.

Among those offerings, two bronze rings sat on the surface right below the inscribed rock.

The information was obtained from the University of Southampton , the University of Alicante and the University of Zaragoza.
