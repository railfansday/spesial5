Scientists Discover Mysterious Toxic Chemicals Floating in the Air Above America

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Scientists conducting atmospheric research in rural Oklahoma unexpectedly detected a toxic class of industrial chemicals never before measured in the air of the Western Hemisphere.

Toxic industrial chemicals used in everything from metalworking fluids to plastics have turned up in an unexpected place: the air above rural Oklahoma farmland.

Researchers detected medium-chain chlorinated paraffins (MCCPs) in the region while investigating an entirely different atmospheric process, marking the first reported detection of these pollutants in North American air.

The University of Colorado Boulder researchers identified the pollutants during a 2023 field campaign near Lamont, Oklahoma. Their findings, published in ACS Environmental Au, also reveal how atmospheric MCCP concentrations fluctuate with temperature.

Using a nitrate chemical ionization mass spectrometer, the team continuously monitored air at an agricultural site for nearly a month. While examining the chemical signatures in the measurements, CU Boulder chemistry PhD student Daniel Katz noticed unusual isotopic patterns that he eventually identified as MCCPs.

“It’s very exciting as a scientist to find something unexpected like this that we weren’t looking for,” said Katz, the study’s lead author. “We’re starting to learn more about this toxic, organic pollutant that we know is out there, and which we need to understand better.”

The instrument detected 18 groups of gaseous MCCPs. Their estimated average concentration was approximately 3.1 nanograms per cubic meter, although the researchers consider this a lower-bound estimate. Concentrations were substantially higher than those reported in remote polar regions, suggesting nearby sources may be contributing to the pollution.

The researchers also observed a consistent daily cycle: gaseous MCCP concentrations increased during warmer daylight hours and declined at night. Their analysis suggests that temperature controls how these chemicals move between the gas phase and airborne particles, potentially affecting how long they remain in the atmosphere and how far they travel.

MCCPs are widely used in metalworking fluids, PVC manufacturing, and textiles. They can enter wastewater and accumulate in sewage sludge, which is sometimes processed into biosolid fertilizer and spread on agricultural fields. Researchers suspect this practice could explain some of the pollution detected in Oklahoma, although they have not confirmed its source.

“When sewage sludges are spread across the fields, those toxic compounds could be released into the air,” Katz said. “We can’t show directly that that’s happening, but we think it’s a reasonable way that they could be winding up in the air. Sewage sludge fertilizers have been shown to release similar compounds.”

MCCPs are chemically related to short-chain chlorinated paraffins (SCCPs), which have faced restrictions because of their toxicity, environmental persistence, and potential to accumulate in living organisms. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency began regulating SCCPs in 2009, and the Stockholm Convention added them to its list of persistent organic pollutants in 2017.

Researchers suspect restrictions on SCCPs may have encouraged greater use of MCCPs as substitutes, potentially increasing their environmental presence.

“We always have these unintended consequences of regulation, where you regulate something, and then there’s still a need for the products that those were in,” said Ellie Browne, CU Boulder chemistry professor, CIRES Fellow, and study co-author. “So they get replaced by something.”

Although the team identified MCCPs in Oklahoma’s air, their exact source remains unknown. Wind measurements suggest several possible sources in the surrounding region, while the observed temperature fluctuations indicate that interactions with airborne particles influence their concentrations. The researchers also detected compounds consistent with products formed when MCCPs undergo atmospheric oxidation.

Further measurements across different seasons and locations could establish how widespread these chemicals are and clarify their atmospheric transport, chemical reactions, and environmental persistence.

“We identified them, but we still don’t know exactly what they do when they are in the atmosphere, and they need to be investigated further,” Katz said. “I think it’s important that we continue to have governmental agencies that are capable of evaluating the science and regulating these chemicals as necessary for public health and safety.”

Reference: “Real-Time Measurements of Gas-Phase Medium-Chain Chlorinated Paraffins Reveal Daily Changes in Gas-Particle Partitioning Controlled by Ambient Temperature” by Daniel John Katz, Bri Dobson, Mitchell Alton, Harald Stark, Douglas R. Worsnop, Manjula R. Canagaratna and Eleanor C. Browne, 16 July 2025, ACS Environmental Au. DOI: 10.1021/acsenvironau.5c00038
