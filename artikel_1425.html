How Amorim picked apart Alvini’s gung-ho approach: Tactical analysis of AC Milan 5-1 Frosinone

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

AC Milan put on arguably their best display of the Ruben Amorim era so far as they beat Frosinone 5-1 at San Siro on Sunday.

Gabriele Calvani’s early own-goal kicked off what would be a miserable night for the Canarini, before Goncalo Ramos broke his drought after starting and finishing a counter-attack. Patrizio Masini’s goal halved the deficit, and kept the game open going into the second half.

From there, though, the Rossoneri ran riot. Ardon Jashari and Alphadjo Cisse both scored within minutes of coming off the bench, finishing counter-attacks, then Christian Pulisic added the finishing touch to make it a five-star night.

It was a win that Milan needed to move back into the top four, also given that only Inter out of the top six sides in the table dropped points. Rohit Rajeev has the tactical breakdown from the win.

Ruben Amorim made just one change to the starting line-up from the side that won against Genoa before the break, with Alphadjo Cisse dropping out and Davide Bartesaghi coming in. It was uncertain from the line-up named what the system would be, as has often been the case with the Portuguese coach so far.

Milan once again deployed a modified version of their 3-4-2-1, with Bartesaghi operating on the left. Alvini, meanwhile, had to cope without his striker Raimondo and opted for a 3-5-2, pairing Ghedjemis and Kvernadze up front.

Frosinone made their intentions clear from the opening seconds. Goalkeeper Palmisani immediately played a direct ball into a targeted zone, with Frosinone committing several players towards the expected landing area to create a numerical advantage and win the aerial duel.

Milan built from deep with three players at the back, while Maignan pushed forward to act as an additional centre-back. This created a 4v3 in the first line of build-up, forcing Frosinone’s front three of Ghedjemis, Kvernadze and Schmid to press deeper.

However, it was Rabiot’s movement that proved decisive in creating the overload for Milan’s opening goal. With Frosinone committing three forwards to the press and keeping two players wide, only two midfielders and three defenders remained to deal with Milan’s build-up.

Although Musah and Rabiot were initially marked, Rabiot dropped deeper into midfield to create a numerical advantage and free himself to receive possession.

This movement directly contributed to Milan’s opening goal. Rabiot dropped between the lines to receive possession, forcing Cittadini to step out of his defensive position and follow him.

Rabiot then laid the ball off to Modric, who immediately played it into the space vacated by Cittadini. Under pressure, Frosinone inadvertently turned the ball into their own net to give Milan a 1-0 lead.

Frosinone looked to commit numbers into Milan’s half, creating ball-sided overloads to progress through the pitch. Their two wingers maintained wide positions to stretch Milan’s wing-backs and create more space in the central areas.

Out of possession, Frosinone adopted a 3-4-3 to match Milan’s 3-4-2-1 in possession. They also maintained a very high defensive line, looking to press Milan aggressively and win the ball back as high up the pitch as possible.

Milan allowed Frosinone to dictate possession, dropping into a deeper defensive block to invite them into their half. Once Frosinone advanced, Milan looked to win the ball back and launch quick counter-attacks.

Frosinone’s lack of a clear attacking structure and, more importantly, an organised rest defence left them vulnerable in transition. Milan exploited this disorganisation repeatedly, with the next four goals all benefiting from the spaces Frosinone left behind.

The second goal perfectly illustrated Milan’s approach in transition. Frosinone had committed ten players into Milan’s half, leaving very little defensive cover behind.

Ramos won possession, and Musah immediately played a vertical pass to release Moreira through on goal. Moreira and Rabiot then drew three recovering defenders towards them before creating the space for Ramos to finish.

Frosinone’s high-risk approach briefly paid off with Masini’s goal. They drew Milan into central areas before pressing aggressively to recover possession. With their players positioned close together, they were able to combine quickly and progress the ball towards Kvernadze.

A deflection off De Winter then took the ball into Ghedjemis’ path, allowing him to set up Masini for the finish.

Frosinone consistently targeted Milan’s left flank, particularly the space around Bartesaghi. The individual duel between Ghedjemis and Bartesaghi became a key feature of the game.

Ghedjemis repeatedly beat his marker in 1v1 situations and posed a threat. Recognising the problem, Amorim took Bartesaghi off at half-time.

After the break, Jashari and Pulisic began making coordinated counter-movements. Pulisic dropped deeper, drawing his marker away and creating space in the wide attacking area, which Jashari then exploited with an advancing run.

The goal was very similar to the movement Jashari made against Venezia, highlighting a recurring pattern in Milan’s attacking play.

Both Cisse’s and Pulisic’s goals came from a counter-attack following a Frosinone corner, for which they did not leave many men back.

For Cisse’s finish, his body orientation suggested he was going to shoot towards the goalkeeper’s right, prompting the keeper to adjust his stance accordingly. Cisse then went the other way, finding the opposite corner.

We wrote something during the break about how Milan are over-performing relative to certain metrics. Above all, their actual goals scored was/is well above their Expected Goals, helped by the fact four players are converting an abnormally high percentage of chances.

With this in mind, the stats make for interesting reading. Milan edged possession with 54%, generating 2.50 Expected Goals, so they again out-performed what they created. And yet, the Rossoneri racked up five big chances which matches the number of goals scored.

So, perhaps everything can be taken with a pinch of whatever type of salt fits your narrative. Many would argue that Milan could have scored more given their brilliant on Sunday, yet xG would have you believe it was a ‘lucky’ margin in a game that should have been 2/3-1.

The visitors also racked up nine corners, the most Milan have conceded all season, but thankfully Amorim’s side managed to deal with them all relatively well.
