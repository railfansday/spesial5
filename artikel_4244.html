10 Sega Genesis JRPGs Most Haven’t Played, But Definitely Should

When you think of the JRPG subgenre, you think of PlayStation or the SNES. I can’t refute that Sony and Nintendo earned their stake in that video game niche as a big Kingdom Hearts and Final Fantasy 7 fan. But there’s one other console that had an expansive library of titles in that area.

Even though it was most known for its war against Nintendo and the start of Sonic the Hedgehog’s 35-year-long running streak, the Sega Genesis had a number of good JRPGs on it over the years. Games like Phantasy Star and Landstalker have been widely noticed, but most of them are only recognized by die-hard Sega fans.

Sega's 16-bit console era has left its mark on the video game industry in ways we still see today.

As a big Sega fan myself, (to a point,) I’m here to help navigate you through some Sega JRPGs you’ve most likely left behind or just weren’t around to experience. Don’t worry, we’ve got plenty to dig into.

Back in the day, and even now, Crusader of Centy has been seen as Sega’s The Legend of Zelda clone. In truth, it has a few elements that help separate it from Link’s adventures, but the biggest is that it was actually made as part of a big Genesis-based RPG push in Japan. We’ll be talking about more of these games as we go.

Out of all the games released through this initiative, I feel like Crusader of Centy is the most well-known. But out of the people who know about it, there’s a stark divide. Retro JRPG fans have likely played it, but how many Sega fans outside of that have actually experienced it? The only thing you might know about it is the fact that Sonic’s in it. Yeah. I said it.

I believe it’s a game really worth playing. It harkens back to an era where RPG and adventure game mechanics weren’t so strictly divided. Crusader of Centy also focuses pretty heavily on exploration and discovery, namely in the way that animal companions give you abilities you only get through mixing them up. There’s a lot here for almost everyone.

I know, I know, calling Beyond Oasis a JRPG is a little controversial. Its top-down, open combat and varying challenges make it hard to call it much of anything. That’s the trouble with these games sometimes. Genres were hardly the big defining points of a game as they are now, and a lot of games got to experiment with what they offered.

In the case of Beyond Oasis, if it has story beats like an RPG, has magic you can level up like an RPG no matter how basic, and has rewards for exploring through the map like an RPG, then it’s an RPG. Even if you disagree, I’m sure you agree that more people need to play this game. It’s one of the few Sega Genesis games that play this way, and it’s gorgeous to look at on top of it. If you haven’t played Beyond Oasis, I highly recommend it.

You’ve likely heard of the Shining Force series if you’re familiar with retro JRPGs. It’s one of the most highly regarded options for the genre that the Sega Genesis had, coming second to Phantasy Star itself. The first two games are on Nintendo Switch Online’s Classic offerings, even. But because of a number of different factors, like its name or its formula changes, Shining in the Darkness isn’t mentioned a lot because it hasn’t been played a lot.

For those not in the know, many of the Shining Force games are tactical RPGs. Shining in the Darkness is the first game in the series, and it was a dungeon crawler instead. It’s a very classic dungeon crawler, too, resembling a strange combination between Dragon Quest’s presentation and Wizardry’s systems.

It very much feels like a Shining game through and through, thankfully. It’s spectacularly paced like the later games are, which makes it a great introduction to the genre. It’s also less of a pain than some of it’s contemporaries at the time. We’ve all had that struggle where a retro game is a bit tougher than we expected, right? You won’t run into that here.

For every Fire Emblem and Final Fantasy Tactics that have managed to beat the odds and become popular and prominent tactical JRPGs, there’s about ten other games that didn’t get as big of a chance to shine. To me, Warrior of Rome is one of them. It leans a bit more close to something like Civilization but it I feel like I’m just playing a Fire Emblem competitor above anything else.

And it’s very fun! The first time I discovered this game, I kind of sat there and played it for three hours, completely transfixed. It’s a fairly rudimentary game, but ordering my soldiers and watching them all fight was still entertaining, even if the graphics weren’t the best the Genesis could do. Besides, Warrior of Rome tracked multiple battles I had across the map in real-time, and that was really impressive to see on the Genesis of all things. I feel like more tactical JRPG fans could try this game out. It’s a nice little snack while you wait for the bigger pillars in this little niche.

We’re back at another Zelda-like action RPG of the old days! Togi O: King Colossus is one of those games that just gets you right into what you want. You’re given simple exposition, you get a mission from the old man you live with, and get out there. If only the game was that easy on its players.

Togi O is a game with a lot of fighting. For some of you, that’s great. For others, it’ll be the most annoying thing you’ve ever dealt with on the Sega Genesis. Enemies are allowed to deal whatever punches they can throw at you, while nine times out of ten, you’re going to be left with a low-range weapon to use. It’s not exactly fair.

You also need to defeat all these enemies In crowded dungeon rooms to get items you need to progress. Items that are part of one big fetch quest, mind you. Argh. But even with all of this, Togi O might be the last stone left unturned for some of you die-hard fans out there. And like Crusader of Centy, it’s a good, retro relic that could use more eyes on it. Give it a try if you want a bit of a nostalgic challenge.

There are many elements and mechanics that JRPGs borrowed from other genres to create their wonderful works.

Back in 1991, Sega entered a strange, fleeting partnership with what’s one of the more obscure yet important JRPG developers out there, Nihon Falcom. This partnership would only last around four years, but it would lead to some important ports of Falcom games under and joint name “Sega Falcom.” One of these would be Dragon Slayer: The Legend of Heroes.

Now, Dragon Slayer: The Legend of Heroes was on a number of consoles, including the SNES, but the Genesis version in particular has impressively better visuals. Plus, if you’re here, you’ll likely prefer the Genesis sound font anyway.

Dragon Slayer: The Legend of Heroes on Genesis does away with the big, bordered display with all of the menus around it and gives you the entire screen to work with. This allows sprites to breathe and big moments and battles to shine more because of it. Even if you’ve played Dragon Slayer: The Legend of Heroes some other way, like maybe with its PSP portable remake, I still think this version is worth experiencing. It really shines on the Genesis. And that might be through the partnership that helped it thrive.

Now this is where things get interesting. There are a lot of great JRPGs on the Sega Genesis that everyone has likely played, but even at the bottom of the barrel, there’s some hidden gems. Braver Battle Saga: Legend of the Magic Warrior is one of those games. It’s an unlicensed Sega Genesis game that dates back as far as 1996. It’s earned notoriety for being a pretty good game despite lacking the golden Sega seal of approval on the box.

It’s a classic JRPG through and through despite that. Complete with quaint little towns, a rip-off ATB system, and battle music that will make you hate how many encounters there are in this game. Great for players who think the JRPGs of the olden days are better than what we’re getting now. Because of how great this game feels and plays, it’s been restored officially with modern releases and an English translation that you can find on Steam. If you’ve been craving a retro hidden gem to come out of nowhere like this, then this game is exactly what you’re looking for.

If you loved Final Fantasy Resonance, then it's time for more JRPG love, Jobs, and pixel art.

If you’ve been digging through Sega Genesis games, looking for all the RPGs that the system has to offer, then you might have heard of this one before. I tend to harp on this one a lot because it’s a baffling and head-spinning obscure game that I feel like those self-proclaimed experts of the genre should check out. Maten no Soumetsu is well-known for being impressive visually and incredibly punishing with its difficulty.

If you feel incredibly powerful because you beat Elden Ring, or just want to suffer with a game that’s truly unforgivingly hard, I dare you to pick up this one and report back to me. Good luck!

If you’re here, I’m assuming you really like mechas. Or anime. Or Fire Emblem. Happen to be a fan the first and third ones? Boy, do I have the game for you! Vixen 357 is a war strategy JRPG that brings mechas and the same staples of Fire Emblem to one fantastic Sega Genesis game.

As you might expect, you control units in the form of human-piloted mechs. I state this kind of obvious fact because Vixen 357 pulls no punches, and it shows you the pilots bleeding before the permadeath mechanics take them away. This, combined with some gorgeous graphics, is why Vixen 357 has been saved and has recently been ported to modern consoles through Ratalaika Games and Shinyuden. Go check it out!

Remember when I spoke about an initiative back in the day that aimed to put more JRPGs on the Sega Genesis in Japan? Some games made its way over, but not all of them. Surging Aura is one of those games. It’s circled around the import and translation scene for years as a great game that was locked behind the lack of a full translation.

I’m happy to report that in the time since I last wrote about Surging Aura, a full English translation patch has been completed! Yes, you’re reading this right. There’s a brand-new (sort of) JRPG that you’ve likely never played ripe for the taking right now! Surging Aura feels like it was meant to be Sega’s answer to Final Fantasy, but it has its own distinct personality and charm to where it feels like its own unique adventure.

We have the character and monster design of the late Mutsumi Inomata to thank for that. If you’re big on JRPGs, that name might sound familiar. She was the character designer for the Tales of series starting with Tales of Destiny. So, if you’ve been a fan of her work, this game is calling out to you specifically. It’s worth your time, I promise.

While some JRPGs disappear, others return from oblivion after years without new releases.
