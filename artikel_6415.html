Cutting more trees could actually help forests store more carbon

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

A new study suggests that carefully managed tree harvesting could increase long-term carbon storage by reducing tree deaths and preserving carbon in wood products.

The surest way to keep carbon in a forest seems obvious: stop cutting it.

Trees left standing continue growing and storing carbon. But as forests age, growth can slow and more trees die, gradually releasing their stored carbon.

Researchers who modeled every forest in China project that cutting more trees, earlier and in the right places, would store more carbon by 2100 than today’s restrictions do.

But the calculation only works when carbon stored in harvested wood products is included.

The gain is a calculation, not a measurement. It depends on the harvested wood being turned into products that last, and it comes from a model run under a single climate model.

Zhen Yu of Nanjing University of Information Science and Technology and Shirong Liu of the Chinese Academy of Forestry led the work, with co-authors at the U.S. Forest Service, the University of Exeter and CREAF in Spain.

The authors wrote that their findings challenge a widely held assumption: cutting the least always stores the most.

China swung from heavy logging to strict protection in the late 1980s, and its forest area has grown ever since.

Over the same years, its wood consumption rose more than tenfold, to 0.534 billion cubic meters in 2020, and more than 40% of that wood is imported.

The country now buys more wood from abroad than any other.

The authors drew on 15.5 million tree measurements from 415,000 inventory plots surveyed between 1999 and 2018.

Harvests got lighter over that period: more plots were cut, but the volume taken per cut fell by nearly half. Meanwhile the number of plots with dying trees kept rising.

Between the sixth and ninth national inventories, harvested volume dropped 46% while the volume lost to tree death rose 24%.

The pattern was strongest in old stands, which lost roughly four to five times as much wood to death as younger forests did.

China sorts its forests into timber forests, protection forests kept for soil, water and wildlife, and multifunctional reserves where commercial logging is excluded.

The reserves held 23% more standing wood per hectare than the timber forests, and their tree deaths rose 53% between 1999 and 2018.

The team ran two futures through an ecosystem model it first checked against the inventory record from 1980 to 2018. One continued current practice.

The other adjusted how much timber to harvest, when to harvest it, and where to cut based on forest age and type.

It opened suitable reserves to regulated harvesting and left protected areas and fragile ground alone.

Under current practice, the model projects that wood lost to tree death would climb to 0.43 billion cubic meters a year by 2100.

Dead trees amounted to about 71% of the timber harvest in 2020, and the authors estimate that by 2100 they would exceed it. More wood would rot than would be cut.

The adjusted plan harvests some stands before their trees die.

The model estimates it would cut mortality losses by 1.82 billion cubic meters (64 billion cubic feet) between 2020 and 2100, a 6.7% reduction.

Timber output would rise from 0.32 billion to 0.44 billion cubic meters a year, a 38% increase.

The extra timber would cut China’s reliance on imports, the authors wrote.

They calculated that the country’s wood self-sufficiency would rise from 59% to 81% under the plan. Domestic production met 46% of demand in 2023.

More cutting leaves less carbon in living trees, and the model shows that. By 2100 the standing stock under the adjusted plan is 2.41 billion cubic meters lower than under current practice.

Counting the carbon held in harvested wood products changes the sum.

Assuming wood use remains at 2020 levels, the model projects 4.2% more total carbon storage under the adjusted plan, equivalent to an additional 520 million metric tons of carbon.

The team’s supplementary accounting puts the climate benefit at roughly 550 million metric tons of carbon dioxide equivalent annually.

None of this is a case for indiscriminate logging. The modeled benefit depends on forest age, ecological limits, and what happens to the wood after it leaves the forest.

The researchers didn’t track soil carbon, which can rise or fall after harvesting.

Their model moves a dead tree’s carbon straight into the soil pool, skipping the years or decades a standing dead trunk can hold carbon.

And every future ran under one climate model across four emissions pathways. The authors wrote that the difference between the two plans is therefore more trustworthy than either plan’s absolute numbers.

The 4.2% figure also rests on wood products being used the way they were in 2020.

How much of a harvested log becomes a beam that lasts 50 years, rather than paper or fuel, is a market question the forest model can’t answer.

The aging-forest problem is not China’s alone, the authors wrote. Europe and North America face the same mix of older stands, rising disturbance and growing demand for wood.

Whether carefully managed harvesting could deliver similar carbon benefits elsewhere remains an open question.

The full study was published in the journal National Science Review.
