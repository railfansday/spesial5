Millions of Americans use a form of birth control that surveys have overlooked

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

More Americans use menstrual cycle tracking to avoid pregnancy than surveys suggested, with use rising in some states.

Tracking the menstrual cycle to avoid pregnancy may be far more common than scientists once believed. Based on national surveys conducted over the years, only a small share of Americans were thought to rely on it.

Around 7% to 8% of US birth control users aged 18 to 44 tracked their cycles in 2022 and 2023. That share nearly doubled by 2026 in three Western states.

Its popularity has also been underestimated because of the way most surveys count birth control users.

The study was led by Chelsea Polis, a principal research scientist at the Guttmacher Institute.

She worked with Rebecca Simmons, a research associate professor of obstetrics and gynecology at University of Utah Health.

No new information was gathered for the project. Data from four large surveys were reanalyzed instead.

Cycle tracking for pregnancy prevention falls under a category of contraception known as fertility awareness-based methods (FABMs).

“I hope that these results are a shock to the family planning community,” Simmons said.

“There’s been a lot more acceptance of FABMs as legitimate methods over time, but most people still aren’t putting them on their radar enough for how important they are in the cultural milieu.”

“This is a really important thing that I’m hoping that people will sit up and pay attention to right now.”

Pregnancy is possible on only six to nine days of each menstrual cycle. These days fall around ovulation, when an egg is released by an ovary.

Some people count days on a calendar to work out when they are fertile. Others check their temperature every morning, and some watch for changes in cervical mucus. A smartphone app is used by some.

To avoid pregnancy, they skip sex on fertile days or use condoms as a backup. People who are trying to conceive can use the same information.

More than a dozen fertility awareness-based methods exist, but they don’t all work equally well.

“FABMs are a method class,” Simmons said.

“They’re not a single method. Within that method class, some of those methods, like the rhythm method, are not very effective, and some are actually comparable to the effectiveness of short-acting methods like pills or patches.”

Two of the surveys covered the whole country. One was the 2022–2023 National Survey of Family Growth. The other was a 2022 survey on access to birth control.

The other two were limited to six states between them. One was conducted in Arizona, New Jersey and Wisconsin. The Mountain West Sexual and Reproductive Health Survey was carried out in Idaho, Nevada and Utah.

Each survey recruited its participants in its own way. The time frame for current use also varied from the month of the interview to the previous three months.

Despite these differences, the four estimates for 2022 and 2023 fell within a narrow band of 6.6% to 8.3%.

The federal survey alone suggests that more than 2.2 million people in the US use these methods.

“Fertility awareness-based methods are used by a meaningful share of US contraceptive users,” Polis said.

“Although many clinicians do not receive robust training on FABMs, our data show that FABMs are more frequently used than several other contraceptive methods that clinicians are routinely trained to provide.”

Gaps in knowledge about birth control are nothing new. Earlier research found that many healthcare providers don’t offer self-injected birth control either.

Picture a woman who keeps track of her cycle and relies on condoms during her fertile days. A national survey would most likely record her as a condom user.

That happens because each person is usually assigned the most effective method they mention. Cycle tracking is ranked low on that scale and is therefore easily missed.

Such combined users made up about a quarter of cycle trackers in three surveys. In the fourth, they were close to half.

When they were added back into the federal survey, the estimate went from 4.3% to 6.6%. The standard approach had overlooked about a third of the people who track their cycles.

People who paired tracking with the pill, an IUD, an implant or sterilization were not counted. They accounted for about one in five cycle trackers, compared with roughly 9% in a 2013 to 2015 analysis.

Several of those methods prevent ovulation entirely. In that case tracking offers almost no extra protection, and the researchers suggested that some users may not realize it.

Cycle tracking appears to be gaining ground, at least in some parts of the country.

Between 2019–2020 and 2022–2023, its use in Arizona, New Jersey and Wisconsin increased from 5% to 6.9%.

A sharper rise was recorded in Idaho, Nevada and Utah. There, the share of birth control users who tracked their cycles stood at 7.5% in December 2022. It had grown to 12.5% by June 2024 and then to 14.5% by January 2026.

Most of this growth came in the first 18 months. The later increase was small enough that it could have been due to chance.

Is the same trend playing out nationwide? That question can’t be answered yet. Because the federal survey has since been redesigned, its latest results cannot be compared reliably with earlier rounds.

Several factors could be involved, according to Simmons. Concerns about hormonal birth control may be one.

The popularity of tracking apps and difficulty getting other contraceptives may be others. None of these reasons was examined in the study.

Social media could be adding to the trend as well. Earlier studies of popular TikTok videos about birth control found cycle tracking was discussed more than any other method.

Among cycle trackers in the federal survey, 63% were married. Only 45% of people using other methods were.

The gap in Arizona, New Jersey and Wisconsin looked much the same, at 60% versus 43%.

Cycle tracking only works when both partners cooperate. Couples in steady relationships may find it easier to decide together when sex should be avoided or a condom used.

A link between relationship commitment and the birth control choices of young adults has been reported before as well.

Some surveys pointed to a role for education too. In Arizona, New Jersey and Wisconsin, 55% of cycle trackers had a college degree. Among other birth control users, the figure was 42%.

No consistent link with religion was found. Cycle tracking therefore cannot be explained simply as a religious practice.

Adults aged 25 to 34 were the most likely to track their cycles in three of the four surveys.

Why more people are turning to cycle tracking is still unclear. Some people might just like it better. For others, getting hold of their preferred contraceptive may have become harder.

“Are people choosing these methods because they’re their preferred method, or are they choosing these methods because they’re out of options?” Simmons said.

“It might be that people are choosing these because they no longer have access to a doctor and they can’t afford the method that they need.”

How people track their cycles is another open question. In the surveys, scientifically tested methods could not be told apart from more casual ones.

Many period tracking apps were never designed to prevent pregnancy. Two apps have been cleared for birth control by the US Food and Drug Administration. Only one of them, Natural Cycles, is still on the US market.

The findings have some limits. All birth control habits were self-reported. Other factors that might explain the differences between groups were not accounted for.

The Idaho, Nevada and Utah survey was also carried out online and in English. Some groups in it were small, so the results may not reflect the rest of the country.

Future research could ask cycle trackers directly what led them to choose the method.

New national data expected in 2027 could reveal whether the growing popularity of cycle tracking reflects a regional trend or a fundamental shift in how Americans approach birth control.

The full study was published in the journal The Lancet Regional Health – Americas.
