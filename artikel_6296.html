Southern right whales are raising calves in places scientists didn’t expect

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Southern right whale mothers off Australia moved to a different nursing bay 54% of the time, crowding into sites where other whales were.

Researchers long thought southern right whales returned to the same bay where they were nursed to raise their own calves.

But off southern Australia, when researchers spotted the same mother with different calves in successive sightings, she was in a different nursing area 54% of the time.

As southern right whales recovered from whaling, mothers and calves gathered in three stretches of coast, while other apparently suitable areas drew few whales.

Researchers think the presence of other whales may help explain why those three areas grew so quickly.

Anne Grundlehner of the Institute for Marine and Antarctic Studies at the University of Tasmania led the work with colleagues from the Australian Antarctic Division.

Her team used nearly five decades of aerial surveys and a photo catalog that tells individual right whales apart by the rough patches of skin on their heads.

The catalog held 501 females seen with a calf in at least two different years between 1978 and 2012. Nearly two thirds, 63.5%, had nursed in more than one of the ten areas where the whales gather.

Among all 767 whales photographed in more than one year, 82.4% were seen in more than one area.

Mothers did come back to the three busiest areas more often. A mother seen in one of those three with a calf was back with her next calf 48% of the time. In the seven smaller areas, the figure was 15%.

“The results surprised us very much,” Grundlehner said in an interview with Earth.com.

Right whales were long believed to memorize the bay or beach where they were nursed as newborns and to return to it again and again, she said.

A single-engine plane has flown the coast of Western Australia each winter since 1976. The route now runs about 1,550 miles (2,500 kilometers), from Perth to Ceduna in South Australia.

Southern right whales spend summer feeding far to the south and migrate to the coast in winter.

Mothers and calves stay in water 16 to 33 feet (5 to 10 meters) deep, close to the beach.

From 1,500 feet (450 to 500 meters) up, the crew spots almost every dark whale over the pale sand, then drops lower for a photograph.

Flights run from mid-August to mid-September, when calf numbers peak, and the annual count climbed from a handful to several hundred.

“When the whales were still a rarity along the coastline, the few whales visiting our coast seemed randomly distributed across suitable habitat. There weren’t any sites that stood out as hotspots,” Grundlehner said.

By the mid-1980s, southern right whales were gathering in three areas that filled up much faster than the rest of the coast.

The hotspots were Bremer and Doubtful Islands Bays, the stretch from Israelite Bay to Point Culver, and the Bunda Cliffs and Head of Bight.

In one of those areas, the number of mothers with calves rose by about 30% a year during the first decade.

Daughters returning to their birth bays can’t account for growth that fast. The team did the arithmetic.

With every female returning to her birth area, a calf every three years and no deaths, an area’s mothers could grow by at most 9.7% a year.

Which areas were busy at the start didn’t predict which became busy later, either. By a statistical measure of clumping, the whales bunched up more each year until about 2000, and the pattern has held since.

“Instead, as the population grew in numbers, the female whales were more likely to select nursing grounds where there were other whales,” said co-author Stuart Corney, an associate professor at the same institute.

The team’s reading is that mothers rely on a memory of past nursing sites plus the sight or sound of other whales, a pattern also proposed for humpback whales.

The three busy areas are more open and exposed than the other bays, which the authors suggest could make other whales easier to see or hear.

When only a few dozen whales reached the coast each year, there may have been no one to follow.

The whales do seem loyal to something, at a larger scale. Most repeat sightings of the same whale fell within about 620 miles (1,000 kilometers) of each other.

Grundlehner told Earth.com the findings imply more flexibility than researchers had assumed.

Grundlehner was asked what the findings mean for protecting nursing areas.

“This is extremely important for effective conservation management: it means that you should not just protect the places with the highest numbers of an animal species you are trying to protect, but all suitable habitat that is available and accessible to them,” she said.

Areas used by few whales today could matter more if the population grows or if climate change alters the coast, she said. The range also needs to stay connected, with nothing blocking movement between nursing sites.

Growth is not guaranteed. Since about 2015, annual numbers in every area have flattened or dipped, and the population is estimated at a tenth of its pre-whaling size.

Grundlehner’s team reported in a 2025 study that recovery had stalled. She told Earth.com that calf numbers from 2023 to 2026 have stayed relatively stable, about 8% below their peak around 2016 and 2017.

The team can’t rule out other explanations for why some bays filled and others didn’t.

Records from before whaling are patchy, so it’s uncertain whether the three busy areas were busy two centuries ago.

The photo catalog also stops in 2012, so the latest decade of movements hasn’t been checked whale by whale.

And a more flexible whale isn’t necessarily a safer one. A population that follows other whales, or returns to a remembered bay, may keep using a place even after conditions there change, the authors wrote.

What draws whales to a particular bay, including local conditions and human disturbance, has to be worked out.

After that, someone has to watch whether the three crowded areas hold if the population starts growing again or shrinks further.

The full study was published in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.
