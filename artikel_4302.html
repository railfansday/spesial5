Murals Interview: How To Create A Mascot Who's Not As Annoying As Ocarina Of Time's Navi

Tanggal: 2026-11-11

Scott Baird is a Contributor from the UK with over a decade of video game writing experience.

Murals is an upcoming action RPG by Dlala Studios that, on the surface, looks a great deal like Hades. The concept of Murals involves twisted and humorous takes on classic fairy tales, like Rapunzel, which is an idea that’s more commonplace than the straight tellings of those stories, with everyone from Disney to Alan Moore turning fables on their head.

Originality isn’t the golden ticket to a video game being enjoyable, and in this case, the differences from genre norms are in the gameplay, not the story. While Murals might look like countless isometric action RPG roguelikes on Steam, it’s going for a linear, narrative-based progression, with crafted levels and enemies, rather than relying on randomness.

Playing it straight is a surprisingly bold move, and it runs counter to the story of Murals. DualShockers recently visited Dlala’s offices to preview Murals, where we got the chance to speak to the studio’s CEO, AJ Grand-Scrutton, about the many choices that went into making Murals.

Q: The idea of fables and fairy tales being twisted and viewed through a modern lens has been done many times before. Were there any specific examples that inspired Murals?

Grand-Scrutton: So obviously the source material was where we went to initially, the grim stuff and the Hans Christian Andersen. But I mean, I loved American McGee's Alice. I was a massive fan of that. There's a lot of like comic books as well, like Fables. I love Wolf Among Us. So I love that familiarity that you recognize the story, but enough difference that you don't feel you're just retreading old ground.

Q: Have any video games inspired the gameplay or design of Murals?

Grand-Scrutton: Obviously, Murals is isometric and it's got pretty chaotic moment to moment. So a lot of modern Roguelikes, your Hades, et cetera. I struggle with permadeath, especially as I've got older. I'm quite impatient and moody. And so kind of what I really loved was like the moment to moment in a lot of modern roguelikes, but I didn't necessarily get on very well with the permadeath loop. So that was a big influence for how Murals came about.

Like, can we take some of those chaotic moment to moment experiences; those combat augmentation systems and deliver them in like a bit longer form content? And also like, you know, weird influences in there as well. You know, like a lot of our world building Psychonauts and a lot of double fine stuff was really prominent in our minds. And, you know, classic combat games like DMC, Bayonetta.

But yeah, it was really just about us trying to capture an experience we didn't feel we could fully play out there already.

Q: The protagonist is mostly silent in Murals, with most of the talking being done by the character’s sidekick, Loaf. How do you go about designing a talkative side character like Loaf in a way that makes them likeable. Were there any concerns they’d become another Navi from Ocarina of Time?

Grand-Scrutton: Yeah, so, I mean, we always are very aware that our style of writing can become... Every character can feel like that very quickly for people. I mean, I think if you look at Battletoads, you look at Illusion Island and you look at Murals, you can tell it's very much like the same writing team that have done them.

Loaf is kind of, in a weird way, is obviously this sentient sketch come to life. He's the most naturally written character because it's often me and Kelsy, my lead writer, just typing what we're thinking about situations or stupid commets. Like, often we'll just be on calls and we'll play through the game. And Kelsy, as well as writing, she actually is Loaf. She does the voice of Loaf. And we'll almost do our own little director's commentaries as we're playing the game to figure out where Loaf comments should go and what would Loaf say in this situation.

I think with humour, comedy in games, we're not unique. There's other people that do comedy games, but it's still quite a niche. And I think you kind of just have to lean into what you are. And I think that we are that self-referential type of humour. We do the fourth wall breaking. There is the modern label of the meta-level stuff in there. And I think it'd be easy for us to go half-arsed or half-cocked on it. But we just kind of leant into it. This is what we find funny, and that's kind of how we write the game.

Q: Why go for such a basic, sketch outline design for Loaf?

Grand-Scrutton: Yeah, I love the juxtaposition. So obviously, as you've seen from the couple of murals you've played, the characters that live in the world are hand-animated, but they've still got depth to them.

The invasive forces, which is the distorted, the enemies, and the hero, the player, are fully animated. And I love this idea that Loaf was just... the hero just made this rough sketch in their notebook or something and accidentally got brought to life. I love Doodlebob, who is definitely an influence from the SpongeBob cartoons.

I just think there's something really nice about... we've got these animated characters that are still not realistic, but almost like they make sense. And then Loaf is just a cartoon. He doesn't really have the rules of physicality that a humanoid would have. He is just who he is. And I think that just gave us some really fun opportunities, and it let us do some silly things with him.

Q: Is Murals intended as a linear experience, or is there more of a roguelike/Hades-style progression, with a hub area, random layouts, and upgrades sticking between runs.

Grand-Scrutton: No, it's very, very linear. Like, we're always up front about that because we know how misleading it can be when you see like an isometric action game. It's very linear. Like, the only real elements of random to it are the order you get the Eeries in.

So, you know, if you do a playthrough, you may get different Eeries to someone else doing the exact same playthrough. But the story, the quests, the enemies themselves, that is all very sculpted.

Q: In the Murals preview, there’s a very generous window for being able to dash out of attacks. Is this planned to be maintained for the final build or is the gameplay balance being tweaked still, out of fear it could make the combat too easy?

Grand-Scrutton: Yes, no, maybe to all the above. We're still tweaking. We'll be tweaking until my Tech Director pries it from my cold dead hands. We definitely have gone generous with a lot of things like this. I think the difficulty you're playing in general is probably on the easier side of what we would balance the final game.

You know, we're looking to make it customizable though, right? In terms of like enemy health, enemy damage, amount of health, you probably notice in the build you're playing right now that you always have enough health drops to get full health basically. That would probably be the generous setting on the slider for health drops. But I think the dash serves a very specific purpose in our game, obviously it's not unique at all.

But we've got a lot of boss fights, environmental hazards and other enemies as you progress through the game where giving the players a generous dash meant we could get more chaotic with the experience. I think, you know, if you look at our game and compare to some of our other peers, I think that's one of the slight difference of our moment to moment.

I think we're not a one versus one experience. We're not one versus many. We're like a one versus some is what I like to say. But we do reasonably small spaces with a reasonably large amount of enemies. And so while the play space is quite chaotic, we wanted to give the player simple enough verbs that they could control so they never felt like the game was being unfair.

DualShockers spoke with the developers of Wo Long 2: Wings of Ember about why a sequel was made and how the gameplay has changed from its predecessor.

Q: Some of the enemies have a flashing light before they attacked. Is this hinting at a parry mechanic for the full version of the game? Do you dare release a game in 2027 with no parrying?

Grand-Scrutton: I mean, we are daring to not release. No, there's no parry. They're very generous tells on some of the enemies that do more damage. We definitely discussed parrying really early on. We discussed parrying, we discussed blocking. But it just, it felt a bit like a hat on a hat for what we were making. Like, you know, we like, we believe in like, depth through simplicity. This is not a big combo based game.

You know, it's not even as combo laden as like our Battletoads was. It's a very simple amount of inputs with like varying weapons and a lot of depth comes through the power-ups. And the problem I find with parrying is like, modern games are amazing.

Like, you know, Expedition 33, all these games are absolutely outstanding. I find when I play them that my gameplay just becomes around parrying. All I do is parry. Like, in a lot of ways, although I love them, I play them multiple times.

I felt that learning to do the block timing in the Arkham games made the combat a little bit less exciting for me because I actually certainly changed my entire strategy in those games to be let the enemy come to me. And I just didn't want that for this. I didn't want the focus to be on parry timing. I wanted the focus to just be like, have a little bit of fun controlled chaos.

Q: Finally, why choose Murals as the game’s name?

Grand-Scrutton: Yeah, we are traditionally, not necessarily publicly, very awful at doing project codenames. Like, the codename for Battletoads was Frogspawn, which is not the most... And the codename for Illusion Island was Illusions because we weren't originally going to call it that.

So Project Murals was the codename for Murals because we love the idea that these worlds are like these fairy tales brought to life by through these painted murals the creator has done. But the team will tell you, I have this obsession, I think Pokémon is the greatest name for a franchise ever because I love a name that is memorable and it's also the name of a thing in the game or in the world. And I love the idea of us having murals because the levels are called murals and it's also the name and it makes sense.

So it's kind of like this dragon I've been chasing and it just fit the game and it worked for me. And there's some funniness in there as well to me through some previous titles we've worked on and stuff in the past where we've tried to put forward names for things and we've been told that the majority of the...

We had a game we were working on years ago, years and years ago, and it was around animatronics. And this was years back. I mean, it was before it was necessarily common lingo but I really wanted to call it Tronics and I was like, oh, how cool is this name? And we got told by our partners at the time that no one knows what an animatronic is so we couldn't do it. And then Five Nights at Freddy's came out a year later and I was like, everyone knows what animatronic is, look.

And we've had similar things with this, like when we'd kind of pitched this to people before the name Murals, people were like, I don't think anyone's gonna really know what a mural is. And I was like, I'm pretty sure people know what a mural is. So it was nice to kind of have that control ourselves.
