Depression may be an early warning sign of Alzheimer's disease

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Changes in mood later in life may offer important clues about what is happening inside the brain, even before problems with memory or thinking become noticeable. New research suggests that the buildup of tau, a protein closely associated with Alzheimer's disease, may contribute to worsening depressive symptoms in older adults.

The study, published in JNeurosci, was conducted by Teodora Markova of Brandeis University and her colleagues. The team investigated how tau levels relate to depression during normal aging and in people experiencing the cognitive (memory, attention, and thinking) difficulties associated with Alzheimer's disease.

Alzheimer's Protein Linked to Worsening Depression

To investigate this connection, researchers analyzed information from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative, a research project that tracks changes in brain health over time. The data included older men and women, some with cognitive difficulties and others whose memory and thinking abilities remained intact.

Participants underwent multiple brain scans designed to detect tau accumulation and completed questionnaires assessing depressive symptoms at different points during the study. This allowed researchers to examine how changes in tau levels corresponded with changes in mood over time.

Among participants with normal cognitive abilities, the researchers discovered a striking pattern. Those whose tau levels increased more rapidly also tended to experience a faster rise in depressive symptoms. Higher tau levels also helped predict more severe depressive symptoms in these individuals.

The results suggest that tau may begin influencing mood before measurable cognitive problems emerge. However, the same relationships were not observed among participants who already had mild cognitive impairment or Alzheimer's disease.

Could Depression Reveal Early Brain Changes?

The findings suggest that tau accumulation during the early stages of brain aging may contribute to the development or worsening of depression. They also raise the possibility that changes in mood could provide useful information about brain health before memory loss becomes apparent.

Researchers believe that following depressive symptoms over time, alongside other assessments, could eventually help identify older adults who may be at greater risk of developing neurodegenerative diseases. However, additional research is needed to establish whether these early changes in mood can reliably predict future cognitive decline.

Markova emphasized the importance of studying these relationships over longer periods, particularly as some participants develop more noticeable depression or begin experiencing cognitive difficulties.

"Future work can continue to track how participants' tau levels and depression symptoms evolve over longer periods of time, particularly as some develop cognitive impairment or more clinically significant levels of depression. Continuing to follow these trajectories, observing how early mood changes relate to later clinical outcomes, and establishing relationships with other methods tracking the development of Alzheimer's disease will provide more clarity on methods to determine which individuals may be at an increased risk."
