How MLB's qualifying offer works, from salary formula to pick compensation

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Free daily briefing on global business news.

The MLB qualifying offer ties a one-year salary to draft pick compensation, and these 10 explainers cover who gets it, what it pays and what happens when players decline

A club about to lose a free agent can turn his departure into draft pick compensation. The price of that option is the risk that he accepts a one-year salary instead. For the 2023-24 offseason, that salary was $20.325 million. The mechanism behind the trade-off is the MLB qualifying offer, a one-year contract a team can extend to its own impending free agent.

The offer cuts both ways. A club collects compensation only if the player declines and signs with another team. If he accepts, the club owes him the one-year salary. A player who declines gives up a guaranteed salary to test the open market. He also enters that market carrying a cost for any rival club that signs him.

The system dates to the 2012 collective bargaining agreement between the league and its players. It replaced the older Type A and Type B classifications, which relied on player rankings. The salary is not negotiated player by player. It is pegged to the average salary of the 125 highest-paid players in the majors and recalculated every offseason. That formula ties the offer to the top of the market rather than to any one player's performance.

A player generally must spend the full season with the same club to be eligible. A player traded midseason is not eligible. A player can also receive an offer only once in his career. When a player declines and signs elsewhere, the signing club gives up draft picks and, in some cases, international bonus pool space. The exact cost depends on whether the clubs involved receive revenue-sharing money or pay the luxury tax. The size of the contract also matters.

Those costs have shaped individual free agencies. Kyle Lohse, a free agent after 2012, remained unsigned into spring training 2013. The draft pick cost attached to him was a factor. Stephen Drew and Kendrys Morales each turned down the 2013 offer of $14.1 million. Both signed in June 2014, after the draft, when the pick compensation no longer applied. Drew went to the Red Sox, and Morales went to the Twins.

The MLB qualifying offer is a one-year contract that a team may extend to one of its own players who is about to become a free agent. The player then chooses between two paths: accept the deal and stay, or decline it and test the open market.

The offer is optional. A club decides player by player whether to make it. Nothing requires a team to extend one to any departing free agent.

The arrangement exists to give clubs something back when a player leaves. If the player turns the offer down and then signs with a different team, his former club receives draft pick compensation.

That conditional link sits at the center of the system. Declining alone does not trigger anything for the original club. Compensation depends on whether the player goes on to sign with another team, not on the rejection itself.

For the player, the offer is a fallback and a decision point at once. Accepting locks in a one-year salary with his current club. Declining keeps every other option open. It also attaches a cost in draft selections to any other team that signs him.

For the team, the calculation runs the other way. Making the offer creates a path to compensation if the player walks. It also carries the possibility that he says yes. In that case, the club is committed to a one-year salary it chose to put on the table.

The qualifying offer arrived with the 2012 collective bargaining agreement. It took the place of an older approach to free agent compensation that sorted players into Type A and Type B classes based on rankings.

The newer system ties compensation to a team's decision to make an offer, rather than to where a player sat in a ranking. A player becomes subject to compensation because his club offered him the one-year deal and he turned it down.

The qualifying offer salary equals the average salary of MLB's 125 highest-paid players. The league recalculates that average every offseason.

The player receiving the offer has no effect on the amount. A team cannot lower it for a modestly paid player or raise it for a star. Every qualifying offer made in a given offseason carries the same one-year salary.

The figure is also the same for every club. A team with a small payroll and a team with a large one face an identical number. That single salary takes up a bigger share of a smaller budget. The same offer can therefore be a heavier commitment for one club than for another.

That makes the offer a fixed price rather than a negotiated contract. A club considering one is not bargaining over terms. It is deciding whether the player is worth that single figure for one more season.

A fixed price also means a club has to be prepared to pay all of it. A player who accepts receives the full amount, whatever he earned the year before.

The benchmark draws on the highest salaries in the sport. When the largest contracts rise, the average rises with them. When pay at the top levels off, the figure follows.

Because it is an average across 125 salaries, no single contract sets the figure. One record-setting deal shifts the average by only a fraction of that deal's size. Broad movement across many top contracts is what moves the number.

The yearly recalculation means the price can change from one winter to the next. A club weighing an offer in one offseason may face a different figure than a club weighing a similar player a year earlier.

For a player, the formula provides a known number. He can compare that guaranteed one-year salary with the offers he expects from other clubs before he responds.

A qualifying offer can go only to a club's own impending free agent. That player generally must have spent the entire season with the same club. A player who changes teams in a midseason trade is not eligible to receive one.

Both conditions have to be met. The first is contract status. The offer is meant for a player whose deal is ending and who is about to reach the open market. A player still under contract for the following season falls outside the system, no matter how long he has been with his club.

The second condition is tenure. The rule generally ties eligibility to a full season with one organization. The club extending the offer has to be the club the player spent the whole year with.

A midseason trade breaks that link. A pending free agent who is dealt during the season cannot receive a qualifying offer from his new team. His old team cannot make one either. He is no longer its own free agent when the offseason arrives.

That limit carries over to trades of pending free agents. A club that acquires one during the season gets his play for the rest of that year. It does not gain the option of making him a qualifying offer once the season ends. The club that trades him away loses that option as well.

Eligibility also decides whether draft pick compensation is possible. Compensation goes only to a club whose offer was declined and whose player then signed with another team. A player who was never eligible for an offer carries no such cost. The club that signs him does not forfeit draft selections under this mechanism.

Eligibility does not oblige a team to act. A club may extend the offer to an eligible player. It may also let him leave without one, in which case no draft pick compensation follows.

A Major League Baseball player can receive a qualifying offer only once in his career. After a club makes him that one-year offer, no team can make him another, whether he accepted it or turned it down.

The limit follows the player, not the club that made the offer. It does not reset when he changes teams. A club that signs him later cannot extend a fresh offer to a player who has already had one.

The limit shapes his later free agency. Draft pick compensation for a departing free agent flows only through a declined qualifying offer. A player who can no longer receive one carries no pick cost the next time he hits the open market.

A club that wants to sign him at that point does not give up draft picks or international bonus pool space to do it. His former club, in turn, cannot collect compensation when he leaves.

The rule applies to players who accept. An accepted offer is a one-year contract. The player can return to free agency a season later. When he does, his club cannot issue a second offer.

Joc Pederson and Martin Perez accepted offers in the 2022 offseason, as did Hyun-Jin Ryu in 2019 and Brandon Belt in 2021. Each of those acceptances used up that player's single offer.

The same limit covers players who decline. Stephen Drew and Kendrys Morales both turned their offers down. Having received one, neither could be given another later in his career.

The limit also holds as the offer's value climbs. A player who received an offer at an earlier, lower amount cannot be tagged again when the figure is higher.

For a player, the compensation attached to his name is a one-time event. For a club, making the offer is a single chance to tie draft pick compensation to that player's departure.

The MLB qualifying offer was worth $13.3 million in 2012, the year the system began under that year's collective bargaining agreement. By the 2023-24 offseason, the figure had climbed to $20.325 million.

Two more figures mark points along the way. The offer was $14.1 million in 2013. For the 2022-23 offseason, it was $19.65 million.

Those four numbers are snapshots rather than a full year-by-year record. Two sit at the start of the system, in its first two years. The other two come from back-to-back offseasons much later. Read in order, each figure is higher than the one before it.

The direction follows from how the number is set. The offer is an average of top salaries, so it moves when that group of salaries moves. Bigger contracts at the high end of the market push the next offer up. A quiet offseason for top-end deals would hold the figure down. The number reflects where elite pay stood in a given offseason. It is not a reward tied to any single player.

The same sum applied to every club that made a qualifying offer in a given offseason. A team with a modest payroll faced the same one-year figure as a team with a large one. That fixed figure takes up a bigger share of a smaller payroll than of a larger one.

A higher number changes what is at stake on both sides. It means a larger guaranteed salary for any player who accepts. It also means a larger one-year bill for the club that made the offer.

That raises the bar for extending an offer. A club has to be willing to pay the full figure for one season if the player says yes. A player weighing the offer compares that one-year sum with what he expects to find on the open market. The higher the figure, the more the guaranteed side of that comparison is worth.

A player who accepts the qualifying offer signs a one-year contract with his current club at that offseason's set salary. He skips the open market, and the club keeps him for one more season instead of collecting draft pick compensation.

Joc Pederson and Martin Perez both took that route in the 2022 offseason. Each accepted $19.65 million, the qualifying offer figure for the 2022-23 offseason. Two players signed at an identical salary in the same winter because the offer carries one price for everyone who receives it that year.

Hyun-Jin Ryu accepted a qualifying offer worth $17.8 million in 2019. Brandon Belt accepted $18.4 million in 2021. Both figures sit below the $19.65 million that Pederson and Perez took. The amount a player accepts depends on the year he receives the offer, not on his own salary history.

Acceptance also changes what happens on the club side. Draft pick compensation exists only when a player declines and then signs with another team. Once a player accepts, that path closes. His club receives no extra draft selection. No rival club forfeits picks or international bonus pool space to sign him.

What the club gets instead is another season of the player's services at a fixed, known price. The deal covers one year only, with no multiyear commitment attached to the offer itself.

For the player, accepting means trading the uncertainty of free agency for a guaranteed salary. He gives up the chance to test what other clubs would pay him. In return, he avoids a market where any signing team would have to give up draft picks to land him.

The choice also carries a career-long consequence. A player can receive a qualifying offer only once in his career, and a player who accepts has used that one offer. When his one-year deal ends, his club cannot attach a second qualifying offer to him. He reaches his next free agency without that draft pick cost following him.

A player who turns down the qualifying offer becomes a free agent on the open market. His former club receives draft pick compensation only if he signs with a different team.

The decline alone earns the original club nothing. Compensation depends entirely on where the player lands. If no other club signs him, no picks change hands.

Turning down the offer means walking away from a guaranteed one-year salary. In exchange, the player can negotiate terms with any club in the league.

That single trigger shapes the calculation for the club that made the offer. Extending it places a price on the player's departure. The club collects that price only when a rival team decides to sign him.

The decline also travels with the player. Any team weighing a contract with him has to account for what it would give up to sign him. The size of that cost varies from club to club.

Each side makes its own trade-off. The player exchanges a guaranteed salary for access to the open market. The original club exchanges its hold on the player for the possibility of draft picks. The signing club gives up some of its draft capital to add him.

When a player turns down a qualifying offer and signs with a new team, that new team gives up draft picks. In some cases it also surrenders international bonus pool space. The size of that cost depends on whether the clubs involved receive revenue sharing or pay the luxury tax, and on how large the contract is.

The forfeiture is one half of an exchange. The club that made the offer receives draft pick compensation, but only if the player signs with a different organization. If he never signs elsewhere, no picks move in either direction.

Three variables shape the exchange. The first is whether a club is a revenue-sharing recipient. The second is whether a club is a luxury-tax payer. Both questions apply to each club in the transaction — the team that loses the player and the team that signs him. The third variable is the size of the contract the player signs.

Those factors mean the price of signing a player who declined a qualifying offer is not uniform across MLB. Two clubs pursuing the same free agent can face different forfeitures based on their financial standing. The compensation that flows back to the team that lost him also depends on that club's own status.

None of the three variables is a direct measure of the player's performance. Two describe the finances of the clubs involved. The third reflects the deal he signs.

The possible loss of international bonus pool space means the penalty for some signings reaches beyond the draft. A single free-agent signing can draw down two separate resources at once.

For a front office, the cost of a free agent tied to a declined offer has two parts. One is the contract itself. The other is the picks, and in some cases the bonus pool space, that the club gives up to sign him. The size of that contract can also change the second part.

Kyle Lohse, Stephen Drew and Kendrys Morales are three examples of how draft pick compensation can weigh on a free agent's market. All three stayed unsigned for long stretches while pick compensation was attached to them.

Lohse reached free agency after the 2012 season. He remained unsigned into spring training in 2013. The draft picks a new club would have had to surrender to sign him were a factor in that wait.

Drew and Morales went through a similar wait. Both declined the 2013 qualifying offer of $14.1 million and entered the open market.

Both players signed in June 2014. By then the draft had already taken place. The pick compensation tied to each of them no longer applied. A club could add either player without forfeiting a draft selection.

Drew signed with the Boston Red Sox. Morales signed with the Minnesota Twins.

In all three cases, the cost sat with the club doing the signing. Any team that wanted Lohse, Drew or Morales before the draft would have given up draft selections to get him. Their former clubs stood to collect compensation only if each player signed somewhere else.

That cost fell on the buyer, but it still shaped each player's options. A club weighing a contract for one of them had to count the picks it would lose alongside the salary it would pay. For Lohse, that added cost was a factor in a wait that stretched into spring training.

The Drew and Morales signings show the other side of that calculation. Once the draft passed, the compensation attached to them ended, and the extra cost to a signing club went with it.

The cases also show the risk a player takes by saying no. Declining means giving up a guaranteed one-year salary for an uncertain market. Drew and Morales each passed on $14.1 million and then spent months without a contract.

The 2012 collective bargaining agreement replaced MLB's old Type A and Type B free agent classes with the qualifying offer. Compensation stopped hinging on where a player ranked and started hinging on a one-year salary offer he could accept or decline.

Under the earlier system, a player's class came from his place in player rankings. Compensation followed from that classification. The qualifying offer took rankings out of the equation.

In their place, the 2012 agreement put two decisions. The club makes one, and the player makes the other.

The club decides first whether to extend a one-year contract to its own impending free agent. A club that makes no offer has no claim to compensation if that player leaves. Under the new structure, compensation starts with an action the club takes, not with a label attached to the player.

The player then chooses between that guaranteed one-year salary and a search for a deal on the open market. His answer, not his ranking, determines whether compensation comes into play at all.

The offer is not tailored to the individual. In a given offseason, every club extending one offers the same amount. A league-wide salary formula sets that figure, not the player's own record. Two players with very different track records face the same number.

That uniform price turns the question of a player's value into a test both sides answer. A club weighing an offer has to decide whether the player is worth that salary for one season. A player weighing it has to decide whether the open market will pay him more than that.

That design puts a price on the club's choice. A team that extends the offer must be ready to pay the full one-year salary if the player accepts. If he declines, that salary commitment falls away.

The framework built in 2012 remained in place through the 2023-24 offseason.
