Karren Brady’s New Agency Raises £104m West Ham Question

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Former West Ham United vice-chair Karren Brady has launched a new commercial agency alongside fellow former Hammers director Nathan Thompson, specialising in sponsorship, hospitality and commercial strategy. The business, craetively called Brady Thompson, aims to help organisations unlock what its founders describe as hundreds of millions of pounds in untapped commercial value.

According to City AM, the pair have reunited just seven months after departing West Ham, with Brady talking enthusiastically about the enormous commercial opportunities available in sport and entertainment.

I have to say, I find it absolutely incredible that Brady has any sort of influence or clout in a business focusing on sponsorship, hospitality and commercial strategy, considering the Baroness was vice-chair when West Ham recorded losses of £104.2m in their last published accounting period. And that’s before we get onto the rather awkward subject of the London Stadium.

West Ham’s accounts for the financial year ending May 2025 revealed a pre-tax loss of £104.2m, a remarkable turnaround from the previous year’s £57.2m profit.

Now, clearly, not every penny of that loss can be attributed to Karren Brady. Football finances are complicated, and player recruitment, wages, transfer dealings and league performance all have a significant impact.

But she was one of the most senior executives at the football club, and surely the financial performance of the business has to form part of any assessment of her commercial legacy.

Indeed, Claret & Hugh previously reported that Brady had even become directly involved in negotiating transfers during the January 2026 window.

I’ve questioned the timing of her departure before, particularly given the financial difficulties and footballing problems that were developing towards the end of her time at the club.

So forgive me if I’m not immediately convinced by the idea that the same leadership can now teach other organisations how to maximise their commercial potential.

But perhaps the biggest irony of all is the decision to launch an agency specialising in sponsorship when, after ten years at the London Stadium, West Ham and the stadium authorities collectively failed to secure a naming-rights partner for the former Olympic venue.

A Premier League football club, based in London, playing in a stadium with a capacity of more than 60,000, and after a decade nobody managed to get a naming-rights deal over the line.

That wasn’t solely Brady’s responsibility, of course. The London Legacy Development Corporation controlled the stadium’s commercial arrangements, and there were disagreements about the value of any potential deal.

In fact, Brady herself pressed the stadium authorities to pursue naming rights, and the London Assembly has documented correspondence in which she expressed West Ham’s willingness to help secure an agreement.

Nevertheless, the outcome remains the same.

As Claret & Hugh reported last year, the stadium operators were warning that a naming-rights agreement might not materialise until 2028 or beyond.

And in my previous article about the missing £4m sponsorship opportunity, I argued that the failure to secure a partner when the stadium first opened had allowed the London Stadium name to become so established that selling it now would be considerably more difficult.

That is hardly a ringing endorsement of the commercial arrangements surrounding West Ham’s move from Upton Park.

Brady told City AM that unlocking commercial opportunities required imagination, experience, relationships and the willingness to challenge convention.

Nathan Thompson, meanwhile, pointed to his experience negotiating commercial deals from different sides of the boardroom.

I’m sure both have considerable contacts within the business world, and there’s no doubt Brady has spent decades operating at decent levels of football administration.

But contacts and experience are one thing. Results are another.

Perhaps the venture will prove enormously successful. Perhaps they’ll secure the sort of sponsorship agreements that other sporting organisations have struggled to attract.

However, from a West Ham supporter’s perspective, it is difficult to look beyond the £104m loss, the club’s subsequent relegation and the decade-long failure to find a stadium naming-rights sponsor.

When somebody starts offering advice about how to unlock hundreds of millions of pounds in commercial value, I don’t think it’s unreasonable to ask why so much potential remained untapped at the football club they helped run for 16 years.

But hey, if all else fails the new company name of Brady Thompson does sound like excellent branding for a luxury soap should they wish to diversify.
