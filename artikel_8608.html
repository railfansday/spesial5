Pitch venture capital firms only if your business could grow fast enough to sell or go public for many times what investors pay, since a fund needs a few winners like that to cover its many losses. If yours will grow steadily instead, fund it with savings or a loan and keep the whole company.

Of U.S. startups that raised early-stage venture capital from 1985 to 2009, about 55% ended at a loss for their investors, Harvard Business School researchers found. The 6% that returned more than five times the money brought in about half of everything investors got back.

So a venture firm looks for companies that could join that 6%, with a large market, fast-growing sales, and a realistic path to being sold or going public. A business that will earn its owner a good living but never sell for many times what investors would pay fails that test, however well it’s run.

The money is expensive. A typical seed round, one of a startup’s first rounds of outside money, sells close to a fifth of the company, and so does the larger Series A round that follows, Carta’s 2026 benchmarks show. Investors also get preferred stock, which usually repays them before your shares get anything when the company is sold, and they often take a seat on your board. Venture funds typically run at least 10 years and then cash out, the SEC notes, so expect pressure to grow fast enough to sell or go public before then.

If you fit, skip the cold email. In a survey of 885 venture capitalists published by the National Bureau of Economic Research, only 10% of their deals came from founders who reached out on their own. The median firm surveyed considered 100 deals for every one it closed. A software founder whose subscriptions grow every month pitches only firms that back early software companies, gets introduced by a founder one of them funded, and raises a $3 million seed round for 20% of her company.

A restaurant owner turned down by every venture firm she pitches opens a second location with a bank loan the Small Business Administration guarantees instead and keeps 100% of the business.
