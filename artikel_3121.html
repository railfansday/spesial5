If a Stock Market Crash Is Coming, History Says SCHD’s Dividend Grew Through the Last Two Bear Markets. But Here’s the Catch I'm Watching.

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

SCHD's history is encouraging, but one recent number changes how I read it.

With the market on edge these days, especially heading into Friday's jobs report, a question keeps nagging income investors: What will happen to my dividends if stocks crash?

The Schwab U.S. Dividend Equity ETF (SCHD -0.97%) has already endured two bear markets since its inception in 2011. The S&P 500 fell about 34% in early 2020 and again by over 24% in 2022. Yet SCHD's annual dividends per share rose about 49% from 2019 to 2022, from a split-adjusted $0.57 to $0.85 per share.

I own the ETF and added to my position last week, so I wanted to know what that record can -- and can't -- tell us. Here's a recent catch in its dividend record that I'm watching very closely.

Adjusted for the ETF's 2024 3-for-1 share split, the Schwab U.S. Dividend Equity ETF paid roughly $0.57 per share in 2019, $0.68 in 2020, $0.75 in 2021, and $0.85 in 2022, according to the distribution history on its website. That's an increase of 17.6% in the year of the COVID crash and 13.9% in the year of the bear market.

However, while the dividend went up both times, SCHD's share price didn't escape the downdraft. Its worst three-month return was -21.6% in early 2020. It also declined during the 2022 bear market.

SCHD's dividend didn't just grow through those downturns. It grew fast. Overall, dividends per share have grown at a 9% compound annual rate from 2020 through 2025. However, over the past 12 months, the payout has only increased by about 2% compared to the prior year.

That's a sharp slowdown, and it matters because the case for a dividend growth ETF rests on that growth. I don't know yet whether it's a blip or a trend. If the pace stays near 2%, the resilience we saw in 2020 and 2022 is worth less than it looks.

It's also worth noting that no two crashes are the same. For example, the 2020 crash was brutal but brief, and the 2022 decline was driven by rising inflation and interest rates. So, a deeper, longer earnings recession would be a tougher test for the dividend.

The slowdown doesn't erase why SCHD's payout held up in the past. The ETF tracks the Dow Jones U.S. Dividend 100 Index, which screens for yield, five-year dividend growth, and financial strength. The index also revamps its holdings once a year, swapping out companies that no longer measure up.

The ETF holds many large companies with long histories of dividend growth. Its top holdings include Procter & Gamble and Coca-Cola. Big, established businesses like these tend to have the cash flow and financial strength to keep paying through a downturn. Procter & Gamble has raised its dividend for 70 straight years, while Coca-Cola is up to 64 straight years. They're both Dividend Kings, companies with 50 or more years of annual dividend increases.

If the last two crashes are any indication, SCHD's dividend will likely keep rising while its share price would probably decline during a market slump. Depending on the catalyst, it might decline less than the S&P 500, due to the more defensive nature of its holdings, its strong dividend income, and its current valuation (at 18.2 times earnings, it's cheaper than the S&P 500 at 23.6 times earnings).

That's not a bad combination if you're collecting dividends. SCHD currently has a trailing 12-month yield of more than 3%. If its share price declines while its payout continues to rise, investors could lock in an even higher yield on newly purchased shares, enabling them to generate even more dividend income in the future.

I'm not selling SCHD because a stock market crash could be coming, as the ETF's history gives me some comfort. It has historically continued paying a higher dividend, even though its share price has taken a hit. However, the catch is that dividend growth has significantly slowed more recently despite a solid economic environment. That's why I'll be watching its next payout announcement in December to see if the slowdown is just a temporary blip or something more concerning.
