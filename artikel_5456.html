How MLB free agency works: 10 rules from opt-outs to compensation picks

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

Free daily briefing on global business news.

MLB free agency runs on service time, qualifying offers, opt-outs and draft pick rules, and these 10 explainers show how each piece shapes where players sign

Free agency in Major League Baseball is rarely a simple auction. Before a player signs with the highest bidder, a set of calendars, salary formulas and draft penalties comes into play. Those rules decide when he can talk to other clubs and what his signing will cost. They can change what a player is paid and which teams bid on him. They also help explain why one star signs in November while another is still unsigned when spring training camps open in February.

Nearly all of those procedures come from the collective bargaining agreement between Major League Baseball and the MLB Players Association. The current agreement was reached in March 2022, ending a 99-day lockout. It is scheduled to expire on Dec. 1, 2026. That date matters for anyone reading about the rules. The next round of bargaining could rewrite parts of the system described here.

The system follows a clear sequence. A player first has to earn enough service time to qualify. His club then gets a short head start to negotiate with him. After that, league rules and contract terms shape the bidding. A qualifying offer can attach a draft-pick cost to a player. A non-tender can push a younger player onto the market early. The competitive balance tax can lead a payroll-conscious club to walk away. An opt-out clause can return a star to the market before his contract was due to end.

This slideshow covers 10 of those mechanisms. It is a selection of key pieces, not a complete catalog of every provision in the agreement. Each slide explains one rule on its own terms, from the six-year service-time threshold to the compensation picks a club collects when a top player leaves. The final slide covers the posting system, which lets Japanese clubs make players available to major league teams before they reach free agency at home.

Taken together, the slides offer a working guide to MLB free agency rules for fans who follow the offseason closely and for readers who are new to it. Terms such as qualifying offer, non-tender, club option and competitive balance tax appear in transaction reports every winter. Each has a specific meaning. Each can also change where a player ends up and how much he earns.

A major league player becomes eligible for free agency once he has six full years of service time and his contract has expired. One year of service equals 172 days on the active roster or the injured list.

The regular season lasts longer than 172 days. A player can therefore earn a full year of service without spending every day in the majors. He cannot bank more than one year of service in a single season, however long the schedule runs.

Clubs and agents write service time as years and days. A player listed at 5.171 has five years and 171 days. One more day would give him six years. Without it, he stays under his club's control for another season.

The path to six years has three stages. Early in his career, a player's salary is set by his club at or near the league minimum. He then becomes eligible for salary arbitration, which covers the seasons before he reaches six years. Free agency comes last, when all 30 clubs can bid for him.

The six-year standard grew out of a long fight over the reserve clause. That clause once bound a player to his club from one year to the next. After the 1969 season, outfielder Curt Flood refused a trade from the St. Louis Cardinals to the Philadelphia Phillies. He sued to challenge the clause. The U.S. Supreme Court ruled against him in 1972 in Flood v. Kuhn.

The clause gave way through arbitration rather than the courts. In December 1975, arbitrator Peter Seitz ruled that pitchers Andy Messersmith and Dave McNally were free to negotiate with other clubs. Owners and the players union then wrote free agency into their 1976 labor agreement.

Service time also shapes when clubs promote their best prospects. Keeping a top young player in the minors for the first weeks of a season can delay his free agency by a full year. The 2022 collective bargaining agreement addressed that practice in part with the Prospect Promotion Incentive. Under it, a club can earn an extra draft pick when an eligible top prospect accrues a full year of rookie service. The player must then win Rookie of the Year or finish in the top three of MVP or Cy Young voting before he reaches arbitration.

For five days after the World Series ends, a team holds exclusive negotiating rights with its own free agents. Once that window closes, all 30 clubs can make offers. The open market then runs through the Winter Meetings in December and often well beyond them.

Inside the window, a player can re-sign with his current club before anyone else bids. His club must also decide by the end of those five days whether to extend him a qualifying offer. Rival clubs may not discuss contract terms with him during this stretch.

MLB's anti-tampering rules bar a team from negotiating with another club's player before the market opens. The league can discipline clubs that break those rules.

After the window closes, agents collect offers and clubs compare their options. A reported agreement is not final until the player passes a physical exam and the club announces the deal.

Shortstop Carlos Correa's winter of 2022-23 shows how much rides on that last step. In December 2022, he agreed to a 13-year, $350 million deal with the San Francisco Giants. The Giants raised concerns during his medical review, which focused on a lower right leg injury dating to 2014. The deal fell apart. He then agreed to a 12-year, $315 million deal with the New York Mets. That agreement also stalled over the medical review. In January 2023, he signed a six-year, $200 million contract with the Minnesota Twins.

The offseason calendar has other fixed dates. In November, clubs set their 40-man rosters. That roster decides which minor leaguers are protected from the Rule 5 draft. Clubs also face a November deadline to tender contracts to players still under team control. Players who are not tendered contracts become free agents.

The Rule 5 draft takes place at the Winter Meetings. Clubs also use those meetings for much of their face-to-face business with agents.

The market does not shut down after December. Manny Machado agreed to a 10-year, $300 million deal with the San Diego Padres in February 2019. Bryce Harper's 13-year, $330 million agreement with the Philadelphia Phillies came at the end of that same month. A player's timing tends to reflect what clubs want to spend, what rival bidders can offer and whether a draft-pick penalty is attached to his name.

The rules set the order of events, but they set no signing deadline. A free agent can stay unsigned past Opening Day while he waits for a better offer.

A qualifying offer is a one-year contract that a club can extend to its own departing free agent. The salary equals the average of the 125 highest player salaries in the majors. It was $20.325 million for the 2023 offseason and $21.05 million for both the 2024 and 2025 offseasons.

The figure is recalculated every year because it follows the top of the salary market. Every player who receives an offer that winter is offered the same amount. A club cannot adjust it for age, position or performance. The same salary can sit well above one player's market value and well below another's.

The timing is fixed. A club must extend the offer within five days after the World Series ends. The player then has 10 days to accept or reject it. If he accepts, he returns for one season at that salary. If he rejects it, he enters the open market with draft compensation attached to his name.

Eligibility has limits. The player must have spent the entire season with the club that makes the offer. A player traded at the deadline therefore cannot receive one from his new team. A player can also receive a qualifying offer only once in his career. Any later trip through free agency comes without the tag.

The system dates to the 2012 collective bargaining agreement. It replaced the older Type A and Type B rankings, which used statistical formulas to sort free agents and set compensation. Under the current rule, the club chooses which players to tag.

Clubs weigh two risks. If the player accepts, the club owes a large one-year salary. That commitment can strain a tight payroll. If the club does not make the offer, it gives up any claim to compensation when the player leaves. Teams with room to spend often tag players they expect to depart. Teams with thin budgets tend to hold back when an acceptance looks plausible.

Players face the reverse calculation. Accepting guarantees a high salary for one season. It also returns him to free agency the next winter, when no tag is possible. Rejecting opens the door to multiyear offers. It also adds a draft-pick cost that any signing club must absorb.

Most players who receive the offer decline it. No player accepted until November 2015, when Matt Wieters, Colby Rasmus and Brett Anderson each took that winter's $15.8 million offer. Players who accept have tended to be those facing questions about their age, injury history or demand on the open market.

A club that loses a free agent after making him a qualifying offer receives an extra pick in the next amateur draft if he signs with another team. The new club pays nothing to the old one directly. The reward comes only as a draft selection.

Compensation applies only when the player rejects that offer, the one-year contract his club can extend after the season. Two outcomes produce no pick at all. If the player re-signs with his former club, nothing is owed. If he accepts the offer, he stays under contract, and again there is no compensation.

Two factors decide where the extra pick lands. One is the size of the player's new contract. The other is the financial status of the club that lost him. The $50 million dividing line arrived with the 2016 collective bargaining agreement, and it remained in place under the 2022 agreement.

Under that framework, a club that receives revenue sharing gets a pick right after the first round if the departing player signs for $50 million or more. If he signs for less, its pick comes after Competitive Balance Round B. A club that paid the competitive balance tax in the previous season receives a pick only after the fourth round. Clubs in neither group receive a pick after Competitive Balance Round B.

The placement matters because of how the draft pays out. The draft now runs 20 rounds. Every selection in the first 10 rounds carries an assigned slot value. Those values add up to a club's bonus pool, the total it can spend to sign its picks. An earlier pick means a larger slot value, a bigger pool and an earlier choice among the available prospects.

The 2022 agreement added a lottery that sets the first six selections among clubs that missed the playoffs. Compensation picks sit outside that lottery. They are slotted into fixed positions after the rounds described above.

The system has changed shape before. When the qualifying offer began under the 2012 agreement, compensation picks were placed after the first round, with no payroll-based tiers. The tiered structure that followed ties a club's reward partly to its finances. Revenue-sharing recipients receive the most valuable placements. Tax-paying clubs receive the least valuable ones.

The compensation pick is a newly created selection. It is not taken from the team that signs the player. That club faces its own separate penalty. The two sides of the transaction run on different rules.

Compensation picks also cannot be traded. Only Competitive Balance Round picks can change hands in MLB. A club that earns compensation keeps the pick and makes the selection itself.

A club that signs a free agent who turned down a qualifying offer gives up draft picks and, in most cases, international bonus pool money. The size of that cost depends on the signing club's payroll status. Clubs that pay the competitive balance tax lose the most.

The tiered penalty dates to the 2016 collective bargaining agreement, which took effect for the 2016-17 offseason. The 2022 agreement largely kept it in place. A club that receives revenue sharing forfeits its third-highest pick in the next draft. A club that neither receives revenue sharing nor pays the tax forfeits its second-highest pick. It also loses $500,000 of international bonus pool money. A club that paid the tax in the prior season forfeits its second- and fifth-highest picks, along with $1 million of pool money.

The earlier rules worked differently. Under the 2012 agreement, which governed the 2012-13 through 2015-16 offseasons, a club that signed a tagged free agent generally lost its first-round pick. If that pick fell in the top 10, the club lost its second-round pick instead. That approach put a club's highest available selection at stake, regardless of its payroll.

The tiered system did not remove friction from the market. A club may value a tagged player less once it counts the lost selection. Pitcher Dallas Keuchel and closer Craig Kimbrel both rejected qualifying offers after the 2018 season. Neither signed during the winter or spring. Both agreed to deals on June 7, 2019, after that year's draft had begun. By then, a signing club no longer had to surrender picks for them. Keuchel joined the Atlanta Braves, and Kimbrel joined the Chicago Cubs.

The international bonus pool is the second half of the cost. Each club receives a pool of money for signing amateur players who are not subject to the draft. Those players come from outside the U.S., Canada and Puerto Rico. Losing part of that pool narrows a club's options in the next international signing period.

Clubs can offset the loss only in limited ways. Most draft picks cannot be traded under MLB rules. Picks in the Competitive Balance Rounds are the exception. International bonus pool space can also change hands in trades. A club that forfeited pool money can acquire more from another team to restore its spending room.

An opt-out clause lets a player walk away from the remaining years of his contract after a set season and return to free agency. The player alone makes the choice, typically shortly after the World Series ends.

Each clause is negotiated deal by deal. Some large contracts include one, and others do not. A player who declines to use it keeps playing under the original terms.

For a player, the clause offers a second shot at the market. He may outperform his salary, or prices around the league may climb after he signs. In either case he can leave guaranteed money behind in search of a larger deal. If he gets hurt or struggles, he stays and collects what he was promised.

That imbalance makes the clause a cost for the club. The team carries the risk of a declining player. It also risks losing one who plays well. A club may agree to the clause in exchange for a lower total commitment or other concessions in the deal.

Carlos Correa's contract with the Minnesota Twins shows the clause at work in a short deal. In March 2022 he signed for three years and $105.3 million, with opt-outs after the first and second seasons. He exercised the first one after the 2022 season and returned to the market.

Alex Rodriguez used one on a much larger contract. His 10-year, $252 million deal, first signed with the Texas Rangers, allowed him to opt out after the 2007 season. He did, and then re-signed with the New York Yankees for 10 years and $275 million.

A player who opts out enters free agency on the same footing as other players. If he meets the eligibility conditions, his former club can extend a qualifying offer and attach draft compensation to his signing.

Clubs can act before the deadline arrives. A team can offer extra years or more money to persuade a player to give up the clause. It can also plan its payroll around his likely departure.

The clause changes how a contract's length should be read. A five-year deal with an opt-out after the third season may work like a three-year deal if the player performs well. Contracts and transaction reports sometimes describe a similar right as a player option, and the two labels overlap in practice. The key details are who holds the decision and which years it covers.

An option is an extra contract year that one side, or both, can choose to keep or decline. The player holds a player option, the team holds a club option, and a mutual option requires agreement from both. Club and mutual options usually carry a buyout that is paid if the year is declined.

These decisions come shortly after the World Series, in the same early-offseason stretch when clubs handle the rest of their roster business. A declined option, from either side, sends the player into the market without a contract.

A player option puts the choice in the player's hands. If he believes he can earn more on the open market, he declines it and becomes a free agent. If he would rather keep the guaranteed salary, he exercises it and stays. A player coming off an injury or a weak season has a clear financial reason to keep the money.

A club option reverses that control. The team can pick up the year at the stated salary, or it can decline the year and pay the buyout. Clubs often decline when the salary is higher than the value they expect from the player. Picking up the option does not commit the team to keeping him. Clubs sometimes exercise an option and then trade the player. Club options also appear often in early-career extensions. There, they give a team control over what would have been a player's first free agent seasons.

A mutual option requires both sides to want the same thing at the same time. A player who has performed well tends to prefer the market. A club whose player has struggled tends to prefer the buyout. As a result, these options are rarely exercised, and the buyout often becomes the payment that matters.

The buyout is a fixed sum written into the contract. It counts as guaranteed money, so a club that declines an option still pays something. It also counts toward the contract's guaranteed value.

Vesting options add a performance trigger. The extra year becomes guaranteed automatically if the player reaches a target in the prior season, such as a set number of innings pitched or plate appearances. That structure shields a club from paying for an injured player's final year while still paying a healthy one.

Options also affect how headline figures read. A deal reported as seven years may include a club option on the final season, which means only six are guaranteed. The contract terms show how many years a team actually owes.

The competitive balance tax charges clubs whose payrolls pass a set threshold. Under the 2022 collective bargaining agreement, that threshold was $230 million for 2022 and $241 million for 2025. Clubs pay tax on every dollar above it.

The rate climbs with repetition. A club over the threshold for the first time pays 20 percent on the excess. A second consecutive year raises the rate to 30 percent. A third or later year raises it to 50 percent. Clubs that exceed the threshold by wider margins also pay surcharges on top of those base rates.

The higher rates do not last forever. A club that finishes a season under the threshold returns to the first-time rate the next time it goes over. The New York Yankees stayed under the 2018 threshold, which reset their rate after years as a repeat payer.

The tax payroll is built from average annual value, not the cash a player receives in a given season. A club takes the total guaranteed money in a contract, including any signing bonus, and divides it by the number of years. That figure counts against the threshold in every season of the deal. A front office can calculate a signing's tax effect before it agrees to terms.

Deferred money changes that calculation. In December 2023, Shohei Ohtani agreed to a 10-year, $700 million contract with the Los Angeles Dodgers. He is scheduled to receive $2 million a year during the contract. The remaining $680 million is deferred and paid from 2034 through 2043. Deferred dollars are discounted to present value for tax purposes. The deal counts about $46 million a year against the Dodgers' tax payroll.

The penalties reach beyond cash. A club whose payroll sits $40 million or more above the first threshold has its highest draft pick moved back 10 places in the next amateur draft. If that pick falls among the first six selections, the club's second-highest pick moves back instead.

The threshold also shapes the market below the stars. A club just under it may pass on a mid-tier free agent whose salary would push its payroll over. A club with room beneath the line can absorb the same contract without a tax bill.

Salary arbitration sets pay for players with three to six years of major league service time. A non-tender sends a player to free agency before he reaches the six-year mark. Both processes shape which players are available each winter.

A player in the arbitration window cannot sign with any club he chooses. His team still controls his rights. He can, however, ask a neutral panel to set his salary when he and the club disagree.

The format is known as final-offer arbitration. The player and the club each submit one salary figure, typically in January. If they do not settle, a panel of three arbitrators chooses one of the two numbers at a hearing. The panel cannot split the difference or pick a figure in between.

That all-or-nothing design raises the cost of an unrealistic filing. A club that files too low risks losing the hearing outright. A player who files too high faces the same risk. Most cases settle before anyone enters a hearing room. Hearings that do go forward are held in February. The panel weighs factors such as the player's performance and the salaries of comparable players.

Some players reach arbitration a year early under a rule known as Super Two. The group includes players with at least two years of service but fewer than three. To qualify, a player must rank in the top 22 percent of that group in service time. He must also have accrued at least 86 days of service in the previous season. A Super Two player gets four arbitration years instead of three. His salary begins to climb a year sooner as a result.

The tender deadline in November is where arbitration and free agency meet. By that date, a club must offer a contract to every arbitration-eligible or otherwise reserved player it wants to keep. A player who does not receive one is non-tendered. He becomes a free agent immediately, with no qualifying offer or draft compensation attached.

Clubs non-tender players for practical reasons. A projected arbitration salary may exceed what the club thinks the player is worth. A player may be recovering from an injury. A club may need to clear a spot on its 40-man roster. A non-tendered player can sign with any team, including the one that let him go.

The non-tender list adds a group of established major leaguers to the market weeks before the Winter Meetings. Unlike veterans who reached six years of service, many of these players are still in their arbitration years when they hit the open market.

A separate group arrives through minor league free agency. These players have spent a set number of seasons in the minors without being added to a 40-man roster. Most sign minor league contracts with new organizations. Some of those deals include invitations to major league spring training.

The posting system lets a Nippon Professional Baseball club make one of its players available to MLB teams before he reaches international free agency. If a major league team signs him, that team pays a release fee to his Japanese club.

The decision to post belongs to the NPB club, not the player. Once a player is posted, he has a 45-day window to negotiate with major league teams. Every MLB club can make an offer. If no deal is reached before the window closes, he returns to his Japanese team for the coming season.

The release fee is tied to the size of the contract and rises on a sliding scale. It equals 20 percent of the first $25 million, 17.5 percent of the next $25 million and 15 percent of any amount above $50 million. The signing team pays the fee on top of the contract. It does not come out of the player's salary.

Posting offers an earlier route than waiting out a career in Japan. NPB players can become international free agents on their own after a set number of years of service with their clubs. A Japanese club that expects to lose a star may post him a year or two early. That way it collects a fee instead of watching him leave without one.

Pitcher Yoshinobu Yamamoto took this route. The Orix Buffaloes posted him, and he agreed to a 12-year, $325 million contract with the Los Angeles Dodgers in December 2023. The fee went to Orix and was not part of the contract total.

Age and experience change the rules. A foreign professional must be at least 25 and have six seasons in a recognized foreign league to sign a standard free agent deal. A younger or less experienced player counts as an international amateur. His signing bonus is limited by each club's international bonus pool.

The Hokkaido Nippon-Ham Fighters posted Shohei Ohtani in 2017. He was younger than 25, so he came to the majors under those amateur limits when he signed with the Los Angeles Angels. He then completed six years of major league service. After the 2023 season, he entered free agency as an established player with no bonus pool restrictions.
