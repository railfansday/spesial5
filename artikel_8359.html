Andrew Skurka on How to Prevent and Treat Blisters

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

This article is adapted from Andrew Skurka’s video for The Trek’s YouTube channel, part of a weekly series. It has been edited and condensed for clarity and readability.

Blisters are a fact of life for backpackers, and at their worst, they can totally shut down trips.

I know a thing or two about blisters. I used to be an avid thru-hiker. I’ve walked about 30,000 miles, including the Sea-to-Sea Route (C2C), the Great Western Loop, and the Alaska-Yukon Expedition. For the last 16 years, I’ve also been running a guided backpacking service, so I’ve been taking care of my feet and other people’s feet as well.

When it comes to blisters, an ounce of prevention is worth a pound of cure. Here are five things you can do before leaving home.

Start testing your footwear on low-risk outings. By footwear, I mean both your shoes and your socks. They need to work together for your feet and the conditions in which you’ll be hiking.

Start with a short hike, build up to a full day, and then try a one-night trip. Once you’re confident that a particular combination works, take it on longer backpacking trips.

Once you find shoes and socks that work for you, buy extra pairs if you can. Six months or a year from now, the manufacturer may have changed the fit, fabrics, outsole, or padding.

In my case, I’ve used DeFeet Wooleator socks for 25 years. When I learned they were no longer being made, I bought eight new pairs. I believe those were the only size larges left on the entire internet. That should give me another two or three years before I need to find new socks.

I’ve also been a longtime user of the La Sportiva Bushido. It’s changed a little over time, but it works great for me. That’s one less thing to think about before a trip.

Go get a pedicure if you want, or grab your toenail clippers after a shower. Get rid of the extra length so your toenails don’t bang into the front of your shoes.

The places I most often see calluses are along the side of the big toe and around the rim of the heel. I also see people develop blisters underneath those calluses.

My approach is to carefully remove excess callused skin with a callus remover before a trip. I’m trying to keep that thick patch of skin from moving as one piece. Don’t do this the night before you leave; give your feet time to readjust.

If you know you always get blisters in a particular spot, preemptively tape that area. You’re better off protecting it at home than dealing with a blister once you’re on the trail.

Once you’re in the field, keep up the prevention routine. Get your feet warm and dry at night; I cover this in more detail in 6 Tips for Dry, Healthy Feet.

During the day, take shoeless breaks. If you’re stopping for longer than about 15 minutes, take off your shoes and socks and let some fresh air circulate around your feet.

Inspect your feet regularly, too. I’m often surprised by how many people don’t discover a blister until they get into their tent at night. Their feet already feel tender from hiking all day, so they haven’t noticed a particular problem developing.

Address hot spots as soon as you feel them. A hot spot might feel warm, irritated, or like a pebble in your shoe that stays in the same place.

It’s tempting to think, “Camp is only an hour away. Everyone’s hungry and tired, and I don’t want to interrupt the group. I’ll deal with it there.” But in that hour, a minor hot spot can become a blister.

Do yourself and everyone in your group a favor: stop and take care of it. Once you have a blister, you’re trying to manage something that’s already sore and may get worse.

My blister supplies are part of my broader first aid, foot care, and repair kit.

If you’re a solo thru-hiker, the small scissors on something like a Victorinox Classic can work. If you’re responsible for a group, you’ll want something more substantial. I carry children’s school scissors: not quite trauma shears, but easier to use than tiny multitool scissors.

The cornerstone of my foot care kit is nonstretch Leukotape.

If you don’t need a whole roll, save the backing paper from a sheet of address labels. Run strips of Leukotape along that backing, then cut them out. You can cut patches to size, round the corners, and peel off the backing without touching the adhesive with your fingers.

I avoid wrapping Leukotape around trekking poles or other objects the way people often carry duct tape. In my experience, rolling it onto itself compromises the adhesive. The label backing makes it much easier to handle.

Leukotape is extremely sticky. Once it’s on your feet, it comes off on its own schedule. Be careful removing it, and don’t put the adhesive directly over a blister: it can pull off the blister’s top layer or the fragile skin beneath it.

I carry a balm, such as Badger Balm, to help reduce friction. I also rub a little onto the outside of Leukotape after applying it. The tape’s adhesive can work through the fabric and stick to a sock; the balm helps prevent that.

I don’t use duct tape for foot care very often, but it’s slippery. I sometimes consider it for friction-related problems when I’m not concerned about moisture collecting beneath it.

Gauze covers the blister itself so the tape doesn’t stick directly to it. Tincture of benzoin towelettes help the tape stick when feet are wet, conditions are humid, or the tape is going on a particularly high-stress area.

I carry a needle for sewing and, when I decide to drain a blister, for that purpose as well. Cleaning the instrument beforehand is essential.

The knife has a more drastic use. If someone has a heel blister that won’t get relief, or pressure around the Achilles tendon, I sometimes cut the back of the shoe’s heel cup to reduce that pressure.

This permanently changes the shoe and reduces its support. I make a small cut, have the person walk around, and check for relief before cutting farther. There may be stiff plastic in the heel cup, so cutting it requires care.

I’m not a medical doctor. These are techniques I’ve learned through trial and error in the field, working with actual people and actual feet. Your mileage may vary.

These are the common blister locations I see and how I approach taping each one.

Blisters on the back of the heel are usually associated with friction against the shoe. Cutting the heel cup is an aggressive place to start, so I generally begin with tape.

I cut a strip of Leukotape to fit the hot spot or blister and round the corners so they’re less likely to peel up. A larger area may need two or three strips.

If conditions are wet or I’m concerned about the tape moving, I apply a benzoin towelette where the tape will stick. If there’s already a blister, I cover it with a small piece of gauze before applying the tape.

I use my scissors to help peel the tape from its backing without touching the adhesive. The gauze should cover the blister without sticking out from beneath the tape.

Blisters under a callus on the side of the big toe are common. I’m wary of trimming a callus in the field, so I usually leave it alone and focus on limiting its movement.

A small patch of tape is unlikely to stay put under that much pressure. I typically wrap the toe instead.

Even for my size 11.5 feet, a full-width strip is too wide, so I trim it narrower. I aim to put the overlapping seam on top of the toe, where it’s less likely to peel than underneath.

If the tape bunches around a curve, I make small cuts where it folds so I can press it flat without wrinkles.

I use the same approach as for the big toe: cut a strip long enough to wrap the affected toe, trim it to roughly half the roll’s width, and apply it with the tape lying flat.

These blisters are tough. The skin is thicker here, and there’s often a callus involved. It’s also a high-stress area, so a small piece of tape may peel off as you walk.

I use a larger piece that extends up the sides of the heel toward the ankle, where there’s less pressure working against it. Wherever the tape starts to fold, I cut it so I can press it flat against the skin.

After taping any of these areas, I rub a small dab of balm onto the outside of the tape to help keep it from sticking to my sock.

I’ve only seen this a few times, but a blister on the ball of the foot might be the worst one. I don’t have a great fix. The goal is to take pressure off the blister by creating a donut around it.

I don’t carry moleskin. If I needed to make a donut, one option I would consider is marking the blister’s location on the hiker’s insole, then cutting a corresponding hole in the insole. The idea is for the blister to sit inside that opening, with pressure distributed around it.

If you develop a blister here, think about shortening your days or your trip. It’s a painful spot and difficult to manage once a blister has formed.

I generally lean toward draining blisters because relieving the fluid pressure can make walking less painful. But opening a blister exposes the fragile skin underneath and introduces a risk of infection. As I mentioned earlier, I’m not a medical professional.

When I decide to drain one, I clean the instrument first and consider where fluid is being pushed as the person steps. I want the opening positioned so it can continue to drain.

Afterward, I wash the area with soap and water and leave the skin over the blister in place. Don’t peel that layer off; let it come off on its own.

If the tape comes off easily, I generally remove it and let the foot dry overnight.

If the tape is fresh and removing it would take skin with it, I leave it alone. I still try to get the foot dry, using dedicated sleeping socks or sleeping without socks, depending on the conditions.

Increasing redness around a blister and pain that gets worse each day are warning signs I watch for. If you suspect an infection, get off the trail and seek medical care. Staying on the trail and hoping it improves is not a good option.
