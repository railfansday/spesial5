Working mothers face widespread discrimination in London’s commercial art world, report finds

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Kate McMillan (left) and Cleo Roberts Komireddi (right) whose research informed the report

The contemporary art world has become increasingly adept at putting feminism, gender equality and the politics of care at the centre of its public-facing programming. Galleries mount exhibitions exploring women's experiences, champion female artists and engage with questions of representation and social justice. But how far do those values extend to the women working behind the scenes?

Not far at all, according to the first ever peer-reviewed study on motherhood and discrimination in London’s commercial art world, published today by the European Journal of Cultural Studies and The Art Working Parents Alliance.

Based on a survey of 50 art world employees working across the UK capital’s galleries and auction houses, including 12 in-depth interviews, research by Kate McMillan, an artist and academic based at King's College London, and the art historian and broadcaster Cleo Roberts-Komireddi exposes a workplace culture in which mothering children often comes at considerable professional cost. As the report puts it: “Becoming a parent is at the root of the glass ceiling, and for many, it represents the glass cliff.”

Three-quarters of survey respondents say becoming a parent has negatively affected their career progression, while more than one in five say they had been explicitly denied promotion. One woman was told she was ineligible for promotion while on maternity leave—a breach of UK law. Just 54% received enhanced maternity pay, compared with a national workplace average of 74.1%.

And these problems are not limited to smaller companies. “We assumed this would affect small art galleries with between five and 20 staff, but we found that big, multi-national galleries are also failing,” McMillan says. As the report notes: “A lack of accountability pervades the sector.”

Roberts-Komireddi believes that the “culture of secrecy” that enshrouds much of the art market—where financial transactions, benefits and networks are opaque and rarely disclosed—contributes to the discrimination women face when they become mothers. “Opacity really serves certain individuals and maintains this sense of fear, which was something I found particularly startling when we did our 12 in-depth interviews,” she says. “Fear and perceived retribution pervade these workplaces and women’s relationships to these workplaces.”

That fear is often felt as soon as women announce their pregnancies. The report documents, among other instances, a pregnant woman being made redundant while experiencing a high-risk pregnancy, an employer attempting to dissolve a pregnant employee’s role and use the money to hire a new salesperson and a woman who miscarried returning to her desk immediately after a hospital appointment because she feared taking time off would damage her career. One interviewee describes feeling that by becoming pregnant she was making herself “vulnerable and less valuable”.

The findings also point to a deeper contradiction within an industry that publicly champions equality but often operates according to an unforgiving model of constant availability, international travel and after-hours networking. The art critic and curator Hettie Judah, who is a co-founder of The Art Working Parents Alliance, describes the colliding of 6pm private views with bedtime routines as the “witching hour” clash.

The difficulties are not confined to maternity leave itself. For many, the return to work can prove more damaging. Interviewees describe coming back to find their responsibilities have been redistributed, their relationships with artists and clients disrupted, or their standing within the gallery diminished. One woman discovered that a colleague who had covered her maternity leave had been given half her role, along with the artists she had spent years representing. Another returned from leave to find there was no desk for her in the gallery’s new office.

Crucially, the report reveals the lack of formal HR structures across the commercial art sector. Only half of the respondents worked for galleries with dedicated HR staff, while just 30 of the 50 said their workplace had a specific maternity or parental leave policy. In some cases, employees were left to research their own entitlements, negotiate their packages and even draft policies themselves. The report’s authors say that a lack of professional HR expertise contributed to instances of discriminatory practice and breaches of the UK Equality Act.

The consequences can be far-reaching. For many women, the choice has been stark: find a way to accommodate the demands of motherhood or compromise their careers. Three-quarters of the women interviewed made changes to their careers after becoming pregnant, with some moving into freelance or independent work, taking less senior positions or leaving the industry altogether. Only a third of survey respondents were offered flexible working on their return, while half were expected to be in the office four or five days a week. One interviewee described the culture as one in which “presentism is more important than doing the work”.

The role of class and wealth is key in shaping these experiences. The report notes how some senior figures have access to live-in nannies, au pairs and domestic help, leaving them less attuned to the financial and practical pressures facing employees who must balance childcare with demanding working hours. One interviewee stated that the management team “all had night nurses, and you know, people helping at night. So, for them to understand that you’d had three hours of interrupted sleep and were expected to be at an opening, and a dinner, get home at 12:30 and then get up at 4:30 again, was impossible”.

For those on more modest salaries, the cost of childcare can make evening events and travel particularly difficult to sustain. The report suggests that the more junior and precarious the employee, the greater the likelihood of experiencing discrimination—and the fewer resources they have to challenge it.

These divisions are not simply a question of wealth, however. They also complicate assumptions about gender solidarity within an industry where women make up a significant proportion of the workforce, but senior positions are often occupied by men. As Roberts-Komireddi points out, the assumption that women will necessarily support other women can be misleading—some of the more surprising testimonies in the report describe male directors showing greater sympathy towards pregnant employees than their female counterparts.

These accounts suggest that the treatment of working mothers is shaped not simply by the gender of those in positions of authority, but by a wider workplace culture in which employees have little power to challenge established practices. Indeed, the report argues that discriminatory treatment is widespread, identifying breaches of the Equality Act in the experiences of all the women interviewed. Yet legal cases rarely make it to an employment tribunal. “In the UK, up until 1 October this year, women only had three months to bring a case to tribunal, which is not a lot of time, particularly if you’re about to give birth,” Roberts-Komireddi says. “There’s a reluctance to go down that route because it’s a long, tiring, expensive process.”

Looking ahead, the report’s authors hope to set best practice in the art industry. “If you look at huge corporations like Google, they have changed their policies in order to retain and attract mothers who make excellent staff—we could learn a lot from them,” Roberts-Komireddi says.
