Rockets must choose carefully in strange 2027 NBA Draft

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

If the 2026-27 NBA season ended today, the Houston Rockets would have roughly a 64% chance of landing a top-five pick.

I thought it was roughly 80%. The Brooklyn Nets and Phoenix Suns both have 40%-ish odds; add them together, 40%.

I was, as is so often the case, corrected on X. If each independent event has a 40% chance of happening, each has a 60% chance of not happening. So anyway, long and short, accounting for that, it's about 64%.

Remember when Rafael Stone said not to judge the James Harden trade until 2030? Well, we're almost in 2027, and it feels pretty safe to credit Stone's vision. The Rockets are 12-4; it's nearly certain that they'll have a lottery pick (and probably two) this summer, and there's a pretty good chance one of those picks lands in the top five. Life is good.

There's always a catch, isn't there? The Rockets draft one of the best centers of all time in the same draft that the best player of all time is drafted in. They change NBA offense, but another team changes it even more. Whatever.

The catch is that this draft is widely viewed as weak. If you've researched it, you've likely come to the same conclusion. There's Kansas' Tyran Stokes, and then…yeah.

At least, the list of (nearly) surefire NBA stars begins and ends with Stokes. That doesn't mean there aren't quality players in the draft:

It means Rafael Stone will have to dig for them.

Caveat One: We're early in the draft cycle. It's entirely possible that someone whose name isn't in the most thorough Excel file on the most obsessive aspiring internet scout's laptop.

Caveat Two: I wrote a whole piece about how the Rockets should be looking at Baba Oladotun, and he's averaging a red-hot 4, 2, and 1. Have your grain of salt on hand.

Right now, I'm fixated on two particular prospects. Illinois' Quentin Coleman is a 6'4″ combo guard. I'm convinced he's got a distinctly positive wingspan – I'd estimate 6'8″ – but Google has no idea. He's averaging 17.8 points, 6.8 rebounds, and 4.4 assists while shooting 55.6% from the field and 37.0% from three-point range on 5.4 attempts per game.

The cool thing about Coleman is that he's a lead ball-handler who plays with the energy of a role player. He reminds me of a young Lonzo Ball, with maybe a bit more on-ball juice and a bit less defense.

The other is Ivan Kharchenkov, a 6'7″, 230-pound wing on Arizona. Kharchenkov almost entered the draft last year. He's an outstanding, aggressive perimeter defender with some ancillary skills: He can pass and dribble some. The problem with Kharchenkov has always been his low three-point volume, which never bodes well for an NBA role player.

This year, Kharchenkov is forcing us to ask a question: What if he isn't a role player? He's averaging 22.6 points, 8.0 rebounds, and 4.6 assists per game.

There are concerns about Kharchenkov. He's still shooting just 4.0 threes per game. It's also been suggested that his strength-based shot creation won't work in the NBA, where men are stronger. If he's forced to fend for himself, Kharchenkov tends to muscle into his defender with a few dribbles to create space for his mid-range.

Those are my guys. You may have your own. The broader point is this: Very few "bad" NBA drafts are as bad as they were perceived to be in hindsight. It does happen, but more often, there are stars hiding in plain sight. At this convergence of fortune and strategy, all the Rockets can do is choose carefully. It could be the difference between finding the would-be best team in the NBA's missing piece, and adding another dud to rot away in the 8th man spot until he's trade fodder.

For now, let's wait and see what the Draft Gods decide.
