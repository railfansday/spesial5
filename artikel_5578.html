Raw eggs in eggnog can carry salmonella — here is how to lower the risk

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

Free daily briefing on global business news.

A guide to whether homemade eggnog is safe, how raw eggs can carry salmonella and how cooking, pasteurized eggs and cold storage lower the risk

Eggnog's thick, custardy body comes from eggs, and many traditional recipes never put those eggs near a stove. Yolks are beaten with sugar, whisked into milk and cream and poured straight into punch cups. That method gives the drink its texture. It also makes a holiday staple a raw-egg drink.

So is homemade eggnog safe? It can be, and the answer depends almost entirely on how the eggs are handled. Raw and undercooked eggs can carry salmonella, a type of bacteria that causes foodborne illness. The chance that any single egg is contaminated is lower than many people assume. It is not zero, though. A punch bowl also spreads one batch across a whole room of guests.

The stakes are not the same for everyone. The CDC and FDA identify certain groups who are more likely to become seriously ill from salmonella. Those groups often share the same table at holiday gatherings. A drink that poses little concern for one guest may be a poor choice for another.

The practical fixes are simple and well established. Heating the egg mixture to the right temperature reduces the risk. So does swapping in pasteurized eggs, keeping the finished drink cold or buying a ready-made carton. None of these steps requires giving up the drink. A few common assumptions do not hold up, however. An egg that looks perfect on the outside can still be contaminated inside. Spiking the bowl with bourbon or rum does not settle the question either.

This guide walks through what raw eggs mean for holiday drinks, one question at a time. It explains how salmonella ends up inside an egg and why eggnog deserves particular attention. It covers who should be cautious, what an infection looks like and how soon symptoms tend to appear. It sets out the cooking temperature the USDA recommends for egg-based custards. It also explains why a thermometer matters more than a spoon test. Later sections look at pasteurized eggs, what alcohol does and does not do, how long eggnog can sit out and lower-risk options from the store. Each section stands on its own, so readers can jump to the question they need answered.

Eggnog made with uncooked eggs is a raw-egg food. It carries the same salmonella risk as any dish built on raw eggs. That risk is low, but it is not zero.

The risk comes from how the drink is traditionally made. Classic recipes add a spirit such as rum, bourbon or brandy to the egg and dairy mixture. Some versions also beat the egg whites separately and fold them in for a lighter, frothier texture. At no point in those recipes does the mixture go on the stove. It goes from the mixing bowl to the refrigerator or straight to the table.

That puts uncooked eggnog in a different category from most egg-rich desserts. Crème anglaise, baked custard, flan and crème brûlée all start with a similar mix of eggs, sugar and dairy. Each of them is heated before it is served. Raw-egg eggnog skips that step entirely. Whatever was inside the eggs ends up in the glass.

That scale matters. A holiday batch combines several eggs in a single bowl or pitcher, and party-size recipes use more. Once the eggs are mixed together, the whole batch depends on every egg that went into it. If one egg is contaminated, the bacteria can spread through the entire mixture. They do not stay confined to one serving.

The batch is then poured out over an evening, often refilled cup by cup as guests come and go. Every person who drinks from it shares the same exposure. One contaminated egg can reach every guest who takes a serving, not only the person whose cup it happened to land in.

The flavor and texture people expect from eggnog do not require leaving the eggs raw. Recipes that heat the egg base or use pasteurized eggs lower the risk. Versions made without eggs remove the raw-egg question altogether.

An egg with a clean, uncracked shell can still carry salmonella inside it. The bacteria can infect a hen's ovaries or reproductive tract and reach the egg before its shell forms.

This route bypasses everything a cook can see. Most people judge an egg by its outside. They look for cracks, stains or traces of droppings, and they throw out anything that looks dirty. Those checks catch some problems. They cannot reveal bacteria already sealed inside the white or yolk.

The strain most closely tied to this kind of internal contamination is Salmonella Enteritidis. A hen infected with it can look and behave normally. She can keep laying eggs that appear ordinary, with no outward sign that anything is wrong.

An egg contaminated this way gives no warning in the kitchen either. It does not smell off, look discolored or taste different. Cracking it into a bowl shows a normal yolk and white. Nothing about its appearance separates it from the other eggs in the carton.

Shells can also pick up bacteria from the outside, through contact with droppings or a dirty nesting area. Washing addresses that surface problem. Most eggs sold commercially in the U.S. are washed before they reach stores. Washing cleans only the shell, though. It does nothing for contamination that began inside the hen.

The same reasoning applies to eggs from a backyard flock, a farmers market or a small farm. Fresh, local eggs are not automatically free of internal salmonella. Any laying hen can become infected, whatever the size of the operation or the way the birds are kept.

Refrigeration slows salmonella growth but does not solve the problem. Cold keeps a small number of bacteria from multiplying quickly. It does not kill the bacteria already present. An egg that went into the refrigerator contaminated comes out contaminated.

That is why the remedy for raw-egg recipes is heat rather than inspection. Cooking eggs to a safe temperature, or using pasteurized eggs, deals with bacteria wherever they sit — on the shell or inside the egg.

Young children, pregnant people, adults 65 and older, and people with weakened immune systems should skip eggnog made with raw eggs. The CDC and FDA identify these four groups as more likely to develop severe illness from salmonella.

The difference shows up in outcomes. A healthy adult who swallows contaminated eggnog may recover without treatment. Someone in a higher-risk group has a greater chance of an infection serious enough to need medical care.

Each group is vulnerable for its own reason. Young children have immune systems that are still developing. Older adults experience a gradual decline in immune function as they age. Pregnancy changes how the body's immune system responds to infection.

The fourth category is the broadest. It includes people receiving chemotherapy for cancer and people taking anti-rejection drugs after an organ transplant. It also includes people living with HIV. Anyone on long-term medication that suppresses the immune system, such as corticosteroids used for some autoimmune conditions, belongs in this group as well.

A single family gathering may include a toddler, a grandparent, a guest in early pregnancy and a relative in cancer treatment. Not every guest tells the host about a pregnancy or a diagnosis. A host pouring from one bowl often cannot know who in the room falls into a higher-risk group.

Making the entire batch with a cooked custard base or with pasteurized eggs removes that guesswork. Either approach means no guest has to be screened, questioned or steered away from the drink.

If a raw-egg version is served at all, clear labeling lets guests choose for themselves. A small card beside each pitcher can state whether the eggs were cooked, pasteurized or raw. Keeping the raw and cooked versions in visibly different containers helps avoid mix-ups. Young children often serve themselves at a punch bowl. A raw-egg batch belongs on a counter or shelf out of their reach.

Salmonella infection usually shows up as diarrhea, fever and stomach cramps, according to the CDC. The agency says those symptoms typically begin between six hours and six days after infection.

That window matters for anyone who drank eggnog made with raw eggs. Illness may start the same evening as a holiday party. It may also start nearly a week later, long after the punch bowl has been washed and put away.

The wide range makes it hard to connect a stomach illness to one glass of eggnog. A person who feels sick on a Tuesday may have eaten dozens of foods since the weekend. Holiday weeks often pack several gatherings, restaurant meals and leftovers into a few days. Any one of them could be the cause.

A simple record helps. Hosts who serve raw-egg eggnog can note which batch was poured on which day and who drank it. If several guests fall ill with the same symptoms, that shared detail is useful to a doctor or a local health department.

The three symptoms can be mistaken for other problems. Rich food and a seasonal stomach virus can both cause cramps or diarrhea. Fever points toward an infection rather than overeating. Symptoms alone still cannot confirm salmonella. A doctor can confirm it with a laboratory test of a stool sample.

Diarrhea drains fluid from the body. Drinking water or an oral rehydration solution helps replace what is lost.

An infected person can also pass salmonella to others. Washing hands with soap and water after using the bathroom and before handling food lowers that risk. The step carries extra weight in a house full of guests sharing one kitchen.

Severe symptoms call for medical care. Warning signs include a high fever, diarrhea that will not let up and blood in the stool. Signs of dehydration, such as passing little urine or feeling dizzy when standing, also need attention. Anyone whose illness is getting worse rather than better should contact a doctor.

Cooking the egg and milk base to 160°F (71°C) turns homemade eggnog from a raw-egg drink into a cooked custard. The USDA gives that target for egg mixtures such as custards. A cooked eggnog base is a stirred custard. The same target applies.

That makes cooked eggnog a close relative of crème anglaise, the pourable French custard sauce. Both start with the same core ingredients: egg yolks, sugar and milk. Both are cooked on the stovetop while being stirred, rather than baked until firm.

The process begins with the dairy. Warm the milk in a saucepan until it steams, without letting it boil. In a separate bowl, whisk the yolks with the sugar until the mixture lightens in color and thickens slightly.

The next step is tempering. Pour a small amount of the hot milk into the yolks while whisking constantly, then add more in stages. This raises the temperature of the eggs gradually. Warmed this way, they are less likely to curdle when they meet the rest of the hot liquid. Then pour the yolk mixture back into the saucepan.

From here, heat control matters most. A heavy-bottomed saucepan spreads heat more evenly than a thin one. That reduces hot spots on the pan floor. A double boiler — a bowl set over simmering water — gives even gentler heat. It takes longer but leaves less room for error.

Keep the burner at low to medium-low and stir without stopping. A silicone spatula or wooden spoon can scrape the bottom and corners of the pan, where egg sets first. If the mixture gets too hot, the yolks can scramble and the texture turns grainy. The base thickens slowly as the egg proteins set.

Once the base reaches 160°F (71°C), take it off the heat. Pour it through a fine-mesh sieve to catch any bits of cooked egg. Stir in vanilla or grated nutmeg at this stage if the recipe calls for them.

Chill the base quickly. Setting the bowl inside a larger bowl of ice water speeds cooling. Pressing plastic wrap directly onto the surface keeps a skin from forming. Cold cream and any spirits are stirred in after the base has fully chilled.

One step in some recipes skips the heat entirely. Some traditional recipes fold in beaten egg whites at the end. Those whites never go through the cooking stage. They stay raw unless pasteurized whites are used.

Only a food thermometer can confirm that an eggnog base has reached 160°F (71°C), the USDA's target for egg mixtures such as custards. Thickening and a coating on the back of a spoon are not reliable substitutes.

The coat-the-spoon test comes from classic custard cooking. A cook dips a spoon into the pot, draws a finger across the back and watches whether the line holds. That check describes texture. It was never designed to measure food safety.

Texture and temperature do not track each other neatly in eggnog. Egg proteins thicken a liquid as they set, but sugar and milk dilute them. A sweeter or thinner base can thicken at a different point than a richer one. Two batches can look the same on a spoon and still sit at different temperatures.

Heat is also uneven inside a pot. Liquid near the bottom and sides runs hotter than liquid at the center or the surface. A spoon pulled from one spot shows what is happening there, not across the whole batch. Stirring for a few seconds before taking a reading evens out those zones.

Where the probe goes matters too. The tip should sit in the middle of the liquid, away from the bottom and walls of the pan. Touching hot metal gives a falsely high number. Digital instant-read models sense temperature near the tip. Dial models read along a longer stretch of the stem, so more of it needs to be submerged.

Give the display a moment to settle before trusting it. A reading taken while the number is still climbing understates the heat. If the base is short of 160°F, return it to the heat and check again.

A thermometer is only as useful as it is accurate. The ice-water method is a simple check. Fill a glass with crushed ice and cold water, stir and insert the probe without touching the glass. It should read 32°F (0°C). Many models can be adjusted if they drift, either with a reset button or a calibration nut under the dial.

Wash the probe in hot, soapy water after each use. That keeps it from carrying bacteria from one food to the next.

Pasteurized eggs let you keep a classic uncooked eggnog recipe while lowering the salmonella risk. Pasteurized shell eggs and pasteurized liquid egg products have been heat treated to reduce Salmonella. They are the standard substitute whenever a recipe calls for raw egg.

The process holds eggs at a controlled temperature long enough to reduce bacteria without cooking the egg. The yolk and white stay liquid. That matters for eggnog, where the drink depends on raw yolks blending smoothly into milk and cream. A pasteurized egg fills the same role in the recipe as an ordinary one.

The two main forms suit different cooks. Pasteurized shell eggs look and crack like regular eggs. They work well in recipes that separate yolks from whites, such as versions that fold whipped whites into the base for a lighter, foamier drink. Pasteurized liquid egg products come in cartons and pour straight into a bowl. They save time when a large batch calls for a dozen or more eggs.

Read the label before buying either one. Most shell eggs on U.S. supermarket shelves are not pasteurized, and the cartons can look nearly identical. Look for the word "pasteurized" printed on the package. If it is not there, treat the eggs as raw.

Liquid products need a closer look too. Some cartons contain whole eggs, while others hold only egg whites or a blend with added ingredients. An egg-white product will not give eggnog the richness or color that yolks provide. Check the ingredient list to confirm what is inside.

Expect a small difference in the kitchen. Pasteurized egg whites can take longer to whip into stiff peaks than untreated whites. A pinch of cream of tartar or a few extra minutes of beating usually helps.

Pasteurization lowers the risk, but it does not protect an egg from contamination once it is cracked or poured. Store pasteurized eggs in the refrigerator, use clean bowls and utensils, and treat the finished eggnog as a perishable drink.

The USDA says alcohol should not be relied on to make raw eggs safe in a drink. Bourbon, rum or brandy changes the flavor and strength of raw-egg eggnog. It does not reliably protect anyone from salmonella.

Liquor is often assumed to sterilize eggnog, and the idea has some basis. Alcohol can damage bacteria at high enough concentrations. Eggnog, however, is not a high-concentration drink. Spirits get poured into a much larger volume of milk, cream, sugar and eggs. Once everything is mixed, the alcohol in the finished drink is far more dilute than the alcohol in the bottle.

Recipes also vary widely. Some call for a heavy measure of liquor in every batch. Others use a splash for flavor, or leave the choice to each guest. Two bowls of eggnog at two different parties can carry very different amounts of alcohol. A home cook has no practical way to measure the strength of a finished batch.

Timing matters as well. Some recipes have the host mix the egg base first and add spirits just before serving. In that case, the raw eggs spend most of their time in the bowl with no alcohol at all. Uneven stirring can also leave some portions stronger than others.

Aged, spiked eggnog — batches mixed with liquor and left to sit in the refrigerator before serving — has drawn attention from food safety researchers. Even so, no proof level or aging period is an established guarantee that raw eggs in the mix are safe to drink.

There is also a practical gap at mixed gatherings. A host who spikes one bowl may make a second, alcohol-free bowl for children and drivers. If both start from the same raw-egg base, the plain version has no claimed protection at all. Children are among the groups the CDC and FDA consider at higher risk of severe salmonella illness.

The more reliable approach is to deal with the eggs themselves. A base cooked to 160°F (71°C) or made with pasteurized eggs lowers the raw-egg risk before any liquor enters the bowl. After that, bourbon, rum or brandy can go in purely for taste. The spirits become a flavor choice rather than a safety measure. The same base then works for both the spiked and the plain versions.

Eggnog is a perishable drink, and the USDA says it should not sit out for more than two hours. That limit drops to one hour when the air is above 90°F. The USDA also says refrigerators should hold 40°F (4°C) or below.

The two-hour clock starts when the eggnog leaves the refrigerator, not when the first guest pours a glass. Time on the counter during setup counts. So does time spent on a buffet table or carried from room to room.

The rule is tied to what the USDA calls the danger zone, the range between 40°F and 140°F. Bacteria grow most rapidly in that range. The USDA says their numbers can double in as little as 20 minutes. A punch bowl left at room temperature sits inside that zone.

Eggnog combines milk, cream, sugar and often eggs. That mix gives bacteria the moisture and nutrients they need. Even a cooked or pasteurized batch can pick up bacteria from ladles, cups and hands once it is served. Keeping it cold limits how fast those bacteria can multiply.

A serving bowl nested inside a larger bowl of ice keeps eggnog cooler on a table. The ice needs topping up as it melts. Another approach is to set out a small pitcher and keep the rest of the batch in the refrigerator. The pitcher can be refilled as needed instead of leaving the whole batch out.

Once the time limit passes, the USDA advice is to discard what is left. Pouring it back into the main container mixes warmed eggnog with the cold supply. The shorter one-hour limit matters at warm-weather events, such as outdoor Christmas gatherings in the Southern Hemisphere.

A refrigerator's dial setting does not show its actual temperature. The USDA recommends an appliance thermometer to confirm the refrigerator stays at 40°F or below. A holiday refrigerator that is packed with food and opened often can run warmer than its setting suggests.

Leftover eggnog belongs in covered containers that go into the refrigerator promptly. Shallow containers chill faster than one deep pitcher, a point the USDA makes about leftovers in general. A lid also keeps out drips and odors from other foods.

Commercial eggnog sold in U.S. grocery stores is made with pasteurized ingredients. That avoids the raw-egg risk of an uncooked homemade batch. Egg-free eggnog goes a step further and leaves eggs out of the recipe entirely.

Pasteurization is a heat treatment applied to the dairy and egg ingredients at a processing plant before the drink is sealed. It takes place under controlled conditions. That control is the main difference between a factory carton and a bowl mixed at home with fresh, uncooked eggs.

Store-bought eggnog sits in the refrigerated dairy case, usually near the milk and cream during the holiday season. The ingredient list typically names milk, cream, sugar and egg yolks, along with spices such as nutmeg. Light or reduced-fat versions replace some of the cream with lower-fat milk. The word pasteurized normally appears on the carton, and buyers can check for it before purchase.

A carton keeps its advantage only as long as no raw eggs go into it. Whisking fresh yolks or beaten raw egg whites into store-bought eggnog for a frothier texture changes that. The added eggs turn the drink back into a raw-egg food.

Egg-free versions come in two main forms. One is a dairy drink made with milk or cream and thickened without eggs. Cornstarch cooked into the milk is a common way to give it a custard-like body. The other is a plant-based nog made from almond, oat, soy or coconut milk. Both typically rely on nutmeg, cinnamon, vanilla and sugar to carry the familiar holiday flavor.

Egg-free nog also works for guests with an egg allergy. Pasteurization does not help that group. The heat treatment reduces bacteria, but the egg proteins that trigger allergic reactions remain in the drink. Anyone avoiding eggs for that reason should read the ingredient list and allergen statement on each package.

None of these options can sit out indefinitely once opened. Commercial dairy eggnog is perishable and belongs in the refrigerator after the seal is broken. Some plant-based cartons are sold unrefrigerated on the shelf. Even those usually need different handling after opening. The storage directions printed on each package spell out what that product requires.
