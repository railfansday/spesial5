Some people don’t use AI at work—and feel judged for it

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

New workplace fear unlocked—and it’s not being yelled at for heated-up fish in the office microwave or getting too merry at the Christmas party. 

As companies continue to invest billions in their AI strategies, nearly half of employees fear judgment for not using artificial intelligence enough at work.  

That’s according to the third annual Inclusion Initiative report from London School of Economics (LSE) and consulting firm Protiviti, which surveyed 3,001 professional employees between March and May 2026. With AI spending forecast to grow to $2.59 trillion this year—up 47% from 2025—the AI transition is no longer a personal choice, but is transforming workplace norms and expectations. 

When AI first entered workplaces, more workers feared judgment or being caught out for using the technology. Now the opposite is true: 48% worry about being judged for not using AI enough, compared with 20% who worry about being judged for using it. 

Almost three quarters of those surveyed (74%) now use AI at work, up from 29% in 2024. But the pressure to adopt AI in workflows has birthed a new kind of busywork—workers are pretending to use AI even when they aren’t. 

A separate report by software firm Visier found nearly half of workers (48%) said they have “exaggerated their AI usage or expertise to colleagues or leadership” to keep up with expectations.

While organizations understand the importance of equipping their workforces with AI tools, there is still work to be done when it comes to supporting employees with AI adoption. 

Only 39% of users have built AI into a standardized way of working, according to the LSE Inclusion Initiative and Protiviti report. Those who have reported saving 27% of time in their work week with AI, compared with 14% for those who haven’t. 

Gen Z is far more likely to have been AI-trained in the past year compared with their baby boomer colleagues. AI-trained employees report saving 22% of their working hours, compared with 13% for those left to figure it out alone. 

And what exactly happens to all that extra time saved by AI? More time for more work, of course. And it’s causing what researchers are calling “workload creep”. 

“AI is creating meaningful productivity gains, but the bigger opportunity is how organizations use the capacity it creates,” said Matt Duncan, managing director at Protiviti, in the press release. “Simply absorbing those gains into existing workloads leaves much of AI’s potential untapped.”

Researchers at Boston Consulting Group found that 14% of people who use AI workflows requiring a high degree of oversight experience “AI brain fry.” This mental fog and inability to focus can result in more mistakes at work, and even employees considering quitting altogether. 

AI or not, job performance is still dependent on people, at least for now. And the LSE and Protiviti report found no consistent association between an employee’s perceived performance and their AI adoption, access, training or workflow integration. Instead, performance stayed tied to confidence in solving problems, conscientiousness, resilience and willingness to learn.

As AI continues to become embedded in everyday work, organizations need to focus on whether it is actually helping employees be more efficient at work, or simply creating time to pile more work on their plates. 

“Leaders should think deliberately about where that capacity can create the most value, whether that means tackling higher-value work, improving quality or giving employees greater space to solve problems and innovate,” said Duncan. 

“The goal shouldn’t just be doing more work faster, but using AI to change what people are able to accomplish.”
