When the Days of Being Wild Come to an End

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

This week, after reading up about the major differences between sea salt and rock salt, I found myself looking at a shot of my mates in an east London restaurant’s smoking area. My friends looked happy—mildly drunk, joyous, their night still full of possibility—and I felt… hmmm… hard to say what I felt. Not jealous, not one bit, but also not relieved that I was home and dry. Maybe the word is content? Happy enough for them without longing for a sip from the same goblet.

It dawned on me that I’ve consciously uncoupled from chaos—an intense long-term relationship that, for many years, I considered an essential part of my personality. Anyone who knew me in or around the summer of 2005—the thrifted grandad shirts, the Kate Moss for Topshop skinny jeans—knows I liked a bit of disorder, a grubbiness to the glamour. We dressed to go out, we went out, we laughed and toasted to nothing and smashed our empty glasses down. I would take sunglasses to pre-drinks because any night of the week could bring me the sun on the horizon. My party trick, without a doubt, was stamina. I have done walks of shame, drenched in thick glitter, at three in the afternoon. I wasn’t interested in looking after myself, or my surroundings, or anything that required sustained effort beyond the immediate gratification of the moment. I just needed somewhere to lie flat between merrymakings. I was rootless and selfish in only the way a person with almost zero real responsibilities, who’s still trying to find their way in the world, can be. Sleep was negotiable, meals were incidental. I wasn’t interested in wellness; I just needed a cigarette for the morning; and I don’t remember hydrating once in a decade—I was too busy getting on with things, preferably at speed.

I rather romanticized being a bit of a disaster. I think we all did back then. Blame Pete Doherty. There was something appealing about running on…erm…adrenaline, partying at capacity, being slightly behind on everything else. I mistook a lack of self-preservation for spontaneity and great swathes of disorganization for having a social life. I didn’t want to become one of those people who had everything Filofaxed; those people had missed the point of being alive.

Nowadays, weeknights find me with my trotters up in front of Red Dwarf, getting a little laundry done before bed. I’ve developed an unexpected appetite for maintenance: clean, high-thread-count sheets, say, or a handsomely stocked fridge. A house that doesn’t look as though a small twister has blown through, clothes that don’t scream French-robber-cum-mime. Doing a four-step skincare routine and going to sleep at a reasonable hour. Reading a physical book.

I have become a man who likes things to be nice. I find myself taking pleasure in the small, unremarkable business of keeping things (and myself) in working order. There’s a time and place for the extraordinary, for the luxurious. But functioning, clean, and considered is eclipsing my more indulgent peccadillos.

I still like being out for dinner, and I still like bars where every drink is three-parts spirit, but I also like relieving the sitter by 11. I’m out with you; I just think, Wow, that was lovely; and then I leave. There’s no FOMO from the Uber, no twinge of the grass once being greener, of my body once being younger and more readily available for adventure. The cravings were gone, the mind-chatter silenced.

I remain suspicious of people who seem to have turned being alive into an elaborate administrative exercise, a series of panic attacks about microplastics. My personality refuses to revolve around my cortisol levels or “sleep hygiene.” But I am beginning to understand that treating yourself like an afterthought isn’t particularly glamorous. Nor is being permanently exhausted. I don’t think I need to spend the rest of my life recovering from last night.

Fatherhood accelerated this realization because fatherhood accelerates all realizations. There’s another person in my house who has very little interest in whether I’ve had enough sleep, whether I’ve eaten properly. She assumes—fairly reasonably?—the adults are taking care of things. Looking after myself, in turn, becomes less of a personal treat and more of a basic courtesy to the human I created. As a byproduct, I’ve discovered the pleasure of an ordinary day that doesn’t require damage control. I’m not dodging hangovers for the baby, per se, but I also don’t see the fun in keeping watch of a zig-zagging toddler while waiting for the Advil to kick in before your about-last-night apology tour. There’s something in a morning without regret, a house without a domestic backlog, an evening that doesn’t need to be wrung dry for every last drop of excitement.

I’ve finally shed the conviction that being interesting requires a destructive streak. I haven’t become a different person, but I’ve redirected my night energy to a place of comfort. I’ve stopped treating my own life as something I can afford to neglect. Chaos and I are still on speaking terms, obviously; a toddler is the absolute essence of chaos. But I’ve decided one source of chaos—with a strict 7 p.m. bedtime—is enough for now. The party isn’t over, I’m just focused on the mornings after.
