Grand Theft Auto: San Andreas is GTA 5 without the finesse - so why is the 2004 classic such a strange delight to return to?

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

There is a place in Whitewood Estates where a promising stretch of blacktop suddenly gives way to the desert. It feels like the road has been erased, a sentence left unfinished. It's quietly strange, a weird bit of urban wrongfooting. But I guess it makes some kind of sense. California, after all, is home to California City, a town that was planned and then never really built. It sits out there now, north of Los Angeles, streets laid out but slowly being eaten by the sand and dirt. Still, 14,000 people lived there as of 2018. Design limitation or something stranger, this digital dead end is a reminder that all of this land, so modern, so ancient - all of this is spook country.

It's fitting I guess that GTA San Andreas is such a strange delight to return to. As I've waited, like everyone else, for GTA 6, I've been back here in the GTA games of the past, via the slightly dodgy remasters from a few years ago. And what's come through for me, spending a few idle mornings messing around in San Andreas, a game I thought I knew pretty well, is how beautifully odd everything is now. The best part of 20 years have given a game that once strained against its host technology a genuine aesthetic. Surprisingly sparse in detailing, as angular and sheer as dream architecture, in love with expanses, San Andreas is perfectly placed, it seems to me, to capture just how weird California can be.

California and Nevada. This is the part I always forget. I mainly think of San Andreas these days through the lens of GTA 5, which gives you LA and its surroundings but lets the single city serve as the star. It has a bigger footprint than the earlier game, sure, but it uses that space to go deep on the detailing and the sense of a singular, fiercely specific place. San Andreas meanwhile offers three cities - and one of them isn't actually in California. Alongside spins on LA and San Francisco, you get a version of Las Vegas. It's quite neat really: the three points of some arcane spiritual triangle. The kind of thing Marvel Whiteside Parsons, aerospace engineer and Actual Wizard, might have scratched in the dust of his backyard to help him control the weather. He lived - and died - in Pasadena, so all this would have been familiar anyway.

There is a classic moment in every GTA. A moment where the map opens up in the early games, or when you explore enough in the later games, and simply get to understand the audacity of what is being attempted here. In San Andreas, and this feels apt, the best way to have this moment is to break the rules, sneaking into the open map before the game is fully ready for you. An early mission allows you to step away from the critical path, as it were, and peg it to the airport in Los Santos, where a plane is waiting to be pinched. Taxi, feel the thrust, and you're off. LA beneath you - sorry, Los Santos - and on the horizon a dinkytown version of the Golden Gate Bridge and the MGM Grand.

Dinkytown is the word. From the sky, the huge map of San Andreas, which still feels like an incredible piece of work - such ambition! - can look like toys laid out on a duvet in the remasters. Coming back from GTA 5, a masterpiece of texture, the cities of San Andreas in particular feel very weird - cramped and condensed and wonderfully stagey, arranged the way a kid might jury-rig them together from shoeboxes and stray cardboard. On your way out of Grove Street early on you pass the Los Santos equivalent of the Hollywood sign, looming so close the way it does in the movies but not in the actual city and you feel you could touch it. Downtown is reduced to a handful of the best buildings - it's always pleasing to see the one from the brilliant first act of Speed made the cut - but still manages to capture the important stuff too, which in this case is the way that modern LA looms somewhat cruelly over the old city, captured with a strange sepulchral dignity in the form of the City Hall, so easy to spot but now so small!

Elsewhere, the ersatz Las Vega Strip looks like a drawing based on memory - a few of the classic shapes are there, but it's a sketch of a place. In this way, I guess, it captures a truth about Las Vegas. Also, perhaps, it's an admission that this is a town comfortably out of the reach of satire. As for San Francisco, it's a 60s cop movie delight to fishtail around. It nails the hills and what more do you need for a game with cars in it?

Taken as a whole these cities feel mesmerising now. And it's the limitations that make them sing, I think: those sheer granite canyons of downtown LA with its towering blocks of architecture and surprisingly thin traffic, with only the very faintest of ambient soundtracks that ensures any gunfire will pop and clap and shake you out of your seat. (Remember, I am playing the remasters and sound effects may be missing.) This is the LA of Repo Man rather than Heat - an otherland where the buildings feel alien, even the air feels alien. At night, palm trees stand out against a sky that glows a deep, clean red. GTA 5 would finesse all of this, the skyboxes and whatnot, but perhaps in that finessing something is lost. (Both games, incidentally, have such a talent for capturing California's kitsch excess that they perhaps miss some of the sweeter truths, such as the astonishing diversity of cultures a city like Los Angeles finds homes for. But how could any game do that justice?)

Dinkytowns aside, what San Andreas does better than GTA 5 is space - the peculiar flat spaces of Southern California in particular, a state in which you can never quite tell where the wilderness ends and the landscaping begins, and where the ancient rocks that announce the horizon can look new, like stage settings, while the freeways feel truly ancient, concrete lizards sleeping on the dirt, bleached by the easy sun. I can lose hours out here. I have had to double- and triple-check that GTA 5 is the bigger game, in fact, because San Andreas still feels absolutely enormous, a trick that works even when you can see the not-Golden Gate Bridge seconds after taking off from not-LAX. Grab a bike and you can spend an absolute age exploring the between-spaces which video game California and real California are both so good at conjuring, stumbling on little towns you will never stumble on again, passing commercial follies, listening as the radio pumps out "All My Exes Live in Texas".

And, paradoxically, the more I play the more I realise San Andreas is very good at the little things in its own odd way. GTA 5 is an astonishing feat of seeing and transposing, but I don't think it has anything across its acres to match the sense of place offered by San Andreas' take on Grove Street, where the story begins. It's just a neighborhood, but as you cross its small space and recross it in the game's opening hours, learning how to eat food and buy clothes and get a haircut and spray paint on walls and all that jazz, the sense that this is your home is grooved and regrooved into the experience. Maybe this is why it's such a thrill to return to this earlier pass at Los Santos. Because this one is where you truly live.
