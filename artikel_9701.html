Database as a service (DBaaS): what it is, what it costs and when to self-host

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Posted on Nov 25 • Originally published at ownkube.io

Database as a service (DBaaS) means a provider runs the database and you get a connection string. The provider owns the servers and the disks. It installs and patches the database software, takes the backups and replaces a machine that fails.

You still design the tables, write the queries and choose how big the database is. You pay a monthly price, usually for an instance size plus the storage it holds.

This guide covers what a DBaaS provider does, what stays your job, what a small and a mid-size Postgres cost on eight providers, and when running Postgres yourself is the better call.

The provider copies the database on a schedule and keeps the copies for a set number of days. You never write a cron job, rotate files or check that a dump finished.

Read the fine print on two things: whether backups are on by default, and whether they cost extra. On Ownkube they are included in the storage price on the Personal and Team plans and turned on per database. Supabase includes daily backups on Pro and sells point-in-time recovery separately.

A nightly backup can only take you back to last night. Point-in-time recovery (PITR) also keeps the write-ahead log, the record of every change, so you can restore the database to a chosen minute. That's the feature you want after someone runs a DELETE without a WHERE clause at 14:05.

Prices for it range widely: Ownkube's 7-day PITR is part of the $0.25 per GB storage price, and Supabase charges $100 a month for each 7 days of retention.

Postgres ships minor releases with bug and security fixes several times a year. A DBaaS applies them for you, usually in a maintenance window with a short restart. Major versions, such as moving from 17 to 18, change the on-disk format, so most providers let you choose when to run them.

A single database instance is one machine. If it fails, the provider replaces it and the database comes back, but writes stop while that happens. A standby is a second copy kept in sync by streaming replication, and on a failure the provider promotes it to primary.

A standby costs about as much as the primary. On AWS RDS, Multi-AZ doubles the instance and storage price. On Ownkube each standby bills as its own box plus its own storage. Fly includes high availability in every Managed Postgres plan, which is part of why its prices start higher.

The provider keeps Postgres running. It has no view on whether your tables make sense. Every migration, every new column and every change to a hot table is still a decision your team makes and tests.

Most slow databases are slow because of a missing index or a query that reads far more rows than it returns. A bigger instance hides that for a while and raises the bill. Run EXPLAIN ANALYZE on the slow queries first, and add an index before you add memory.

Each Postgres connection is its own process with its own memory, so small instances accept fewer connections. Render lists 100 connections on its small and mid-size plans. An app that opens a connection per request, or many app replicas each holding a pool, can hit that ceiling long before the CPU is busy.

Use a connection pool in your app, or a pooler such as PgBouncer in front of the database. Some providers run the pooler for you.

You pick the instance, and you pay for it whether the database is busy or idle. Storage only grows, and most providers make shrinking a disk hard. Start small, watch memory and disk, and move up a size when the graphs say so.

The prices below come from each provider's own pricing page as of 2026-11-25. They are list prices in USD a month for a single instance, without taxes or bandwidth.

AWS RDS is US East (N. Virginia), Single-AZ, on gp3 storage, which starts at 20 GB, so the small database pays for 20 GB. Railway assumes 0.1 vCPU of average database CPU, and each extra 0.1 vCPU adds about $2. Neon is modelled always on. DigitalOcean includes 10 GiB of storage on the 1 GiB node and 60 GiB on the 4 GiB node. Supabase includes 8 GB of disk and $10 of compute credit in Pro.

The headline price leaves out what each provider includes. Ownkube includes 7-day PITR in the storage price on Personal and Team, Supabase adds $100 a month for the same 7 days, and Fly includes backups and high availability in every plan.

High availability is the biggest swing: RDS Multi-AZ doubles the instance and the storage, which takes the 4 GB database to about $106 a month. Neon and Railway go the other way, because they bill by what the database uses, so a quiet database costs less than the always-on figures here.

Ownkube Compute runs managed Postgres (version 17 or 18) on fixed-size database boxes. You pay a flat monthly price for the box, plus $0.25 per GB of storage.

Backups are off by default, and you turn them on per database. Turning them on or off restarts the database. Each standby bills as its own box plus its own storage, so a DB 4 with 50 GB and one standby comes to $81 a month.

Databases run in two regions, Germany and Mumbai. A database is private to your apps by default, and you can open public access per database when an outside tool needs to connect.

A DBaaS is the right default for most small teams. Backups that stopped running weeks ago or a disk that filled overnight usually go unnoticed until the day you need a restore, and the provider watches for both. Self-hosting is a better fit in a few cases.

If a teammate has set up streaming replication, tested a restore and tuned postgresql.conf before, the provider's main value shrinks. You trade a monthly markup for their time, and that can be a good trade.

Managed Postgres limits superuser access, the extensions you can install and some server settings. An app that depends on a custom extension, a specific build or deep tuning may only run on a server you control.

Provider storage rates run from $0.125 to $0.35 per GB a month. A database that holds a few terabytes and changes little can cost much less on a server with large local disks. That holds only while someone keeps its backups and restores tested.

Local development, CI and short-lived test databases don't need backups or failover. Postgres in a container is enough. Our Postgres in Docker guide covers that setup.

For a production database with no one on call, the arithmetic usually favours a provider. A missed backup, a full disk or a failed upgrade costs more than a year of the price difference. If you want failover on your own servers, read Postgres high availability first to see what it takes.
