AIOps for IT Operations: What It Is

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Posted on Nov 12 • Originally published at calidadinfotech.com

AIOps for IT operations means applying machine learning to your operational data (metrics, logs, traces, alerts and change records) to group related alerts, flag unusual behaviour and suggest likely causes, so on-call engineers can triage incidents faster. It doesn't replace monitoring or SRE practice. It can only be as good as your SLOs, alerts and telemetry.

AIOps is a set of machine learning techniques that sit between your monitoring tools and your on-call engineers. They turn a flood of signals into a short list of incidents with context attached.

AIOps helps when you have too many alerts. It can't help when the alerts you need don't exist, or when nobody owns what they point at.

Look closely at those numbers. AIOps can work on the first four, because grouping and ranking cut how many pages engineers get and how often they ignore one. It does nothing about the fifth. If no alert fired, there was no signal to correlate. That's an instrumentation gap, and you close it with better telemetry and symptom-based alerts, not a smarter model.

Not every AIOps feature carries the same risk. Grouping duplicate alerts is cheap to get wrong, because an engineer can still see the underlying alerts. Automatically restarting services or closing tickets is expensive to get wrong. Start in the top-left of this chart.

AIOps needs consistent telemetry, change events, an accurate list of services and owners, and labelled incident history. Without all four, correlation is just guessing.

Keep enough history to cover your normal cycles. If traffic follows a weekly pattern, a model trained on four days of data will flag every Monday as an anomaly.

SLOs tell you which alerts matter to users. If you feed AIOps alerts without that, it learns to rank noise.

Google's SRE guidance is direct about this. The Monitoring Distributed Systems chapter says effective alerting has "good signal and very low noise". It recommends putting far more effort into catching symptoms than causes, and says alerting configuration that's rarely exercised (less than once a quarter for some teams) should be up for removal.

The SRE Workbook chapter on alerting on SLOs shows how to put this into practice. Alert on how fast you're using up your error budget (the burn rate), not on raw thresholds. With a 99.9% SLO over 30 days, a constant 0.1% error rate uses up the budget exactly by the end of the window, which is a burn rate of 1. Spending 5% of the budget in a single hour takes a burn rate of 36. The workbook calls multiwindow, multi-burn-rate alerts the most appropriate approach in most cases.

This often cuts paging by more than any tool would. It also gives you a clean definition of "an incident that mattered", which you'll need to judge an AIOps pilot. One test is worth more than the rest. Hand the vendor three past incidents you already understand and see whether the tool reaches the same conclusion. If it can't explain incidents you've already solved, it won't help with the ones you haven't.

AIOps is useful for one specific job: cutting a stream of alerts down to a few incidents with likely causes attached. It works best on top of good telemetry, SLO-based alerts, clear ownership and labelled incident history, and it can't stand in for any of them. Do the SRE groundwork first, then pilot one service against a baseline and let the numbers decide.
