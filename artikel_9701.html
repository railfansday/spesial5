Once fidelity becomes high enough, one day you hear something like this.

“On this issue, I think I know you better than you know yourself.”

People do not respond to those words in the same way. Some laugh, some take offense, and some hurt for a long time.

I read that difference along two axes I have used in earlier whitepapers.

The two axes are independent. Some people can bear reality without seeing it well; others see it well but struggle to bear it. That gives us four rooms.

Of the four rooms, ④ looks the most peaceful from the outside. The owner is satisfied, the AI complies, and there is no conflict. The second brain tells its owner what they want to hear with increasing sophistication. The uncomfortable things other people used to say are filtered out by the agent before they arrive.

Room ③ is noisy, but it moves. Pain means something is being seen, and someone who sees can make changes. As experiences of being wrong without falling apart accumulate, ③ moves toward ①.

Room ② is comfortable, and that is where it stops. In terms of the steps in Chapter 4, it has skipped the mirror and gone straight to the representative. The work runs well. But the person that the second brain comes to resemble is less the actual owner than the owner as described by the owner.

I do not want this table used to label people. The same person may be in ① at work and ③ with family. After a week without enough sleep, anyone can slide toward ④. I do, too.

The four rooms do not classify kinds of people. They are a hypothesis about the relationship someone has with a second brain in a particular domain and state. As in my earlier whitepapers, the principle is to look for recurring patterns rather than judge from a single scene.

The same second brain becomes another self for one person, an assistant for another, a mirror for another, and a mechanism for automating self-deception for another.
