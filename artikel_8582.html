Mailchimp Review – Easy to Use, Pricey Once Your List Grows

Mailchimp lets a small business send polished marketing email without a designer or a developer. Its templates and drag-and-drop editor handle the design, and its integrations bring in your customers and their purchases from the store or website you already run. The trade-off is a price that rises with each tier of contacts you store.

Ben Chestnut and Dan Kurzius started Mailchimp in Atlanta in 2001 as a small-business email service on the side of their web design agency, and the company is still based there. Intuit, the maker of QuickBooks and TurboTax, bought it in 2021, and Mailchimp says it now has more than 11 million users.

Mailchimp sells four plans: Free, Essentials, Standard, and Premium. All four share the same email builder, sign-up forms, landing pages, and directory of more than 300 integrations, and the paid tiers add automation, testing, and reporting.

The drag-and-drop builder starts from more than 130 templates on paid plans, while the Free plan gets a limited selection and puts Mailchimp’s branding on every email. Paid plans also let you schedule sends.

Standard and Premium add generative AI, still in beta, that drafts and rewrites copy in the editor. They also let you send templates you code yourself in HTML and use dynamic content, which changes parts of an email for each reader.

Automation flows send emails on their own when a contact does something, such as joining your list or leaving items in a cart. Essentials limits each flow to four steps. Standard and Premium allow up to 200 flows, with branches that send contacts down different paths and more than one starting point.

A/B testing comes with every paid plan. It tries up to three versions of a subject line, sender name, content, or send time on part of your list, then can send the winner to the rest automatically. Standard and Premium add multivariate testing, which compares up to eight versions at once, plus send-time optimization, which picks a send time from your list’s past clicks.

Every plan can build sign-up forms and landing pages, and a landing page can sit on a Mailchimp address or on a domain you connect. The integrations directory lists more than 300 apps, including Shopify, WooCommerce, Square, Wix, Squarespace, Stripe, Canva, Zapier, and QuickBooks Online. A connected store such as Shopify syncs customers and purchases into Mailchimp, which uses them for product recommendations and abandoned-cart emails.

Every plan includes basic campaign reports. Standard and Premium add custom reports, advanced segmentation, and predictive segments that group customers by predicted lifetime value or likelihood to buy.

The price rises quickly. Standard costs $45 a month at 1,500 contacts, $100 at 5,000, and $135 at 10,000, almost seven times its starting price. Essentials runs $26.50, $75, and $110 at the same sizes.

Discounts are narrow. A new customer who skips the trial and buys a plan at 10,000 or more contacts gets 15% off the first 12 months, and nonprofits and charities get 15% off on request. Essentials and Standard come with a 14-day free trial that bills the plan’s price when it ends unless you cancel. If you send only a few times a year, Pay As You Go sells email credits with Essentials’ features instead of a subscription, though the credits expire after 12 months.

Text messaging costs extra as well, since Mailchimp SMS Marketing is a paid add-on that needs Mailchimp’s approval of your application first.

For a big list you email a few times a month, look at Brevo, which prices its plans by the emails you send rather than the contacts you keep. Its Standard plan, which also includes automation and A/B testing, costs $69 a month for 20,000 emails, enough to reach 10,000 contacts twice. Mailchimp’s Standard plan costs $135 a month at 10,000 contacts, though it allows 120,000 sends and five seats to Brevo’s one. Brevo’s free plan holds up to 100,000 contacts and sends up to 300 emails a day.

Essentials and Standard come with 24/7 email and chat support, and Premium adds priority support and phone calls, which are in English only. The Free plan gets email support for its first 30 days only. Standard includes one hour-long onboarding session in your first 90 days, and Premium includes up to four.

Reviews depend on where you look. Mailchimp averages 4.3 out of 5 from about 13,000 reviews on G2, a software review site, but 2.5 out of 5 from about 1,600 on Trustpilot.

Mailchimp makes it easy for a small business to start an email list that works with the store or website it already runs, and Standard is its best plan while the list is small. At 500 contacts, $20 a month buys automation flows, multivariate testing, generative AI, and an onboarding session.
