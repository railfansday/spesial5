Strong light fields have unlocked entirely new possibilities to tailor coherent electron trajectories, engineer band structures and shape emergent phases of matter all-optically1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8,9. Unravelling the underlying quantum mechanisms requires a visualization of the lightwave-driven electron motion10,11 directly in the band structure. Although photoelectron momentum microscopy has imaged photoexcited electrons averaged over many cycles of light12,13,14,15,16,17,18,19, subcycle band-structure videography has been limited to small electron momenta8,20. Yet, lightwave-driven elementary processes in quantum materials often occur throughout momentum space. Here we introduce attosecond-precision, subcycle band-structure videography covering the entire first Brillouin zone and visualize one of the most fundamental but elusive strong-field processes: non-adiabatic Landau–Zener–Majorana tunnelling. The interplay of strong-field-driven acceleration within the Dirac-like band structure of graphene and periodic Landau–Zener–Majorana interband tunnelling manifests in a coherent subcycle displacement and distortion of the carrier distribution at the Brillouin zone edge. The extremely non-thermal electron distributions also allow us to disentangle competing scattering processes and assess their impact on coherent electronic control. Our panoramic view of field-driven electron motion enables a new quality of microscopic understanding of strong-field control of quantum materials.

According to the adiabatic theorem, a time-dependent, two-level quantum system remains in its ground state during a slow evolution21. By contrast, driving the system faster than its intrinsic timescale through an avoided energy crossing can create a finite occupation of the excited state by means of non-adiabatic transitions called Landau–Zener–Majorana (LZM) tunnelling22,23,24. This mechanism is central for strong-field light–matter interaction7,10,25,26. Yet, a direct observation of this foundational quantum effect in momentum space is still missing. The unique properties of graphene27 form the ideal test bed for observing LZM transitions. Its quasirelativistic electron dispersion and the robustness under irradiation with intense light have made this material a prime test ground for lightwave electronics26,28, including the demonstration of the first lightwave electronic logic gates7 and Floquet–Bloch states18,19. This has opened revolutionary prospects for quantum control, quantum sensing and quantum computation applications10,11,29.

Understanding and shaping the opportunities offered by lightwave control of electrons requires a direct observation of how LZM transitions dictate strong-field light–matter interaction. Attosecond spectroscopy has reached sufficient temporal resolution to access these dynamics30,31, and time- and angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (trARPES) in combination with momentum microscopy32 has visualized electron dynamics directly in the band structure of quantum materials or in the orbitals of molecular layers12,13,14,15,16,17,18,19,33. trARPES with subcycle temporal resolution8,20 has combined these approaches but offered only limited 1D momentum space access. To track coherent strong-field phenomena and scattering-induced momentum redistribution, observing carrier dynamics across the entire momentum space is imperative.

Here we expand subcycle band-structure videography to the entire first Brillouin zone (BZ). The new platform overcomes previous restrictions of momentum microscopy and subcycle ARPES, increasing the momentum area accessible with subcycle resolution by more than two orders of magnitude and the available field strengths by one order of magnitude. Exploiting few-cycle mid-infrared (MIR) field transients with large peak amplitudes, we drive coherent electron dynamics at the BZ edge of graphene. This approach reveals the simultaneous displacement and distortion of the light-driven electron distribution, uncovering the interplay between coherent intraband and interband dynamics underlying LZM transitions in graphene. Moreover, tracking the non-thermal subcycle distributions in 2D momentum space reveals how different scattering processes affect lightwave-driven dynamics.

When low-intensity, short-wavelength light interacts with electrons in a solid, it can induce perturbative interband quantum jumps in momentum regions in which the occupied and unoccupied bands are separated by an integer multiple of the photon energy (Fig. 1a). In graphene, the non-trivial pseudospin texture influences the dipole moments between the lower and upper halves of the Dirac cone, leading to a distribution of excited carriers only along the direction orthogonal to the light field34,35. Conversely, a strong low-frequency field transient can adiabatically accelerate carriers within their bands on subcycle timescales, deflecting the Fermi surface in momentum space according to Bloch’s acceleration theorem (Fig. 1b). During their excursion, electrons experience an energy gap, which, in graphene, varies linearly near the K point to form an avoided crossing. Thus, field-driven acceleration may drive prototypical LZM transitions, which leave a markedly different occupation in momentum space compared with Fig. 1a. If the driving field is periodic, the phase accumulated between consecutive non-adiabatic events—the Stückelberg phase—results in so-called Landau–Zener–Stückelberg–Majorana (LZSM) interference36,37.

To access this regime, we drive electrons in epitaxially grown monolayer graphene on SiC (ref. 38) with intense phase-stable MIR pulses (central frequency, 30 THz). After a delay time, t, extreme ultraviolet (XUV) pulses (central photon energy, 21.7 eV) photoemit the electrons with subcycle resolution. Their energies and momenta are detected with a time-of-flight photoemission momentum microscope (Fig. 1c and Methods). In equilibrium, the photoemission signal maps the occupied band structure. The high XUV photon energy and the large acceptance angle of the momentum microscope capture the electronic structural dynamics throughout the entire first BZ, including its six corners, in a single measurement with sub-10-fs resolution.

Figure 2a shows several momentum distribution maps in the vicinity of a K point at selected energies. The in-plane component of the p-polarized XUV radiation driving the photoemission process is set parallel to the kx-axis. Owing to the matrix elements in the photoemission process, the intensity exhibits the characteristic horseshoe-like distribution for energies far below the Dirac point39. The influence of the dark corridor at ky = 0 and positive kx − K values is suppressed for energies close to the Dirac point, ED, and the photoemission intensity forms a disc. To ensure that the relevant dynamics occur along a direction in which the two branches of the Dirac cone have equal photoemission intensities, we apply an s-polarized MIR field along the ky-axis. For large negative delays (t = −70 fs), a slice of the photoelectron intensity for kx − K = 0 (Fig. 2b) shows two linear bands with a slope of ħvF (with the Fermi velocity vF = 10.7 Å fs−1) and a Fermi level located 200 meV above the Dirac point. With increasing t, we observe an apparent shift of the overall band structure owing to momentum streaking20 of photoemitted electrons in vacuo by the MIR field. This effect can be used to retrieve the MIR waveform at the sample surface (Extended Data Fig. 2a and Methods). Compensating for this shift isolates the subcycle electron dynamics within the band structure.

A corresponding femtosecond snapshot of the band structure for t = −13.6 fs shows a highly non-thermal lightwave-driven electron distribution (Fig. 2b). In the upper half of the Dirac cone (E − ED ≥ 0), the ky > 0 branch is occupied up to about 1 eV, whereas the ky < 0 branch is completely unoccupied. Half a cycle of the MIR carrier wave later (t = 6.3 fs), the field direction is inverted and the electron distribution is mirrored. The extreme carrier redistribution on subcycle timescales clearly proves the non-perturbative nature of the light–matter interaction. This scenario is expected for the small Keldysh parameter near the Dirac point (γ = 0.075; Methods). Still, it is notable that the few-fs timescales accessible with our experiment allow us to see lightwave-driven intraband acceleration despite the extremely fast scattering processes in graphene33,35,40,41,42,43,44,45. Asymmetric electron distributions in momentum space correspond to the excitation of an intraband current. By tracking the imbalance in the band occupation for positive and negative ky momenta (Fig. 2c, inset and Methods), we extract the lightwave-driven intraband current density and directly compare it with the MIR driving waveform (Fig. 2c). Although the current density shows a similar envelope and oscillation frequency as the MIR transient, there is a non-trivial phase shift between the two waveforms.

From Bloch’s acceleration theorem20,46, we would expect that, in the absence of decoherence, the momentum accumulated by electrons follows the integral over the MIR field, resulting in a phase shift of π/2 between the current and the field. A Fourier analysis (Methods) allows us to retrieve this phase delay with attosecond precision. Notably, the average delay of 4.9 ± 0.2 fs is much smaller than the value associated with a fully coherent evolution (8.3 fs). Simulations based on the semiconductor Bloch equations (SBEs)6 in the relaxation time approximation (Extended Data Fig. 3 and Methods) reproduce this phase shift for a mean scattering time of 9.0 ± 0.7 fs. As non-adiabatic LZM transitions rely on the coherent and unperturbed field-driven evolution of charge carriers, this sets the time and length scales relevant for lightwave electronics. Despite the short scattering time, electrons in graphene can travel almost 100 Å owing to their large velocity of vF = 10.7 Å fs−1.

Most notably, the current is accompanied by an ultrafast increase of the total conduction-band population within the strongest MIR half-cycles (Fig. 2c, grey shaded area). Perturbative interband transitions are too weak to become effective on subcycle timescales and, in equilibrium, straightforward vertical interband transitions are prohibited in the doped sample by Pauli blocking. Hence, the observed electronic structural dynamics attest to an interplay between strong-field-driven intraband and interband currents. Next, we unveil the microscopic mechanisms by imaging the full 2D momentum space. With this capability, unavailable in previous subcycle photoemission experiments8,20, we can directly target the coherent light–matter interaction regime and the subsequent relaxation dynamics.

Before the interaction with the MIR transient, the equilibrium carrier distribution in the conduction band (E − ED ≥ 0) of n-doped graphene can be approximated by a disc centred about each K point (Fig. 2a). If the electrons were solely subject to an accelerating electric field, they would, in accordance with Bloch’s acceleration theorem46, collectively move along the ky direction and we would merely expect a time-dependent centre-of-mass (COM) displacement of this disc. Although the streaking-compensated 2D momentum maps (Fig. 3a) indeed show pronounced oscillations of the COM displacement with the frequency of the strong driving field and a maximum amplitude of 0.078 Å−1 (Fig. 3b), they also reveal a further notable change of their shape (Fig. 3a,c). The observed deviations from the initial shape of the distribution are on the same order as the COM displacement.

Already after the first MIR field cycles (Fig. 3a, t = 3 fs), the Fermi surface is strongly elongated from 0.054 Å−1 to 0.096 Å−1 in the direction parallel to the field, whereas the change in the orthogonal direction is only 0.014 Å−1 (Fig. 3a, Gaussian fits). The Fermi surface is no longer circular but takes on an elliptical shape. The time-dependent change in the ky width (Fig. 3c, green line) directly correlates with the field strength. Furthermore, this width oscillates at twice the MIR carrier frequency while the COM displacement tracks the electric field similarly to the current plotted in Fig. 2c. Because the maximum broadening coincides with the peak COM displacement, we can rule out motion blur as the origin of the elongation in the ky direction. Notably, the electron distribution encompasses the Dirac point at all delay times, even for the largest displacements.

All of these intriguing dynamics can be microscopically explained by a full quantum-mechanical solution of the SBEs6 (Methods), which account for intraband and interband transitions as well as LZM-type interband coupling, on equal footing. A systematic switch-off analysis pinpoints how specific aspects of coherent strong-field light–matter interaction manifest in the observed dynamics (Extended Data Fig. 4a). The periodic COM shift of the electron distribution is clearly associated with field-driven intraband motion of charge carriers. Yet, the distribution retains its circular shape and separates from the K point for large displacement if interband dynamics are switched off. Once interband coupling is included, the theory shows the same elongation of the carrier distribution as the experiment (Extended Data Fig. 4b). The theory also qualitatively reproduces the increase in carrier density in the conduction band (Extended Data Fig. 5), confirming that interband transitions at the K point act like a well that feeds electrons into the upper part of the Dirac cone on subcycle timescales.

These transitions originate from strong-field interband coupling, whereas conventional perturbative interband photoexcitation is suppressed by orders of magnitude under our experimental conditions (Extended Data Fig. 6). Owing to pseudospin selection rules34,35, photoexcitation would occur in momentum regions with |kx − K| > 0, located perpendicular to the polarization of the incident light (Extended Data Fig. 6a). By contrast, the coupled intraband and interband dynamics of the LZM scenario leads to the observed unidirectional elongation of the electron distribution parallel to the electric field (Extended Data Fig. 6b). Simulations with varying field strengths confirm that this feature is characteristic of the strong-field regime (Extended Data Fig. 7).

The region in which LZM-type tunnelling can occur is determined by the field strength, E0, and the energy gap, which increases linearly with |kx − K|. For E0 = 1.57 MV cm−1, the transition region is confined to momenta |kx − K| ≤ 0.08 Å−1 (Fig. 3a, t = 3 fs), corresponding to a Keldysh parameter of γ ≤ 0.63. Accounting for scattering and limited momentum resolution, this value is in good agreement with the original LZM formula \({P}_{{\rm{L}}{\rm{Z}}{\rm{M}}}=\exp \left(-2{\rm{\pi }}\frac{\hbar {v}_{{\rm{F}}}}{2|e|{E}_{0}}{|{k}_{x}-K|}^{2}\right)\), which predicts diabatic transitions in a momentum range |kx − K| ≤ 0.04 Å−1 (probability 1/e2 width; Methods). This confirms that the linearly dispersing Dirac cone of graphene is a perfect realization of the original model Hamiltonian23. And even Landau, Zener and Majorana’s assumption that the energy levels intersect at a constant speed holds true, as the strong driving fields propel the electrons past the intersection point in less than 1 fs—much faster than the MIR oscillation period (Methods).

The interplay between intraband motion and LZM tunnelling also explains the modulations of the width observed at twice the driving frequency (Fig. 3c). Because the region close to the K point features the lowest tunnelling barrier, electron injection into the upper part of the Dirac cone becomes most efficient in the few-fs time window between a field extremum and the nearest peak displacement of the pre-existing population, which occurs twice per optical cycle. Scattering shortens this time window, shifting the optimal injection time. This situation should result in a step-like increase of the population in the conduction band, with a phase determined by the interplay of Pauli blocking, the evolution of the field amplitude and scattering (Methods). However, a step-like increase can only be reproduced by simulations using extremely strong dephasing that destroys the quantum memory between successive LZM transitions (Extended Data Fig. 9). For realistic dephasing rates, the step-like character transforms into pronounced 2ω oscillations. Their presence in the experimental width of the electron distribution suggests interference of electrons owing to their accumulated phase between individual tunnelling events, emphasizing the role of quantum coherence in light-field-driven devices.

Although LZM dynamics dominate at early delay times, scattering limits the time frame for fully coherent light–matter interaction. At t = 26.3 fs (Fig. 3a), the initially anisotropic electron distribution broadens also along the kx direction and approaches a circular shape with a radius of about 0.065 Å−1, before it contracts to a more equilibrated and isotropic distribution (t = 233 fs). This ultrafast change of the elliptical distribution (Fig. 3c) reflects the interplay between field-driven acceleration, LZM transitions, scattering and carrier relaxation. Our SBE simulations (Extended Data Fig. 10 and Methods) predict distinctive momentum fingerprints for electron–electron and electron–optical phonon scattering. 2D mapping thus allows us to identify their signatures (Fig. 4a). Neglecting all forms of scattering results in a broadening (up to 0.053 Å−1) and displacement (about 0.107 Å−1) of the carrier density along the ky direction and the distribution remains elliptical after the MIR pulse (t = 233 fs), retaining the momentum hallmark of LZM transitions. Moreover, the phase delay of the microscopic current (Fig. 4b) is approximately π/2, as expected for undamped intraband motion. Introducing electron–electron scattering, the initially strongly broadened and displaced momentum distribution (t = 27 fs) partially thermalizes (t = 233 fs) and shrinks to a circular shape with a radius of about 0.038 Å−1. Still, the peculiar angular dependence of electron–electron scattering41,47 retains a large ellipticity on shorter timescales, and the phase delay is only marginally affected. This is caused by the reduced phase space for scattering imposed by the pseudospin texture of graphene.

Simulations including scattering with Γ point longitudinal (LO) and transverse (TO) optical phonons reproduce the experimentally observed dynamics very well. For t = 27 fs, the electron distribution shows a substantial broadening in both the ky (about 0.051 Å−1) and the kx (about 0.041 Å−1) directions, associated with an isotropic redistribution of the charge carriers. This leads to a nearly circular distribution whose COM is shifted by only 0.008 Å−1 at t = 27 fs, much less than in the previous cases. This results from a large change in the phase delay (Fig. 4b), approaching a value of π/4. After the pulse (t = 233 fs), the distribution relaxes to a fully circular shape with a radius of approximately 0.031 Å−1. Both the phase delay and the time-dependent broadening of the electron distribution (Fig. 4c) are in good qualitative agreement with the experiment. We can, thus, identify the key limiting factor for coherent control dictated by the fastest momentum redistribution channel, which, in graphene, is electron–optical phonon scattering (Fig. 4b, solid blue line).

Our experiment opens the momentum horizon of subcycle photoemission over the entire first BZ of basically any quantum material. This universal tool enabled us to directly observe LZM transitions in the band structure of a solid. Attosecond-precision videography of graphene unravels how interband LZM tunnelling couples to field-driven intraband acceleration along the Dirac cone and how scattering influences and limits a fully coherent evolution. This platform may soon address other key questions of condensed matter physics and ultrafast information technologies, from optical band-structure engineering and phase transitions to light-field-driven sculpting of molecular orbitals.

The subcycle momentum microscopy setup (Extended Data Fig. 1a) is based on two ytterbium-doped potassium gadolinium tungstate (Yb:KGW) amplifiers (CARBIDE, Light Conversion Inc.). Each amplifier generates near-infrared light pulses with a duration of 250 fs, a centre wavelength of 1,030 nm and a pulse energy of 1.5 mJ, at a repetition rate of 50 kHz. In the probe arm, the second harmonic of the output of a four-stage noncollinear optical-parametric amplifier (NOPA; ORPHEUS-OPCPA, Light Conversion Inc.)48 yields carrier-envelope phase (CEP)-stabilized pulses centred at 400 nm with a pulse duration of 20 fs. These pulses are focused into a high-pressure argon gas jet to generate high-order harmonics (Extended Data Fig. 1b) in a vacuum beamline49. The seventh harmonic at a photon energy of 21.7 eV is spectrally selected by two multilayer mirrors (Extended Data Fig. 1b, grey shaded area). They feature a bandwidth of 0.9 nm around 57 nm and thus support a Fourier-limited pulse duration of less than 10 fs. The XUV pulses enter the experimental ultrahigh-vacuum (UHV) chamber, the momentum microscope32, through a 200-nm-thin aluminium foil, which also filters out the ultraviolet driving pulses.

Because the count rate in a time-of-flight photoemission momentum microscope is limited to only about one electron per laser shot, a high repetition rate is key for keeping measurement times at a reasonable amount while ensuring good statistics. In our experiment, this requirement entails the challenge of pushing the generation of the atomically strong, few-cycle phase-stable MIR pump pulses to new limits for a tabletop setup. Here the generated phase-stable MIR pulses feature, to the best of our knowledge, record-high average powers of up to 1 W and electrical peak field strengths of more than 200 MV cm−1. These field transients are obtained by difference frequency generation in a gallium selenide crystal between the outputs of two parallel (N)OPAs (ORPHEUS-MIR, Light Conversion Inc.)50. They feature pulse durations down to sub-50 fs and can be tuned between 4 µm and 15 µm. A small portion of the laser fundamental is picked off to pump a home-built single-stage NOPA. It is optimized to generate 10-fs pulses centred around 825 nm, which are used for electro-optic sampling (EOS) of the MIR waveforms. Extended Data Fig. 1c shows a typical MIR transient detected by EOS. A pair of wire-grid polarizers allows us to precisely control the polarization direction and power of the MIR pulses, which are then coupled into the UHV chamber through a diamond window.

The intense MIR pump (s-polarized) and the XUV probe (p-polarized) are spatially overlapped in the vacuum chamber with a custom-built off-axis parabolic mirror featuring a small aperture for XUV transmission and are focused onto monolayer graphene with an angle of incidence of 70°. A piezo-controlled delay stage in the excitation beam path allows us to set the delay between the MIR and XUV pulses with a temporal precision of 230 as. The emitted photoelectrons are detected by a time-of-flight momentum microscope (lens system: GST mbH; detector: HEX40, RoentDek)51. Multihit detection ensures linearity of the signal up to a repetition rate of 20 kHz. The combination of the 21.7-eV probe pulses with the large acceptance angle of the momentum microscope allows access to momentum regions up to ±2.14 Å−1, as compared with ±0.16 Å−1 in previous subcycle ARPES experiments8,20. The energy resolution is limited by the broad spectrum of the probe pulse. The bandwidth of the XUV pulse is tunable, setting a limit for the energy resolution of 180 meV. The momentum resolution is better than 0.01 Å−1. The custom-designed UHV chamber is shielded by a μ-metal liner and maintains a base pressure of 2 × 10−10 mbar. The samples were measured at room temperature.

Inducing field-driven dynamics in the monolayer requires a field that is parallel to the plane of the monolayer. To maximize the corresponding field component, the MIR pulse is s-polarized. The polarization of the XUV pulse is set to be perpendicular to the MIR pulse to prevent crosstalk between their fields that could introduce coherent artefacts effects in the observed dynamics. Finally, the rotation of the sample is chosen such that the MIR-induced momentum streaking in vacuum is minimized at one of the K points. This requires one K point to be at k∥ = 0, with k∥ being the momentum direction parallel to the electric field of the MIR pulse. This alignment also minimizes matrix-element-induced asymmetries along k∥ = 0 in the observed energy and momentum range. Although an asymmetry along the kx direction might persist, it is rather small at the observed energies. This is because the dark corridor only begins to set in at relatively high excitation energies, which do not occur along the kx direction in the experiment. This may also explain the slightly larger broadening observed in the simulations in which the matrix element was neglected.

For sample preparation, an n-doped 6H-SiC(0001) wafer was used as a substrate for growing an epitaxial monolayer graphene with 0° rotational alignment relative to the substrate lattice (0°-EMLG). The wafer was initially degassed in UHV at 880 °C for 30 min. The temperature was subsequently increased to 1,050 °C and maintained for 30 min under an external flux of Si atoms, resulting in a Si-rich (√3 × √3)R30° surface reconstruction. The sample was then further annealed at 880 °C for an extra 30 min, again under Si flux, resulting in the Si-rich (3 × 3) surface reconstruction. With this procedure, the oxide layer is removed from the SiC surface and atomic cleanness is achieved. In the next step, 0°-EMLG was obtained by annealing the sample at 1,400 °C for 30 min in borazine (B3N3H6) atmosphere with a partial pressure of 1.5 × 10−6 mbar. The borazine molecules act as surfactants on the heated SiC surface. They inhibit the formation of the conventional 30°-rotated graphene layer but instead force the homogeneous graphene layer in an unconventional 0° orientation with respect to the substrate lattice. For more details on the preparation of the 0°-EMLG, see refs. 38,52,53. Following growth, the surface morphology and crystalline structure were characterized using low-energy electron microscopy and low-energy electron diffraction. The average width of terraces on the SiC surface is approximately 160 nm and the surface is fully covered by 0°-EMLG. Subsequently, the sample was shortly exposed to air, transported in static vacuum (0.1 mbar) to the experimental chambers and degassed at 350 °C for 3 h before the measurements. The photoemission experiments reveal that the 0°-EMLG sample is n-doped, with the Fermi level approximately 200 meV above the Dirac point.

The acceleration of photoemitted electrons in vacuum allows for an in situ reconstruction of electric field waveforms20. In addition to acceleration within the sample, the s-polarized field also accelerates electrons after they have left the material. To extract Δk∥, the extracted photoemission data are shifted stepwise along k∥ and compared with a reference spectrum without a field. Within a fixed momentum window centred on the Dirac point, spanning +160 meV to −1.5 eV relative to the Fermi level (a range that, at equilibrium, encompasses predominantly the valence band), the sum of absolute pixel-wise differences between the measured data and the reference is computed at each iteration. The iteration with the smallest overall error gives the value for Δk∥. Repeating this procedure for every delay point, t, allows a reconstruction of the applied field E∥(t). A parameter that is frequently used to distinguish between the weak-field and strong-field regimes of light–matter interaction is the Keldysh parameter

It was introduced by Leonid Keldysh in 1965 as the ratio between the carrier frequency of light ω and the tunnelling rate of an electron with mass \({m}_{{\rm{e}}+{\rm{h}}}^{\ast }\) through a barrier Δ, to show that tunnelling and multiphoton ionization in strong electromagnetic fields follow essentially the same physical laws54. This ratio scales linearly with frequency ω and the inverse of the electric field strength E0. Tunnelling dominates for γ ≪ 1, which holds for sufficiently strong fields and/or low frequencies. Equivalently, it can be expressed as \(\gamma =\sqrt{\Delta /{2U}_{{\rm{P}}}}\) by comparing the barrier Δ with the mean kinetic energy of a free electron in an oscillating electric field of amplitude E0, the ponderomotive potential \({2U}_{{\rm{P}}}={e}^{2}{E}_{0}^{2}/4{m}_{e+h}^{\ast }{\omega }^{2}\). Although similar considerations can be made for quasirelativistic motion of electrons in gapless graphene, it is more realistic for the present experiment to take the full Dirac cone with its distribution of barriers and effective masses into account (Extended Data Fig. 7a). In that case, we obtain a kx-dependent Keldysh parameter26

because both the gap, Δ = 2ħvF|kx|, and the effective mass at the gap, \({m}_{e+h}^{\ast }=\hbar |{k}_{x}|/2{v}_{{\rm{F}}}\), depend on the distance from the Dirac point. By identifying Δ with the MIR photon energy of 124 meV, we get γ = 0.075 for the peak electric fields of 1.57 MV cm−1. If we could switch off intraband acceleration, the relevant transition energy from the valence band to the unperturbed conduction band would increase to two times the doping energy of 200 meV, that is, to Δ = 400 meV. Even then, γ = 0.24 would comfortably place our measurements in the strong-field regime.

The microscopic intraband current density for each time instant, j(t), is directly extracted from the individual photoemission measurements, as in Fig. 2c. After compensation of momentum streaking, the photoemission intensity is integrated in two momentum regions with ky < 0 and ky > 0, respectively, and normalized by the total number of carriers in the two momentum regions at equilibrium. Subtracting the time-dependent number of counts with positive and negative ky components, we obtain a dimensionless value proportional to the microscopic current. To reveal the maximum amplitude of the current density, this procedure is performed for all carriers above the Dirac point (E − ED ≥ 0) within the momentum region |kx − K| < 0.15 Å−1. The result is then calibrated to physical values by multiplying with the Fermi velocity and carrier density in the conduction band of graphene with a Fermi level located 200 meV above the Dirac point, which can be computed from the density of states27. This yields a maximum current density of j(t) = 38.2 A cm−1. This value is very high, as it is even larger than in previous studies on topological insulators8,20, in which the scattering times were two orders of magnitude longer. Given the higher Fermi velocity and field amplitude, a higher current could be expected in the presented experiment. Yet, in a Drude-like regime, this would not be sufficient to compensate for the much higher scattering rate. This confirms that, in ballistic regimes, the current does not follow the Drude model. The same procedure is applied to the results of the simulations based on the SBEs.

After several driving cycles, the observed dynamics of the doped and a hypothetical undoped sample are expected to be similar (Extended Data Fig. 8).

For an empty upper Dirac cone, we expect the carrier injection rate owing to LZM tunnelling to closely follow the magnitude of the electric field and the electron population to increase stepwise, with the highest injection rates occurring at the largest absolute field values. In an n-doped system and for high injection rates, as in the present experiment, Pauli blocking modifies this simple picture (Extended Data Fig. 5a). Electrons can only be injected when there is sufficient empty space in the conduction band near the K point. Consequently, intraband transport plays a decisive role in determining the injection timing. Carrier injection happens most efficiently during the intervals between the extrema of the electric field and the carrier displacement. Our simulations clearly show that this results in a phase delay between the population jumps and the field, which grows as more population has been transferred. Beyond the effects of Pauli blocking and scattering, the injection dynamics can be strongly influenced by quantum interference59. Here we find that they can cause a deviation of a step-like increase in the conduction band population (or electron distribution width) by transforming the plateau regions, following a strong injection phase, into local minima through destructive interference of electron wavepackets (Extended Data Fig. 9). Although the signal-to-noise ratio of the measured conduction band population does not allow us to resolve the jumps in the population during carrier injection experimentally, the dynamics of the increase of the carrier density can be well reproduced with the theory.

The simulations also show that the elongation of the conduction band electron distribution in momentum space is closely tied to the carrier population (Extended Data Fig. 5b). Notably, the weak oscillations in the width of the electron distribution, which are superimposed on the stepwise increase, become more pronounced with scattering (Fig. 4c). This effect is probably caused by a shortening of the effective injection time window. In the absence of scattering, the electron displacement follows the vector potential and is therefore phase-shifted by π/2 relative to the electric field. With scattering, the phase shift between the electric field and the displacement of the electrons shrinks, such that periods of rapid population increase occur only a few femtoseconds before an extremum in the displacement (Extended Data Fig. 5). Experimentally, the oscillation of the width of the electron distribution at twice the frequency of the driving field is clearly visible during the first three cycles of the electric field, that is, at times when there is still substantial carrier injection (Fig. 3c). The observed relative phase with respect to the electric field transient and the COM displacement agrees well with the simulations that include scattering. The data thus support the picture of a well that is periodically driven by the electric field and opened and closed by the oscillating carriers in the conduction band.

The new experimental platform presented here was developed to give direct access to strongly driven electron dynamics without relying heavily on theory or models. This philosophy also translates to data processing: our conclusions only rely on features present in the raw data. The previously described streaking compensation is the first and only step in the data analysis pipeline that is required to ensure that the observed dynamics can be attributed to processes that are happening in the solid and not the vacuum. Following that, the general strategy is that filtering is only used to remove technical noise and signals related to the matrix elements before the data are analysed or to enhance visibility in the figures. Simulations based on the SBEs in relaxation time approximation reveal how the phase delay between an applied waveform (central frequency, 30 THz) and the lightwave-driven current scales with the scattering time. For very small scattering times, the phase difference converges to zero, whereas for longer scattering times, it converges to π/2. The strongest change in the phase delay can be observed for scattering times on the order of a half-cycle of the driving waveform.
