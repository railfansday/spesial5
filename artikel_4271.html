Respect Your Elders: The London to Brighton Car Tour, Michigan Style

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

We leave behind launch control and triple digits to experience slow thrills.

From the November/December 2026 issue of Car and Driver.

More than double that if you count New Londons. There are about 30 Brightons, which I think means there are somewhere around 700 ways one could set up a London-to-Brighton antique-car run. Which, of course, is a totally normal thing to plan, right?

Maybe a few of you with an interest in early automobiles recognize the combination of London and Brighton from the long-running English vintage-car meet that travels between those two cities every November. First run in 1896 by an intrepid group of early—very early—car enthusiasts, the Emancipation Run was held to mark one of the first increases in speed limits: from single digits (4 mph) to a downright scorching 14 mph.

The London-to-Brighton event, or, as it’s now known, the RM Sotheby’s London to Brighton Veteran Car Run, looks like fun, so we decided to have a go between our own London and Brighton in Michigan with some antique American cars.

Several things lined up well. Our course between London Township and the cute main street of Brighton was 57 miles, which is a good parallel to the U.K.’s 97-km (60-mile) route. Plenty of tree-lined country lanes connect the towns, a benefit when organizing a road trip for vehicles whose max speed may be more than 14 mph but still lags behind modern highway pace. Most important, there’s a fantastic community of early-20th-century-car collectors in Michigan, so finding folks who were willing to take out their Fords, Oldsmobiles, Pullmans, and REOs was no problem.

What was a problem was finding a date. Every time I suggested a weekend, I’d get a flurry of texts and emails from the would-be participants. “So sorry,” said Mark Turner. “There’s a steam-car rally that weekend.” It might have also been Turner who couldn’t do another date because of a meet for vintage Bentleys. “I don’t know if I could do June 6th because we have an HCCA [Horseless Carriage Club of America] tour down in Georgia,” said Brent Terry, with apologies. Eventually, I just picked something and hoped a few of the crew would manage to fit it into their busy, brassy schedules.

An 1865 regulation required a flagger with a red warning cloth to walk ahead of any roadgoing mechanical vehicles and limited their max speed to just 4 mph on turnpikes and highways and 2 mph in cities, towns, and villages in the United Kingdom. The law was initially intended to apply to steam-powered road locomotives, which were growing in popularity in England and threatening traditional railways. As cars entered the chat, drivers chafed at the restrictions. An 1896 act of Parliament brought that speed limit up to 14 mph and was thus celebrated by English motoring clubs with a 60-mile rally, an annual tradition now under the 60-mile-long name of RM Sotheby’s London to Brighton Veteran Car Run.

Michigan being Michigan, the day before our run brought pouring rain. It turns out vintage-car people are shockingly willing to brave weather. “I believe it rains a lot in England,” said Rick Lindner. So, we showed up, as did eight shining examples of the earliest entries in American motoring, from a 1904 Ford Model AC owned by Lindner to a late-model 1913 Buick Model 31 helmed by Larry and Joyce Schramm. On the Car and Driver side, we brought doughnuts, passengers, and a lot of questions about old cars.

After the third time I asked someone for the cubic-inch displacement of their engine, John Grace (who organizes his own vintage-car run in the fall and lent his 1911 Regal to teenage brothers Jonathan and Matthew Sinclair for our tour, and whose wife, Dorothy, once drove a Model T across America, making either one of the Graces very qualified to tell me what’s what in prewar car stuff) shook his head. He told me that while he can go look it up for me, owners of cars from this era don’t generally describe their engines in displacement. “We measure in horsepower,” he said. “I never hear anybody ask about cubic inches for early cars.”

It makes sense. In the modern era, cubic inches tend to reflect power output, but back in the days when a Curved Dash Oldsmobile might have a single jug measuring 95 cubic inches and making only four horsepower, it was more useful to announce your car’s 30 horsepower than, say, its 200 cubic inches.

Grace answered this, and all my other questions, with thoughtful enthusiasm, which is something I noticed about all the antique-car people I met that day.

“The older the cars, the nicer the people,” said Larry Schramm. For the hobby to have a future, he said, it needs younger people to ask questions and learn how to drive these cars. Plus, “we’re all in the same boat.” If your car isn’t the one broken on this run, it might be on the next.

Antique-car drives are a bit like off-roading; it’s best to go in pairs so someone can pick you up and get you to the finish. That happened to Ted O’Dell, when his 1913 Buick Model 31 started running a temperature. When I talked to him about it, he was unbothered. Breakdowns are just a part of the process, as they were when the cars were new. It’s historically accurate to get a flat tire.

Even though I’d invited people through different contacts, most of the participants knew one another. Many of these owners have multiple cars, maybe a big four-door for longer tours and a Model T for local runabouts. They loan one another parts and cars and smudgy business cards for the one place that still sells horsehair upholstery stuffing.

We all gathered in a parking lot across the street from Darana Dragway. The vehicle owners hunched over crank starters to coax engines into action amid a chorus of grunts and pops, both mechanical and human. Clouds of tire smoke drifted over from the drag-strip side as smooth-bodied race machines revved big V-8s to numbers that would reduce our two- and four-cylinders to steaming puddles of Babbitt. As an example of divergent evolution, it was stark, but a show of mutual interest implied that while the bodies and power outputs were different, there was a shared soul among us.

Once our great eight were puffing and clattering, we left the dragsters behind in our own clouds of smoke (oil, not tire). I rode in David Liepelt’s 1915 Model T Ford at the head of the line. For Liepelt and his T, this marks a return to C/D. They were the stars of our Model T versus Model S story back in February 2014, and Liepelt is still mad that then–tech director Don Sherman beat his Ford in a Tesla, so we let him lead the tour.

Liepelt set off at a pace that would have shaken the drivers of 1896. He was all limbs, pulling the throttle lever back to full acceleration, upping the spark advance, rocking the gear-selecting pedal into high, and adjusting his Ruckstell two-speed axle. It was like watching an octopus drive a car. I had to tell him to slow down to make sure we didn’t leave anyone behind. It’s impressive what 22 horsepower is capable of, and while acceleration wasn’t going to be the challenge, nobody in the early 1900s was much for a panic stop. “Not good, but better than nothing,” Liepelt said as he barreled through an Ann Arbor intersection. If we were braking, I wasn’t aware. A line in my notebook reads, “It’s a very specific thrill to be in a car you could fall out of.”

Affordability was a theme among the group, which may come as a surprise to people who’ve grown up hearing terms like “Brass Era” and “prewar” only in relation to megabuck Bugattis and Duesenbergs on concours lawns. Those cars still command big prices, but more common brands have come down as older owners die and pass on to their family a garage full of acetylene headlamps and wood-spoke wheels. Our group was unanimous in suggesting a Model T as a gateway drug, including lensman James Lipman, who is now on his second T purchase (the first is shown above).

“The Brass Era ones may be worth $15,000 to $20,000,” said T owner Brent Allen. “But you can pick up a running T for sometimes $7000, $8000 easily.” Learning to drive a Model T is a challenge, but Allen said that shouldn’t put anyone off. Other owners will be eager to teach you, and there are so many reproduction parts. If you have a frame and an engine block, you can build a whole car from catalogs.

That thrill was a recurring theme. It’s the caress of wind in your hair like the first time you rode a bicycle. It’s the nervy pleasure of sitting the canter on a horse. It’s the very real possibility that you’ll never be able to stop. In a world where most cars can hit 100 mph before the end of an on-ramp, it’s amazing how exciting 35 mph can be when it takes all your extremities and maybe one or two of your passenger’s to keep the vehicle out of the nearest hedge.

For Jonathan Sinclair, the youngest of our drivers at 16, learning on a vintage car ruined him for modern transportation. When he was told that he couldn’t take the driver’s test in the family Model T, his mom had to force him to do his remaining training hours in a newer car. “I don’t feel anything!” he complained. “After the involved process of driving an older car, you get in a modern car, and suddenly you don’t have to worry about anything. It feels odd.” He and his brother, Matthew, had been planning to bring their own project, a 1911 Brush, but then the Graces offered them the key to the Regal.

Our summer intern, Alayna Wilkening, joined the Sinclair brothers in the Regal, where she was given the job of watching the MotoMeter hood ornament for signs of overheating. Her official report was they experienced no difficulties aside from having to explain to multiple onlookers that the Regal was a Regal but not a Buick.

Behind them, senior news editor Caleb Miller was riding in luxurious and secure comfort, cushioned in the azure-blue cabin of Daniel Coffman’s 1910 Pullman. I noticed with no small amount of jealousy that he had a nice sturdy roof support bar to hold on to in the turns. His post-run report was that his desire to purchase a vehicle of this vintage has only increased with each exposure. He also rode back with Liepelt and declared the trip “probably the most exhilarating 53 mph I’ve ever experienced.”

Editor-in-chief Tony Quiroga was spared the terror of Liepelt’s sporty T, instead getting the full touring-car experience in Wes Raynard’s flashy red 1909 Oldsmobile Model X3, which cost about $2000 when it was new. “This is the only one known to still exist,” Raynard told me. A quick look at past auction prices shows that the cherry Olds changed hands earlier this year for $55,105, which is considerably more than its original purchase price but a downright bargain to own a one-of-one survivor.

“The ride is so smooth,” Quiroga told me at our rest stop. He looked mildly disappointed that things were going so well. “I was expecting the adventure to be more adventurous. All of these enthusiasts know their cars so well that they could tear down the entire thing and rebuild it on the side of the road. Only everything is working, and we’re ahead of schedule.”

Despite Quiroga’s desire for more chaos, the overall consensus was that the inaugural C/D London-to-Brighton (Michigan) was a great success, and the gang plans to do it again in 2027. Barring any conflicts with the steam-car rally.
