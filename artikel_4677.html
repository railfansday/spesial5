Can a Sleep Divorce Save Your Marriage?

Part of being a family lawyer is knowing how to divorce a couple. Meaning, how to extricate two people from a legal union so they can stand on their own two feet as they move forward independently and, if applicable, as co-parents afterward. This is the obvious part of what I do. The other, less obvious role I have is figuring out whether someone who ends up on a call with me actually needs me for a dissolution.

I suspect I’m dealing with the latter situation when someone tells me they aren’t sleeping, they’re up all night because their partner’s waking or keeping them up, and that it’s negatively affecting their marriage in every way imaginable: they’re irritable, resentful, experiencing weight gain, and a slew of other health problems. They’re also too tired to have sex, so they’ve stopped or practically stopped. But, and this is key, they also tell me they love their spouse and the life they’ve built together and aren’t sure if they want to take such a hard approach, at least just yet.

I’m not a medical doctor, but I’ve been married twice, this time to my forever partner, so I recognize how important sleep is for quality of life and how different sleep habits can cause a host of issues if you don’t keep them in check. This is why I suggest getting to the bottom of what’s keeping you awake at night and, if need be, engaging in what’s colloquially known as a sleep divorce, where you and your partner stay married and do everything married couples typically do—except sleep in the same bed. But before going this route, here’s what I suggest doing first.

According to a 2017 study, more than one-third of Americans don’t get enough sleep, and it’s taking a toll not only on people’s physical health but on their mental health as well. The researchers leading the study suggest that those who are sleep-deprived find it more challenging to be positive-minded than those who are adequately rested, and that an inability to be positive is a common symptom of depression. Depression, another study finds, can increase the likelihood of divorce. Based on these conclusions, it might be worth considering whether your thoughts about the quality of your marriage are actually rooted in the quality of your sleep or something else.

The most obvious way to find out whether poor sleep is the culprit in your marital woes is to actually start getting a good night’s sleep. If you’re keeping your partner up at night, or your partner is keeping you awake because of an apparent medical issue, such as snoring, it’s time to visit a specialist for a sleep study. A sleep study provides data so a medical practitioner can understand what’s going on physically during the night and address it.

If your health is being negatively affected by a partner disrupting your sleep, what’s causing them to wake you may pose a similar or even greater health risk for them. If you aren’t sleeping because your partner is simply inconsiderate, it’s time to address that as well with a conversation and some ground rules.

Trial and error should become your M.O. If you’re first attempting to get to the bottom of a medical mystery, it may take weeks to months to get the answers you need and, once you do, to treat the issue. In the meantime, you may want to try a few other approaches.

That may include adjusting the bedroom temperature and reducing any light coming in. It may also include the person without the medical issue heading to bed first so it’s easier for them to fall asleep, getting earplugs, or moving to a different location once they’ve been woken up. If all else fails, it may be time to try sleeping in separate rooms, aka, get a sleep divorce.

A lot can happen in the bedroom in the moments before falling asleep, and if you’re retreating to separate rooms at this time, you may want to do the following so you and your spouse don’t become further disconnected, causing a sleep divorce to become a precursor to a real divorce.

Spending time together before bed, engaging in personal touch that’s not intended to lead to sex, is an important practice to maintain. Hold hands while watching TV, lay your head on your partner’s shoulder, cuddle under a blanket. If you’re comfortable visiting each other’s bedrooms and doing these things there until it’s time to actually go to sleep, try that. Communicate about what’s comfortable for both of you, then make it a habit.

If you’re finding that due to sleeping in separate bedrooms, sex isn’t happening organically, schedule it or make a point to suggest it. Given you’re not in the same bed where sex may be easier to initiate, you may have to rely on using your words more than in the past. Sleeping together less shouldn’t equate to communicating less. Even better is if it leads to communicating more. Speaking of which…

Whether you’re planning on having sex or not, or are basking in the afterglow, lying together in bed before falling asleep is when couples have their most intimate conversations. A sleep divorce can interfere with that if you let it. So don’t.

Not only should you make time for these conversations, but you should savor them. The more quality conversations you engage in, the closer you become. In my experience, spouses who invest time and attention into each other and their marriages tend to stay married. Where you sleep is far less consequential than what you do when you’re awake.
