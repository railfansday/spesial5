Over the last year, a backlash against surveillance technologies from companies like Flock Safety has caused agencies in communities, including Los Angeles and Dayton, Ohio, to reevaluate or cancel their contracts for tools such as license plate reader cameras and drones.

A recent story in The Guardian points to dozens of schools, mostly college campuses, where contracts with Flock have been discontinued or where student protests and activism are pressuring administrations to cut ties. Some of the schools that have walked away from Flock include Cornell University, Emory University, Penn State University and Stanford University, where a coalition called Safe Stanford successfully organized to convince school officials. At Stanford, the university chose to switch from Flock to technology from Genetec, which stores data for only an hour and doesn’t share information with third parties.

Some of the most recent campus protests against using Flock technology have been on the University of Iowa campus, where 2,000 petition signatures were delivered to campus police, and at the University of Kentucky, where student groups chanted, “Flock out! Flock off!”

Flock, which is one of several companies, including Axon Enterprise, Motorola Solutions and Palantir Technologies, that provide surveillance hardware and software to law enforcement, has been heavily criticized for license plate reader failure rates and abuses within law enforcement.

In an email to CNET, the Atlanta-based company declined to offer the number of colleges or schools it works with, though a recent research paper published in Policing & Society put the number at around 150.

Flock stressed in its email to CNET that customers it contracts with own and control their own data.

“Students deserve to feel safe at school, and Flock license plate reader technology has a measurable impact on helping to solve crimes and increase public safety,” the spokesperson said. “Flock LPR cameras capture a point-in-time image of the rear of a vehicle. Flock does not use facial recognition, cannot identify who is inside a vehicle and cannot determine who the vehicle is registered to.”

When government agencies like police departments or county sheriff’s offices employ a company like Flock to provide surveillance services, it typically involves installing cameras in public locations and pointing them at street intersections or on buildings.

College and high school campuses may have different rules for on-campus surveillance and for public schools. They might also be bound by state laws that also apply to government agencies.

Peikoff said that most courts have allowed the collection of the types of data Flock Safety collects. However, Peikoff said that problems can arise regarding how long agencies such as police departments or schools can retain the data they collect. In the past, activists have pointed to abuses that have occurred when law enforcement workers inappropriately accessed or shared information collected by Flock, in some cases, using it for stalking.

“Maintaining a vast, queryable database of location histories of individuals about whom there’s no particularized suspicion not only presents an opportunity for abuse, but is in itself, I believe, a Fourth Amendment violation,” Peikoff said.
