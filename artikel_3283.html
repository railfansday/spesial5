‘3:30’ Filmmaker Shlok Choudhari & Producer Sunil Sadarangani On Making A Women’s Mental Health Awareness Noir In South Mumbai

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

Sometimes all it takes is a little gusto and a cold pitch on LinkedIn to get a film rolling. At 23, Shlok Choudhari left college and followed his dream of filmmaking with his first short, 3:30. Starring Priyanka Zemse, the black-and-white psychological noir follows a woman navigating Mumbai during the titular dark hour as medication, memory, and a growing sense of danger blur together. Choudhari drew on two real events he witnessed to explore trauma and misdiagnosis.

After screening at the Oscar-qualifying Tasveer Film Festival, the film is headed to the Yellowstone International Film Festival in Mumbai. Below, Choudhari and producer Sunil Sadarangani talk to Deadline about taking courage in filmmaking, developing themes around mental health awareness, and having an eye towards the future. DEADLINE: This is your first short film, and you’re 23. You should be in school, don’t you think? What compelled you to make this short? Watch on Deadline

SHLOK CHOUDHARI: Well, I made this film when I was 22. I was in my second year of college studying advertising and journalism. With all due respect to journalism, and I appreciate the work you all do, I didn’t want to study it anymore. I love movies.

CHOUDHARI: I grew up watching movies, and I still watch at least one a day. So, I’ve always wanted to make movies, but I would usually drop the idea until I eventually felt I had to do something. So, I wrote 3:30 and went on LinkedIn to cold-pitch people. Sunil [Sadarangani] was one of them, along with my executive producer, Jimmy Velarde, and other producers. We raised money, and I hired a crew also on LinkedIn, and we made the film.

DEADLINE: Sunil, what went through your mind when this young filmmaker cold-pitched you on LinkedIn?

SUNIL SADARANGANI: I wear a lot of hats, and there’s so much catfishing on LinkedIn. When I got Shlok’s first message, I thought, “Who is this guy catfishing me?” His profile picture was a little dodgy, kind of a far-off black-and-white shot. He was wearing some suit, and I thought, “What’s going on here?” But he kept messaging me. [Laughs]. He was persistent and talked about film history and the palette and noir style that he wanted for the film. You can pull things from the internet, but I instinctively felt there was something there. Then he sent me his presentation deck, and I was impressed by how he put it together. But he hadn’t made a film before. So, when he told me that, I wanted to give him advice on the way he was going about reaching out to other crew members to make this whole thing happen for himself. I told him to gather an experienced crew and go to the location where he wanted to film so we could see something on film. He wouldn’t have time on set, and he had a limited budget.

He wanted to shoot in South Mumbai, where I’m from. Not many films are shot in that area because it’s highly sensitive after the terrorist attacks in 2008. There are still a lot of police patrols and surveillance there today. However, I thought it was an ambitious idea and that, along with his vision, attracted me to the project. When he came back with footage, I could see on film what I’d seen on paper in his presentation. So, I said that I was in and that I would love to collaborate and help in any way I could.

DEADLINE: Shlok, talk more about the difficulties of filming in South Mumbai.

SHLOK CHOUDHARI: There were numerous obstacles. I wanted wet streets, with the white light glimmering—I love that look in black and white. That’s why I wanted to shoot during the monsoon season, but I didn’t know how difficult it would be. It was the first time I’d seen such a big boom mic and everyone with their setups and lights. I had no idea it would be like that.

I thought I was going to go with my iPhone, and then suddenly there was a whole crew. The crew attracted a lot of attention, and there were cops at every location. Even though I had permission, they kept coming by. I had a good assistant director who handled the cops and got them to look the other way while we were filming. Running on schedule during monsoon was another obstacle, but it was a ride. It was my film school in a way.

DEADLINE: Right, so when you lost the desire for journalism in college, did you switch to filmmaking?

CHOUDHARI: I was at Kelkar College in Mumbai. I was studying journalism and advertising, but I didn’t complete it.

DEADLINE: Wait. You dropped out of school?

CHOUDHARI: Yeah, I dropped out to make this.

CHOUDHARI: They’re happy now. They’re fine. [Laughs].

SADARANGANI: Now he’s getting international recognition. They’re happy. [Laughs].

CHOUDHARI: This is my first international trip ever [and I’m here in Los Angeles], and it’s so huge.

SADARANGANI: He’s never traveled abroad, so he’s loving it here in LA.

DEADLINE: And you’re bringing this story internationally across several film festivals, which is great for sexual harassment and mental health awareness, I think. Talk a bit more about the inspiration behind 3:30.

CHOUDHARI: 3:30 stems from two real events I witnessed. My ex-girlfriend had witnessed a traumatizing experience: She saw her brother kill her father and grandfather, then kill himself. He had schizophrenia. She was seeking psychiatric care and going to a psychiatrist every week, but from my perspective, the medication wasn’t doing her any good. I felt it was triggering trauma, giving her more hallucinations and bringing her down. I would tell her, “Maybe it’s the medication,” and she would say, “No, it’s prescribed. It’s written, so it’s right. It has to be right.” She had this belief in authority. I had a lot of fallacies too, things you think are right one day and later wonder how you could have believed them. The next month, we changed psychiatrists and found out she had been misdiagnosed. I thought, “Wow, this is interesting.”

Meanwhile, I knew a girl, a nine-year-old who was very close to me. She was sexually assaulted by a family friend who lived next door. He had a son about my age and a family. The girl was too young to understand what had happened to her. She was brutally assaulted. The police and nurses asked her what happened, and then we found out. I thought, “This is just crazy. I had to honor these stories.” I could go online and tell people about them, but I wanted to express them. I thought if I put these two things together, I might have a story worth telling. That’s how I came up with 3:30.

DEADLINE: And for the folks at home, I want to make sure you get your point across here about the message of the film too. You wouldn’t describe it as anti-medication, right?

CHOUDHARI: No, no. This is beyond a cautionary tale, and it’s not just about awareness. There is treatment, but psychiatric treatment is often trial and error. A psychiatrist may try something that works, or it may not. Be aware that you don’t have to commit to it. You can seek another opinion. I want it to raise awareness, but also to make people aware that they may be in a situation without realizing it. Be aware that it can be trial and error. Definitely seek psychiatric help and support for mental health.

SADARANGANI: Medication can help bring some balance to what’s going on internally, especially after experiences like these. But human connection and psychiatric help can also help balance things out. Having another person as an anchor can help, especially for women dealing with sexual trauma. That can affect people around them, too.

CHOUDHARI: There was a lot of opposition when I was starting out. I cold-pitched people in Mumbai, and they said the story was too bold. They said, “How can you make something like this?” They said it was unethical and that they wouldn’t support it. Mumbai, and India in that sense, can be conservative. But this is true. I witnessed it. I’m not making it up. I held onto my guns and kept reaching out to more people. Then I connected with Sunil, Betty, Jimmy, Paul and my other producers.

DEADLINE: How did you find your lead actress, Priyanka Zemse?

CHOUDHARI: It was at a fashion event. She was there as a guest, and I went up to her and asked, “Would you like to be in a movie? This is the subject matter. Would you like to be part of it?” That piqued her interest, and she said yes, though not immediately.

SADARANGANI: The interesting thing about Priyanka is that Shlok connected with me on LinkedIn, and I was asking him questions, putting on my journalist hat. I asked if he had anyone in mind to play the lead, and he mentioned Priyanka. I had met her two years earlier at the Tasveer Film Festival in Seattle, where one of her other films was screening. We had connected there, so I already knew who she was. It made the connection feel meant to be.

I also want to recognize the other women producers and crew members. Our executive producer Betty J. Butler has been a pillar of support, and this kind of abuse is also part of her personal experience. She connected with the story and supported Shlok throughout the process. You need a team to make a story happen, and Betty and the other women on the crew encouraged and pushed Shlok. Our editor, Navtika Sharma, did a great job, as did our hair and makeup artist, Ronicka Ralph. We had a good balance of people on the film.

DEADLINE: I know you mentioned earlier that black and white was always part of your vision, but what was the inspiration behind it? Why was this the right way to tell the story to you?

CHOUDHARI: I love film noir and always wanted to make one. So, I started studying where it originated and how it developed over time. I found it had a lot to do with German Expressionism after World War I, which was about externalizing internal conflicts. There was a lot of vibrancy and expressive imagery. You see it in films like The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari and Fritz Lang’s Metropolis and M. I love mise-en-scène and the way those films expressed themselves. They weren’t defying the laws of physics; they were creating an exaggerated reality. Their themes reflected the socioeconomic turmoil of postwar Germany.

After the rise of Hitler, filmmakers like Billy Wilder and Fritz Lang came to Hollywood and brought their point of view. That helped lead to film noir. Noir had its own themes, including the Cold War, detective stories, and taboo subjects. It wasn’t just dramatic lighting. I asked myself whether film noir was the right treatment for a story about misdiagnosis and sexual assault in 2025 or 2026. I thought it was. I’m so thankful to our cinematographer; we were like brothers. He was wonderful and experienced; I’m glad he chose to work with me.

DEADLINE: What has the film festival experience been like?

SADARANGANI: In addition to the Tasveer South Asian Film Festival in Seattle, we got into the City of Angels Film Festival in LA. There we won Best Feminist Film.

DEADLINE: What was it like to receive that award as a male director?

SADARANGANI: I think it was the subject matter. Lisa K. Crosato, the festival director and founder, was taken by the story Shlok told through 3:30. The festival had previously been called the City of Angels Women’s Film Festival, advocating for women’s stories and female filmmakers. Lisa felt strongly about the subject matter, and that’s how we received the award.

DEADLINE: Shlok, what would you like to do next?

CHOUDHARI: I want to make one more short, and I’m also developing my feature. They’re personal stories. They have to be personal. I write every day. So far, the universe, the force, has been with me. It’s a privilege. I’m taking one step at a time, but I know what I want to do next.
