At last, I've finally played Faraway, an enigma I've been waiting 16 years to see

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

"We're given so much beauty by the universe".

For 16 years I've been watching the skies. It was 16 years ago, back in the first giddy rush of smartphone gaming, that I saw a 30-second trailer for a game set in space. It was a game about a comet, hurtling through the unknown. It wasn't clear what you'd do in this game, whether you'd control the comet itself or the space it moved through. It wasn't clear if this was some kind of arcade game, or a new spin on the endless runner, or even just a kind of cosmic sandbox. All I had was the comet and a name: Faraway. Faraway! I was captivated.

Faraway is one of those wonderfully cludged together childhood words, like "nonesuch". Where is the edge of the galaxy? Oh, it's faraway. Where is the edge of the universe? Oh, there's nonesuch thing.

Okay, Faraway came with one other bit of info I could cling to at the time. It was being made by Steph Thirion, a musician and designer and, I sensed, many other things too. At the time Faraway was announced I'd just played his game Eliss, which was an early smartphone hit. In the absence of any more information on Faraway itself, I had to make do with that. Maybe Faraway would be a bit like Eliss?

Eliss itself, though? Oh, boy. This thing is beautiful, frantic, strange, compelling and indescribable. And think what that means, just for a second, for something to be both compelling and indescribable. I am driven to do these things, but I don't really know what these things are. I'm prodding little bits of colour around, maybe planets, maybe molecules? I am combining them, gathering them, turning them into something greater together than they are by themselves?

Last week I spoke to Thirion - as for why, well, we will get to that - and at the start I asked him about Eliss, about how it felt to make a game that was so hard to put into words, and if he actually had any words for it himself.

"I love that question," he laughs. "I guess I would say that for Eliss, it was all about that interaction of moving things, moving those little circles with each of your fingers individually." He pauses, thinking back. "It's not something I had envisioned. I was programming, and I was basically testing touch controls because I was trying to build a different game. But then I was playing with that [stuff]. I was like: that feels really nice. Can I build a game around that? So I guess there is something beyond language about that interaction. I was attracted to it in that way. It wasn't rational. It was a feeling. It feels good."

It's both thrilling and slightly melancholic to think back to the days of Eliss. Apps were in ascendence, but nobody fully understood them yet. There weren't yet consultants or established monetisation pathways. There wasn't even much agreement on the best ways to approach the touchscreen. Eliss and other weird, memorable, hard-to-talk-about games were all part of this Cambrian explosion. You'd get up in the morning and check socials and there was a new game everyone was enthusing about. There was a new world to get lost in.

"I would say that's true even outside of mobile," Thirion tells me. "Just indies in general. There was a lot of excitement going around and a lot of eyeballs. What are people gonna think about next?" He takes a long breath. "It felt like there's a lot of opportunity for new things and new ideas, and people were excited about that. Everything was set up for innovation. It felt like I was part of something."

This is the world that Faraway and its comet first emerged from, streaking across a night sky in which any kind of discovery felt possible. "The real genesis was actually just before Eliss," Thirion says. "I was really spending a lot of time thinking about a possible career in video games. I was an interaction designer and programmer doing interactive experiences, mostly for advertising.

"And for some reason, the iPhone was the thing that made me think I could do something on my own, and what I'd like to do is probably video games. So I was I was thinking a lot about that, maybe obsessively, to the point where there was a night that I was in the countryside in France and just looking at the stars and thinking: it would be great to have a video game where you can fly in the sky that I'm looking at, in this 2D firmament, right? That was it."

The next step towards Faraway came at GDC in 2010, with a call for games that could be controlled with a single button. "I started prototyping," Thirion says. "I tried a few things, and I had this idea of using this kind of physics-y thing about swinging around stars. It actually worked pretty quickly."

Then? Sixteen years pass. What was happening during this time?

It's a long answer with, perhaps, a short answer buried inside it. Let's deal with the long answer first.

"It's not like I worked 16 years on this game," Thirion laughs. He explains that the first prototype was begun in 2010 for the GDC one-button competition. It was playable by March. "That was basically at the beginning," he says. "I was like: This is a very simple game, and I'm just going to put it out in a couple months."

And after that? "I started to have greater ambitions, and then life happened," he says. He didn't work on the game for seven years, and then Annapurna Interactive found him and asked him to return to Faraway, offering support. "And so, after that, the journey was to turn [the game] into an audiovisual experience.

"And so I started doing an audiovisual experience, but it was still aesthetically like Ellis: low-resolution pixel art. I had the ambition of taking it to a different language. I basically wanted to make the jump from procedural low-resolution to procedural infinite resolution!" He ended up with a very smooth, almost vector-like aesthetic. It looks beautiful, but it came at a price. "That took a lot of effort," he says. "Also to make it performant? That required a rewrite of the game into a completely new engine. It was a new language for me as well, so I had to learn this new language."

All of this is extremely convincing, as are a hundred other bumps in the road. It makes sense. I've written about games for twenty years, and my sense is that game development is hard, and that it takes time.

But there's something else Thirion says. And this makes me wonder if part of the truth as to why Faraway took so long to make is actually a lot simpler, and a lot more human.

"I was thinking a lot about Tetris," Thirion says. "I had just started making video games, and I thought a lot about Tetris because it's a very simple rule set that seems to be compelling whether you're two hours in or 1000 hours in, right? And that's like kind of a rare thing.

"And at the time we were talking about: are game's art and so on. And I was like, man, to me, Tetris is art. It does something almost impossible. At least now I look at it and I think it does something almost impossible. But at the time, I was like, well, why are games growing in complexity and quantity instead of staying simple and going deep? And so, I was really asking myself the question: where can I find that balance of just being compelling at any skill level with a very minimal rule set, and that kind of took me into this rabbit hole of experimentation."

It's hard for me not to sympathise with this. Thirion had worked on something for a very short period of time, and he'd found something that felt - well, that felt kind of eternal, boundless in there. A comet swinging through space with one touch controls! What could be more pleasing, more engaging than that? But with that came a lot of challenges. It wasn't just a question of what to add. Adding things would be easy. It was how to create a deep experience that didn't overburden the whole idea and make you lose sight of its simplicity. Put like that, 16 years doesn't seem like a very long time at all.

That's his journey, anyway. As for mine? Well, 16 years is a lot of time if you aren't a comet. I continued writing about games. I got married. I had a daughter. My daughter grew up and went to primary school and is now at secondary school. The whole games industry transformed and then transformed again and is transforming again now. I started learning to swim!

And throughout all this, every few years I would email Thirion and ask if he was ready to talk yet, whether he was ready to talk about Faraway and whether his comet was still blazing somewhere in some private night sky.

Earlier this year I looked him up once more and saw a new trailer. It was short again, and there was the comet I had seen all those years ago. And then, at the end, there was a date. A release date. That comet was finally swinging by the earth.

And so a few weeks ago I loaded up a game I had been waiting to play for 16 years and which I realised, as I paged through the early screens, that I still knew absolutely nothing about. Well. Do you want to hear about it? Or would you rather discover it for yourself?

I would rather you discover it for yourself. But if you don't want to, listen:

Faraway is a game about a comet. The comet is racing through space, and you are the comet, even if you only control it indirectly. Touch a button - there is only one button - and you can bind the comet to the gravity of a nearby star. Keep your finger pressed down on the button and you will remain gravitationally bound, which means you can use stars to adjust your course, your trajectory, your speed. Let go of the button and you're off again. Off into the depths of space. And out there, new stars await.

From this simple interaction, Faraway builds a constellation of challenges that I find both simple to understand and - yes - incredibly difficult to adequately put into words. I'm not going to tell you too much, because I really want you to play this thing, but Faraway at its core is a bit like sewing, a bit like driving, a bit like counting and making shapes and a bit like desperately trying to stay alive. That's it. It's beautiful and serene, but it's also desperate. It's abstract and reliable and I find it extremely moving to play.

More than anything, I think I love the feel of it. The steady arc of the comet bends and flexes beneath you as you push and hold the button. There's a thrilling sense of just about bending something elemental to your will. There's beauty to be found in timing your escape from an orbit, and the orbit paths themselves, when you press and hold the button and new trajectories announce themselves, have a Spirograph delight to them. Does it really matter what you do in a game when it has stuff like this in it?

Now, I am weird, and I spend a lot of my time on Earth thinking about the wilds of space and the vast distances and the places we will never get to see and all the things that are so hostile to life that we wouldn't survive a second in their presence. I think about the Wow Signal and the Great Attractor and Tabby's Star and all of that beautiful, terrifying stuff. It calms me down. It puts my own problems in perspective. It's probably the closest thing I get to any kind of religious observance.

And Thirion thinks about this stuff too. I know because, after 16 years, I asked him. "It really resonates with me," he says. "I do spend a lot of time thinking about [the universe]. I don't know why there is anything at all, right? And the fact that we can never answer that question, and the fact that there are many questions that we'll probably never be able to answer.

"But I also spend a lot of time looking at the clouds and looking at the grass and how the light hits the drops of water. There's so much beauty. We're given so much beauty by the universe, and we're so lucky.

"And the game comes out of that a little bit. I don't know exactly what the game is about, but maybe you could interpret it as: why am I here? Why is life so short? Why is life so long?"

He laughs. "I often ask myself: why is there anything at all? And how is it so beautiful?"
