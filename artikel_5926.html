Trump’s real estate company continued inking deal after deal around the world during his presidency, enriching regimes and the administration in the process. Trump’s children saw their wedding parties sponsored by sanctioned Russian oligarchs, and they traveled around the world monetizing their last names, while governments plied Trump with everything from luxury watches to luxury jets and beyond.

The result of all this was a spectacular midterm defeat for Republicans. Now, the Democrats riding the blue wave know that voters expect massive change going forward, especially as it pertains to cleaning up these Augean stables of corruption. There are plenty of directions that the new Congress could go in, and there are plenty of scandals and policies to choose from. But when it comes ending the kind of corruption that has effectively privatized U.S. foreign policy, here are some clear places to start.

Because Democrats will control both chambers of Congress, and because there is little likelihood that Trump will sign any legislation passed into law, the logical place for Democrats to begin is with hearings. Democrats will be able to appoint committee chairs—or even form select committees—to oversee and direct investigations and wield subpoena power to force targets to comply with investigations.

Trump’s family should be the first focus. Americans watched the president’s children grow spectacularly wealthy through an impressive array of international scandals. There is, for instance, the role of Donald Trump Jr. in helping oversee World Liberty Financial, a crypto firm that received a covert investment from the United Arab Emirates while the country received high-end chips that had been previously blocked. Then, there’s Jared Kushner. The president’s son-in-law has been at the center of U.S. policy on everything from failed negotiations between Russia and Ukraine to failed negotiations with Iran.

The problem wasn’t just Kushner’s lack of regional or technical expertise. Rather, it was his conflicts of interest. Kushner’s private investment firm has received billions of dollars from Iran’s geopolitical adversaries, and it has profited from things like Israeli arms manufacturing and expansion in Palestine. The Russian government has meanwhile begun dangling trillion-dollar arrangements in front of Kushner and his allies.

But the family can’t be the sole focus for congressional investigators. They should also target pardons, sanctions, and prosecutions. There is a long list of crooks and kleptocrats who have been openly bidding to get a presidential pardon or have legal cases against them dropped, including the former president of Honduras, who was convicted on drug-trafficking and bribery charges, and India’s wealthiest businessman, who was charged with fraud and bribery only to have his case expunged. The Trump administration has also wielded sanctions to protect corrupt governments around the world—for example, by targeting anti-corruption advocates in Brazil.

Finally, one particular issue is at the core of voters’ concerns about corruption: campaign finance. Congress should highlight how the United States’ porous campaign finance laws have opened the door to corrupt forces, including foreign regimes, looking to pocket American politicians. The country’s corporate secrecy laws present the perfect ways for foreign forces to mask their financing, and their corruption, of U.S. politics. Thanks to the combination of anonymous shell companies, unlimited caps on corporate election spending, and unenforced campaign finance regulations, it is now “virtually impossible to discern the extent of foreign-influenced corporate spending in U.S. elections.”

These hearings, investigations, and subpoenas will help show American voters just how badly the Trump administration’s corruption has damaged the country’s interests. And with 2028 right around the corner, Democrats should also begin preparing more dramatic steps they can take if they win back the White House and are in a position to pass meaningful legislation.

There are plenty of sweeping anti-corruption reforms needed, but they fall into a handful of key buckets. The first is rebuilding. For all the damage he’s done, Trump has largely left the bones of the United States’ anti-corruption policies in place, preferring non-enforcement to outright repeal. This means that a future Democratic administration can immediately reinstate and fully fund the anti-corruption task forces and offices that Trump dissolved. It should also announce that it will start enforcing the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act again, restore the now-defunct shell company registry, and close a range of anti-money laundering loopholes that many American industries still enjoy.

But you can’t simply rebuild. You have to expand. In tandem with congressional allies, a future administration should create new anti-money laundering requirements that cover everything from the crypto industry to law firms. It should also make the U.S. shell company registry public, accessible to anyone in the United States, and expand it to cover institutions like trusts. Legislation must also be passed to explicitly ban profiteering by presidents and their family members and to reform the president’s pardon powers.

From there, Democrats must begin thinking broader, and they must begin building an anti-corruption architecture that can last for decades. They should create a new Anti-Corruption Bureau to coordinate legislative and executive efforts and serve as a clearinghouse for the government’s anti-corruption work. More ambitiously, Democrats should begin exploring constitutional amendments to tackle corruption. This process is one of the key tools available for overturning Supreme Court rulings that both allowed unchecked campaign financing and severely restricted definitions of bribery and corruption, leading directly to the current morass.

These reforms are all eminently doable. And while there are plenty of clear domestic reasons for pursuing them, they are also crucial for rebuilding U.S. foreign policy. Voters expect nothing less.
