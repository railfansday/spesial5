The Gun Industry Is Divided Over Federal’s New High-Pressure 6.5 Creedmoor Ammo. Here’s Why

Tanggal: 2026-11-11

Published Nov 10, 2026 1:00 PM EST

The latest for gun hunters and competitive shooters.

Late last month Bergara became the first gun manufacturer to explicitly state Federal’s 6.5 Creedmoor Plus Peak ammo is not approved for use in its bolt-action rifles. This reverses earlier guidance that the new high-pressure cartridge technology was compatible with Bergara’s existing 6.5 Creedmoor rifles.

The 6.5 Creedmoor Plus Peak, introduced this summer, has the same dimensions as a traditional brass 6.5 Creedmoor cartridge and was specifically designed by Federal Ammunition for use in existing 6.5 Creed rifles. It is not a new cartridge, but an improvement to an existing cartridge. This unusual dynamic is part of the regulatory and potential liability concerns currently vexing the gun industry.

Federal’s proprietary steel-alloy case technology contains higher pressures that drive bullets at faster speeds for better performance. Plus Peak operates at 80,000 psi, compared to about 62,000 to 65,000 psi for brass 6.5 Creed cartridges. This is possible because the Peak Alloy case material that Federal has developed is much stronger than brass. It acts as a containment vessel insulating the action and barrel from the extra pressure without inflicting extra stress on the rifle.

The ammunition has undergone substantial testing by Federal, a dozen gunmakers and component companies, the U.S. military (which has ordered 40 million cases), and other industry professionals.

And indeed, Bergara rifles were originally deemed “compatible” with this 6.5 Creed Plus Peak ammo, according to previous guidance from Federal and media coverage of the ammo’s launch in June. But on Oct. 29 Bergara announced that the new ammunition “is NOT approved for use in any Bergara rifle chambered in 6.5 Creedmoor.”

“These cartridge pressures haven’t been evaluated under SAAMI’s standard development process,” reads the statement, referring to the Sporting Arms and Ammunition Manufacturing Institute. “Ammunition operating outside SAAMI pressure standards for its caliber should not be used in any rifle unless Bergara formally confirms compatibility for that model.”

SAAMI sets the gun industry’s self-imposed guardrails with voluntary performance standards for firearms, ammunition, and suppressors. Bergara did not cite any incidents of catastrophic failures or injury related to shooting the new case technology through its rifles. (No one we spoke to for this story reported being aware of a catastrophic failure while testing the Plus Peak in existing 6.5 Creed rifles.)

Bergara announced in 2025 it would launch rifles in 7mm Backcountry, which debuted the same high-pressure cartridge technology as the Plus Peak. Various online distributors and retailers indicate Bergara’s HMR Pro and MG Lite models would be available in the 7BC, but the company doesn’t currently offer any rifles chambered in 7BC on its website. It’s unclear if any preproduction or production models were ever manufactured. Bergara declined to comment for this story.

In a June statement, SAAMI acknowledged the development of the 6.5 Creedmoor Plus Peak and the creation of a working group — at Federal’s request — to evaluate the new case technology on “performance, pressure, testing, and interchangeability standards.”

SAAMI and its member companies are also discussing “the technical issues and opportunities presented by advanced cartridge case technologies and their interaction with existing firearms and suppressors.”

The organization stopped short of taking a position on the ammunition and instead urged hunters and shooters to contact their rifle’s manufacturer before shooting Federal’s 6.5 Creed Plus Peak.

“At this time, SAAMI’s guidance stays the same,” SAAMI’s senior director of technical affairs, Alan Serven, tells Outdoor Life. “As this is not a standardized cartridge, we can’t really say anything definitive about it, so we are directing folks to the manufacturer of their firearm. And we do caution that an answer from one manufacturer cannot be interpreted as a blanket statement … It’s on a case-by-case basis with each of the manufacturers and potentially down to model.”

On Nov. 5 the sporting-goods retailer Scheels sent an internal memo to its employees announcing any Plus Peak ammo should not be stocked on store floors or made available to customers.

“As of yesterday morning we have added a ‘DNS’ Do not Stock rating to all SKUs of Federal 6.5 Creedmoor +Peak ammunition,” reads the memo, obtained by Outdoor Life. “The Kinetic Group [Federal’s parent company], gun manufacturers and SAAMI are continuing to review this product. Once we have more information on their review and the stocking of the 6.5 Creedmoor +Peak, we will make a determination on how to proceed.”

Scheels has put all direct orders for Plus Peak on hold, according to the memo. The retailer also instructed its ammo and gun teams to accept any returns from customers and put any received Plus Peak “somewhere where we can ensure it will not make its way to the floor to be sold.” When Outdoor Life called a Scheels store asking about Plus Peak availability and the possibility of ordering any, the employee who answered the phone replied that no, this was not possible because “corporate put the kibosh on this until SAAMI figures out the safety and pressure issues.” The store policy affects all five SKUs of Plus Peak.

“Federal’s Peak Alloy case technology is the wave of the future for rifle ammunition,” Kinetic Group’s VP of marketing Jason Nash tells Outdoor Life, noting that Federal sought to work with as many rifle makers as possible in its development of high-pressure loads. “Over the past seven years Federal has manufactured millions of rounds in multiple cartridges and firearms. Federal has the utmost confidence in this new technology and its function in today’s firearms.”

Some gunmakers have confirmed to OL that all their existing 6.5 Creedmoor rifles are compatible with Plus Peak and they have no safety concerns whatsoever. Others have remained quiet on the advisability of using Plus Peak in their 6.5 Creedmoor rifles. Bergara remains the only rifle maker who has publicly disallowed its use in its bolt-action rifles. (Break-action rifles from CVA, Bergara’s sister company, are also not approved for Plus Peak, according to Federal’s site.)

This about-face on the use of Plus Peak in Bergara rifles raises many more questions than it answers. For instance, is this hesitancy around the 6.5 Creedmoor Plus Peak rooted in legitimate concerns of consumer safety? Or is it ultimately driven by the potential threat that some companies see to their bottom lines? Here’s what to know.

Although the conversations around Plus Peak in SAAMI’s working group are confidential, industry insiders are keyed into the crux of the issue. Here are two of the primary camps.

No one seems to be disputing the performance gains of the Plus Peak ammo. Instead, the debate is over how to standardize it.

“It definitely is an interesting idea what Federal is doing,” says one gun manufacturer who spoke on the condition of anonymity. “The industry itself is not politicking against it. The industry is not against new technology. We know and understand new products are what drive business. That’s not the issue. There’s probably some ammunition companies that are bitter or angry they didn’t think of it first, but that’s also neither here nor there.”

According to this source, the problem is the new technology currently puts too much onus on gunmakers without the opportunity to earn revenue. In fact, it poses a big expense for bigger manufacturers.

“For those of us who played by the rules and built guns to SAAMI’s standards, now we have an ammunition out there that’s outside that spec. And we’re at a conundrum to say, ‘Is it safe or not safe in our guns,’” says the gunmaker. “So one [option is] we have to go back and retest all our guns at an immense cost to verify those guns will pass these pressures.”

Some guns will inevitably pass, they say, but other guns may not. This could result in a laundry list of liability concerns in which gun companies must painstakingly note — online and in owners manuals — which rifle models are compatible and incompatible with Plus Peak.

The biggest fear is that a customer will get hurt. It’s also a bad look for any existing guns that don’t pass, given that it “may expose ‘weaknesses’ that don’t exist” in rifles that were built to spec for standard 6.5 Creed loads.

Another option for gunmakers is to simply not retest guns and not comment on (or in Bergara’s case, publicly come out against) the 6.5 Creedmoor Plus Peak.

“It does kind of stand in the face of why we have SAAMI and why we self regulate,” says the source. “So it’s a legitimate concern. We won’t approve [Plus Peak] either. Will our guns pass it? Most likely. But we’re not chomping at the bit to go through all the testing to verify that. We have other things we need to worry about.”

The right way to have handled this new ammo technology, in this gunmaker’s mind, was to have rolled out a new cartridge that could have more easily received SAAMI approval and allowed gunmakers to collaborate on new rifle builds.

“We would have all happily engaged in a new caliber that did this, like the 7 Backcountry. We knew the pressures, and we could build a gun specifically to handle that caliber with those higher pressures. No problem. It’s the retro-fitting for everybody. Why should we go to a pile of work? Just build a new caliber. It’s a very cool technology. We’re all about that. We’re not dragging our feet because we’re old and crotchety. We’re trying to protect our business and this industry.”

In that scenario this ammunition wouldn’t be a 6.5 Creedmoor using Plus Peak technology — it would be a whole new cartridge called, for instance, the 6.5 Creedmoor Plus Peak. Of course, this would require shooters to buy a new rifle.

“The difference is meaningful,” says Outdoor Life shooting editor John Snow of this hypothetical scenario. “And presumably, you’d have to have firearms marked with that new cartridge name in order to be in compliance with what SAAMI has deemed acceptable.”

Federal has treated the 6.5 Creedmoor Plus Peak not as a new cartridge, but an improvement on existing 6.5 Creed ammo. The difference, again, is that it contains higher-than-SAAMI-approved pressures that drive bullets at higher velocities.

These souped-up loads give hunters and shooters the ability to get 6.5 PRC-like performance out of their existing 6.5 Creedmoor rifles.

To some, the nature of Plus Peak is so monumental that industry disruption is natural and even necessary.

“We are embracing high-pressure technology like Peak Alloy,” says Sean Murphy, director of sales and marketing for Proof Research (and an occasional OL contributor). “It’s a development similar to switching from black to smokeless powder.”

Some of the more nimble gunmakers, like Proof, have already tackled the testing they need to feel comfortable about giving customers approval to run the higher-pressure load in its guns. That’s because Proof predicts this high-pressure ammunition is going to be a major player in the future, and the company is working to develop barrel and rifle technology to complement it by maximizing accuracy and performance.

“Proof Research has extensively tested Federal Peak Alloy cases in multiple cartridges, including 6.5 Creedmoor and 7 Backcountry,” says Murphy. “In 6.5 Creedmoor alone, thousands of rounds have been fired through hunting, target, and semi-automatic precision rifles with no case failures or firearm damage. Additionally, many thousands of rounds have been fired by Proof with Peak Alloy 5.56 ammunition, also without issue … Chamber-bore obstruction testing across every Proof Research receiver design showed Peak Alloy cases outperforming equivalent brass cases, with a greater safety margin at 80,000 psi than brass cases at 60,000 psi.”

The Montana-based rifle maker is also one of the most advanced barrel makers in the country. It spent years developing its PXT barrel, which Proof says accomodates much higher pressures and velocities without shortening the barrel’s lifespan.

“During our PXT barrel testing and development, more than 10,000 rounds of 7 Backcountry were fired — including 5,000 and 3,500 on individual receivers — with no damage, and wear levels comparable to brass-cased ammunition,” says Murphy.

The company also issued an apparent rebuttal to Bergara’s announcement of Oct. 29 with posts to Instagram and Facebook that same day, declaring full support and formal endorsement of Peak Alloy cartridges in its rifles.

“We have tested all our rifles — and many from other manufacturers — extensively with the Plus Peak ammunition and it’s proven to be completely safe,” says Murphy.

As part of its development and testing of the Plus Peak, Federal worked with a dozen gunmakers and related businesses. Federal confirmed to American Rifleman this summer that certain rifles from five of those companies are compatible with Plus Peak: Christensen Arms, Weatherby, Seekins Precision, Geissele, and Nemo Arms. Federal matches guidance from SAAMI that hunters and shooters should contact their gunmaker to specify which models are approved.

“Our testing has shown 6.5 Creedmoor +Peak operates in a variety of 6.5 Creedmoor firearm platforms,” Federal advises in the Plus Peak guide on its website. “Refer to your rifle manufacturer for recommendations on compatibility with high-pressure ammunition.”

Christensen Arms echoes similar language on its website. However, all existing Christensen Arms rifles chambered in 6.5 Creedmoor are compatible with the Plus Peak ammo, a spokesperson confirmed to Outdoor Life. The additional seven brands who worked with Federal include Area 419, Forster Products, Proof Research, Pure Precision, Hill Country Rifles, Short Action Customs, and Springfield Armory.

SAAMI has good cause to be extra cautious, points out Snow, since it’s a voluntary organization. The gun industry has regulated itself for a century — the result of a handshake deal with the government after World War I — and would like to continue avoiding federal oversight “like that of the auto industry,” says Snow.

But one reason gunmakers may come out against Plus Peak in their rifles has nothing to do with safety, cost, or liability.

“It’s possible that companies could see poor accuracy with the Plus Peak ammo and not recommend it,” says another industry insider, who also spoke on the condition of anonymity. “High chamber pressures, especially with some barrels and rifling designs, can cause accuracy problems that aren’t safety issues.”

Federal has patented its high-pressure cartridge technology, which means other ammunition makers can’t leverage it without paying for the privilege.

“There are rival companies that would arguably like to see this new and innovative technology put into a box and shoved off to the side,” says Snow. “Because they don’t have the ability to capitalize on this trend currently, and it’s potentially bad for their business if Plus Peak succeeds.”

The Peak Alloy case technology used in the 6.5 Creed Plus Peak was pioneered in the relatively new 7mm Backcountry, which debuted in January 2025 and was rooted in development of high-pressure ammunition for the military.

When the 7mm Backcountry was introduced, we tested more of it than anyone (besides Federal’s engineers) by shooting every SKU and running it in a dozen rifles. Although the 7BC delivered on its promise of velocity and produced accuracy equivalent to factory ammo in other cartridges, we encountered one major problem with almost every rifle. After 40 to 120 rounds, the 7BC rifles developed very stiff primary and secondary extraction. It turned out this was a common issue for other testers and shooters, too.

This issue, ultimately, was rooted in the case dimensions of the cartridge — not poorly cut chambers, as some people have speculated. After receiving updated 7BC ammo and testing the new batches in rifles that were previously showing these issues, we’ve found that the extraction issues have been resolved.

Those initial extraction issues in the 7BC were — erroneously — interpreted as effects of the increased pressure of the cartridge on the guns themselves. That doesn’t appear to be the case. In reality, most rifles chambered in 7mm Backcountry aren’t constructed differently from similar models designed for brass-cased cartridges like the 6.5 Creed.

So in other words, concerns over pressure issues from the new cartridge materials from the 7mm Backcountry to the 6.5 Creedmoor Plus Peak appear largely unfounded.

The 7 Backcountry was accepted by SAAMI without the current speed bumps, in part, because it’s an entirely new cartridge. It required brand-new guns manufactured specifically for the cartridge. Gunmakers could either opt to produce a gun chambered in 7BC — or not. Federal’s choice to offer the Plus Peak in an existing cartridge was a different route, but a calculated one.

“The reason Federal went with the 6.5 Creedmoor is because the 6.5 Creed was introduced in 2007 and is only available in modern firearms that are of recent vintage,” says Snow. “So it is extraordinarily unlikely, I would almost say impossible, that we would see, for example, a Plus Peak .308 or .223 offered commercially. That’s because there are way too many actions and rifles of older vintages that cannot be vouched for.”

Serven could not provide a timeline on when SAAMI’s Plus Peak working group might disclose its findings.

“Our primary mission is safety, so we are proceeding with that in mind. That concern is the overriding factor,” says Serven. “So we are not in a hurry. The priority is that we get the right answer, not a fast answer.”

SAAMI’s acceptance of new cartridges standardizes the case and load across ammo makers. The organization’s blessing is not required for the sale and use of ammunition, and many niches of the hunting, shooting, and military world operate outside the bounds of SAAMI approval. This includes everyone from brands like Buffalo Bore Ammunition to wildcat cartridge developers to the federal government itself. It also means you can still get your hands on 6.5 Creedmoor Plus Peak.

Federal currently offers the Terminal Ascent Plus Peak for sale on its site at a limit of two boxes per customer. The four other loads are “currently unavailable.” Plenty of YouTubers have been able to get their hands on the ammo, and none we’ve seen have reported safety issues.

“The stakes are pretty consequential,” says Snow of how SAAMI decides to handle the Plus Peak ammo. “On the one hand, are we going to move with less friction and resistance into the era of high-pressure, high-performance cartridges? Or are there going to be speed bumps and other impediments put in place that effectively throttle or discourage the adoption of this new technology?”

The push to adopt high-pressure rounds has come from the U.S. military, which has mandated it to maintain an advantage over “near peer” adversaries (such as Russia and China) on the battlefield. The military wants rifles capable of defeating newer body armor, and the way to do that is to drive bullets from existing guns at greater speeds.

“The government has been crystal clear about what they want and expect,” says Snow, “which is that high-pressure cartridges are happening and need to be here sooner rather than later.”
