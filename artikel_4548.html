Ask the Right Question to Find the Right Answer

What do the following scenarios have in common?

The manager, parent, and husband in these scenarios are all trying to solve a problem that they perceive as being caused by another person.

We can think of the manager, parent, and husband in these scenarios as the “director,” and the intern, teen, and spouse as the “actor.” The actor does something the director perceives as a problem. The director imposes a solution.

But the director’s solution is likely to be ineffective.

The office workers will be annoyed and may be confused if the hastily written dress code prohibits clothes they normally wear. Meanwhile, the intern won’t read the memo.

The teen will stomp off to their room, spend the weekend scrolling social media, and their grades will continue to drop.

The spouse will resent the husband and will defiantly keep their phone out at all times.

In each case, the director missed a key step in the problem-solving process: They did not communicate with the actor. As a result, they did not fully understand the problem. You can’t effectively solve a problem you don’t understand.

In addition, in each case, the director is imposing some action on the actor. The actor doesn’t necessarily understand why they have to do the action, or agree that it is useful. As a result, they will resist taking the action.

Let’s look more closely at scenario number two: the teen punished for a bad report card. In the parent’s mind, the “bad report card” is the problem. And perhaps the parent’s thought process is, “It’s my job to discipline my child. Grounding is a good way to do that.”

But the parent hasn’t talked to the teen to understand why their grades have dropped. Suppose the problem is that the teen has been increasingly depressed due to social isolation. In that case, the parent’s proposed solution is harmful.

The problem isn’t the report card. The report card is a symptom. The real problem is what underlies the symptom. That’s what needs to be solved.

Depending on what the problem is, the solution will be drastically different. If it’s social isolation, the parent might provide emotional support. If it’s an issue with handing in assignments, the parent could work with the teen and their teachers on executive function skills. If it’s a lack of sleep causing difficulty concentrating at school, the parent and teen might discuss strategies for restful nights.

While the scenarios above may seem straightforward, in real life, it can be tricky to identify when you’re trying to solve problems that you don’t fully understand. When you’re in the director role, how do you know when you don’t have the full picture? Use this four-step process.

Try to articulate the problem in terms of its harmful impact on a person or people.

Consider the bad report card. Who is this hurting, and how? The parent might decide that poor grades will hurt the teen’s future prospects.

At this stage, as the director, you might realize the only person impacted is you—and the solution lies entirely with you, not the actor. For example, if your coworker with chronic allergies drives you up the wall with constant sniffling, you might just need to deal with it, or wear earplugs, or move your desk. You can’t order your coworker to be healthier. Similarly, an exasperated parent might be tempted to discipline a rowdy toddler—but what needs to be fixed is the parent’s capacity for patience, not the toddler’s age-appropriate behavior.

How do they see the situation? Do they agree it’s a problem? If so, do they have any proposed solutions? In this stage, keep your ears open and listen far more than you talk. You need the actor’s help to learn as much as you can about the situation.

The teen might say, “I don’t care about my grades; I care about volunteering 20 hours a week at the hospital”—which comes as news to the parent.

The office dress code memo from the first scenario above shows how this step is often skipped in organizations. In this example, the manager avoids a potentially uncomfortable conversation by imposing an organization-wide solution. The manager could simply talk to the intern about workplace dress expectations—or the manager might realize, when asking “who is harmed, and how,” that this is a non-problem because nobody is harmed by the intern wearing a hoodie to work.

Step 3: Re-articulate the shared problem.

Restate the problem you identified in Step 1 in a way that both you and the actor can agree on. This is the actual problem that takes into account both the director’s observations and the actor’s context.

For example, “How can we balance school expectations with volunteer work?” Both the parent and the teen could agree that this is a problem deserving a solution.

Depending on the situation, you might do this alone or collaboratively with the actor. Either way, your solution will come closer to solving the shared problem, and since it takes the actor’s concerns into account, the actor is more likely to cooperate.

Let’s return to scenario number three, the phone-addicted spouse, to see the entire process in action.

The director thinks both he and the actor are harmed. They deserve each other’s attention at family mealtimes, and ignoring each other in favor of devices will weaken their marriage.

Instead of beginning with judgment—“You’re always on your phone”—the director begins with a concern: “I want us to stay connected, so our marriage stays strong. I’ve noticed you’ve been keeping your phone on the table at dinner, and I feel like I can’t keep your attention.”

The actor responds, “I agreed to be available for emergency work calls in the evening. The extra pay is going toward our vacation. So I need my phone by my side.”

Step 3: Re-articulate the shared problem.

The director says, “I see. Could we find a way for you to be available for work but not be distracted by the phone when you’re not on a work call?”

They decide together that the phone will stay plugged in in the next room, but with the volume turned up so the actor will hear it ringing and can answer immediately.

Notice how different this solution is—and how different the problem is—than the husband-director’s original idea.

Next time you find yourself thinking that your problem would be solved if everyone would follow your directions, pause. Remind yourself that the problem, as you perceive it, is more likely to be a symptom than the real underlying problem. And then follow the steps above to get closer to an effective solution and a stronger relationship.
