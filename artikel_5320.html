Amazon discriminated against Iranian job candidate under global hiring policy, Ontario tribunal rules

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

by Todd Bishop on Nov 27, 2026 at 2:21 pmNovember 27, 2026 at 2:21 pm

An Ontario human rights tribunal ruled that Amazon violated provincial law when it dropped a Toronto software engineer from its hiring process because of his Iranian citizenship.

The company’s internal policy, applied worldwide, barred some Iranian citizens from jobs involving access to Amazon’s encrypted technology, according to the ruling.

In a Nov. 16 decision, the Human Rights Tribunal of Ontario ordered Amazon to pay 15,000 Canadian dollars (about US$10,500) plus interest to Mohammadhasan Owlia, whose hiring process it ended in 2020. The tribunal rejected Amazon’s argument that U.S. export controls required the policy.

Export controls are U.S. rules that limit who can get access to certain American technology, including some encryption software.

The tribunal found that Amazon never showed that the mid-level software engineering job for which Owlia applied would have involved any restricted technology. Expert witnesses for both sides agreed that the U.S. rules don’t ban hiring Iranian citizens, though a company may need a government license before giving them access to certain technology.

“Stated simply, Amazon’s export controls compliance policy was overbroad,” wrote Cyndee Todgham Cherniak, the tribunal vice-chair who decided the case.

Amazon said in a statement to Canadian news outlets that it is committed to “fair and inclusive hiring and to following the laws wherever we operate, including Ontario’s Human Rights Code,” and that it will review the decision and meet its obligations under it.

The ruling was reported earlier by the Toronto Star and the Globe and Mail. We’ve asked Amazon whether the policy remains in effect elsewhere, including in the U.S.

Owlia, a University of Toronto engineering graduate who held an open work permit allowing him to work in Canada, applied for the job in 2019 and passed Amazon’s online technical assessment. On a pre-interview call in January 2020, a recruiter noticed he had studied in Tehran and told him Amazon had a policy against hiring Iranians.

Amazon’s guide for recruiters, quoted in the decision, says to end the process in those cases and tell candidates only that the company “has decided to not move forward with the hiring process.”

The policy, as quoted in the decision, also covers citizens of Cuba, North Korea, Sudan and Syria. It covers candidates, employees, temporary workers and contractors with access to Amazon’s encrypted technology, except lower-level warehouse jobs. It applies globally, but it exempts U.S. citizens, U.S. permanent residents, refugees, people granted asylum, and anyone whose most recent citizenship or permanent residency is from a country not on the list.

Owlia’s lawyers noted that an Amazon recruitment manager acknowledged during cross-examination that the policy remained in effect, according to the ruling. She also testified that Owlia would have been eligible once he became a Canadian permanent resident in 2021.

The tribunal’s orders apply only to hiring in Ontario by Amazon Fulfillment Services ULC, the Canadian subsidiary named in the case. Within six months, Amazon must revise the policy and the recruiter guide to require compliance with Ontario’s Human Rights Code there, and its Ontario-based HR staff must take human rights training.

The tribunal declined to award about C$92,500 (about US$65,000) in lost wages, calling the claim too speculative.

“In my view, having to comply with multiple laws is a cost of doing business,” Todgham Cherniak wrote.

In the U.S., the Immigration and Nationality Act protects U.S. citizens and nationals, eligible permanent residents, refugees and people granted asylum from hiring discrimination based on citizenship status. Workers on temporary visas generally fall outside that specific protection. The Justice Department has warned employers that screening out applicants from particular countries can still amount to national origin discrimination.

The U.S. Commerce Department says that giving Iranian nationals access to controlled technology or certain software can require a license, including when the transfer happens in the U.S. or a third country.

A similar dispute surfaced at Booking.com in 2022, when the company canceled the employment contracts of 11 Iranian hires living in Iran just before they were due to start at its Amsterdam headquarters. Booking.com cited complications in hiring and relocating them and said the move had nothing to do with discrimination, Skift reported. An internal document cited a possible “sanction risk” in employing Iranian residents, according to NL Times.

“I’m proud that I stood up for my right to be judged on my ability to do the work, instead of where my birth certificate was issued,” Owlia said in a statement released by his lawyers at Toronto firm Kastner Ko.

Owlia is now a Canadian citizen and an engineering manager at a Toronto software startup.
