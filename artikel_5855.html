Recent service outages not state-sponsored attacks, DNI Jay Clayton testifies

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton outlines national security threats including terrorism and Iranian plotting, saying recent cyber service disruptions are not state-sponsored attacks, nor are they related.

Recent service outages across U.S. systems stem from routine technical and maintenance issues – not a coordinated foreign cyberattack, Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton testified Wednesday, even as he warned lawmakers that "superintelligence" is rapidly escalating the speed and sophistication of future cyber threats.

"Based on what we know today, after a great deal of digital diligence, these are separate service outages associated with maintenance and technical changes, " Clayton told the House Homeland Security Committee's "Worldwide Threats to the Homeland: 25 Years After 9/11" hearing. "They are not a coordinated or state-sponsored attack."

Clayton acknowledged public concern over the timing of the recent outages, telling the panel that the American people are right to ask questions, but intelligence reviews point away from foreign interference.

"We have no indication that these events are related," Clayton said in response to persistent questioning from Rep. Dan Goldman, D-N.Y. "But let me answer something that the American people should know, which is the speed of compute and the level of capability of applications that use that compute is increasing substantially, which means legacy systems that were old six months ago or a year are a lot older."

The intelligence community remains on alert and continues to monitor for any malicious cyber activity targeting U.S. networks or law enforcement partners, Clayton said in his opening statement.

"After doing a great deal of due diligence inside the government with the private sector, with people in the private sector whose job is cybersecurity, we have no indication that these incidents are related, that they're coordinated," Clayton reiterated in the question and answer session of the hearing. "We also have no indication that they're a result of a state-sponsored malign actor."

Beyond the immediate outages, the intelligence chief warned lawmakers that rapid advancements in artificial intelligence – also referred to as "superintelligence" – are fundamentally altering the digital battlefield and raising the stakes for homeland defense.

"Let me note that superintelligence is reshaping the cybersecurity landscape, " Clayton said. "It is sharply increasing speed, scale, and sophistication of offensive and defensive cyber operations. And it's not just cyber. This goes to national security. It goes to the battlefield and it goes to commerce. Superintelligence is changing the way we do business, the way we fight wars and the way we combat terrorism every day."

During his opening remarks, Clayton outlined a broader heightened threat environment 25 years after the Sept. 11 attacks. While declining to discuss classified intelligence in an open setting, he noted that full briefings have been provided to the Gang of Eight and congressional intelligence committees.

Among the primary threats highlighted in his testimony:

Surveillance authorities: Clayton urged Congress not to let Section 702 of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act (FISA) expire, calling it the "single most indispensable tool" for early warnings on foreign threats to the homeland and U.S. troops.

"No other program can replicate the speed and effectiveness of FISA 702," Clayton said. "Letting it expire would simply be a mistake."
