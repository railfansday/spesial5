I Went to Gee’s Bend to See Its Famous Quilts. Nothing Prepared Me for the Beauty

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

I chew my tongue, concentrating on keeping my needle from wandering off its line. Over my shoulder, Doris Pettway Mosely watches my hands with the patience of someone who has been quilting since she was 11. She gently corrects my angle and sewing style, while Mary Margaret Pettway does the same for the rest of our small class. We laugh through most of it. Then Doris looks over my work and tells me my stitches are nice and short, and I sit up a little straighter.

I’m spending my morning at the fifth annual Airing of the Quilts Festival in Gee’s Bend, Alabama, where two of the community’s most celebrated quilters are teaching a roomful of beginners how to stitch 12-by-12-inch quilt squares. Mary Margaret, a fourth-generation quilter, has shown her work in major museums across the country. Doris, the daughter of celebrated quilter Leola Pettway, still sews her mother’s signature Z pattern alongside her own vivid, scrap-pieced designs. “I learned at four years old,” Mary Margaret tells me, explaining that she picked up the craft from her mother, Lucy T. Pettway. In their house, quilting was a chore like mopping or sweeping the yard, and the boys learned too.

Colorful booths at the Airing of the Quilts Festival in Gee’s Bend, Alabama

After class, I wander the festival grounds, where quilts hang from clotheslines strung between wooden posts. A quilt of nested squares in navy and sky blue hangs next to one in butter yellow, punched with blocks of chartreuse and red. Another glows neon coral. Farther down the line, rings of pale pink, olive green, and rose-print fabric radiate out from a single center square, a take on the classic Housetop pattern. Inside the River Gallery, a quilt called Remembering Dinah is pieced from faded denim and mustard corduroy, its back pockets and frayed knees left intact. It’s a reminder of where this tradition began.

Children learning to quilt in Gee’s Bend, Alabama, in 2010

Today, Gee’s Bend quilts hang in more than 40 museums, but they began as something far more practical. This small town takes its name from Joseph Gee, who established a cotton plantation in this bend of the Alabama River in 1816. Enslaved people and generations of their descendants stitched quilts to keep their families warm during winter and to brighten their homes, piecing them together from worn-out work clothes, denim, and whatever fabric scraps they had. Each year as summer gave way to fall, women hung their quilts outside to air before the cold set in, a tradition that inspired the Airing of the Quilts Festival, launched in 2022 by nonprofit organizations Freedom Quilting Bee Legacy and Sew Gee’s Bend Heritage Builders.

A quilt on display in Gee’s Bend, Alabama

Among the community’s earliest known named quilters was Dinah Miller, who was taken from West Africa as a girl in 1860, likely aboard the Clotilda, the last known slave ship to reach the US. She settled in Gee’s Bend in the 1890s and passed her craft down. During the civil rights movement, the quilts became a financial lifeline. Black residents who tried to register to vote risked losing their jobs and the land they lived on to white landowners. In 1962, officials shut down the ferry connecting the community to the county seat, making it difficult to register to vote, and cutting them off from grocery stores, banks, schools, and medical care. The ferry didn’t run again until 2006.

Four years after the closure, the community established the Freedom Quilting Bee, a cooperative that gave local women an income their white employers couldn’t take away. When Father Francis X. Walter, an Episcopal priest and Mobile native, saw the bold quilts on a trip to Wilcox County, he helped organize auctions in New York, where the striking designs caught the attention of tastemakers like Sister Parish. The quilts went on to sell at Bloomingdale’s and Saks Fifth Avenue.

“The Quilts of Gee's Bend” exhibit at the Corcoran Gallery of Art in Washington, DC, in 2004

The art world caught up decades later, when a landmark exhibition, “The Quilts of Gee’s Bend,” opened at the Museum of Fine Arts, Houston, in 2002, introducing the quilters to audiences far beyond Alabama. Today, their work is celebrated for its bold colors, inventive patterns, and astonishing craftsmanship. In 2006, 10 of the quilts appeared on US postage stamps. And this summer, Ralph Lauren launched a limited-edition collection of apparel, quilts, and home goods featuring designs by quilters Claudia Pettway Charley and Loretta Pettway Bennett.

But for design lovers, the festival offers something no museum can: the chance to see the quilts in the place they were made, and to meet the individual women who made them. They’re still sewn by hand, too. “[An authentic] Gee’s Bend quilt is not quilted by a sewing machine,” Mary Margaret says. “And it’s important to understand that every quilt is not alike. Everyone who quilts is not alike.”

Our quilting class ends the way it began, with plenty of laughter as we tie off our last stitches. Everyone wants a photo with Doris and Mary Margaret, and a few students have even brought books featuring the quilters’ work to be signed.

Outside, the festival is in full swing, with more than 40 booths devoted to quilters selling their quilts and wall hangings, along with puzzles and notebooks printed with Gee’s Bend patterns, doormats, quilted totes, and pot holders.

Last year’s festival drew more than 2,500 people and over $870,000 in visitor spending for the state, and Mary Margaret says guests come from as far as France and England. “They want to immerse themselves in Gee’s Bend,” she says. Doris looks forward to it all year. “I get to meet a lot of people I’ve never seen before,” she says. This year, she was especially excited to meet a 97-year-old quilter from Marion, Alabama, whose work she admires.

I’ve visited plenty of places where Black history is told in the past tense, on plaques and behind glass. Gee’s Bend feels different. Here, the history is alive, and the people who inherited it are setting the terms. That hasn’t always been easy. Over the years, says Mary Margaret, quilters have seen their work copied and sold without receiving compensation. The festival allows the public to buy directly from the quilters, at their booths or even from their porches, and know that the money is going straight to the artists.

Mostly, my day with the women of Gee’s Bend feels like a family reunion. The experience takes me straight back to childhood afternoons with my grandmother, who taught me to mend holes in pockets, sew patches onto my jeans jacket, and replace a button before anyone noticed it was missing.

Mary Margaret started even younger than I did, and now her own son and daughter are quilters themselves, working with patterns passed down through generations of the family. “To me a quilt says home,” says Mary Margaret. “It says warmth. It says care. It says time.”
