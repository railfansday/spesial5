Roth or Traditional 401(k) – Pay the Tax When Your Rate Is Lower

A Roth 401(k) taxes your contributions now, and withdrawals are tax-free once you’re 59½ and the account is five tax years old. Choose it when your tax rate today is lower than the one you expect in retirement, and the traditional option when it’s higher. If you can’t tell, split your contributions between the two.

At year-end 2025, 98% of the workplace plans Vanguard runs offered a Roth option, yet only 18% of the people in those plans had chosen it, according to Vanguard’s How America Saves 2026.

Traditional contributions come out of your pay before income tax, and every dollar you withdraw is taxed as income.

The two leave you the same amount after tax whenever your tax rate is the same now and in retirement, as long as each costs you the same take-home pay and you invest them the same way. A 22% tax takes 22% whether you pay it going in or coming out. So the choice comes down to the gap between your rate today and your rate in retirement.

Lean Roth if you’re early in your career, in the 10% or 12% federal tax bracket, or expecting a higher rate later. Lean traditional if you’re in your peak earning years, in the 32% bracket or higher, and expect a lower rate once you retire. In the 22% or 24% bracket, or whenever you can’t tell, split your contributions, which the IRS lets you do in any proportion.

Both sides count toward one limit of $24,500 a year. Where your plan allows them, catch-up contributions raise that by up to $8,000 a year from the year you turn 50, or by up to $11,250 in the years you turn 60 through 63. If your wages from your employer topped $150,000 last year, those catch-ups must go in as Roth.

Unlike a Roth IRA, the Roth 401(k) has no income limit, so a high salary never shuts you out. Since 2024 it also has no required withdrawals during your lifetime. That can settle a close call in its favor. Use the same rate test to choose between a Roth and a traditional IRA. Whichever split you pick, contribute at least enough to get your full employer match.

Set the split where you set your contribution rate, on your plan’s website or through HR, and recheck it in any year your income jumps or drops.
