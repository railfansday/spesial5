Forget ‘Twin Peaks,’ Ben Affleck’s Forgotten 7-Episode Mystery Was Ahead of Its Time

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

Amanda M. Castro is a Network TV writer at Collider and a New York–based journalist whose work has appeared in Newsweek, where she is an Associate Editor, and The U.S. Sun, where she previously served as a Senior Consumer Reporter.

She specializes in network television coverage, delivering sharp, thoughtful analysis of long-running procedural hits and ambitious new dramas across broadcast TV. At Collider, Amanda explores character arcs, storytelling trends, and the cultural impact of network series that keep audiences tuning in week after week.

Born and raised in Puerto Rico, Amanda is bilingual and holds a degree in Communication, Film, and Media Studies from the University of New Haven.

Ben Affleck is having quite the moment on Netflix. His latest thriller, Animals, has held the No. 1 spot on the streamer's global English-language movie chart for four consecutive weeks, pulling in another 4.7 million views and 8.4 million hours watched between October 26 and November 1, 2026. But long before his latest streaming success, Affleck tried his hand at something completely different: creating one of network television's strangest mystery series.

In 2002, Affleck and his producing partner Sean Bailey co-created Push, Nevada, a surreal mystery about a mild-mannered IRS agent who stumbles into a desert town filled with corruption, cryptic clues, and slow-dancing strangers. It sounded like Twin Peaks meets The X-Files — until ABC added one wild twist: Viewers could win a million dollars by solving the mystery themselves. The result was a show too ambitious, too strange, and too ahead of its time to survive more than seven episodes.

Push, Nevada followed Jim Prufrock (Derek Cecil), an accountant who receives a mysterious fax suggesting embezzlement at the Versailles Casino. When he heads to Push — a nowhere town drenched in neon and paranoia — he discovers that its residents haven’t filed a tax return since 1985 and that an ominous company, Watermark LLC, seems to control everything from the police to the local dive bar. Oddballs populated the noir-ish mystery: Scarlett Chorvat’s Mary, a “Slo-dancer” warning Prufrock to get out while he still can; Jon Polito’s corrupt casino boss; and Armand Assante’s slick criminal known only as “Mr. Smooth.”

Four Netflix characters, four completely different ways of showing up for somebody. This is the one who fits how you actually do friendship, and what having them in your life would be like.

You do not do friendship at half volume, and neither does he. What you want is somebody who says yes to the plan before you have finished describing the plan.

You are the friend who makes things happen, and so is she. Together you would be mildly exhausting to everyone around you and completely delighted with yourselves.

You have no patience for pleasant lies, and she has no ability to tell them. What looks like coldness from the outside is the most reliable friendship on this list.

You look after whoever is having the worst day. So does he. It is the least strategic way to move through the world and the best possible quality in a friend.

It looked incredible, with cinematography by Jim Denault, known for Boys Don’t Cry, and a moody palette of reds, golds, and shadows. Critics compared its tone and aesthetic directly to David Lynch’s Twin Peaks — from the quirky characters to the eerie score and slightly stilted dialogue. But while Lynch’s small-town surrealism invited obsession, Push, Nevada asked for something even stranger: participation.

Each episode contained hidden clues — web addresses, code words, even on-screen numbers — leading viewers closer to a real-life prize of $1,045,000. Fans could follow the story across fake websites, emails, and even physical mailers in what producers dubbed “the three-ball theory” of engagement: viewers could wade in, swim, or dive deep. The experiment made Push, Nevada one of the earliest examples of transmedia storytelling on network TV.

Unfortunately, it also made it unwatchable for casual audiences. The mystery was dense, the tone self-serious, and the clues often so obvious (highlighted in red boxes during reruns) that the whole thing felt like homework. Competing against CSI and Will & Grace, Push, Nevada was canceled after seven episodes — though ABC still had to finish the contest legally. The winner, New Jersey native Mark Nakamoto, solved the final puzzle and claimed the million-dollar prize, but the show itself vanished into cult TV history.

The longtime friends have something bigger in mind for their next chapter.

Today, Push, Nevada feels like a bizarre artifact from the early 2000s — part prestige mystery, part ARG, part marketing stunt. However, despite its flaws, it accurately predicted how audiences would one day crave deeper, more interactive storytelling through shows, websites, and games.
