If a Market Correction Is Coming, Here's the Exact 3-Step Strategy I'm Using to Prepare

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

The next stock market crash is always around the corner. Keep calm and check your asset allocations.

By Ben Gran – Nov 19, 2026 at 10:00AM EST

At first glance, the stock market seems to be having a decent year. As of Nov. 15, the S&P 500 index (^GSPC +0.76%) was up about 10.5% in 2026 and 13.33% in the past year. But there are warning signs brewing.

Ever since reaching a new record high on Oct. 6, the S&P 500 has declined by about 3.3%. That includes a recovery from a post-election day swoon when the index lost about 4.4% of its value between Nov. 4 and Nov. 5.

Are stocks going to stabilize from here, or are we about to go through another S&P 500 correction (10% or more decline)?

I don't know. In the short run, stocks might crash, they might skyrocket, or they might go sideways. But I believe that in the long run, no matter what happens on Wall Street tomorrow, buying a broadly diversified portfolio of stocks with low-cost index funds is a smart move.

Here's what I'm doing to prepare my portfolio for long-term success even if a market correction is coming tomorrow.

Checking on your investment accounts too often can be a bad thing if it causes you to overreact and make knee-jerk changes to your investments. Try not to obsess about short-term fluctuations in your account balance. Keep in mind that if you're a long-term investor with five, 10, or 20 years (or more) to let your money grow, you don't need to worry about what that one big number on the screen says today.

But if you're feeling queasy about the latest financial news headlines, now might be a good occasion to check in on your portfolio. Ask yourself:

If your stocks have recently had a big run-up in value and you're losing sleep about a future stock market crash, now might be a good time to slightly rebalance. You don't have to do too much; you could (for example) shift 5% or 10% of your assets from stocks to bonds.

Or you might not want to make any changes, and that's fine. But taking a quick look at the big picture of how your investments are performing, and where your future investment contributions are going, is a good move to make now, before a market correction happens.

I believe that a big reason why many investors make short-sighted moves, such as selling stocks at a loss, or trying to "time the market" by moving money between stocks and cash, is because they don't have enough emergency savings. If people have enough cash in the bank for financial stability and short-term needs, they're more likely to be able to hold onto their long-term investments during short-term volatility.

The money that I have invested in the stock market is my "long-term" money. It's not my emergency fund. It's not money that I might need today or tomorrow. Before a stock market correction, it's a good idea to reevaluate your emergency cash savings. Ask yourself:

If you're losing sleep about a stock market correction and you don't have a solid emergency savings fund, now might be a good time to save more cash. Don't try to be a hero by swooping in and buying stocks at a discount if you don't have enough cash to keep a roof over your head. First things first: make sure you've got some cash socked away.

And if you have more than "enough" emergency savings, you might want to consider putting the extra cash to work by buying stocks.

This sounds boring, but it's true: if the stock market crashes tomorrow, I'm going to try to do nothing. I'm going to keep working, earning money, and saving. Every month I'm going to keep putting a chunk of my income into the stock market with low-cost index funds like the Vanguard Morningstar Total Stock Market ETF (VTI +0.66%).

The Vanguard Morningstar Total Stock Market ETF holds a portfolio of 3,519 U.S. stocks of all sizes and sectors. In the past 25 years since the fund's inception in May 2001, it's delivered annualized returns of about 9.4%, and 10.95% in the past five years (as of Oct. 31).

Index funds aren't glamorous or flashy, but that's OK with me. VTI might not beat the S&P 500 index, and that's fine with me too. Buying VTI lets me own the entire U.S. stock market, including small-caps and mid-caps. I just want to own "all the stocks" so I can earn the average stock market return in the long run.
