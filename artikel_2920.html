Not Nvidia. Not Micron. If I Could Buy and Hold Only 1 Artificial Intelligence (AI) Semiconductor Stock Through 2030, This Would Be It.

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Taiwan Semiconductor offers a compelling way to capitalize on the artificial intelligence (AI) chip revolution without having to pick a winner among competing chip designers.

By Adam Spatacco – Nov 26, 2026 at 5:54PM EST

10 stocks we like better than Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing ›

For much of the last four years, semiconductor stocks have been some of the biggest beneficiaries of the artificial intelligence (AI) revolution. While Nvidia (NVDA -3.91%) has become the king of AI accelerators, Micron Technology (MU -4.53%) is capitalizing on unprecedented demand for memory chips.

Although both companies have attractive long-term growth prospects, competition could complicate their investment narratives over the next several years. Moreover, as the AI infrastructure landscape evolves, identifying which chip architectures will dominate the market is becoming more challenging.

Fortunately, there's one semiconductor company that stands to benefit from virtually every major development across the AI chip landscape: Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing (TSM -1.96%).

Between its graphics processing units (GPUs), networking equipment, and CUDA software ecosystem, Nvidia has an exceptionally durable competitive moat. Nevertheless, Advanced Micro Devices is investing aggressively in its Instinct accelerator portfolio, while some of Nvidia's largest customers are developing custom silicon.

Broadcom has emerged as an important partner in the market for application-specific integrated circuits (ASICs), most notably through its relationship with Alphabet and its tensor processing units (TPUs). Meanwhile, Microsoft and Amazon are also developing their own chip portfolios, including Maia, Trainium, and Inferentia.

Another potential source of competition is Space Exploration Technologies' collaboration with Intel on Terafab. Terafab is Elon Musk's semiconductor manufacturing initiative that could eventually reduce SpaceX's dependence on third-party chip suppliers. Although commercial-scale production remains uncertain, the project underscores a growing appetite for vertically integrated semiconductor solutions.

Micron faces a different set of challenges. While the proliferation of generative AI has ushered in a memory supercycle, memory-intensive applications such as humanoid robotics and autonomous vehicles should introduce additional tailwinds over the next several years.

However, Micron competes against Samsung and SK Hynix in the high bandwidth memory (HBM) market. Furthermore, memory pricing has historically been cyclical, leaving producers vulnerable to fluctuations in supply, demand, and profitability.

While Nvidia and Micron could both continue thriving, there is an alternative way to invest in the semiconductor industry without betting on a particular chip designer or memory manufacturer.

Unlike Nvidia and AMD, Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing (TSMC) primarily manufactures semiconductors designed by other companies. Its customer base includes businesses spanning GPUs, custom AI accelerators, smartphone processors, and automotive chips.

Against this backdrop, if Nvidia continues to dominate the AI accelerator market, TSMC benefits. Should AMD start to capture additional market share or custom ASICs become increasingly popular, TSMC still stands to benefit because it manufactures chips across these competing architectures as well.

Interestingly, Taiwan Semi also has exposure to the memory supercycle. While the company isn't a producer of conventional dynamic random-access memory (DRAM), its collaboration with SK Hynix involves manufacturing logic base dies for next-generation HBM4 solutions.

Moreover, TSMC's advanced packaging technologies -- particularly its chip-on-wafer-on-substrate (CoWoS) platform -- play a critical role in integrating AI processors with HBM. As a result, growing demand for more sophisticated memory configurations creates additional markets for TSMC beyond traditional wafer fabrication.

The most compelling aspect of TSMC's competitive positioning is the sheer magnitude of its market share. Industry estimates suggest that Taiwan Semi controls roughly 73% of the global pure-play foundry market. By contrast, the next closest competitor sits at just 6%.

Although TSMC isn't technically a monopoly, its dominance gives it the characteristics of one. The company's manufacturing expertise, technological edge, production scale, and customer relationships are simply too difficult for the competition to replicate at scale.

During the third quarter, Taiwan Semi generated $46.7 billion in revenue, representing 41% year-over-year growth. Meanwhile, the company's gross margin reached nearly 65%, and its operating margin expanded to about 59%.

Management expects revenue to reach $53.3 billion in the fourth quarter, implying 58% year-over-year growth. These results illustrate how TSMC's manufacturing scale is translating insatiable semiconductor demand into robust profitability.

Despite these impressive financial trends, Taiwan Semi stock trades at a forward price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio of 15.7. That valuation profile looks attractive for a business generating this level of growth and profitability while commanding such a dominant market position.

Rather than attempting to identify which chip designer or memory producer will emerge victorious over the next several years, investing in the company that manufactures many of the industry's most in-demand products looks like a compelling strategy.

TSMC's architecture-agnostic business model, durable competitive advantages, and reasonable valuation make it a compelling semiconductor stock to buy now and hold through the end of the decade.
