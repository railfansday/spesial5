The Music of Thanksgiving: Thirty Voices at One Table

Thanksgiving as a musical holiday? What possibilities!

Christmas has its carols and the Fourth of July its rousing bands. Thanksgiving is usually defined by motion and aroma, the joyful hum of conversation, the clatter of silverware (or plasticware), the scent of favorite holiday family foods, perhaps the sound of bickering siblings (both young and adult), and the sigh of aahs when the turkey finally emerges from the oven.

This year our gathering will have a new sound: two grandmothers leading the family in song.

We’re bringing together two extended families, about thirty people under one roof. The youngest is three years old; the oldest is seventy-six. Many ages, memories, opinions, football fans, and personalities are all under one roof. After dinner, or perhaps before everyone hits the food sleepies, we’ll sing “Over the River and Through the Wood,” followed by “You Are My Sunshine.”

Some will know every word. Others may remember only the chorus. The youngest may sing a few words, hum, add their own sounds, or even bang out the beat with a wooden spoon. What fun as the elders turn down their hearing aids. Someone may come in early; someone else may be a beat behind. And of course, good old Uncle, who we won’t name, can’t carry a tune. This is not a choir that has rehearsed together, although Ethan and Sara will help guide us. We’re gathering as a family, not auditioning for a TV talent show.

The two songs we chose to begin carry different kinds of familiarity. “Over the River and Through the Wood” belongs to the season, with its image of traveling to a family gathering. “You Are My Sunshine” is a song many people have known since childhood, although the version one person remembers may not be quite the version another learned. We may have to help each other with a word or two, and someone may remember a verse the rest of us have forgotten.

The song sheet is a simple idea, but we like the question at the bottom. “What else should we sing?” might bring up a song someone’s parent used to sing, a tune from a school play, or something the children are listening to now. A suggestion may come with a story the family has heard many times before, but still loves to hear. Sometimes a song is a favorite because it’s funny, easy to sing, or simply familiar.

The toddler might not know all the words, and the teenagers might roll their eyes before joining in, increasing the volume of the room. Someone might begin to think about doing the dishes, but for a few brief moments, we might have the gift of nobody checking a phone — just the collective shared wave of sound and rhythm.

Singing together can help coordinate our breathing and even influence patterns in our heart rates. But you don't need to measure any of that to notice what happens when people sing together. We listen to one another, adjust our voices, and try to stay with the group.

A large family gathering doesn’t often ask everyone to do the same thing at once. In smaller Thanksgiving gatherings, we’ve gone around the table asking questions like, “What are you grateful for" and "What have you done to help someone else feel grateful?" We use a homemade placemat asking about gratitude.

People catch up in small groups. The children move in and out of the room. Someone is tending to the food while someone else clears a place at the table. Singing gives us a few minutes with one shared activity.

Hopefully, a song sheet encourages all voices and squeals to join in. Inviting family members to suggest a favorite tune shifts the dynamic to active participation and gives everyone a voice. When you ask people what they want to sing, you are really asking: What memory do you bring to this table? What tune makes you feel at home?

The songs aren’t performance, just connection. We know that someone may have to retrieve a wandering toddler or may pause while a plate is passed or a child asks for something from the kitchen. The music may stop and start. In a gathering of thirty people, a perfectly uninterrupted performance seems unlikely.

Afterward, we’ll return to the conversations already underway, half of us forgetting what we were talking about anyway. People will share dessert, tell stories, and catch up with relatives they don’t see every week. Later, when the house is quieter, there may be time for a longer conversation or a cup of coffee. Those parts of the day matter, too.

Gratitude is often a quiet reflection, perhaps first thing in the morning, or quietly before falling asleep. Thanksgiving is a social ritual, giving us the chance to make our gratitude visible and communal.

Long before the final slice of pie is served, a shared song reminds us why we gathered in the first place. Sure, we'll also eat too much, complain about how stuffed we are, and probably fall asleep earlier than planned.

As the holiday draws to a close and the house settles down, the music we made may linger in memory. Maybe during the car rides home, someone hums “You Are My Sunshine.”

When the final chord fades, what remains is the warmth of the ensemble, how it made us feel, collectively, and tunes we will hold on to for next year.
