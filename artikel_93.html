‘I didn’t notice his body being purple’: Byron Bay doula left newborn to inflate a jumping castle, court hears

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Inquest into deaths of four babies born in free births or at home in northern NSW between 2022 and 2024 hears ‘something was seriously wrong’

Baby DH was born in an inflatable pool, in a candle-lit room in his parents’ home near Byron Bay, with no one medically trained present. There are two very different versions of what happened next.

In the parents’ version, the doula reassured them Baby DH was OK, then left to inflate a jumping castle while their newborn turned purple, became floppy, and struggled to breathe. In the doula’s version, everything seemed OK but she suggested they seek more help if they wanted to.

The New South Wales coroner’s court heard the two competing narratives on Wednesday as part of an ongoing inquest into the deaths of four babies born in free births or home births.

The babies died in NSW’s northern rivers region between 2022 and 2024.

“The evidence will be that from his first minutes of life, [Baby DH] showed signs that something was seriously wrong,” the Lismore courtroom heard.

“His parents saw those signs. They raised them again and again with their doula, who they had engaged to watch for exactly that. Their doula, in this case, was a woman named Amy Lou Smith.

“Each time the parents raised their concerns, they were reassured by Amy Lou. An ambulance was not called until moments before [Baby DH] stopped breathing.”

His father described the baby as floppy after birth. “His colour, his whole body was purple with his hands and feet darker still,” the court heard, and Smith told them some purple in the hands and feet was normal.

“I didn’t notice his body being purple,” Smith told the court.

Told the baby’s colour wasn’t improving, Smith said “as long as his abdomen isn’t purple”, according to evidence from the parents. The mother was shocked. His whole body had been purple all along. His temperature was low, 34.5C. Smith suggested trying to get it up to 37C.

“I believed if they had real concerns, that they would call an ambulance and not be texting a non medically trained doula,” Smith told the court.

Smith, the inquest heard, described his laboured breathing as “cute little noises”. “I didn’t say that,” Smith told the court.

The court heard Smith told the couple Baby DH would have been put on oxygen if he was in hospital. But Smith later told the court she didn’t know what the hospital would have done.

He had strange lumps on his body, and wasn’t feeding. Smith suggested they get the lumps checked out, and warned that babies sometimes take a while to latch on to the breast, according to the parents’ evidence.

The court heard Smith left to set up a jumping castle for her son’s birthday. She sent the couple a picture of the castle later, as they were trying to contact a doctor, the court heard.

Smith told the court the castle was already set up, and the couple was happy for her to leave.

“Everything felt like it was OK,” she said.

Eventually, the parents spoke to the GP they had tried to contact earlier. The GP told them to call triple zero. They did, and did CPR until the paramedics got there. But Baby DH was declared dead at the hospital.

After the father had made the emergency call, Smith also told them to call an ambulance, and sent them instructions on infant CPR.

Throughout the hearing, Smith denied that any of her advice or comments constituted clinical advice.

Neonatologist Dr Paul Evans, the court heard, had found Baby DH had a complex heart defect, one that would have shown up on an X-ray. The baby could have been treated, and a birth plan with proximity to a major hospital and specialist cardiac care prepared, the court heard.

The child’s death was not inevitable, Evans found.

While pregnant, Baby DH’s mother decided to rely on a doula. She first approached a woman the court heard was likely to be Sita Tara Kali, a self-described birth keeper who was also involved in the care of another baby previously mentioned during the inquest. But Kali didn’t feel she could help, so they turned to Smith.

The court heard the couple paid for an online “sovereign birth course” for Smith and was reassured that she had enough experience. They agreed on an emergency plan.

Smith, who became a birth doula in 2020, was quizzed on her experience in court.

She has a degree in fine art and has worked as a social worker and a youth worker.

She told the court she is not medically trained in any way but did an online “full-spectrum doula course”. She learned CPR but was not familiar with how to apply it to a newborn – she denied she had given the parents the impression she was.

She had no training about a newborn’s colour, breathing, or feeding, but she did go to a retreat where she learned about referring a woman to more support if needed.

She had been present at 15 births over four years, nine alongside a midwife, and conceded she didn’t know what a low-risk birth would look like. She had read a couple of books about birthing, but they were more “spiritual”, the court heard.

She agreed that she now believes the best model of care for a home birth is for a clinically trained midwife to work alongside a doula.
