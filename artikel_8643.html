A mantra (from the Sanskrit man, meaning “mind,” and tra, meaning “tool” or “instrument”) is an instrument of thought: a sacred word, sound, or phrase repeated aloud or silently to anchor and focus the mind.

While its origins are rooted in ancient Vedic, Hindu, and Buddhist traditions, mantras are used today across both spiritual practices and secular psychology for anchoring focus in meditation, calming the nervous system, or cognitive reframing.

Denny Giamazzo has relied on a mantra throughout his life to get through many difficult times and challenges, and he still lives by it today.

“Someone else out there has had it worse. They have come back stronger, and they are doing more with less. I am not allowed to feel sorry for myself.”

When Giamazzo talks about facing adversity throughout his life, he means literally. Born two months premature and never finding out who his biological father is, young Denny’s life began in Worcester, Massachusetts with his mother. At least he was supposed to be with her. Giamazzo recalled numerous times when she would leave him for hours or even days at a time without him knowing where she went. An upstairs neighbor would watch him if he knew young Denny was alone.

By the time he was five, his mother passed away from AIDS and Giamazzo entered the foster care system. He would eventually live with his aunt. As tragic as that story appears, Giamazzo managed to find a silver lining that serves him to this day.

He said, “I think what I took from that was just an insane amount of resiliency.”

That resiliency would be an anchor throughout his younger years. Giamazzo would stay with his grandfather while his aunt worked, but an uncle also lived there and was physically abusive. This continued into Giamazzo’s teenage years.

“We got into it a lot,” he recalled. Fortunately, he found another anchor in sports and fitness. He played organized baseball before high school but went on to play basketball and football at the high school level. The physical challenges and improvement that came from playing served as driving forces for him.

“At a young age, I just loved putting my body through things and seeing what I was capable of.”

Giamazzo joined the Army in 2006 and served for nearly seven years. During that time, he was deployed to Afghanistan from 2010 to 2011. Giamazzo was simply looking for direction when he entered the military, but the infantry team leader found more than that as his career progressed.

He shared, “I really didn’t join with this burning desire to go serve my country or anything like that. That’s what it turned into after really getting embedded in that lifestyle and working with the guys I got to work with every day.”

Giamazzo did not leave Afghanistan unscathed. He needed two right shoulder surgeries, and like many soldiers, he had to navigate Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) in the aftermath of that deployment. Once again, Giamazzo leaned on the resilience he forged in childhood to move forward.

“That inner strength and those lessons of resiliency that I learned early on as a kid have kind of been a barometer for me my entire life,” he explained. “It led me through just about any pain, adversity, or objective that I’ve hit and then had to overcome throughout my entire life.”

Giamazzo also credits the gym and training. He trains five days a week and will mix it up between weights and High Intensity Interval Training (HIIT). He particularly likes the CrossFit workout “Cindy.”

A 2023 review by researchers at Duke University and Mount Sinai found that regular exercise can be just as effective as medication for easing depression. Having a positive and aggressive outlet that negatively affected no one else is something Giamazzo leaned on then and continues to now.

“It really has been probably the most integral part of addressing my PTSD.”

Giamazzo has found several ways to turn negativity into positivity and is now committed to sharing what he learned with others. His experiences hold more lessons than a single interview can capture, which is why he put them into his book, Wired for Action, which serves as both an autobiography and a guide for others who may be facing their own challenges.

Giamazzo said, “It’s easy sometimes to feel like we’re on an island by ourselves. That’s just not the case.”

He also hosts the Be the Standard Podcast, where he and co-host Mike Barnes cover a variety of topics such as men’s mental health, combat trauma, PTSD, identity, personal responsibility, discipline, faith, leadership, and more. The public speaker and leadership consultant also directly helps others break self-destructive habits and explore new ways to meet their potential despite the traumas they face.

“I would say that for each individual, it’s on them,” he shared. “It comes down to ‘What type of person do I want to be? What action do I need to take to reach those objectives?’ If you know what you want out of life, you know exactly the type of person you want to be, and you’re willing to put the work in, I think that’s going to give you a substantial amount of purpose.”

That is advice that comes from experience, but Giamazzo offers a shorter version that could be another mantra for those needing a source of hope to take with them into the future.

“Cindy” is one of the more popular CrossFit benchmark workouts and can be used for either a quick challenging full-body session or for a way to monitor your fitness periodically. All you need is a pull-up bar and your bodyweight.

Perform as many rounds as possible in 20 minutes of these exercises.

Track the number of rounds you can complete and set a goal to beat it in the future. Beginners can shoot for 8 rounds and work their way up from there. The competitive CrossFit (Rx) standard is 20 rounds, with 25 and more being considered “Elite.”
