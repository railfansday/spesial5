On the Road Again — John Muir Trail Day 9

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

I admit, I was nervous about going back on the John Muir Trail.

The good news was that I did not have a big coughing fit in the night.  I woke up with a forgotten dream, I went to the bathroom, but I fell back to sleep and overall slept pretty decently.   At 4:45 a.m. I checked the time and decided, “Well the alarm is going off at five a.m., so I may as well get up.”

I could only eat half of the chorizo breakfast burrito.  It was delicious, but so big.  I mean huge!  It also seemed like my hiker hunger had been satisfied by all the barbeque I ate, so that was a factor too.

As  I packed, I kicked myself when I realized that I should have bought some more hand sanitizer – or looked for it in the free hiker boxes at the Mount Williamson Motel.  My bottle started leaking constantly on trail, to the point that I now kept it inside two nested ziplock bags.  This was an essential item for my personal hygiene.  How could I forget that?

I also practiced my play part a little bit.  I was trying to distract myself, to calm down, but I could tell I was on edge.  After two days off trail, to include a stop in the Emergency Room, this was understandable.

My driver, Mark, showed up right on time.  I was two minutes away from being ready.  Then I threw the stuff in his car and we headed off.  Good-bye to Independence, CA!

As we drove, I asked Mark about his background.  He came from the Bay Area originally where he used to be a commercial fisherman.  Then he sold his boat and bought the house next door to his brother, who lives out here and works as a geologist.  It did not sound like Mark had any regrets about the move.

The ride was 45 dollars, so I gave him an even fifty.   The sun just began peeking out above the eastern mountains when I set off on the trail.  It was 6:30 a.m.

I must admit when I first picked up my pack, having filled it with all of the food for another (probable) six-day journey, I was dismayed.  I thought, “Oh my god, I’m gonna carry THIS thing!  How??”  But once I started walking it started to feel sort of normal – well, heavy normal – and I stopped focusing on the weight.

Despite my trepidation, it felt good to be headed back into the wilderness.

Based on our descent three days earlier, I realized I would be able to see not just the Valley but the actual road and parking lot for quite a long time.  I remembered in descending it had felt very nearby, but then we had taken a long time to arrive.  So, I was prepared for a long, slow uphill.  Being mentally prepared it seemed to go steadily and, while it felt hard, it did not feel too hard.  I felt okay.

I was still coughing a little bit as I advanced, and I was still producing some mucus.  This meant I was not as much better as I had hoped, but I was definitely a whole lot better than I was before starting the antibiotics.  And I still had three days of treatment to go.

Eventually I made it to the area of the extended fall of giant boulders.  That was where I had first seen the road when descending, so I knew that I was now leaving the road behind me.   Halfway through, in the middle of this jumble of rocks, I saw a family.

This IS the boulder field I describe, but taken four days earlier during our descent. That is why Sam — who was no longer with me — is in the picture.

It was strange, they were neither ascending nor descending.  They were just hanging out in the boulders – a father, a mother, three kids about 6-12 years old, and a fluffy little brown dog, maybe a doodle or something?

They seemed a bit shook up, so I stopped to ask them what was going on.  They replied that a bear had just come into their camp site and eaten their breakfast!  I guess they figured the bear wouldn’t come after them in the rock pile.

Apparently, the dad was cooking pancakes on their grill and turned away just for a moment or two.  That was all the advantage the bear needed to move in and eat the whole batch!   The little dog barked furiously to defend their turf, but fortunately the bear did not pay it the slightest mind.  He just ate all the food and then lumbered into the forest.  They even managed to take a picture (from a distance!) of “bear eating pancakes” that they showed me.

Now I have encountered a few bears, and at this point I generally have more respect for them than fear of them.  (Though I would be terrified if I found myself between a sow and her cubs, of course!)  So, I talked to the family and reassured them, and we all walked together back to their campsite.  I urged the dad to report the incident to a ranger, telling him, “This bear is a juvenile, and it sounds like it’s making a habit of doing this around here.  When a bear gets accustomed to humans and human food, the outcome is usually bad – especially for the bear.”

Before parting, they told me something hilarious.  The little brown doodle dog?  Its name was “Bear.”

After that little episode I continued on and soon after I had a very large mammal edging closer behind me!  But it was a horse, not a bear, and it was leading a pack train of a horse and two-or-three mules.  One distinctive element on the John Muir Trail is stock animal poop.  Sometimes it is annoying, trying to avoid stepping on it when it is fresh, but other times it is helpful, in confirming that you are on the right track.   This was the first actual horse/mule train I had seen and I now found out how fast they go.  Answer:  It is not fast, but still a lot faster than I go, uphill at least!  I pulled off the trail and let them pass me.

High alpine lake en route to Kearsarge Pass.

I continued up and up at a kind of steady pace.  It felt hard, but not overwhelming, and before I knew it, I could see the Kearsarge Pass up ahead.  I was doing my usual routine of counting out 60 right-foot paces, or only 30 if it was very steep, before stopping to briefly catch my breath.  This cycle forces me to push myself a little bit and soon enough, I had reached the pass.

For some reason, both times I crossed it, Kearsarge felt like Grand Central Station.  There just always seemed to be a lot of people up there.   Still, I stopped, had my second breakfast, and chatted with the crowds.  It was only 9:30, so I made it up there in three hours.   Given my circumstances – a 69-year-old recovering from a lung infection ascending over 2,500 feet — I thought that time was quite good.

Some of the crowd at Kearsarge Pass, elevation 11,760 feet

I proceeded down the other side until I came to a fork in the trail.  Left was the route Sam and I took coming up.  Now I went right, a new route (for me) that would reconnect me with the John Muir Trail just a little further north than we left it.

From our navigation app, Sam and I guessed this other trail passed behind a ridge above us, but it turned out to be a higher altitude route within the same valley.  A few miles later the path intersected the main John Muir Trail.

This was a symbolic moment of return after my lengthy detour to resupply and get medical help.  I went a short way further to rest under a big tree and celebrate with my lunch.  Welcome back to the John Muir Trail; hooray!

I barely dared to confess to myself that my goal for the day included crossing not one but two passes.  It seemed a little crazy, but the layout of campsites and distance to my next resupply kind of dictated it.  With Kearsarge behind me, the next challenge was going to be Glen Pass.  However, I wasn’t too worried about Glen Pass.  The elevation gain would only be about 1,200 feet, or less than half of Kearsarge.

Oh, was I wrong!   It always seems to work out like that:  expect something difficult and it seems relatively easier than expected; expect something easy and it will often surprise with its difficulty.

The trail itself was rough and rocky, and several portions were very steep.  Also, I was tired from my morning exertion, and it was then the middle of the afternoon.  The sun was beating down relentlessly and there was no shade.

Finally, I tried out my umbrella in its intended role as a sunshade.  The problem was attaching it to my backpack in a way that would shade me and still be hands-free.  I had the gizmo designed for taking care of this, but it was hard to work it, especially with the splint on my finger.  When it was finally in place it worked fairly well.  I would give it a “D” for set-up and a “B” for operation.

Even shaded, Glen Pass was really hard.  There were some switchbacks, but while these are often converted into relatively smooth trails, going up to Glen Pass they were full of uneven rocks.  Getting ever more tired as I advanced, it became easy to imagine slipping or hurting myself.

Finally, I made it to the top of Glen Pass.  Hooray!  On most passes the ascent and descent meet at roughly the same point.   With Glen Pass, however, you arrive at one spot and then you have to walk along the edge of the ridgeline for about 100 yards or more before reaching the spot where you begin your descent.  On that ridge I stopped and had my second lunch.

Selfie atop the ridgeline of Glen Pass; check out my hands visible in the sunglasses…

Naturally, I was hoping the north side of the pass would be easier.  It was not.  True, I did not have to stop to catch my breath all the time, but I still had to tread very gingerly, because a twisted ankle or a fall were real possibilities on this quality of trail.

Eventually I got down lower where there were a whole series of scenic little lakes in the shadow of the mountains.  The first two were Upper Rae Lake and Middle Rae Lake and the trail traversed a narrow isthmus between the two of them that included a stream crossing.  Earlier in the season I expect you would wade through that crossing, but I was able to stay dry on stepping stones as I made my way across.

An island sat lonely in the middle of Upper Rae Lake

It was late afternoon as I entered the Rae Lakes campground on the Southeast side of Middle Rae Lake.  I was a tired puppy, because I had done two passes.  It wasn’t quite as much total elevation gain as Mount Whitney, but the difference is less than 300 feet, and I was doing it with a fully laden pack.  For someone still on antibiotics, it felt like quite an accomplishment.

At first, I thought I would cowboy camp away from the crowds at the campground.  (Cowboy Camp:  When there is little threat of rain, sleeping under the stars without a tent.)  Then I  found a sloped tent site, but the angle of slope was too much for restful sleep.  Finally, I came down to where there were many other campers and where there was a bear box.  With all my food, that box was a big  draw for me.

I set up opposite two very nice women who were bantering back-and-forth.  Eventually I went over to introduce myself to Serena and Evelyn.  I told them I was blogging, and they said I could take their picture and post it in my blog.  So, Serena and Evelyn, if you ever read this, you’re going to see yourselves in the blog!

You can tell just by looking them what a great attitude they had. Happy trails Serena and Evelyn!

Looking at tomorrow, I saw another long day.  Like Glen Pass today, I had a big climb to Pinchot Pass at the very end of the day, but at least it’s only one pass.  I hoped to have enough energy to make it, because I really didn’t want to start falling further behind.  I already lost two days in Independence because of my health, after all.  I decided an early start would be advisable, and I looked forward to escaping the Rae Lake crowds.

As the sun set and my cold dinner soaked, I reflected on the day.  It was a bear, no doubt about it (but maybe a small pun about it…).  But I had come through it and was back on the John Muir Trail.   We will see what happens next, but I was far more optimistic than I had been.  Amazing what 14 miles and a few antibiotics can do for a person’s attitude, right?
