Todd Boehly Dissects How How Daniel Katz’s Trading Desk Acumen Fed Into A24 Success: “Very Few Entertainment People Understand Math”

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

Todd Boehly gave an insight into his no nonsense approach to investing in sports and entertainment in the concluding fireside chat of the Qatar Film Committee’s Doha Industry Days over the weekend.

The billionaire investor’s holding company Eldridge has some $75B in assets including stakes in Backrooms distributor A24, and Fulwell Entertainment as well as the Los Angeles Lakers, the Los Angeles Sparks, the Los Angeles Dodgers and the Trent Rockets among many more.

The holding is also a partner with Deadline owner Penske Media in the Penske Media Eldridge unit encompassing live TV entertainment specialist Dick Clark Productions as well as events such as the Golden Globes and SXSW. Boehly first connected with A24 cofounder Daniel Katz, when the latter headed Guggenheim Partners’ film finance group in New York, while he was president of the global investment firm before leaving to create Eldridge. Watch on Deadline

“One of the things I believe is that there’s very few entertainment people that understand math… the math of entertainment is really about understanding how are you taking risk and then how are you distributing risk,” he said in the chat entitled “Playing to Win”.

“It’s no different than any other industry, if you’re thinking about what amount of risk you are willing to take and then how are you going to feed that risk,” he added.

Boehly said Katz had impressed him from the start for his ability to identify and work with risk during his time with Guggenheim Partners.

“Daniel’s real magic, when he worked on our trading floor from, let’s call it like 2008 to 2012, he didn’t take a lot of risks, but he said ‘no’ a lot,” he said.

“There was a slate that was done by a group called Stark Investments, and one of their big films was Poseidon. I remember it showed up on our desk. And Daniel was immediately like, this is a disaster. It’s going to cost so much money, you know, they’re not going to be able to make any return on it.”

The $160M disaster movie by Wolfgang Petersen went down in film financing folklore after it faltered at the box office and failed to gross enough to cover its P&A costs.

Boehly provided $19M in seed money for A24 as Katz launched the company in 2012 with David Fenkel and Joh Hodges. It was last publicly valued at $3.5 billion in June 2024.

“Everyone told me in Hollywood that there’s no room for another distribution company,” recalled the businessman. “His early successes with Spring Breakers and The Bling Ring were really structured finance trades.”

“You’re not really allowed to talk about that in the entertainment world because everything’s creative and creator driven… what Daniel was really good at, because he sat on a trading desk and he understood risk and he understood math, he was really good at figuring out how am I going to get my money back using different windows.”

Starring James Franco, Selena Gomez, Vanessa Hudgens, Harmony Korine’s Spring Breakers premiered in competition in Venice and went on to gross $31M.

“Spring Breakers cost a couple million bucks to buy, and he ended up making 10 million bucks or so very quickly on, because he really understood that it’s a structured finance straight,” said Boehly.

He added that beyond Katz’s business savviness, the company had also understood how to tap into social media to generate audience engagement as well as how to plug into trends of the moment.

“One of the things that A24 has done an amazing job at is being able to read the cultural zeitgeist. I think Civil War was really well timed; Marty Supreme was very well timed with Timothée Chalamet and his ascension as probably one of the top actors for the next couple of decades.”

Boehly also highlighted A24’s strategy of growing a cohort of directors and actors, who returned to work with the company on a regular basis.

“He wants repeat customers. He wants the directors coming back… he wants the actors coming back… because then it’s not a transaction, it becomes a recurring business with enterprise,” said Boehly.

“So many people think about the film business, ‘Oh, it’s a transaction’. But if you’re really thinking about how to build an enterprise, then you need to make sure that you have a recurring revenue base. And the way you do that in this industry is you get repeat users.”

He also gave insight into how Katz constructs A24’s slate in a way that enables it to work with established names and newcomers and turn a profit, suggesting that over a portfolio of 15 films, nine would be “the core bread and butter”.

“It’s a budget of 25 to 40 million bucks. It’s the expectation that he’s going to make 1.35 times,” he explained. “And if you think about the math of it again, you get 0.8 times your money back right away or very quickly. So, when you think about the risk that you have, you’re in for 0.2. If you make 1.35 and your risk capital is 0.2, but you’re going to make six, seven times that 0.2.”

“And then he’ll take three big swings and make something that has the capacity to become multiple films out of the content… he’ll take 1.2 times. So, he’ll modify his expectations on the rate of return to achieve something that might have more enterprise value or longevity on the back of it.”

Boehly suggested the final three films would be by up-and-coming young directors, and would require time, energy and effort rather than big financial investments and risk.

“He’s always thinking about how do I build that portfolio in order to have my bread and butter, have the opportunity to have something that’s got longevity and then find someone who’s going to be special in the industry,” he said.
