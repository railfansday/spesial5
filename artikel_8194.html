From Day Hiker to Backpacker: My First Thru-Hike on the Trans-Catalina Trail

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

I’ve always lived with a chip on my shoulder.

I’ve always been on the short side, always first in line when elementary school teachers would determine line-up order by height. We were poor growing up — Section 8 kind of poor — living in the suburbs east of Los Angeles. My father died when I was two and my younger brother just six weeks old. My mother put herself through nursing school after his death, but she instilled in us at a young age: we would have to work hard, very hard, for the things we wanted in life.

Because of this chip on my shoulder — chips, really — I’ve lived my entire life fueled by the fact I had things to prove to the world. As a young, short, poor, fatherless girl, that fire was lit under my ass at a very young age and it hasn’t gone out.

I tell you all this to not tease out sympathy for me, but rather to give you an understanding of who I am, where I came from and my reasons for getting into backpacking. To put it simply, backpacking is hard, long, grueling and can often be unforgiving. I want to see how I can measure up against it.

But putting things simply isn’t why I’m here, nor is it why you’re here reading this blog, amongst the many other blogs. So we’ll call this is a preface.

My name is Bria Manning, and I’m a 30-year-old sports multimedia producer living in rural southwest Montana. That’s me now. Before that, though, I was a California girl, born and raised in the suburbs of Los Angeles by my mother, a registered nurse. I’m the middle child with an older sister and younger brother. Though I was the most difficult for my mother to raise, if you were to ask her now who her favorite child is, she, like any good mother, would respond, “I love all my children equally for different reasons.” But I call BS. I know deep down it’s me.

For as long as I can remember I’ve had The Itch: to leave, to adventure, to romp and explore. As a kid, I wrote semifictional short stories about my life. In them, I drove a black Chevrolet Avalanche — the same truck my crush’s mother drove — and I would take off on the road whenever The Itch compelled me to. Now that I’m a grown up, that hasn’t changed; I’m merely living my teenage fantasy dreams out. My entire life, I’ve loved my freedom more than anything.

In Southern California, The Itch pulled me out towards the neighboring hills of Whittier. The hills hugged the community so tightly, and there was never a shortage of trails for me to explore. In high school, I’d ride my bike to the trailhead in those hills, lock it up and then continue on foot. I liked the solace of the dusty trails and the fact there was an entire network of them so I could go down any path I wanted. I loved exploring different routes, taking in different scenery, trying for a more challenging elevation profile on certain days long before I could even tell you what an elevation profile was.

As I grew older, my love for hiking also grew. I began planning road trips around day hikes — to Big Bear, to the Sierra, to Bryce Canyon, to Las Vegas, to Sedona. I had — have — a group of friends who equally loved the outdoors, and my 20’s were defined by these trips around California and the Southwest to bear witness to natural beauty and escape the bustle of metropolitan life.

For all my excursions, though, and for all my love of the outdoors and adventures and even for The Itch to which I so willingly and often submitted to, I was always a day hiker. I in fact did not go on my first backpacking trip until this past summer, after nearly two years of living in Montana.

Backpacking was something I’d always wanted to get into. My sister, her husband and their friends spent nearly every summer backpacking in the Sierra in their 20’s and 30’s. Every year, I’d tell myself, “Next year. Next year I’ll go with them.” But things always got in the way — undergrad, then COVID, then grad school, then a career. After I moved to Montana to be with my boyfriend (now fiance) Kade, I was looking 30 straight in the face.

My move to Montana is what also brought me to Jessica “Stitches” Guo. In 2025, she became the first known woman to link the Continental Divide Trail and Great Divide Trail in a calendar year. My Instagram algorithm brought her journey to my attention.

At the time, I was working as the sports editor of a local Montana newspaper. It was the summer, a slow time for sports desk. I thought meeting up with her as she came through Anaconda would be the perfect opportunity to profile her for our newspaper. As it turned out, it became so much more for me.

I met Guo at a local market and followed her around as she did her town chores. I was so curious as to what a resupply looked like on a trek such as this, what sort of food thru-hikers ate. I drove Guo to the hiker hut in town and conducted my interview with her there, learning all about her life — how she’d come to want to link the CDT and GDT, how she had hiked the Pacific Crest Trail already too.

But I was most curious about how she’d begun. So often, we join an influencer or content creator’s journey online while they’re in the midst of it, whether it’s their first one or their third one or their tenth. As someone who’d always had an interest in backpacking but was also intimidated about where to start, I wanted to pick Guo’s brain about how and why she had gotten started.

I asked her if there was one determining factor that led her to her decision to hike the PCT.

“I felt like, if I didn’t do it then, that I was never going to do it,” Guo told me. “Like I feel sometimes you realize that time just keeps going by and by and by. You can say, ‘Okay, next year, next year, next year,’ just keep putting that up. You’ll be 70 and you’ll be like, ‘Oh, you know what? I wanted to hike this trail.’ And life only gets busier. I think if you have a goal, I think you should first go do it.”

I went home that evening and told Kade about my conversation with Guo. He knew I’ve always had an interest in backpacking.

That’s when I channeled my inner Stitches and I told myself and Kade, “No, not ‘next year’ or the year after that. Actually next year.”

Settling on the Trans-Catalina Trail was an easy, simple decision for me. I’d had my eye on it my whole adult life, having been born and raised in Southern California. I’m even sure that, while on a fifth-grade GATE field trip to Catalina Island, the trails we had hiked were on the Trans-Catalina Trail. Even then I remember how brutally hot and exposed the trail was, and I was struck by the power lines that looped around even in the island’s rugged interior.

It seemed like the ideal first multi-day backpacking trip: just about 40 miles over four days, with very established campgrounds with potable water. No wildlife to be wary of save the buffalo, but coming from grizzly country I wasn’t too concerned. The elevation profile, while daunting at nearly 10,000 feet of gain overall, should be challenging but nothing I couldn’t handle, especially coming from living at over 5,000 feet of altitude.

I’ll save specifics for how I trained for this hike for another blog, but I will say that it was just a little over a year ago that I promised myself I would hike the Trans-Catalina Trail in 2026 for my first thru-hike. I wanted to do something big and special for myself for my 30th birthday, but early October is still much too hot in Southern California. Spring was an option, but that would leave me just a couple months to train for it — and it would be limited to just the gym, because while I’m acclimated to Montana climate, there is still no way in hell I’m doing a winter backpacking trip to prepare.

So that left the fall. Thanksgiving week seemed the safest option; it’s still quite warm in Southern California on Thanksgiving but never extremely hot. Plus, Kade decided he would come with me for this first experience. As a business owner in small-town Montana, using the Thanksgiving holiday to hike would be the most doable option for him. That gave us the entire spring, summer and early fall to get us into backpacking shape.

I awoke early on January 1 of this year very differently than I had in the New Year’s Days of past. No hangover, not even very sleepy — Kade and I had retired early, like responsible, boring adults.

While Kade lingered in bed with our dogs, Roxy and Gigi, I got up and set my laptop down on the dining room table. I had spent the past couple months diving into all the research into the Trans-Catalina Trail. One of the things I’ve grown to love most about backpacking training is the research — spending hours zooming in and out and around on onX Backcountry, studying the trails and terrain; reading about the history of the area I’ll be exploring; watching countless YouTube videos of hikers’ journeys. In the year leading up to this trip, which in exactly a week of this writing I’ll be embarking upon, I have already saved handfuls of shorter thru-hikes and backpacking routes on All Trails for Kade and I to do next summer. That’s another facet of this backpacking journey I’ve grown to love: it gives you a tangible goal to work towards.

By the time it was January 1 and permits and campgrounds reservations for the Trans-Catalina Trail had opened up on the Catalina Island Conservancy’s website, I knew exactly which campgrounds and sites I wanted. A year early, and I had our itinerary down. I had logged each day by mileage and elevation gain and executed a game plan based on my research. Kade, not much of a planner, was happy to stand back and let me plan and research away.

About 20 minutes later — and a couple hundred dollars less in my bank account, as the campsites for two people on Catalina are quite hefty — and we were all booked to hike the Trans-Catalina Trail from November 24 to November 27. Now it was time to spend the year preparing for it, to see if this is something I could actually do.
