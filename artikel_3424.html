Kerry Kennedy on Her Brother RFK Jr.: “We Were Very Close, but He Changed”

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Kerry Kennedy is not in Milan on political business, but it’s hard to rein in the enthusiasm of some Americans following the 2026 midterm elections. Democrats won decisive majorities in both the House and Senate, along with 24 of the 36 governors elections held this month. It was a blue tsunami. Kennedy—a lawyer and human rights activist born in 1959 to dynasty Democrats Robert and Ethel Kennedy—is in a celebratory mood.

She’s in town for one night only to attend a charity dinner in support of her foundation, the Robert & Ethel Kennedy Human Rights Center. But first she sits down with Vanity Fair Italia, and over the course of our chat, she takes her beloved poodle, Scamper, for a short walk; sips two cappuccinos (strictly decaf), one of them iced (she apologizes to the Italians present); eats some gianduiotti chocolates; gestures animatedly; and smooths her very blonde hair.

But above all, she never stops smiling: “In America you can only go so far in the attempt to avoid the rule of law and erode democracy,” she says, making clear that her political statements are delivered in her personal capacity, “because at a certain point, even if you’re the most powerful and the richest man there, democracy will bring you down. [The president] made a mess all over the world, but in the end, in America, democracy was stronger.”

From left: Robert Kennedy Jr., Joseph P Kennedy Jr., Tory Kennedy, and Kerry Kennedy on January 1, 1994

Vanity Fair: What do voters want today?Kerry Kennedy: They’re upset about high prices. And they don’t like the corruption.

What do you think will happen to Donald Trump and his associates?I think that [Representative] Jamie Raskin is now going to be the head of the Judiciary Committee. I happen to know him very, very, very well because Jamie and I studied for the bar together. He’s brilliant, he’s determined, and he hates anything that harms our Constitution. I think that Trump is going to face impeachment. The rumors are that Trump’s going to fire [Pete] Hegseth, but if he isn’t fired, he’ll certainly be impeached.

What will be the repercussions of the blue tsunami for Tehran?Iran’s leader at the moment, [Mojtaba Khamenei], has seen his father murdered by Trump. His head of state was murdered by Trump. His spiritual leader was murdered by Trump. Now Trump wants to make a peace deal with this guy. The only way to make progress is for the Iranians to think that it’s not Trump but someone else who’s negotiating.

And what about Ukraine?The fact that Donald Trump sold out Western values in Ukraine for a barrel of oil from a mad, thieving, murderous dictator in Russia shocked the American people and shocked people around the world. Congress has the power of the purse, and now it’s going to exercise that power. But…

But?He’s still the president for two more years. We didn’t get the White House.

Ethel Kennedy and daughter Kerry Kennedy circa 1979

Who is your ideal candidate for the 2028 presidential election?Anyone whose last name is Democrat.

Do you think [President Barack] Obama played a role in the Democrats’ success?Obama is beloved. The only person who’s beloved more is Michelle [Obama]. And when I watched his speeches, he was so clear and concise about the differences between the Republicans and the Democrats, with his moral authority and his joy.

You’ve talked about how your mother supported your father. Can you share an example of that?My father was working for the Senate, and he was investigating organized crime’s infiltration of the Teamsters union, stealing pension funds from the rank-and-file Teamsters. There was a journalist from the New York Post who was also investigating, and somebody threw acid in his face. He was permanently blinded. A few days later, my mother got an anonymous letter saying, “Your children will be next.” She took her three oldest kids, put them in the back of her car, drove them to the Capitol, walked into the hearing room, and sat in the front row while daddy was interrogating Jimmy Hoffa, the head of the Teamsters.

She was saying, “You’re not going to get to me through my children, and you’re not going to get to my husband through me. We are a fortress and we’re coming after you.” I don’t know how she grappled with the trauma.

She probably did it for her children.She needed to be there for us. She was an athlete. And if you feel physically mighty, it’s very helpful when you have to also be psychologically mighty. She would get up and go swimming, then horseback riding, sailing, and after a sail, she would go for a swim. She also had a very deep faith. She went to Mass every day, prayed before and after every meal, and she made us read the Bible out loud at the dinner table every night.

Are you that devout too? Yes. In December, I’ll step down from being president of this organization. I’ll stay on the board and I’ll be president emeritus and I’ll still be involved, but I want to go to divinity school.

Stephen Colbert and Kerry Kennedy on December 11, 2013

At what university?I don’t know. I have to apply and be accepted.

In 2008 you wrote the bestseller Being Catholic Now. What does it mean to be Catholic in 2026?For Americans, we have had this extremely disruptive, terrifying time for the last two years, and there are existential threats that everybody in the world is facing. And suddenly there’s an American pope who’s saying, “When you feel this is wrong, it is wrong. When this president is allowing ICE agents to go into American cities and start shooting people, that’s wrong. We need to have compassion toward one another. We need to have love toward one another. Every human being is valuable.”

He’s one of the few voices—not one Republican senator has stood up, and other leaders around the world really have not stood up. Mark Carney did from Canada.

Pedro Sánchez [Spain’s prime minister] did a little bit.Yes, but not much. The pope has been the main person, and I think that’s renewed people’s faith because it’s very simple as a political leader to call on people’s anger and hatred and fury. It’s a simple trick to get elected. But what people really want you to do in the end is call upon their better angels and say, “You’re good. I see that the God in me sees the God in you.” That is the alternative message, and that’s the spiritual message. I think that it’s created a revival of faith. My daughter Cara is 31 years old, and every Sunday night, she and all of her friends go to church and it’s packed.

What lesson did you learn from your parents?When we were kids, when we would have a battle, and there were lots of battles, my father always did the same thing. He would bring two of us into a room. My brother had to be silent, and I would tell my father what happened and all the horrible things my brother did. And then I’d have to be silent, and he would tell his story. And then it would be clear that neither of us was completely innocent. We would have to kiss and make up and go to our rooms to read for an hour. His message to us was his message to our country, which is: You have to stop thinking of your adversary as your enemy and think of them as your brother and sister.

Your brother, Robert F. Kennedy Jr., currently the secretary of health in the Trump administration, is known for his anti-vax views. You’ve often said, “I love him very much, but I don’t agree with him.”We were very close, but he changed and went way over there. And that’s where he is today. But we have much more in common than not.

Robert F. Kennedy Jr and Kerry Kennedy on May 20, 2015

Do you discuss politics today?I’m not going to go talk to him about vaccine policy. That’s not going to change him, and he’s not going to change me. So when I’m with him, I want to talk about, how are his animals? How are his children? His daughter, who is my goddaughter, Kyra, lives here in Milan, and she’s getting married to an Italian, and I’m so happy for her. There are many things to talk about besides vaccines.

Your shared past also includes the assassination of your father. Who told you that news?We were all together in the Ambassador Hotel in Los Angeles and we were watching the returns, but I was eight and I went to sleep. I woke up very early the next morning because I was still on East Coast time, and I went into the living room area of my hotel room. I turned on the TV to watch Bugs Bunny. That’s how I learned.

Did you guys see a therapist when you were little?Of course not. It was 1968. If it happened today, every kid would get their own therapist; back then, you dealt with it.

Really?Other students were reading Charlotte’s Web, and I was trying to figure out if somebody was going to kill someone in my family. What happens if Mommy dies? Are they going to split us up? Who could take 11 kids? Houses aren’t big. Where do we go? Would we see each other on Christmas?

Were you a happy little girl before that day?When I was a child, my parents really didn’t separate my father’s life and work from our home. So Daddy would call my mother and say, “You know what? There are three or four of us. We’re trying to work out this [policy] issue. Could we come home for lunch?” Sure. And then he’d call back half an hour later. “Actually, now we’re 12.”

They sat at the dining room table, and there would be three or four kids doing homework and eating peanut butter and jelly sandwiches and asking them to pass the milk. And then they would be talking about these issues. My mother would come in and say, “Time to play football.” And everybody would get up and go out. For us kids, it felt like we were part of something bigger.

Your latest book, which has just been published, is a biography of your mother: Ethel Kennedy: The Extraordinary Life and Bold Legacy. What kind of person was she?My mother loved men. If you’re a woman, you’re great, and you’re her friend. But when a man was watching, it was incredible. Her whole life, that’s the way she was. When she was 96 and her mind was kind of in and out, I called her from my hotel in Milan and I said, “I’m here. Can I bring you anything? Do you want some limoncello or a bag of that beautiful spaghetti that they sell at the airport? What can I bring you from Italy?” And she answered, “An Italian boyfriend would be nice.”
