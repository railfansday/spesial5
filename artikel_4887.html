What “Food Is Medicine” Actually Means, According to a Cardiologist

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

Plus, Chef Marcus Samuelsson shares easy ways to sneak more plant-based foods into your diet.

Despite what fitfluencers and gym bros might have you believe, eating healthy is about a lot more than counting macros and calories. Every day, scientists are learning more about how our eating habits can influence health beyond just our weight, including blood pressure, cholesterol, blood sugar, and risk of developing heart disease.

That's part of the idea behind a buzzy approach to health care known as “food is medicine,” which contends that what we put on our plates can be a meaningful part of preventing and managing disease.

The challenge is understanding what actually helps and turning that knowledge into meals we want to keep eating. To unpack this, Future You host and editorial director of Men’s and Women’s Health Rich Dorment sits down with Marcus Samuelsson, the acclaimed restaurateur and author of the new cookbook, Back to Green, and Louai Razzouk, MD, a cardiologist at NYU Langone Health.

Here’s a look at some of the themes covered in the episode:

Samuelsson says that beso is an Ethiopian drink that combines roasted barley with water or milk of your choice. “You just shake it and this is the best protein shake you can have,” he says. You can find the more specific recipe in Back to Green.

He grew up eating foods like beso as well as shiro, a stew made from chickpea flour, and other Ethiopian “superfoods” that he incorporates into his dishes today.

Dr. Razzouk says it doesn't mean that you can treat a disease with food alone. “It does mean that you may be able to prevent it by having a healthier and more balanced diet, and possibly minimize how many medications you need to be on,” he says.

Dr. Razzouk says that while you may still need to be on medication, food can make a “huge” impact when it comes to managing conditions like diabetes or high cholesterol. “Food impacts our level of inflammation, our insulin sensitivity, our weight,” and our blood pressure, he says.

The first warning sign that a patient’s diet may be less than ideal is if they get most of their food from Uber Eats or another delivery service. “Most of the stuff that we order in, especially living in New York City, contains a lot of saturated fats, a lot of added salt, and a lot of processed food,” he says. He discusses a couple more red flags in the episode, including meal timing.

In his early 20s, traveling in Southeast Asia and Singapore opened his eyes to a new way of eating—and the idea that there’s great food to be found all over the world.

A plant-forward diet doesn't have to exclude animal protein, he says, pointing to the Mediterranean and DASH diets as examples of eating plans that incorporate some meat. “What I tell patients is that typically if you have a choice between getting a source of protein from an animal source versus from plants, then I would try to get it from plants,” he says.

“What I tell patients is that I want you to enjoy your life,” Dr. Razzouk says, adding that fad diets typically don’t work. “What we would like you to do is find a balance,” he says, eating in a way that's enjoyable and that benefits your health.

It's a lentil salad with a coconut turmeric vinaigrette—and Samuelsson breaks down why the flavors work so well together, plus ingredients you may have in the fridge or pantry that can level it up even further.

He’ll often bring home new ingredients (e.g. spirulina) and encourage his son to look up the health benefits before they cook with it together.

Dr. Razzouk says diets high in fiber not only may lower your risk of colon cancer, but they also may lower your level of LDL cholesterol and triglycerides, which impact your risk of cardiovascular disease. It also helps keep you full.

Cooking together can be an enjoyable family activity, Samuelsson says. “When I cook waffles and pancakes with my kids, we have fun,” he says. “The kitchen is a mess, but iPads are down, we're spending time together.”

Samuelsson suggests chickpeas, sea moss, and the fruit soursop. Dr. Razzouk suggests nuts (walnuts in particular) and extra-virgin olive oil.
