8 Near-Perfect Prime Video Shows With 10 Episodes Or Fewer

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Some of the best Prime Video TV shows are 10 episodes or fewer, proving that length isn’t always what matters. For folks who like to dive deep into stories and invest in characters, multi-season shows can be especially appealing. Some series also need multiple seasons to feel satisfying, as they’re too complex to fit into a small number of episodes. For instance, Breaking Bad wouldn’t have been nearly as impactful without five seasons. I also can’t imagine the complex time-travel mystery Dark working as a miniseries. There are too many moving parts. However, longer isn’t always better.

Plenty of showrunners need only ten episodes or less to tell the story they’re eager to share with the world. The creators know what they want to say and aren’t going to prolong the story just because they can. A perfect example is Netflix’s Adolescence, which only needed four episodes to become a masterpiece. The same idea of "short but incredible" applies to Prime Video’s The Underground Railroad, which was added to The Criterion Collection, an archive of the most influential and important TV shows.

Other miniseries don’t quite reach perfection, but they're extremely close, despite being ten episodes or fewer. There are also TV shows that got canceled after a single season, but still fall into the “nearly perfect” category. Eight of these standout series were made by Prime Video or have the “Prime Video Original” label.

Few Prime Video shows have been as celebrated as Spider-Noir. Set in 1930s New York City, the series follows a hardened private investigator named Ben Reilly, who used to double as a superhero named The Spider. However, mobsters and a mysterious femme fatale bring him out of retirement. From the stellar writing to the beautiful cinematography, Spider-Noir elevates superhero television to blockbuster, feature-film levels.

The show blends noir elements, like character archetypes and high-contrast hard lighting, and comic book-style camera work. They filmed in black-and-white and true-hue full color. They included uncommon Spider-Man villains, which is refreshing. Plus, the cast included talented actors like Nicolas Cage and Lamorne Morris. As disappointing as it was for folks to learn Spider-Noir was canceled, I think it’s a good thing. The show is nearly perfect, if not actually perfect, as is, and another season could’ve weakened it.

The BBC and Prime Video collaboration, Small Axe, is brilliant, and it doesn’t get nearly enough attention. Technically speaking, Small Axe may or may not be a film series, depending on who you ask. Functionally, though, it’s no different from the miniseries on this list, especially since only one episode runs 90 minutes or more. Inspired by true stories, the anthology focuses on the experiences of West Indian Black immigrants living in London between the 1960s and 1980s.

When Small Axe was released, it sparked a massive debate because it received film nominations and awards from some institutions while receiving TV nominations and awards from others.

The writing draws audiences in, and the themes are just as poignant today as they were when the events unfolded. Another major selling point is the stacked cast, with John Boyega (Star Wars) and Letitia Wright (Black Panther) as standout performers. Plus, the creative team did beautiful technical work, especially cinematographer Shabier Kirchner.

Based on the titular book by Michelle Frances, The Girlfriend is a gripping thriller with plenty of moral grayness. Daniel, a momma’s boy, brings his new girlfriend, Cherry, home, and his mom, Laura, starts to question her motivations for being with him. This creates a push-and-pull. The episodes swap back and forth between the perspectives of Cherry and Laura, forcing viewers to question who’s telling the truth.

The Girlfriend drops viewers straight into a violent confrontation, but it puts them in a different room, raising questions about who’s doing what. As it goes on, the show explores themes of greed and power without feeling heavy-handed. It leans into melodrama and campiness, rather than taking itself too seriously, which is refreshing in the age of prestige TV. Robin Wright and Olivia Cooke are fantastic as Laura and Cherry, and their strong chemistry creates a fierce enemy dynamic.

Étoile was Gilmore Girls creator Amy Sherman-Palladino’s second attempt at making a ballet-focused TV show, and the second time the nearly perfect show was canceled after a single season. In the Prime Video show, professional ballet companies in New York City and Paris are struggling, so they swap their most talented dancers to generate enough business to save them. The show is a love letter to ballet and the hard-working athletes and creative minds who bring the medium to life.

From the jump, Étoile is a hard sell, and the weak first episode doesn't help. But after that initial setup episode, the show is extremely entertaining. It has the same rapid-fire dialogue and wit that Sherman-Palladino is famous for, but with better representation and without the problematic humor. The story and characters are engaging. Plus, they cast real dancers, and the choreography is stellar.

Prime Video’s horror-comedy series Swarm was the right length at seven episodes, without unnecessarily dragging the story out. The story follows Andrea “Dre” Greene, a young woman and obsessive fan of global pop star Ni’Jah. After her sister dies by suicide, she snaps and starts killing in the name of her favorite singer. Swarm won't be for everyone because of its heightened surrealism and genre-blending satire. However, it’s still nearly perfect, even if it’s niche.

This serial killer origin story shows how loneliness and parasocial relationships can lead to dangerous obsession, and it’s chilling. Dominique Fishback gives an award-worthy performance, making Dre, a completely irredeemable character, engaging. Dre’s spiral is like a train wreck you can’t look away from because Fishback’s magnetic. Billie Eilish also captivates as the grandiose cult leader. The gorgeous cinematography is gritty and highly saturated. This heavily stylized visual matches the intentional campiness.

In 2024, Prime Video brought My Lady Jane by Brodi Ashton, Cynthia Hand, and Jodi Meadows to the screen, and it was phenomenal. In history, a 16-year-old Lady Jane took the throne for nine days to keep a Protestant in power. My Lady Jane reimagines the story, with division between humans and humans who can turn into animals instead of Protestants and Catholics. Lady Jane unwillingly marries Lord Guildford Dudley, then she’s unexpectedly put on the throne. My Lady Jane is a hilarious romp.

The TV show is raunchy, profane, and funny. The color palette is candy-pop, and the costumes are stunning. Emily Bader is a badass Lady Jane, and her chemistry with Edward Bluemel is tantalizing. My only criticism is that Prime Video canceled it too soon. The show was only halfway through the book, but it at least ends at a reasonable point instead of a giant cliffhanger, making it watchable.

Set in 1943 during WWII, the TV version of A League of Their Own centers on two storylines. Carson Shaw goes to Chicago to try out for the Rockford Peaches team in the All-American Girls Professional Baseball League and makes the team. Max Chapman is turned away from tryouts because of race, and she finds another way to play baseball. The stories weave together well, with three-dimensional and nuanced characters who feel real.

The comedy is entertaining, and the story has a lot of heart. The A League of Their Own cast is perfect. The baseball team has LGBTQ+ players, which is a reality of women's sports. For the record, the queer subtext absolutely existed in the original movie. What keeps this show from being perfect is that they used a digital baseball added in post-production, which is distracting at points. Also, the cancellation makes the ending feel slightly unsatisfying. Despite those two issues, A League of Their Own is still deeply enjoyable.​​​​​​​

The Prime Video miniseries The Narrow Road to the Deep North was horribly, horribly mismarketed, which is the crux of most negative audience reviews. This show is not a period romance. However, setting aside the marketing, the miniseries is nearly perfect, albeit extremely difficult to watch. Set during World War II, the story centers on a soldier named Dorringo Evans before, during, and after he’s taken as a prisoner of war at a Japanese labor camp.

Jacob Elordi gives a powerful performance. The visuals are beautiful, using lighting and color to convey the main character’s deterioration. The stark contrast between each time period, created by the non-linear timeline, makes the story more impactful. I will warn potential viewers that The Narrow Road to the Deep North is not a fun watch. It's an unbelievably bleak show and depicts the brutality of war, including starvation and physical torture. If you can cope with that, it’s a meaningful watching.
