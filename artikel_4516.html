The Courage to Question Our Own Certainty

Posted November 25, 2026 | Reviewed by Monica Vilhauer Ph.D.

There are a plethora of potentially polarizing issues these days in all aspects of our lives! It seems that we can hardly get a point of view on the table before a fellow conversationalist debunks our idea and discredits us along the way. Take your pick from a bushel full of igniting issues, whether it be global warming, wars, increasing the tax percentage for the very wealthy, or the strengths and weaknesses of our favorite political personalities; there will be no shortage of strong opinions. You can also be assured that before the full set of points of view are exposed or explored, one or more folks will have already decided what the correct point of view should be.

Our positioning and decisions on an issue seem like they happen in the blink of an eye. This instant judgement can create ill will, stress for us and the other, and can challenge or ruin long-standing relationships. Furthermore, it will likely lead to a suboptimal exploration and resolution of the salient points of view. The pivot that I would like to explore is realizing that although our interpretations and positions usually feel quite certain to us, they most likely do not represent the whole of the reality of that situation; they are a narrower, biased slice of the problem or solution.

I would like to explore what can happen when we can loosen our grip on our story/point of view, and instead consider what might ensue if we instead question our own certainty. This, of course, would require slowing down enough to really listen first and then explore the fuller reality of the situation together; then, with skillful listening and openness to other ways of thinking, we can have a meaningful and civil dialogue.

We seem to be experiencing a plethora of fractured conversations, competing accounts of the truth, information overload, and fear about the future, all of which are leading to what we might call “contemporary exhaustion.” And if the impact of this on us personally wasn’t enough, the relational tension as well as the societal and political challenges are at an all-time high. It is so much more likely that answers to our difficult questions will land somewhere in the middle of the two opposing extremes being debated, or in a yet-to-be-discovered “third alternative” idea that cannot possibly be uncovered because of the anger, tension, and lack of listening in the interchange. Just to ground this idea in one statistic: the American Psychological Association's Stress in America survey, "A Crisis of Connection," reported that 62% of adults considered societal divisions a significant stressor in their lives.

If you are willing to have the “courage to suspend the certainty” of your initial interpretation and allow for more information, thoughts, and feelings to emerge between you and another, amazing things can happen. We all have the tendency to confuse the story we have crafted and are trying to tell with the whole truth about a situation. Perhaps a better way to proceed is to remain open to what we have not yet explored or understood, remain open to another way of thinking about a situation, and remain open to the other person. If we could find the grace to do so, we might learn to see and understand more deeply before we judge. All of this ultimately requires that we develop the courage to question our own certainty. If we could do so, it is very likely that our world would become a much better, kinder place to live and thrive.
