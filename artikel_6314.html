Denver Airport Taco Bell Worker Sues Over Unpaid Security Time. So Much For My $1.70 Burritos?

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

I had barely finished celebrating $1.70 burritos at Denver Airport when View From The Wing highlighted a lawsuit that might help explain why airport food usually costs so much more. No, we did not coordinate our Taco Bell coverage. Apparently, we just had different concerns about what happens before someone hands me a burrito.

A former Taco Bell food preparer at Denver International Airport is suing the restaurant’s operator over allegedly unpaid time spent passing through security and traveling to the restaurant before clocking in. The Colorado state court lawsuit also alleges failures to provide paid rest breaks, according to Law360.

Meanwhile, I had just written about finding surprisingly affordable Taco Bell food in Concourse A. A reader noticed the coincidence and wondered about the relationship between my cheap burritos and Gary’s discussion of employee compensation.

My first instinct is to distinguish getting to work from doing the job. Most employees do not get paid for their commute, even when traffic makes it miserable. An airport restaurant operator does not control the security checkpoint, either.

But Colorado’s wage rules give the worker a substantial argument. They specifically include security or safety screening, and waiting for it, among activities that can constitute compensable time worked. The rules also provide circumstances in which travel between work locations must be paid once work has begun.

That raises a question beyond the checkpoint itself: if required screening starts the paid workday, does the subsequent journey to the restaurant count too?

At Denver Airport, getting through security does not necessarily mean you are anywhere near your workstation.

Taco Bell can reasonably argue that the screening and journey remain part of the commute and the lawsuit, on its own, does not establish that wages were unlawfully withheld. Still, Colorado’s express inclusion of security screening makes this a claim worth taking seriously.

I often roll my eyes at airport food prices. My recent Denver visit included a nearly $20 barbecue salad and a $6.25 street taco that left me much less satisfied than Taco Bell.

But this dispute illustrates a genuine expense associated with operating inside an airport.

If employees must be paid while waiting for screening and making their way to the restaurant, the operator is paying for time before anyone starts preparing food or serving customers. That expense has to be absorbed somewhere. Some of it could end up in menu prices. Indeed, it must.

Every overpriced airport meal is not suddenly justified, but workers should not forfeit compensation required by law so I can enjoy a cheap lunch. It is a more convincing explanation for a price premium than simply charging more because passengers have nowhere else to go…

I hope those $1.70 burritos stick around. After praising them, I would prefer not to discover that I had written their farewell tribute.

But if airport employees must be paid for additional time getting through security and reaching their workplace, higher operating costs will put pressure on those bargains. I can appreciate affordable food and recognize that providing it inside an airport comes with expenses the neighborhood Taco Bell does not face. Gary and I managed to cover both sides of that equation without even comparing notes!
