Breast cancer, the most diagnosed cancer among women, is stratified by gene expression patterns into luminal and basal-like subtypes, and the latter is associated with worse prognosis1,2,3. Recent advances in single-cell technologies have underscored the presence of intratumour heterogeneity, which poses substantial challenges in cancer treatment and achieving long-term disease-free survival4,5,6,7. Here we extensively profile epigenetic heterogeneity in a cohort of treatment-naive human luminal tumours, which were obtained directly after surgical resection. By applying a single-cell epigenetic approach based on cytometry by time-of-flight, we identify a recurrent subpopulation of basal-like cells in luminal tumours with a conserved epigenetic signature across patients. These basal-like cells, also identified by deconvolution of whole-genome DNA methylation patterns, are characterized by high expression of aggressive oncogenic traits and downregulation of hormone receptor signalling. Direct comparison of single-cell proteomic and genomic profiling of monomethylation and trimethylation of histone 3 on lysine 4 revealed that these marks are key features associated with loss of luminal identity. Finally, we highlight the potential of concomitant single-molecule profiling of histone modifications on cell-free nucleosomes in blood as an epigenetic liquid biopsy approach for early diagnosis of breast cancer. Moreover, the epigenetic basal signature measured in blood is linked to prognosis, which enables the identification of patients at increased risk for disease relapse.

Gene expression profiles classify breast cancers into luminal A or luminal B (hormone-receptor-positive (HR+)), HER2-positive and basal-like subtypes. Among these, basal-like subtypes largely overlap with triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC)2,3. These classifications guide treatment management and prognosis. Luminal HR+ breast cancer generally responds to endocrine therapy with favourable outcomes, yet many patients relapse8. Single-cell and spatial studies have revealed substantial intratumoural heterogeneity among subtypes, which potentially drive treatment resistance and recurrence4,5,6,7,9.

Chromatin organization, mediated by DNA and histone post-translational modifications (PTMs), facilitates the acquisition of epigenetically distinct cellular states in genetically identical cells10,11,12. Although single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and single-cell chromatin-accessibility sequencing (scATAC–seq) delineate intratumour heterogeneity13,14,15,16, the contribution of chromatin patterns remains unclear17. Single-cell profiling of histone marks, such as trimethylation of histone 3 on lysine 27 (H3K27me3), implicates chromatin states in breast tumour heterogeneity and treatment resistance18,19. However, these methods measure a limited number of chromatin marks, which hinders analyses of complex patterns and interactions. We developed a cytometry by time-of-flight (CyTOF) method to systematically profile multiple histone PTMs and decode population-specific chromatin patterns to examine heterogeneity among cancer cells20,21,22,23.

Tumour epigenetic states are also reflected in blood. Cell-free, plasma-circulating nucleosomes, released mainly by dying normal and cancer cells, retain tissue-of-origin epigenetic patterns24,25,26,27. Our single-molecule imaging platform, epigenetics of plasma isolated nucleosomes (EPINUC), captures multiple epigenetic features on plasma nucleosomes28. This method detects colorectal cancer, including low-burden early-stage disease28, and enables minimally invasive molecular profiling and treatment monitoring of paediatric brain tumours29.

Here we present a holistic epigenetic analysis of primary treatment-naive breast tumours and blood cell-free nucleosomes. Linking recurrent intratumoural epigenetic patterns to circulating nucleosomes connects tumour epigenetic heterogeneity with its potential clinical implications. This work reveals epigenetic alterations as key mediators of intratumour heterogeneity and potential biomarkers for early diagnosis and prognosis.

To assess heterogeneity in breast tumour histone modifications, we performed epigenetic focused CyTOF on fresh, surgically resected tumours from treatment-naive women and quantified histone modifications at single-cell resolution (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table 1). The cohort included mainly luminal HR+ tumours and only one basal triple-negative (TN, HR–) tumour (n = 12; Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Fig. 1a,b), as standard neoadjuvant therapy excluded most TN cases from treatment-naive eligibility. Following batch correction and normalization, uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) analysis of all 12 tumours revealed that tumour cells largely occupied patient-specific regions, whereas stromal cells clustered together across patients. This result aligns with previous studies that measured single-cell genomic differences13,14 (Fig. 1b, Methods and Supplementary Fig. 2a). Partial overlap among luminal tumour cells from different patients probably reflects shared lineage-specific epigenetic programs. As expected, stromal cells expressed vimentin and αSMA, whereas tumour cells expressed the epithelial keratins, EpCAM and E-cadherin (Fig. 1b,c and Supplementary Fig. 2b,c). Notably, the basal TN tumour differed from HR+ tumours, showing low expression of the luminal marker GATA3 and high expression of the basal marker cytokeratin-5 (K5; also known as KRT5) and mesenchymal vimentin markers (Fig. 1b,c and Supplementary Fig. 2d). Stromal and tumour chromatin patterns also differed, a result that underscores their role in dictating cellular identity (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 2e). CyTOF analysis of MCF7 (luminal) and four basal-like TN cell lines validated that GATA3 and K5 expression patterns can differentiate luminal and basal-like identities. Moreover, SHAP analysis30,31 identified these two markers as the strongest discriminators of tumour type (Fig. 1e,f). Using multiple cell-identity markers, we derived a PermCell luminal signature (Methods) that accurately distinguished the identities of the cell lines (Fig. 1g).

We next examined intratumour heterogeneity, focusing on the cancer cells of each luminal breast tumour separately. UMAP clustering and density-based spatial clustering of applications with noise (DBSCAN) across all features revealed three subpopulations in each tumour, which we termed cycling, luminal and basal-like (Fig. 1h,i and Extended Data Fig. 1a,b). These subpopulations were also conserved in two different luminal patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models. This finding validates the observed heterogeneity as an inherent feature of luminal breast tumours and excludes contamination of normal cells (Fig. 1j and Extended Data Fig. 1c). MCF7 cells showed similar luminal and basal-like heterogeneity, although most cells were cycling, as expected for a cell line (Extended Data Fig. 1d). In tumours, only a luminal subset cycled and expressed high levels of Ki-67, a result consistent with previous immunohistochemistry (IHC) and scRNA-seq studies14,32. CyTOF Ki-67 levels correlated with pathology estimates (Fig. 1k). Cycling cells also showed high levels of multiple chromatin modifications, both in raw measurements and following normalization to core histones (Fig. 1i, Extended Data Fig. 1a–c and Supplementary Fig. 3). We validated this result by repeating CyTOF analyses of two tumours (14 and 18), adding the established cell cycle marker phospho-Rb (pRb). pRb-positive cells showed an increase in core histones, as expected from cycling cells, and a larger increase in histone PTMs (Supplementary Fig. 3). Accordingly, the epigenetically inferred cycling fraction strongly correlated with Ki-67 levels (Fig. 1l).

Most cancer cells strongly expressed GATA3 and scored highly for the luminal signature, consistent with clinical classification (Fig. 1h, Extended Data Fig. 1a,b and Supplementary Fig. 4a). Moreover, in each tumour, the PDX samples and the MCF7 cells, we identified a minor recurrent subpopulation that acquired basal-like identity, with high K5 expression and a reduced luminal signature (Fig. 1h–j, Extended Data Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 4a). This phenotypic diversity is consistent with previous single-cell studies of human and mouse breast tumours14,21,33,34. Notably, luminal and basal-like cells also had distinct histone PTM signatures (Fig. 1h–j and Extended Data Fig. 1). Accordingly, UMAP plots based solely on histone modifications revealed separation of the subpopulations, which validated the epigenetic basis of this phenotypic diversity (Supplementary Fig. 4b). In MCF7 cells, epigenetic UMAP and clustering similarly identified several clusters, including a K5hi basal-like population (Fig. 1m). These basal-like cells with reduced luminal signalling appeared closer in UMAP space to the TN cell lines, which provided further support for their basal-like identity (Supplementary Fig. 4c). Independent CyTOF experiments, additional MCF7 parental cell culture and populations derived from independent single-cell clones revealed similar subpopulation patterns, a result that argues against a genetic basis for these effects (Fig. 1n and Extended Data Fig. 2a–c). Moreover, individual PDX tumours derived from the same donor and grown independently in different mice showed similar subpopulation proportions, which provides support for a dynamic and plastic relationship between cell states (Extended Data Fig. 2d). Overall, our data revealed inherent intratumour heterogeneity in luminal tumours, with epigenetic variability between distinct subpopulations.

We further explored the characteristics of the cycling, luminal and basal-like subpopulations. Unsupervised clustering grouped corresponding subpopulations across patients, which confirmed their robustness and similarity (Fig. 2a). Across tumours, luminal cells expressed oestrogen receptor (ER), GATA3 and cytokeratin-8 and cytokeratin-18 (K8/18; also known as KRT8 and KRT18, respectively), whereas basal-like cells downregulated these luminal markers and highly expressed K5 (Fig. 2b). As these subpopulations also exhibited distinct and conserved chromatin patterns, we applied a machine-learning algorithm to predict intratumour classification35. In each tumour, a model trained on a random subset predicted the remaining cells with 94% accuracy, which confirmed the robust differences between subpopulations (Fig. 2c and Extended Data Fig. 3a). It also assigned 78% of unclustered ‘noise’ cells with >95% confidence, generating classifications that aligned with the original clustering and UMAP positions (Extended Data Fig. 3b). SHAP analysis identified the features driving each subpopulation. In detail, histone PTMs, particularly the four acetylation marks, were leading predictors of cycling cells despite overlap of individual marks with basal-like cells (Extended Data Fig. 3c,d). Polycomb-associated H3K27me3 and H2Aub also contributed to the features, a finding consistent with previously observed Polycomb deregulation and pro-proliferative activity in breast cancer36,37,38.

Luminal and basal-like separation was similarly epigenetic driven, with H4K20me3, H3K36me3 and H3K9me2 marks having the highest SHAP scores (Fig. 2d). Across tumours, basal-like cells consistently had increased H3K9me2 and H3K4 monomethylation and trimethylation, whereas luminal cells had enhanced H4K20me3, H3K36me3, H3K9me3 and H3K27me3 marks (Fig. 2e). These results align with subtype-associated histone PTM signatures39 and reported roles for H3K4 demethylases, such as JARID1B, in luminal–basal transitions40. Both luminal and basal-like cells showed similar pRb distribution, which provide support for the conclusion that these results reflect distinct cell states rather than cell cycle differences (Extended Data Fig. 3e). Similar epigenetic signatures distinguished luminal and basal-like populations in PDX models (Extended Data Fig. 3f). Accordingly, epigenetic-only basal-like PermCell scores accurately captured basal-like cells in tumours, PDXs and MCF7 cells (Fig. 2f and Extended Data Fig. 4). Overall, our computational framework confirms that there is recurrent epigenetic heterogeneity in HR+ breast tumours and highlight chromatin patterns associated with basal-like K5hi cells.

Consistent with the CyTOF results, tumours with higher fractions of epigenetically defined basal-like cells had fewer ER-positive and progesterone receptor (PR)-positive cells, as measured by IHC (Extended Data Fig. 5a,b and Supplementary Table 2). Basal-like cells were also more abundant in worse-prognosis luminal B than luminal A tumours. This result is in agreement with studies showing that these tumours exhibit features shared with the basal-like subtype3,41,42,43 (Fig. 2g). Although tumour mutational burden was low across the cohort, it correlated positively with the CyTOF-defined basal-like fraction, which parallels the greater genomic instability typically associated with TNBC44,45 (Fig. 2h and Supplementary Table 2). TP53 mutant tumours likewise contained more basal-like cells (Extended Data Fig. 5c), a finding reminiscent of single-cell studies of glioblastoma showing that genetic mutations may affect balance between cellular states46. Overall, these associations between pathological and molecular characteristics link the epigenetically distinct basal-like subpopulation to a more aggressive disease phenotype.

To characterize these cells functionally, we performed FACS to sort cells from tumour 19 and then low-input mass spectrometry. After excluding stroma through pan-cytokeratin staining, cells were sorted according to K5 levels to enrich for basal-like cells compared with the luminal population. K5low cells highly expressed luminal markers and proteins associated with oestrogen signalling, whereas basal-like K5hi cells expressed proteins involved in oncogenic pathways, such as MYC–mTOR signalling (Fig. 2i–k). Moreover, multiple chromatin regulators differed between these subpopulations, including the H3K4me1 methyltransferase SETD7 and histone demethylase KDM4A, consistent with the histone PTM differences observed by CyTOF47,48 (Extended Data Fig. 5d). KDM4A, enriched in K5hi cells, targets H3K9me3 and H3K36me3, which potentially accounts for their reduced levels and H3K9me2 accumulation in the basal-like population49. Comparisons with a normal mammary single-cell atlas50 revealed hormone-sensing programs in K5low cells and an alveolar progenitor signature in K5hi cells (Fig. 2l), a result consistent with their enrichment in luminal and basal TN tumours, respectively50.

To gain a mechanistic understanding of these findings, we sorted MCF7 cells using K5-specific and GATA3-specific antibodies and performed transcriptomic profiling, comparing basal-like cells (K5hiGATA3low) to bulk MCF7 or extreme luminal cells (K5lowGATA3hi; Extended Data Fig. 5e,f). Notably, basal-like cells were enriched for epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) genes (P < 0.05; Extended Data Fig. 5f). We then focused on differentially expressed cell surface markers to enable live sorting, which revealed that CD74 was significantly upregulated in basal-like cells in both MCF7 and tumour 19 proteomic analyses (Fig. 2m and Extended Data Fig. 5g). Live-sorted CD74hi cells exhibited increased migratory capacity in migration and wound-healing assays, which provided further support for the more aggressive phenotypes of basal-like cells (Fig. 2n,o and Extended Data Fig. 5h). Together, these single-cell and bulk proteomics and functional assays identify epigenetically distinct K5hi basal-like cells in luminal tumours associated with aggressive traits such as reduced luminal signalling, elevated oncogenic signalling and increased migratory potential.

The distinct chromatin and phenotypic states of the basal-like subpopulation led us to explore whether intratumour epigenetic heterogeneity is also apparent in DNA methylation patterns. Thus, we conducted deep whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) on three luminal tumours from our cohort. DNA methylation patterns accurately capture breast cancer subtyping, as shown by The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)51. We next jointly deconvoluted the TCGA and our WGBS data into functional archetypes (V1–V3), with each tumour positioned in the deconvolution space. Stratifying the data into three archetypes revealed the expected separation of basal (TN) tumours from luminal (HR+), with archetype V3 associated with basal tumours (Extended Data Fig. 6a). Tumours 18, 19 and 23 from our cohort clustered with the luminal tumours, as expected from their bulk clinical subtyping. Correlation of the archetype contribution with RNA expression confirmed the association of V1 and V2 with luminal transcriptional programs, whereas V3 was associated with EMT, a result consistent with previous studies of TN tumours52,53,54 (Extended Data Fig. 6b and Supplementary Fig. 5a). To improve resolution, we added a fourth archetype (V4). This analysis revealed another layer of diversity: basal TN tumours were strongly associated with V3, which corresponded with reduced oestrogen signalling and increased oncogenic signalling, whereas luminal tumours were more heterogeneous and spread across the V1 axis (Fig. 3a,b and Supplementary Fig. 5b). Notably, V1 was strongly enriched for EMT, thereby potentially capturing an intermediate-transitional state between luminal and basal-like programs. Moreover, the percentage of CyTOF-measured, vimentin-positive cells, indicative of EMT, in tumours 18 and 19 differed accordingly (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 5c). Tumour 19, with a higher V1 contribution, also comprised more vimentin-positive cells, as assessed by CyTOF. These results support the use of WGBS deconvolution to capture functionally distinct cellular states in tumours, such as luminal cells undergoing EMT.

To directly link the CyTOF-defined subpopulations with DNA methylation patterns, we sorted K5hi and K5low cells from tumour 5 and tumour 23, followed by enzyme-mediated methylation sequencing (EM-seq). Functional enrichment of differentially methylated promoters revealed hypermethylation of oestrogen-response programs in K5hi cells, whereas genes associated with aggressive and oncogenic features, including KRAS signalling and mesenchymal or basal-state transitions, were hypomethylated (Extended Data Fig. 6c,d). We next derived a CpG island (CGI) methylation signature to distinguish the two states using CGIs that were concordantly differentially methylated across both tumours. In TCGA breast cancer data, this signature scored higher in basal-like tumours, which indicated that the K5hi methylation program resembles basal-like tumours (Extended Data Fig. 6e). K5hi samples also showed higher contribution to the basal-associated and EMT-associated archetypes than K5low samples, in both the three-archetype and four-archetype models (Extended Data Fig. 6f,g). Thus, the CyTOF-defined basal-like population exhibits basal-like and EMT-related methylation programs, which reveals another layer of intratumoural epigenetic heterogeneity.

Having identified distinct DNA methylation programs associated with tumour cell states, we next assessed cell-to-cell variability by clustering read-level methylation patterns across 100-bp genomic bins and calculating empirical entropy as an estimation of cell-type heterogeneity55. Genes near high-entropy bins were associated with functional terms related to poor differentiation, tumour aggressiveness and basal compared with luminal identity (Supplementary Fig. 6a). This result suggests that empirical entropy, by capturing diverse DNA methylation states, can be used as a proxy for cell-to-cell heterogeneity and reveal functional differences between subpopulations. We next calculated the mean entropy for each CGI (Extended Data Fig. 7a). As expected, most CGIs were unmethylated across all cells56, thus showing zero entropy. The variable CGIs (entropy > 0) were functionally enriched for EMT and the related Hedgehog signalling pathways, which indicated that these are differentially regulated in tumours57 (Extended Data Fig. 7b). This analysis suggests that intratumour variability in methylation patterns may be associated with cell identity transitions.

To further explore intratumour entropy and its relation to cell identity, we leveraged a published multiomic study of luminal breast cancer that included single-cell expression and chromatin accessibility measurements13. Focusing on cancer cells, we observed intratumour heterogeneity in luminal and basal-like expression patterns, as previously described14,54 (Supplementary Fig. 6b). Using the ATAC–seq modality, we calculated the within-peak (WIP) fraction, a metric of epigenetic noise58, whereby a low-WIP fraction may indicate a destabilized or noisier epigenome, which supports cell plasticity. For each tumour, we correlated the WIP fraction with chromatin accessibility of basal-like and luminal gene signatures. Cells that scored high for EMT and basal-like identity showed lower WIP fraction, a finding reminiscent of the higher DNA methylation entropy associated with these signatures (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 6c). Single-cell multiome analysis of a freshly resected independent luminal tumour reproduced the basal–luminal continuum and association between basal identity and reduced WIP fraction (Supplementary Fig. 6d,e). To explore whether the increased epigenetic noise is associated with higher transcriptional heterogeneity, we divided cells into quartiles according to a published basal–luminal index59 and basal-like and luminal gene sets (Methods). Indeed, across all tumours, cells with a high basal identity showed higher transcriptional heterogeneity (Extended Data Fig. 7c). In line with these observations, the CyTOF-defined basal-like subpopulation also showed higher epigenetic heterogeneity (Extended Data Fig. 7d). Moreover, read-level methylation entropy was higher in K5hi-sorted compared with K5low-sorted populations in both tumours analysed by EM-seq, with higher variability among the K5hi 200-cell replicates (Supplementary Fig. 6f,g). Together, our analyses across DNA methylation, chromatin accessibility and histone PTMs indicate that the basal-like subpopulation exhibts a noisy epigenetic landscape and increased transcriptional heterogeneity.

Our CyTOF analysis highlighted a global increase in several histone PTMs in the basal-like subpopulation, including H3K4 monomethylation and trimethylation (Fig. 2e). To explore the genomic distribution of these marks, we conducted single-nucleus CUT&Tag (snCUT&Tag) on three luminal tumours from our cohort60. Similar to the CyTOF data (Fig. 1b), stroma cells clustered together across patients, showing H3K4me1 and H3K4me3 deposition on fibroblast and endothelial markers. By contrast, tumours clustered separately, which indicated inter-patient variability (Extended Data Fig. 8a–d).

In each tumour, malignant cells clustered into distinct H3K4me1-defined or H3K4me3-defined states (Fig. 3e and Extended Data Fig. 8e,f). Differential analysis of H3K4me1 distribution between clusters highlighted a recurrent group of cells across tumours, annotated as ‘cluster 2’, with significant H3K4me1 enrichment at genes associated with basal-like identity and EMT (Fig. 3f,g and Extended Data Fig. 8g,i). In tumour 15, cluster 1 was also enriched for basal-like genes (adjusted P < 0.05), but not EMT. H3K4me3 similarly identified basal-like subpopulations in all tumours (Fig. 3h and Extended Data Fig. 8h,i). Accordingly, peaks associated with these clusters showed enrichment for transcription factor motifs linked to EMT and basal-like identity (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Next, we trained a classifier to identify H3K4me1-marked and H3K4me3-marked genes that contribute to variation between the clusters. For both histone methylations, these genes were enriched in terms related to breast cancer cellular identity, including oestrogen signalling, basal-like and mesenchymal identity (Supplementary Fig. 7b–e). Overall, our analysis provides support for chromatin-defined cell states in luminal tumour cells, including a basal-like state distinguished by H3K4me1 and H3K4me3 distributions.

The above snCUT&Tag results aligned with the heterogeneity observed by CyTOF, which led us to compare the single-cell datasets and integrating both genomic and protein information. CyTOF-defined basal-like cells showed global increases in both H3K4 methylation marks. We reasoned that sequencing reads per nucleus may reflect global levels of the target modification. Single-cell epigenomic analyses generally assume similar global modification levels, discarding read-count information, as in our analysis above61 (Fig. 3e–h). Departing from this convention, we examined whether read counts capture additional aspects of epigenetic heterogeneity. Across all three tumours and both modifications, read counts negatively correlated with luminal identity signatures and positively with basal-like and mesenchymal signatures (Fig. 3i and Extended Data Fig. 9a,b). Consistently, cells with high read counts, potentially indicative of elevated global levels of H3K4me1 or H3K4me3, showed less enrichment of these active marks at luminal identity genes. Although requiring further validation, this direct comparison suggests that read counts may serve as proxy for global levels of histone modifications to capture additional aspects of epigenetic variation.

To further integrate these datasets, we label-transferred the CyTOF classification (Figs. 1 and 2) onto the H3K4me1 snCUT&Tag dataset. CyTOF-predicted basal-like populations were enriched in the basal-like clusters identified by H3K4me1 genomic profiling (Fig. 3e and Extended Data Fig. 9b). For tumour 15, cluster 1 was also predicted to comprise basal-like cells, consistent with its higher H3K4me1 deposition on basal-like-associated genes (Fig. 3g and Extended Data Fig. 9c). CyTOF-predicted basal-like cells also showed higher H3K4me1 deposition at EMT and basal-like gene signatures, which further confirmed their distinct epigenetic and functional features (Extended Data Fig. 9d). To validate these results, we sorted luminal PDX models and MCF7 cells by K5 expression, followed by H3K4me1 CUT&Tag. K5low-specific peaks were enriched near oestrogen signalling genes, whereas K5hi-specific peaks were associated with basal-like and mesenchymal programs, which confirmed the distinct H3K4me1 landscapes between subpopulations (Extended Data Fig. 9e). Together, the protein abundance and genomic distribution data implicate H3K4me1 and H3K4me3 as epigenetic determinants of the basal-like subpopulation in luminal tumours.

The repressive mark H3K9me2 was also identified as a strong contributor to the epigenetic basal-like signature (Fig. 2d,e). Thus, we conducted H3K9me2 snCUT&Tag on tumour 8, which was also profiled for H3K4me1. Given that H3K9me2 occupies large genomic regions, its distribution is not readily interpretable in relation to gene activity, especially in low-coverage single-cell datasets. We therefore leveraged the H3K4me1 data and trained a classifier, assuming opposing genomic distributions of enhancer-associated H3K4me1 and repressive H3K9me2 chromatin62,63, to identify malignant versus stromal cells (Extended Data Fig. 9f). We next clustered tumour cells by H3K9me2 genomic distribution and applied the same H3K4me1-based inference framework. This approach predicted strong enrichment of basal-like cells in cluster 4, consistent with elevated H3K9me2 deposition on oestrogen-related genes (Extended Data Fig. 9g–i). This cluster also showed the highest H3K9me2 read counts per nucleus, which provided support for its correspondence to the H3K9me2hi basal-like cells identified by CyTOF (Extended Data Fig. 9j). Overall, these analyses highlight an epigenetically distinct basal-like population with globally increased and redistributed active and repressive histone modifications over genes associated with luminal-basal identity and EMT.

Our multimodal analysis linked the chromatin landscape to intratumoural cell-state diversity. We next aimed to translate our tissue-level findings to a liquid biopsy test based on our single-molecule EPINUC technology, which profiles the epigenetics of plasma-isolated nucleosomes28,29. We tailored EPINUC for breast cancer by developing single-molecule systems to quantify the breast-tissue-related proteins K5, L1CAM and MUC1, and added the histone PTMs H4K16ac and H4K20me3 (refs. 64,65,66,67) (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Table 5 and Supplementary Fig. 8a–c). Nucleosomes from plasma were captured and their combinatorial modifications imaged by TIRF microscopy, whereas protein biomarkers were quantified in parallel single-molecule antibody-capture assays (Fig. 4a). The expanded 29-parameter panel was applied to a cohort of plasma from 77 women with luminal breast cancer and 30 healthy women, and the results revealed significant differences (Supplementary Table 6). For example, cancer samples contained more H3K9ac and H4K16ac nucleosomes (Fig. 4b). As PTMs were normalized to nucleosome abundance, these differences reflect the epigenetic state rather than tumour burden. We also used EPINUC to measure PTM ratios and co-occurrence, which revealed increased bivalent (H3K4me3 and H3K27me3) nucleosomes, as previously observed in colorectal cancer and pancreatic cancer28. Indeed, bivalency is altered across cancers68, and can prime drug-tolerant persister programs in breast cancer19.

L1CAM and MST1 were also elevated in plasma from patients with breast cancer, a result in agreement with the association of high MST1 with poor prognosis in ER+ tumours69 (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 8d). Principal component analysis (PCA) of predictive features separated healthy individuals from patients with breast cancer, which highlights the ability of EPINUC to capture differences between these groups (Fig. 4d).

We next applied machine-learning-based classifiers to diagnose luminal breast cancer from EPINUC data. SHAP analysis of XGBoost models trained on all patients, or only stage T1 cases, identified multiple histone PTMs and protein biomarkers as informative (Fig. 4e). Notably, both models emphasized combinatorial features, particularly H4K16ac and H4K20me3 co-occurrence, thereby highlighting the ability of EPINUC to quantify such combinations. In particular, H4K16ac activates transcription of transposable elements70, which reside in H4K20me3 heterochromatin71, and because transposable elements are often upregulated in cancer, elevated H4K16ac and H4K20me3 might be a cancer-associated phenomenon. Finally, we applied several classifiers to assess the diagnostic contribution of the expanded EPINUC panel in discriminating healthy individuals from patients with cancer. Use the original 20 EPINUC parameters28 produced an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.85 (Supplementary Fig. 9a), whereas the expanded 29-parameter panel achieved 0.95, including among patients with early-stage T1 cancer (Fig. 4f,g). Unlike mammography, the sensitivity of which declines with dense breast tissue72,73, the features and performance of EPINUC were unaffected by different BI-RADS breast density scores, including patients with heterogeneous and extremely dense breast scores (Supplementary Fig. 9b,c). To assess the robustness of the EPINUC-based classifier, we applied the model trained on the original cohort (MAYO-1; Fig. 4f) to an independent, separately processed set of plasma samples collected 6–12 months later (MAYO-2). Following batch correction (Methods), the pre-trained model accurately distinguished healthy from luminal breast cancer, achieving an AUC of 0.98 and high classification accuracy (Extended Data Fig. 10a,b).

To generalize our findings, we analysed pretreatment plasma from patients with luminal breast cancer at two independent medical centres (Supplementary Table 6). EPINUC distinguished patients from healthy controls with high accuracy in both cohorts and showed comparable sensitivity and specificity in TNBC (Extended Data Fig. 10c–e). Pooling all luminal samples across centres (n = 188), a model trained on 75% of the data achieved an AUC of 0.96 and robustly classified the remaining 25% despite mixed sources and batches, a scenario that reflects real-world clinical settings (Fig. 4h, Extended Data Fig. 10f and Supplementary Fig. 10). Performance was similar for luminal A and luminal B tumours (Extended Data Fig. 10g). Although retrospective, these results support the use of high-resolution, multiparametric plasma epigenetic analyses for cancer diagnosis.

To further explore the association between tumour and plasma epigenetic patterns, we analysed a small cohort of luminal tumours with histone PTM mass-spectrometry data39, which were similarly stratified by disease recurrence39. H3K4me1 and H3K9me2, which marked the basal-like cells in our CyTOF data, were significantly higher in patients who developed recurrence, a result that further links this subpopulation to aggressive tumour traits (Figs. 2e and 5c). Finally, for patients analysed in Figs. 1–3, we obtained preoperative blood samples, which enabled direct correlation of tumour (CyTOF) and blood (EPINUC) epigenetic patterns. Most histone PTMs correlated positively, with the recurrence-associated H3K4me1 and H3K9ac marks showing the strongest positive correlation, a result that provided further support for their prognostic value (Fig. 5d and Extended Data Fig. 11h). Although requiring larger validation, these data suggest that circulating nucleosomes report both the tumour presence and its epigenetic state, a finding with potentially important implications for clinical management.

To examine the prognostic power of EPINUC to predict disease recurrence, we applied machine-learning classifiers on the multiparametric data. The best model achieved an AUC of 0.81, and outperformed predictions based on classical prognostic parameters such as stage, grade and subtype (Fig. 5e–g and Extended Data Fig. 11i). To assess reproducibility and to benchmark established assays, MAYO-2 was focused on samples with available OncotypeDX scores74. Despite its smaller size, EPINUC predicted recurrence with an AUC of 0.92 (Fig. 5h). Across patients with OncotypeDX data from both cohorts, scores did not differ between disease-free and relapsed groups, whereas EPINUC retained discriminatory power (Fig. 5i, Extended Data Fig. 11j and Supplementary Fig. 11). Combining assays did not improve performance, which indicated limited added value from OncotypeDX in this setting. Overall, the data suggest that epigenetic signatures of tumour cell subpopulations, such as basal-like cells, may be captured in blood and provide insight into disease biology beyond the static tissue-based prognostic window. Pending larger prospective validation, these findings provide support for the use of EPINUC for early diagnosis and recurrence-risk stratification.

Single-cell studies have revealed clinically relevant phenotypic diversity across cancers, yet the epigenetic mechanisms fuelling this heterogeneity remain poorly understood75,76. Our analysis of luminal breast tumours uncovered tumour cell diversity consistent with previous transcriptomic and proteomic approaches14,21,33,34,54. Quantitative mass-spectrometry also showed differences in global histone modification levels between breast cancer subtypes, which provides support for the idea that chromatin regulates cell identity39,77,78. Altered H3K4 demethylation can drive loss of luminal identity40, which suggests that changes in ER signalling, even without ESR1 mutations, may promote luminal-to-basal plasticity. Here we performed single-cell profiling of histone PTMs in luminal breast tumours. By measuring multiple global chromatin modifications at single-cell resolution, we identified a recurrent epigenetic signature of K5hi basal-like cells across tumours, supported across modalities by single-cell genomic profiling of H3K4 methylation.

Reconstitution of the full heterogeneity spectrum in single-cell-derived MCF7 clones and independently grown PDX tumours provides support for epigenetically driven, interconverting states rather than genetically distinct clones. Subpopulation frequencies varied among patients and may be influenced by mutations that bias state acquisition, as reported in glioblastoma46. This plasticity may enable adaptation to therapeutic pressures, which then promotes resistance and progression. Accordingly, proteomic intratumoural heterogeneity increases with tumour grade independently of genomic heterogeneity, thereby implicating epigenetic mechanisms7. Whether epigenetic therapies can limit plasticity and state transitions remains to be tested.

Breast cancer prognosis depends strongly on early detection, yet accurate screening while minimizing false positives remains challenging79,80,81,82. Detection of molecular changes before clinical progression could guide treatment adaptation and maximize first-line benefit83. Tumour-level histone PTM signatures distinguished luminal breast cancer cell states, whereas EPINUC analysis of pretreatment plasma samples provided proof-of-concept for outcome prediction. Notably, H3K4 methylation marks were elevated in both basal-like tumour cells and in plasma from patients with recurrent disease, which suggests that there is a link between this subpopulation and disease outcome.

Unlike CyTOF, which resolves tumour cell populations, plasma measurements integrate signals from damage of healthy tissue, the tumour microenvironment and local and systemic immune responses29,84. Nevertheless, patient-matched histone PTMs analyses in tumours and blood revealed a positive correlation for most marks, with the highest correlation observed for the recurrence-associated marks H3K4me1 and H3K9ac, which provides support for their prognostic value. Overall, our tissue and plasma chromatin dataset reveals epigenetically driven intratumoural heterogeneity in breast cancer and supports the use of EPINUC as a potential approach for breast cancer diagnosis and prognosis.

For scRNA-seq, we recommend computing on log-normalized counts after library-size normalization and optional regression of technical covariates; for CyTOF, arcsinh or z-score scaled channels work well.

In each tumour, a training set of cells was constructed, including only cells that were successfully classified as luminal, basal-like or cycling, keeping class-distribution and size equal between samples. From each tumour, 80% of these cells were randomly assigned to the training set, and the remaining 20% were used as the validation set. We used an XGBoost model35, with a learning rate of 0.1, a maximum depth of 10 and 1,000 estimators. The model achieved a prediction accuracy of 94% on the validation set. The trained model was subsequently applied to cells that were not assigned to a subpopulation by DBSCAN (cluster –1), and cells predicted with >95% confidence were assigned to the corresponding luminal, basal-like or cycling subpopulation for downstream analyses (Extended Data Fig. 3b). The contribution of each feature to the XGBoost classification was evaluated using SHAP scores30,31, which reflect feature contribution to the classification rather than relative expression levels.

Vendi scores96 were computed based on the epigenetic CyTOF panel (histone PTM and chromatin modifiers). For each sample, cells were stratified by class label. To control for differences in cell number across classes, we repeatedly subsampled m = ⌊n_min/2⌋ cells without replacement from each class, where n_min was the smallest class size in that sample. Marker intensities were first normalized using a global z-score computed across a balanced sample of cells from all datasets. For each rarefaction, epigenetic marker values were discretized into 20 uniform bins, and the normalized dual Vendi score was computed on the resulting cell-by-marker matrix. Reported values correspond to the mean across 5,000 rarefactions, with uncertainty intervals defined by the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the rarefied score distribution.

For each tumour, the most contributing genes that led to accurate classification (around 400 genes, about 90% accuracy) were compared against gene signatures from MSigDB85,116.

Pseudo-bulk coverage tracks were generated by aggregating fragment-level snCUT&Tag data from cells assigned to each cluster. For each sample and histone mark, fragments corresponding to the basal-like cluster and to all remaining clusters were pooled separately. To enable direct comparison, the non-basal-like group was downsampled to match the total fragment count of the basal-like population. Genome-wide coverage was computed from fragment intervals and normalized to counts per million (CPM), producing bedGraph files that were subsequently converted to bigWig format for visualization. Tracks were visualized using pyGenomeTracks.

Pseudobulk chromatin profiles were generated from single-cell data and used for peak calling with MACS3 (ref. 118), with narrow peak mode applied to H3K4me3 and broad peak mode to H3K4me1. Unified peak sets for each sample and histone modification were annotated using ChIPseeker107, and genomic feature distributions were summarized as percentages of total peaks per sample, with promoter subcategories merged before visualization. Differential association of peaks to clusters was evaluated in a one-versus-rest framework. For each peak, signal presence was binarized and detection fractions were compared between the target population (cluster) and remaining cells using a one-sided two-proportion z-test. Effect sizes were computed as log2 fold change of CPM-normalized mean signal. P values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure, and peaks were considered significant at adjusted P ≤ 0.05, log2 fold change ≥ 0.25, and a minimum detection rate of 1%.

Motif enrichment analysis was performed using the Signac framework114 with transcription factor binding profiles obtained from the JASPAR database119. Motifs associated with a pre-defined list of EMT and basal-like state regulators, defined separately for enhancer (H3K4me1) and promoter (H3K4me3) signals, were examined, using a nominal significance threshold of P < 0.05. Multiple testing correction was computed using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure in the subset of tested transcription factors per sample, which reflect the reduced hypothesis space.

To compare the read count with gene activity, for each tumour the number of counts per cell (‘nCount_RNA’) per cell was correlated (Pearson’s) with the epigenomic gene signature score (UCell computed on TFIDF normalized115 data).

Using scGLUE120, we performed multimodal integration of the H3K4me1 snCUT&Tag dataset with the CyTOF dataset. For each of the cell-identity markers present in the CyTOF antibody panel, we identified the relevant gene in the gene activity matrix and used those to form the weakly informative connection for scGLUE. After running scGLUE, we trained an XGBoost classifier on the CyTOF data in the joint latent space to predict the cell label and used the classifier to perform label transfer to the snCUT&Tag modality.

For cell annotation of the H3K9me2 dataset, the complementary H3K4me1 dataset from the same nucleus extraction was used. In brief, 20-kb bin matrices were generated for both H3K4me1 and H3K9me2 datasets, keeping cell labels derived from the H3K4me1 analysis. A classifier was trained on the labelled H3K4me1 dataset, which achieved >95% accuracy, and applied to the inverted z-score matrix of H3K9me2, using the generally opposing genomic distributions of H3K4me1-associated regulatory chromatin and H3K9me2-associated repressive chromatin as an inference framework62,63.

For gene association analysis, H3K9me2 was analysed without peak calling, as this mark forms broad repressive domains rather than sharp focal peaks. Instead, promoter regions (–2 kb to +200 bp relative to the TSS) were tiled into 200 bp windows, and fragment counts were quantified across these promoter-proximal tiles to generate a promoter occupancy matrix. The resulting matrix was normalized using SCTransform115 and used for UMAP embedding and clustering. Because H3K9me2 is a repressive histone mark, high gene-set scores were interpreted as increased repressive occupancy over the corresponding loci rather than increased transcriptional activity.

Blood samples were collected in EDTA tubes and transferred ambient for processing. Plasma was separated by centrifugation and stored at −80 °C.

PEGylated-biotin-coated and PEGylated-poly(T)-coated microscope slides were prepared as previously described28,29. In brief, glass coverslips were cleaned extensively (sonication rounds with acetone and Alconox), hydroxylated by incubation in 1 M KOH and passivated with amino-silanization chemistry (3% 3-aminopropyltriethoxysilane, 5% acetic acid in HPLC ethanol). Surface functionalization along with the first passivation step was performed by applying mPEG: PEGylated-biotin/PEG-azide solution (10:1 ratio). PEGylated surfaces were further passivated with MS(PEG)4. Anchoring of dT50 to PEGylated-poly(T) surfaces was done via click chemistry (10 µM 5′heyxynyl-dT50 (IDT), 2 mM CuSO4).

Antibodies were labelled using a biotin conjugation kit (Abcam, ab201796) and Alexa Flour antibody-labelling kits (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A20181, A20183 and A20186) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Labelled antibodies were further purified by size-exclusion chromatography using Bio-Spin 6 columns (Bio-Rad, 7326200). Antibodies are listed in Supplementary Table 5a.

Quantification of the five protein biomarkers was conducted as previously described28. PEGylated-poly(T)-coated coverslips were assembled into a ChipSop flowcell (chip 2-28 mini Luer platform Fl. 342, 30001180), which generated a 14-lane flowcell. Streptavidin solution (0.2 mg ml–1 in PBS) was added to the surface for 15 min and washed with IMB2 (10 mM MES pH 6.5, 60 mM KCl, 0.32 mM EDTA, 3 mM MgCl2, 10% glycerol, 0.1 mg ml–1 BSA and 0.02% Igepal). Biotinylated antibodies were incubated on the surface in IMB2 for 30 min, followed by washing with IMB2. Next, the plasma sample was added to the flowcell and incubated on the surface for 30 min, followed by three washes with IMB2 to allow binding of target proteins. Fluorescently labelled antibodies (detection antibodies) were introduced to the surface for 60 min, washed with IMB2 and imaged.

Imaging was performed on a TIRF microscope manufactured by Nikon (Eclipse Ti2-E LU-N4 TIRF) with a CFI apochromat TIRF ×60 objective lens and TRF49904, TRF49909 and TRF89902 filter cubes (Chroma) for the 488 mm, 561 mm and 647 nm lasers, respectively. Images were taken with the ×1.5 magnification setting, which resulted in fields of view (FOVs) of 148 µm × 148 µm using an Andor Zyla 4.2 Plus camera. At least 50 FOVs were imaged per lane.

PEGylated-poly(T)-coated coverslips were assembled into an Ibidi flowcell (Sticky Slide VI hydrophobic, IBIDI), generating a 6-lane flowcell and further passivated with 5% BSA for at least 30 min.

Plasma nucleosomes were processed as previously described28 with minor modifications. Nucleosomes were polyA tailed by the following reaction: 20 µl plasma was mixed with 10 µl of 10× Green buffer (Enzymatics), 416 µM CoCl2 (Enzymatics), 1:60 protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma), 83.3 µM dATP (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 5 µl Klenow Fragment (3′→5′ exo-, NEB), 3 µl T4 polynucleotide kinase (NEB) and 4 µl terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT, Enzymatics). Following incubation at 37 °C for 1 h, TdT in the samples was inactivated by the addition of EDTA at final concentration of 5 mM.

Next, plasma samples containing polyA-tailed cell-free nucleosomes were incubated with antibodies (final concentration 1:60) for 30 min at room temperature to allow for the formation of antibody–cell-free nucleosome complexes. Next, samples were loaded on the surface and incubated for 15 min to allow hybridization of polyA tails to dT50 oligonucleotides. The flowcell was washed once with IMB (12 mM HEPES pH 8 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 40 mM Tris pH 7.5 (Gibco), 60 mM KCl (Sigma), 0.32 mM EDTA (Invitrogen), 3 mM MgCl2 (Sigma), 10% glycerol (Bio-Lab), 0.1 mg ml–1 BSA (Sigma) and 0.02% Igepal (Sigma)), and cell-free nucleosomes were visualized by hybridizing 25 pM Cy3-dT23 in IMB to the free portion of polyA tails for 10 min. We supplemented Cy3-dT23 solution with TetraSpeck beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific), used as fiducial markers to allow correction for stage drift in image analysis. The flowcell was washed once with IMB, then filled with IMB, followed by time-lapse imaging of 5 cycles every 15 min with the 3 laser channels across all positions.

Imaging was performed on a TIRF microscope as described above obtaining 100 FOV (148 µm2) images per lane or on a high-throughput single-molecule imaging system at Epivision obtaining 100 FOVs (200 µm2) images per lane. The focus was maintained throughout the run using IR-assisted autofocus system.

Image analysis was performed with the open-source software CellProfiler (v.3.1.9; http://www.cellprofiler.org/) as previously described28. In brief, time-lapse images of antibody-binding events and TetraSpeck beads were aligned, stacked and summed to one image. Antibody spots can be differentiated from TetraSpeck bead spots based on spot size and intensity. Summed antibody images were aligned with cell-free nucleosome images to count colocalization events. For biomarkers analysis, antibody binding events were quantified. To minimize the surface batch effects, signals were normalized to the surface activity (average antibody signal across all lanes of the surface).

PPS analysis on the data was conducted using a previously published algorithm (https://github.com/8080labs/ppscore). In brief, by calculating a cross-validated decision tree (repeated (10,000) 4-fold cross-validation) for the target variable (for example, diagnosis) using only one of the markers, it is possible to determine which of the markers in the datasets contributes most to the target variable.

XGBoost classifier was applied to the data, using all features measured and their combinations. Feature contribution was assessed by SHAP.

For ROC curves, for each classification task, the most important features were selected using the Boruta algorithm (with a random forest classifier)121. An additional feature engineering step was used to generate combinatorial combinations (multiplication, division and SQRT) of the selected features, and another round of feature selection was performed using Boruta. The final features selected were used either in a random forest classifier or in a soft voting classifier consisting of RF, XGBoost and LDA classifiers. The final classifier was used to generate a ROC curve with the associated confidence intervals using 10-fold cross-validation and 1,000 bootstrap repeats.

To enable comparison across independently processed EPINUC cohorts, batch correction was applied to account for differences in antibody labelling degree. In brief, the reference dataset was subsampled to balance group sizes, and feature-wise means and standard deviations were calculated. The independently processed cohort was first standardized and subsequently rescaled to match the mean and variance of the reference dataset. This approach allowed alignment of feature distributions across cohorts while preserving relative differences between samples.

Histones were enriched from FFPE tissues as previously described122. Approximately 4 µg of histone octamer was mixed with an equal amount of heavy-isotope labelled histones, which were used as an internal standard123, and separated on a 17% SDS–PAGE gel. Histone bands were excised, chemically acylated with deuterated acetic anhydride and in-gel digested with trypsin124. The samples were separated by reversed-phase chromatography, acquired on a Q Exactive HF instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and analysed using MaxQuant and QualBrowser, as previously described124. For each histone modified peptide, a percentage relative abundance (%RA) value for the sample (light channel) or the internal standard (heavy channel) was estimated. Light to heavy ratios (log2 transformed) of %RA were then calculated and are reported in Supplementary Table 8.
