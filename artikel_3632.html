Chuck Schumer and Hakeem Jeffries Face Democratic Leadership Revolt After Midterms

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

The latest conflict brewing in the Democratic Party’s cold war between the blue dogs and the insurgent progressive wing is the fight over its next congressional leaders. But with no alternate candidates stepping up to run, it’s hard to determine how real the threats to replace Senator Chuck Schumer and Representative Hakeem Jeffries actually are.

At least six incoming Democratic senators—including Michigan’s Abdul El-Sayed, South Carolina’s Annie Andrews, Minnesota’s Peggy Flanagan, Illinois’s Juliana Stratton, Kansas’s Adam Hamilton, and Maine’s Troy Jackson—have said they won’t support Schumer for majority leader. Michigan senator Elissa Slotkin has called for a “a new generation of leadership.” Even Cory Booker, who praised Schumer for helping engineer last week’s blue wave, stopped short of saying whether he would support him. On Monday, Richard Blumenthal did the same, but walked back his comments later in day, saying he would support Schumer. And Jon Stewart said that the senator “does not understand the urgency of the moment and what the future demands.” Regardless, Schumer is confident. His aides say he has enough votes to become the next majority leader.

In the House, several incoming Democrats—including Claire Valdez, Angela Gonzalez-Torres, Darializa Avila Chevalier, Melat Kiros, Adam Hamawy, Chris Rabb, and Donavan McKinney—have been noncommittal with their support for Jeffries, while Ohio democrat Don Leonard has threatened to seek alternative leadership unless Jeffries changes his position on Israel.

There’s an online effort to prevent Jeffries from becoming Speaker of the House. A change.org petition demanding that House Democrats reject Jeffries as their next Speaker has attracted more than 1 million signatures as of Sunday, with actor Mark Ruffalo among those encouraging people to sign. But scroll to the bottom of the petition, and you’ll find a disclosure: Its creator, Nicole Whitted, is a paid “Change Ambassador” who receives compensation from change.org in connection with the signatures. “Thousands of organizations and brands worldwide run similar programs and in the social-cause space specifically, it’s a way to encourage people with reach and influence to use their voice for causes they care about,” Whitted told Vanity Fair in a statement.

Whitted also said she was traveling to Washington, DC next week to urge congresspeople to vote against Jeffries, and to meet with Representative Ayanna Pressley, whom Whitted told VF she’ll be supporting for Speaker. Pressley’s team did not respond to Vanity Fair’s request for comment sent on Sunday. Neither did her closest congressional allies, Rashida Tlaib, Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, or Ilhan Omar.

Reports indicate that some congresspeople have been flooded with calls from constituents urging them to find new party leadership. But one Democratic aide told Vanity Fair that they’ve noticed what they claim is a coordinated effort to undermine Jeffries on social media, including AI-generated videos attacking Jeffries that have gotten tens of thousands of engagements.

The Brooklyn congressman does have good relationships with members of both the moderate “blue dogs” and the party’s progressive flank. Ocasio-Cortez has already backed him, and last month, Representative Ro Khanna declared that “Hakeem Jeffries is going to be Speaker.” “Don’t believe what you’re seeing on the internet,” the aide said. “It’s not reality.”

Schumer’s allies, meanwhile, have been reminding skeptics of the work he did to deliver the Democratic majority. The New York senator helped recruit candidates including Roy Cooper and Sherrod Brown, while the Democratic political operation supporting Senate candidates spent lavishly on competitive races. According to final figures provided to Vanity Fair, Senate Majority PAC and its affiliates spent nearly $407 million across races in eight states, including $150 million in Ohio, $67 million apiece in Iowa and Michigan, $65 million in Maine, and $40 million in Texas, all of which flipped. Another $14 million went toward Kansas, with smaller investments in Mississippi and South Carolina—$2.2 million and $750,000 respectively. Some of that money helped elect candidates who are now skeptical about whether Schumer should stay in charge.

In the House, each party will choose its leadership this month, but in order to become Speaker, a candidate still needs to get a majority of the entire chamber, usually 218 members, to vote for them when the new Congress convenes in January. It’s a little easier in the Senate, where the Democrat will only need to win a simple majority of their own caucus in an anonymous early December vote to become majority leader.

El-Sayed has suggested Maryland senator Chris Van Hollen, although a source close to Van Hollen told Vanity Fair that he isn’t planning to run, instead setting his sights on a potential presidential campaign in 2028. “Prior to selecting a majority leader, Senator Van Hollen believes the caucus needs to have an honest and open conversation about how we move an agenda forward that reflects the will of the American people,” says a Democratic aide familiar with the senator’s thinking. “He’s made clear that Democrats need to use their power to immediately end the illegal war with Iran, advance a robust pro-worker kitchen table agenda, and fight political corruption.”

Another name circulating among Democratic aides is Hawaii senator Brian Schatz, a relatively young progressive who has developed relationships across the party’s ideological factions and is already pursuing the number-two leadership position being vacated by retiring senator Dick Durbin.

“He’s the heir apparent,” one Democratic aide told Vanity Fair, describing Schatz as someone who could appeal to both progressives and moderates. Asked about speculation surrounding his leadership ambitions, Schatz’s spokesperson offered a noncommittal response: “I don’t have anything new to share on this but will keep you posted if that changes.”

Some Democrats are also hesitant to replace their experienced leaders after they’ve been given this narrow opportunity to get stuff done with control of both chambers of Congress. “I don’t think you’d want somebody new who’s, like, learning the ropes while we have the opportunity to really get meaningful legislation passed for the American people,” another Democratic aide said.

Still, the fighting online and on cable news exposes a tension within the Democratic Party. Moderates see the blue wave as vindication of the party’s disciplined, affordability-focused strategy. Progressives see it as a mandate for a more ambitious agenda—and a rejection of the establishment’s familiar faces.

Both factions are claiming the election as their own victory. But before they can fight over what to do with their new power, they’ll have to decide who’s going to lead them.
