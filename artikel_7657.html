BigLaw partners with Big Tech to design and deploy custom AI tools

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

In recent months, Big Tech has invested heavily in law.

On Oct. 15, Microsoft unveiled Copilot for legal, which builds on what it previously introduced as its Legal Agent in Word. In September, OpenAI debuted Astra for Law: GPT-6 Astra, a new model that is tailored for professional legal work. One month earlier, Google announced Gemini Enterprise for Legal. And in May, Anthropic introduced Claude for Legal.

At the same time, several BigLaw firms have increased their investment in generative artificial intelligence. Rather than just using these platforms, they are working with major tech providers to build their own custom AI tools. As part of these partnerships, they receive the resources needed to design specific solutions that fit their workflows, while OpenAI and others see firsthand how AI is shaping the legal practice.

Sullivan & Cromwell was one firm that teamed up with OpenAI to develop a new tool and announced its collaboration at the same time as the Astra for Law model. Called the Agreement Analyzer, it integrates the firm’s workflows with OpenAI’s ChatGPT Enterprise and analyzes agreements using select precedents and negotiating playbooks from the firm or its clients.

The tool provides a more thorough analysis of those materials than any standard large language model and not only identifies potential issues, but includes rationales for them and ways to address them, says John Savva, a partner at Sullivan & Cromwell who created the Agreement Analyzer and worked with OpenAI to refine it.

Sullivan & Cromwell’s lawyers already used ChatGPT, so teaming up with OpenAI seemed like a logical next step, Savva says.

“They were looking for law firms to collaborate with to see what law firms and OpenAI working together could develop and what potential that would hold for the future,” Savva says. “Since we already had prototypes in hand, it was natural for us to see what we could do to further develop those with OpenAI.”

Initially, the Agreement Analyzer could be used by one individual at a time, but OpenAI engineers helped Savva modify it so that it could be more broadly available in just a few months, he says. An initial trial group is testing and providing feedback on the tool, which will eventually be rolled out to the entire firm.

“It’s really shown us that you can take a tool very rapidly from a draft prototype to something that could be deployed through a collaboration like this,” Savva says.

Ropes & Gray was another early partner of OpenAI. Ahead of Astra for Law’s launch, the firm worked with OpenAI to develop an AI-powered tool that speeds up the diligence review process.

The tool replicates the exact steps the firm’s attorneys take when they dig into large volumes of documents, including identifying risks and critical issues, and it produces a comprehensive diligence report in just hours. With those reports quickly in hand, attorneys can spend more time providing strategic counsel to clients before transactions.

Currently in the testing phase, the tool is consistently providing better results than other off-the-shelf diligence applications, says Josh Lichtenstein, a partner at Ropes & Gray who helps deploy AI at the firm.

“What the process looked like for us was a combination of the engineering expertise from OpenAI plus our own vision as lawyers of how a tool should work,” says Lichtenstein, who noted Ropes & Gray began working with the tech provider about three months ago. “And it was really the combination of the two that led to, I think, a much better outcome than we would have gotten otherwise.”

In addition to OpenAI, Ropes & Gray speaks regularly with Anthropic, Google and other tech providers about new AI tools and how to incorporate AI in their work, says Howard Glazer, a partner and head of practice AI at the firm.

“We will talk to anyone,” Glazer says. “As a firm, we are total believers that AI will transform the industry, and so we are intent on being at the front of the wave.”

These partnerships are mutually advantageous, he adds. While tech providers learn more about the inner workings of the legal profession, Ropes & Gray is creating cutting-edge solutions that ultimately benefit its clients.

As another example, the firm partnered with AI-powered contract drafting company Draftwise to deploy its legal ontology platform within its private funds practice. It connects and structures partnership agreements, side letters and other insights from the firm’s fund formation work and allows lawyers to access and leverage the collective experience to enhance negotiations.

“Where it adds the most value for clients, we forge partnerships to build capabilities around our own methods and experience,” Glazer says. “Our partnerships with OpenAI and Draftwise tie back to our broader AI strategy—building AI around our clients’ needs and our relationships with them, and using that knowledge to amplify our lawyers’ judgment.”

Among other firms partnering with Big Tech is Freshfields. The London-based firm announced in April that it would collaborate with Anthropic to deliver AI-native legal services as well as create novel AI legal workflows.

“Our approach in the Freshfields Lab has always been to build on the best available technology. Claude’s capabilities have become an essential part of our proprietary AI-powered solutions,” said Gerrit Beckhaus, partner and co-head of Freshfields Lab, in a statement. “With this collaboration, we are going further: co-developing agentic workflows with Anthropic that can handle multistep legal tasks end-to-end. For our clients, that translates into faster, more precise and more scalable legal services.”

There is plenty of room for bespoke AI tools in the legal profession, says Savva, who expects to see more as the delivery of legal services continues to evolve.

“Firms will, I think, frequently want to develop a tool that differentiates their approach from those of others,” Savva says. “Just as lawyers and firms have differentiated approaches, the tools will naturally have differentiated approaches as well.”
