Billionaire Stanley Druckenmiller Dumped Google Parent Alphabet in Favor of Another Trillion-Dollar AI Giant

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Duquesne Family Office’s billionaire boss swapped one trillion-dollar artificial intelligence (AI) hyperscaler for another.

Although earnings season -- the six-week period when most S&P 500 (^GSPC -0.32%) companies report their quarterly operating results -- garners most of the glory on Wall Street, Form 13F filing season can be just as valuable. A 13F allows investors to track which stocks Wall Street's savviest money managers bought and sold in the latest quarter.

Billionaire Stanley Druckenmiller of Duquesne Family Office is an asset manager with a lengthy track record of outperforming Wall Street's benchmark stock indexes. According to Duquesne's newest 13F, he sent shares of Google parent Alphabet (GOOGL -0.23%)(GOOG -0.29%) to the chopping block in the third quarter, while at the same time piling into Elon Musk's Space Exploration Technologies (SPCX +2.14%), better known as SpaceX.

Duquesne Family Office's billionaire boss has been a busy bee. Image source: Getty Images.

Duquesne Family Office's 13F shows that Druckenmiller sold all 336,300 Class A (GOOGL) Alphabet shares, worth more than $120 million as of June 30, during the September-ended quarter.

Profit-taking is the most logical explanation for this selling, but it may not be the only one.

For instance, Druckenmiller is an active trader. The average top-20 holding in Duquesne's investment portfolio has been held for less than seven months. This shows that its billionaire boss isn't afraid to ring the register when the opportunity presents itself.

But historical precedent and valuation may have also played a role in Stanley Druckenmiller's decision to dump his fund's entire position in Alphabet.

In a May 2024 interview with CNBC, he claimed, "Artificial intelligence (AI) might be a little overhyped now, but underhyped long term." Having invested over several decades, Druckenmiller likely recognizes that every major tech innovation has needed time to mature.

Furthermore, Alphabet's otherworldly spending on the AI infrastructure build-out resulted in the company reporting negative free cash flow (FCF) for the September-ended quarter. Though Alphabet still possesses a rock-solid moat in internet search and is delivering jaw-dropping sales growth from Google Cloud, a short-term shift to negative FCF may have left Druckenmiller skittish about the company's near-term prospects.

At the other end of the spectrum, Duquesne's billionaire chief scooped up 202,125 shares of SpaceX, worth over $30 million at the end of September.

The lure of SpaceX is its leadership as a space infrastructure and AI hyperscaling giant. The company's prospectus pointed to a $28.5 trillion addressable market, $26.5 trillion of which traces back to its AI services. SpaceX has lined up several graphics processing unit-leasing deals, indicating there's substantial demand for its services.

While SpaceX broke records with its initial public offering, it's anything but a safe or surefire investment.

For example, SpaceX's corporate bond prices have been sinking almost precipitously since their issuance in June. Bond prices sink when traders become worried about a company's ability to repay its debt obligations. Musk's company has yet to demonstrate that its operating model is time-tested.

Furthermore, SpaceX arguably represents the greatest fleecing of retail investors in Wall Street's storied history. Rather than employing the typical 180-calendar-day lockup period for insiders, Musk's company is using a staggered and accelerated lockup schedule that allows some high-ranking executives and early investors to cash out well before the 180-day mark.

It's hard to argue with Druckenmiller's track record of outperformance, but SpaceX is rife with red flags.
