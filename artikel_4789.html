When Therapy Forgets What It Means to Be Human

Posted November 19, 2026 | Reviewed by Margaret Foley

These days, when you hear mental health professionals say that they want clients to understand their conditions better, they are usually referring to diagnostic labels. It is a shame how much mental health conditions have been reduced to labels.

If you want to understand why you are feeling down, the thinking goes, you need to understand depression. If you are nervous about things that are happening in your life, you need to understand anxiety. If you are having difficulties in social situations, you need to understand autism. And if your mood tends to fluctuate easily, you need to understand bipolar disorder.

What makes this even more troubling is how much these conditions are reduced not just to labels, but to labels related only to physical functioning. When it comes to mental health issues, the questions are often reduced to labels, and human beings are reduced to physiological machines.

It is almost as if people are treated as robots. Understand what is going wrong with a robot’s mechanics, and you can fix it.

But what if your psychological condition is just part of being human?

What if your depression is due to a lack of meaning or purpose in life? What if your anxiety is due to a very real fear caused by life being uncertain? What if your difficulties with people are due to the negative ways people act toward you, or uncertainty because social interactions do not come with any sort of guidebook? And what if your fluctuating mood is related to recognizing that you have a limited time on earth and are trying to find the best ways of navigating obstacles toward making that time meaningful?

For example, think about someone who comes to counseling feeling depressed because they spent years working toward goals that no longer seem meaningful. They may be having difficulty sleeping, have little motivation, and feel sad much of the time. Those symptoms might justify a depression diagnosis, but does that diagnosis really explain what the person is experiencing?

Understanding the person's struggle with meaning and purpose may be much more important than simply saying they have depression and need to understand that diagnosis better. Saying “you’re searching for more meaning and purpose in your life” fits more with what it means to be human than saying “you have depression.”

It is unfortunate that authors, artists, and philosophers recognize that being human involves functioning on multiple deep levels, but psychologists and counselors often do not.

Life is difficult. Life is complex. Navigating that complexity can be difficult, troubling, and emotionally taxing. Feeling like something is missing, something is lost, something is difficult to navigate, or something is meaningless can be a very real source of emotional distress.

Yet mental health professionals may not recognize this.

Medical thinking and pharmaceutical companies seem to dominate mental healthcare to a large degree, and this can lead to minimizing or abandoning altogether the idea that human beings can experience true emotional distress because they are looking for their lives to have direction, meaning, and purpose and just cannot find it.

None of this means that physiology, neurology, or medication should be ignored. It means that understanding these things is not the same as understanding a human being.

If you are seeking a therapist or counselor, this is something to keep in mind. Does the therapist or counselor reduce your suffering to just physiology or something wrong with your body or neurology? Do they seem to act primarily as a cheerleader for pharmaceutical companies, where the answer keeps coming back to medication? Or do they truly look at the complexities of what you are facing in your life?

If there is a lot of focus in your therapy or counseling on physiology and medication, this does not mean that your counselor or therapist is doing something wrong. But it may be worth bringing up. Asking your therapist or counselor whether there is room to discuss deeper questions in life can be an important step for moving therapy in a more helpful direction. It is important to consider whether you are truly being treated as a human being and not just a machine.

If we compare ourselves to machines, we know that human beings function on a much more complex level. Machines do not worry about meaning or purpose in their lives. Machines may recognize that they have an expiration date, but they do not worry about getting certain things done within that timeframe. Machines understand the tasks ahead of them and work on those tasks until the time they shut down, but they do not worry about what it means if they do not get those tasks done.

Human beings worry about living purposeful lives and filling their lives with meaning. Human beings feel lost when things that seem important are missing from their lives. Machines do not. They may recognize that things are missing as a fact, but they do not have any sort of emotional response to it.

Saying that, as a human being, you are not a machine may sound like a cliché. But it is really unfortunate when you look at how often human beings are treated as machines.

As a human being, it may often be up to you to speak up and say, "Treat me as a human, not as a machine."
