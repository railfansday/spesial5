It’s not every day that an email makes international news before toppling global stock markets and beheading the organizational chart of one of tech's household names. But if you’re Jensen Huang admitting to at least one extramarital affair to all 40,000-plus employees of Nvidia, it all makes too much sense.

Since the story broke about the married-with-children billionaire CEO behind the world’s most powerful AI-chip company sleeping with not only an alleged Chinese spy, but also an employee, the internet has gone giddy with memes, commentary, and endless speculation about what goes on inside closed rooms–bedrooms and boardrooms alike. I’ll leave the economists to make predictions about the future of the chip market without its leading company's CEO at the helm, and politicians to quibble about what this means for U.S.-China ties going forward. As a psychologist, though, I think now is a good time to revisit the long-established predictors of marital fidelity, which is less of a black box and more of a formula.

Much investigative journalism has gone into the dark and mysterious convergence of events leading to Huang meeting and subsequently falling for Alice Huang, the purported Chinese operative, as well as the more quotidian circumstances that led to his affair with Kimberly Powell, a subordinate executive. But as much as extenuating situations can promote infidelity, personality matters too.

A while back, researchers conducted a series of studies to show just how well a person’s humility and honesty predicted their capacity to lie in their romantic relationships. Tech billionaires are known for many things, but humility is not one of them. Curiously, the research on humility and relationship dishonesty also found that proud/dishonest people should also be more likely to suspect their relationship partners’ dishonesty (thanks to our tendency to project ourselves onto others). But in the Jensen case, he appeared to defy this trend when he failed to be suspicious of his alleged spy-mistress.

Of course, the personality traits that made Jensen into both a billionaire and a straying husband are only part of the equation. The situation always matters, and the link between power and cheating has long been established. Time and again, studies have shown that people who naturally have more power or are temporarily made to feel more powerful are more likely to cheat on their relationship partners.

While this trend is based in part of the finding that power makes us more impulsive, likely to act, self-focused, and psychologically distant from other people, it’s also moderated by beliefs. Because people like to validate themselves, power seems to have these negative effects on relationship fidelity only when the person has positive thoughts about cheating. For those who think of cheating negatively, power doesn’t make them more likely to be unfaithful.

Money has a similar effect. In one study, men who were made to feel richer were less satisfied with their partner’s physical attractiveness and more likely to approach an attractive woman. Other research has found that being extremely financially focused in general predicts lower relationship satisfaction.

There is at least one more layer to the latest Nvidia scandal: the organizational context. A researcher once called affairs an “occupational hazard” because they were more likely in some organizations than others. Relative to people in other jobs, those in higher administrative positions, and in technology, were the least likely to say sex with someone other than your spouse was always wrong. This is also consistent with findings from AshleyMadison, the dating site for married people interested in affairs, showing that the top industry for their users was IT and Engineering.

Finally, something has to be said about the fact that the Jensen affairs were years in the making, and so the fact that the world is only finding out about it now suggests collusion has been happening this whole time. (We can't speculate on if or when his wife became aware of his infidelity.) Psychologists call this collective cheating. While the original work on collective cheating focused on other forms of dishonesty than the relational kind, the idea was the same: Sometimes our concern for the group makes us turn a blind eye, and that is at least part of the problem.
