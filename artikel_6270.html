How air pollution contributes to lung cancer even in people who have never smoked

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

A new review explores how fine particles in polluted air may fuel lung cancer by creating conditions that help cells with existing mutations grow.

Tiny airborne particles can encourage lung cancer by changing the conditions around cells that already carry harmful mutations.

A new review explains how inflammation helps this happen, including in people who have never smoked.

The study was led by Kezhong Chen at Peking University People’s Hospital. The international team organizes existing evidence into 12 features spanning cancer’s beginnings, growth, and spread.

The link with air pollution is well established. The International Agency for Research on Cancer classifies outdoor air pollution and its particulate component as carcinogenic to humans.

Much of the review focuses on PM2.5, particles measuring 2.5 micrometers or less across. Their size allows them to reach deep into the lungs.

Several cohort studies linked each additional 10 micrograms of PM2.5 per cubic meter to a 7–16% higher lung cancer rate. These estimates describe associations across populations, rather than an individual’s chance of developing cancer.

Pollution also varies in composition. Particles from traffic, coal burning, and other sources carry different mixtures of metals and organic chemicals.

Cancer-driving mutations can exist in apparently healthy lung tissue without immediately producing a tumor. What happens around those cells may help determine whether they become dangerous.

In mouse experiments discussed in the review, PM2.5 drew immune cells called macrophages into the lungs. These cells released interleukin-1 beta, an inflammatory signal.

That environment changed the behavior of alveolar type 2 cells, which help maintain the lung’s air sacs. Cells carrying an EGFR mutation acquired a more immature, progenitor-like state that enabled tumor formation.

This gives pollution a role beyond causing fresh DNA damage. It can also encourage the expansion of cells that already carry mutations.

The same particles can also damage DNA and interfere with its repair. Both routes may contribute, depending on exposure and the cells involved.

The authors arrange their 12 features across three stages: initiation, progression, and metastasis.

Early changes include vulnerable lung cells, DNA damage, and altered control of gene activity. Persistent inflammation helps create conditions in which abnormal cells can survive.

As tumors develop, the framework describes changes in metabolism, blood-vessel growth, and immune defenses. These changes can help cancer cells obtain resources and escape attack.

Later mechanisms may help tumor cells move through tissue and enter the circulation.

Experimental evidence also suggests pollution can alter distant organs, potentially making them more welcoming to spreading cancer.

The strength of evidence differs between these mechanisms. Many links still need to be tested directly in human lung tissue.

The review describes clinical patterns that deserve closer investigation, particularly among never-smokers in East Asia.

In one Taiwanese screening study, more than 80% of positive findings involved ground-glass or partly solid nodules. These appear hazy on scans, rather than entirely solid.

Nearly 18% of affected patients had multiple primary lung cancers. That means separate tumors arose independently, rather than one cancer simply spreading.

Molecular studies also point to more frequent EGFR mutations and fewer mutations overall than in smokers’ tumors. Some show changes associated with APOBEC enzymes and lack the familiar tobacco-related signature called SBS4.

None of these features can, by itself, identify pollution as the cause of someone’s cancer.

Exposure alone does not explain everyone’s vulnerability. Older adults generally showed stronger associations between particulate pollution and lung cancer.

The review also describes evidence that inherited susceptibility matters. UK Biobank findings linked higher genetic risk and greater pollution exposure with increased vulnerability.

Yet the research map poorly matches the pollution map. South Asia, Southeast Asia, and Africa carry substantial burdens, while much of the evidence comes from Europe and North America.

Differences in fuels, housing, workplaces, and access to care make it difficult to assume findings transfer unchanged between regions.

The review gathers evidence from earlier studies; it does not validate a new diagnostic test. No established PM-specific biomarker can yet identify affected patients.

Human studies must disentangle pollution from occupation, indoor smoke, and socioeconomic circumstances. Those exposures often overlap.

Laboratory conditions present another problem. Some animal studies place unusually large doses directly into the airways, while short experiments poorly represent years of exposure.

Flat cell cultures also miss the lung’s architecture and interactions between different cell types. The authors want more realistic exposure models, longer follow-up and detailed analysis of human tissue.

The team also wants better records of personal exposure, using tools such as wearable sensors alongside neighborhood pollution measurements.

Many screening programs prioritize smoking history. In the United States, Preventive Services Task Force recommendations target certain adults with substantial smoking histories.

The authors argue that pollution exposure should help inform future risk models, alongside age and other factors.

Establishing who benefits from screening will still require evidence that balances earlier detection against unnecessary investigations.

“We wanted to trace the multiple layers of that pathway and to give early screening and intervention for high-risk groups a scientific basis,” the authors concluded.

The study is published in the journal The Innovation.
