Ending a romantic relationship can be heartbreaking. Whether you initiated the breakup or were dumped by the love of your life, most people experience some distress when a romantic relationship ends. This leads many people living through a painful breakup to ask: How long will my heartbreak last?

For some people, post-breakup symptoms are mild in intensity and subside over time. In a longitudinal study of 156 young adults assessed before, during, and after a romantic breakup, for example, Acolin and colleagues (2023) found an increase in depressive symptoms immediately following a breakup that generally returned to pre-breakup levels within three months.

However, others experience intense, impairing, longer-lasting symptoms that are sometimes associated with depression, anxiety, and other diagnosable mental health conditions (Field, 2025). In an important longitudinal study completed over 30 weeks, Verhallen and colleagues (2022) examined depressive symptoms following a romantic breakup in 87 women. Results yielded four distinct trajectories for post-breakup symptoms: a ‘resilience’ group who recovered most easily; a ‘fast recovery’ group who experienced distress but recovered; a ‘slow recovery’ group who improved but continued to struggle; and a ‘chronic distress’ group who did not improve significantly over time.

Not all romantic breakups will result in intense distress and some individuals are more vulnerable to symptoms than others (Dailey et al., 2024; Farber et al., 2024; Fernandes et al., 2025; Gehl et al., 2024). Many factors can influence the intensity of post-breakup symptoms including:

Although short-term post-dissolution distress is common for anyone going through a breakup, some people struggle with more intense, longer-lasting symptoms. Having a positive view of one’s ex; living together or having plans for a future; having poor coping skills, lower self-esteem, or an insecure attachment style; being youngerr; and being left (versus leaving) tend to make symptoms worse.

The good news is that breakups also provide an opportunity for growth, learning, and self-exploration. In a study of 1846 adults in the United States recruited to explore how psychotherapy facilitates coping, for example, Farber and colleagues (2024) found that 74.6% of the sample attended psychotherapy after a breakup and found it moderately to very helpful. Getting social support by talking to friends, ending contact with the ex, engaging in hobbies or work, finding a good therapist, and writing or journaling about the breakup experience facilitates post-breakup recovery and healing.
