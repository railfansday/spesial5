What came before land plants? New genetic clues reveal a complicated story

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

A new genetic study traces the complex history of land plants’ closest algal relatives, revealing repeated shifts from single cells to filaments.

Every tree, flower, and blade of grass on Earth belongs to a lineage that evolved from freshwater algae roughly 500 million years ago.

Reconstructing what those ancient ancestors looked like means looking closely at their living relatives.

New genetic evidence suggests that the common ancestor of land plants’ closest living algal relatives was a single cell.

Within that algal group, filamentous growth evolved independently at least seven times. The findings reveal a branching history in which multicellular forms arose repeatedly.

The study was led by researchers at the University of Göttingen.

The group in question is the Zygnematophyceae. This is a class of freshwater algae whose simple appearance once made them seem unlikely candidates for land plants’ closest living algal relatives.

Some are just single cells. Others form filaments.

The most famous is Spirogyra, that green slime recognizable by its spiral chloroplasts winding through each cell.

For years, more complex-looking algae like Chara and Coleochaete seemed like better candidates for land plant ancestors. Genomics flipped that assumption on its head.

Maaike Bierenbroodspot conducted the research during her PhD at Göttingen and is now a postdoc at the University of Maastricht.

“Many zygnematophytes look deceptively simple, but their genomes reveal a remarkably deep and complex evolutionary history and diversity,” said Bierenbroodspot.

“We can begin to see evolutionary events that remain invisible when we study only a handful of model species.”

Previous phylogenomic work on zygnematophytes had relied on just a handful of genomes. To fill the gaps, the team generated 43 new transcriptomes.

These capture RNA produced by genes active under the sampled conditions.

Combined with existing data, the final dataset covered 104 species and 2,243 genetic loci.

The resulting family tree divides the Zygnematophyceae into five orders: Desmidiales, Spirogloeales, Zygnematales, Spirogyrales, and Serritaeniales.

One of the most striking features is the branch leading to modern Spirogyra species. It is 6.2 times longer than the branch to its sister group, the Desmidiales.

That extreme genetic distance hints at either massive extinction within Spirogyrales, vast unsampled diversity still lurking in freshwater habitats, or dramatically accelerated rates of molecular evolution.

The team then used the family tree to reconstruct the likely appearance of the common ancestor of all zygnematophytes.

The analysis strongly favored a single-celled ancestor.

Filamentous growth then evolved independently multiple times across the tree. The researchers count at least seven separate transitions from single-celled to filamentous body plans.

That number gets even more interesting when you consider that not all filaments are built the same way.

Spirogyra and Zygnema form what biologists call bona fide filaments – cells dividing and staying connected in true multicellular threads.

But desmids, which are typically unicellular, sometimes form “chain-like filaments.” These are individual cells that simply remain stuck together end-to-end after dividing.

The distinction matters. These are fundamentally different solutions to the problem of becoming bigger than a single cell.

The genomes also revealed something profound about how land plants assembled their molecular toolkit.

Genes once thought to be exclusively plant inventions turn up scattered across the zygnematophyte tree.

This happened in a patchy, lineage-specific pattern telling a story of dynamic gain and loss rather than steady accumulation.

“These algae have genes that are part of what we call the ‘land plant heritage,’” said Cäcilia Kunz from the University of Göttingen.

“They show that some of the molecular components associated with important plant functions have much deeper evolutionary histories than the land plants themselves.”

“At the same time, their distribution across algal lineages shows that this evolutionary toolkit was dynamic.”

Take the PYR/PYL/RCAR genes, which in land plants encode receptors for abscisic acid – the stress hormone that helps plants survive drought.

These genes show up in Mesotaenium and Zygnema but are absent from desmids like Penium and Closterium.

That suggests they were present in the common ancestor but lost in certain lineages.

Or consider CAD – cinnamyl alcohol dehydrogenase – a key enzyme in the phenylpropanoid pathway that ultimately produces lignin, the molecule that makes wood hard.

CAD homologs turned up across the zygnematophyte tree. Yet, none fell into the classes typically found in land plants.

Instead, they formed their own distinct groupings, with protein structure predictions suggesting they function more like sinapyl alcohol dehydrogenases.

The findings suggest that related enzymes existed before land plants, although their functions may have differed.

The histidine kinases tell a similar story. These ancient signaling proteins, which in land plants help perceive light and the hormone cytokinin, have undergone massive expansion in zygnematophytes.

PAS-domain-containing histidine kinases – involved in light sensing – turned out to be particularly abundant and diverse.

The study represents the most comprehensive phylogenomic framework for zygnematophytes to date, but the authors are the first to acknowledge its limits.

The dataset covers 104 species out of more than 4,000 described, leaving much of the group’s diversity unexplored.

Within Zygnema alone, molecular clock analysis places the divergence between two species at 236 million years ago, underscoring just how ancient these lineages are.

“Our study gives us a much better map of the evolutionary landscape surrounding the origin of land plants,” said Jan de Vries from the University of Göttingen.

“The more diversity we include, the more we see that evolution was not a simple progression from single-celled algae to increasingly complex plants.”

“Instead, different lineages experimented with different body plans and molecular solutions over hundreds of millions of years.”

“Understanding this diversity is crucial if we want to reconstruct the biology of the algal ancestors from which land plants emerged.”

The study is published in the journal PLOS Biology.
