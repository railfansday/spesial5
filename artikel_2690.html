Misery Index: Big Ten's reign at the top of college football might be over after rash of QB injuries

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

In college football, bragging rights are almost worth as much as trophies. Yes, after an eight-year drought (which was preceded by an 11-year drought), winning three straight national championships was a huge deal for the Big Ten. But while the hunk of metal that Michigan (2023), Ohio State (2024) and Indiana (2025) got to take home is nice to look at, the element that's unique to college football is being able to tell the SEC "We're better than you" and having a scoreboard back it up.

At least at the top of the league, the Big Ten was better than the SEC for three straight seasons. Winning the College Football Playoff with three different programs, while the SEC wasn't even represented in the championship game, made it inarguable.

It was fresh and exciting for a league that couldn't hold a candle to the SEC's success in the modern era.

But now, thanks to a crazy run of quarterback injuries, it's over.

Yes, the Big Ten will have a representative in this year's CFP. But after UCLA's expensive loss to Michigan and Indiana's puzzling performance at Washington, there's now a scenario where the Big Ten is a one-bid league and doesn't even come within sniffing distance of the championship game.

As we sit here after the penultimate week of the regular season, it's fair to say the Big Ten's four best teams this season were Oregon, Ohio State, Indiana and UCLA. All four will end the season with major quarterback problems that almost certainly will crush their chances of a deep run.

On Sept. 26, Oregon's Heisman contender Dante Moore suffered a concussion after a dirty hit by USC's Desman Stephens and hasn't played since. Oregon went into a nosedive and fired the offensive coordinator but has played better since. At 8-3 and ranked No. 13, however, the Ducks have a very narrow path to a playoff bid.

Near the end of Ohio State's 19-15 win at Indiana on Oct. 17, Julian Sayin took an awkward hit that caused a significant arm injury. Freshman Luke Fahey wasn't ready to take his place, but the Buckeyes' problems are deeper than one player. They've spiraled on both sides of the ball and are completely out of the mix now at 7-4 after a 31-28 loss at Nebraska.

Indiana's Josh Hoover has been banged up for much of the season, and at times backup Grant Wilson has looked more effective. But in Indiana's 27-24 loss at Washington, Curt Cignetti played both and didn't get great results from either. At 9-2, Indiana's problem is that USC remains its best win. The committee will likely punish them for that in next week's rankings.

And now UCLA, which has been the darling of the sport this year, might be down to its third quarterback after Ty Dieffenbach was carted off late in an 18-16 triple-overtime loss to Michigan. If UCLA loses to USC next week to finish 10-2, its playoff prospects could be very much in doubt.

It's getting so desperate in the Big Ten that the school's best shot to win a fourth straight national title might be … Iowa? And the Hawkeyes, ranked No. 15 this week, would probably need to win the Big Ten to get in — which would require the perfect combination of results next week just for them to qualify for that championship game.

This is mostly just a string of bad luck for the Big Ten. But it's also a trend we should probably get used to now that these teams will have to play 10 power conference games to qualify for the 24-team playoff beginning next year.

UCLA coach Bob Chesney suffered his first loss at the school against Michigan on Saturday. Joe Robbins/Icon Sportswire via Getty Images)

Because of how talent has been spread around in the NIL era, teams are struggling with depth more than they ever have while also playing a more physically demanding schedule. That combination leads to a cascade of injuries. For instance, if you have a decimated offensive line, your quarterback and running backs are going to be more at risk. It's all connected, and winning a national title will be surviving a war of attrition.

The Big Ten, it seems, is poised to lose that war as the 2026 season winds down. Whether it's Oregon and Ohio State getting knocked off the national title path or UCLA struggling to hang onto its once-in-a-lifetime dream season, the Big Ten's reign is coming to a luckless end.

It's rare that the Misery Index honors an entire conference rather than one team, but the circumstances of this season have been so spectacularly fluky and frustrating that America's most miserable fan base for Week 12 is anyone associated with the Big Ten conference.

ACC: The future of Bill Belichick will be a fascinating topic as the season winds down. At one point, it truly looked like the Tar Heels were turning the corner, playing a better brand of football and showing proof of concept for what Belichick-style football could be at the college level. But what does it all amount to at the end? After a 41-24 loss at Virginia, North Carolina is 9-14 under Belichick overall and needs to beat NC State next week to become bowl eligible. The Tar Heels will probably get that done and be able to claim some progress, but the 74-year-old Belichick was not hired to build slowly. Even under the best of circumstances, you've got a short window with the most successful NFL coach of all time, and every year that passes without a major breakthrough will naturally lead to questions about whether North Carolina should just start all over again. If you're in the mood for generosity, the Tar Heels lost to Louisville by two, to Miami by three, to Syracuse by one in overtime and to Clemson by eight. If they just make a couple more plays in two of those games, everything looks different. But when you zoom out and ask if the Belichick gambit was worth its time, North Carolina can't argue it's paid off at all yet. Whether Belichick should back for Year 3 is an interesting question. If North Carolina feels close enough to give it one last try, Belichick should get that opportunity. But if this mediocrity is what it's going to be like in 2027, why waste everyone's time?

Big 12: Maybe the only consistent theme of the Deion Sanders era at Colorado is how often the head coach comes into the press room after losses and talks about how his team is so much better than what it says on the scoreboard. "This is a good, solid football team," Sanders said after a 35-28 loss at Cincinnati. "I know the record is not indicative of that, and it's disappointing and I don't even understand … it don't seem like to me the better team was victorious, but they were because they did what they needed to do." Enough of this — not just the refusal to acknowledge reality but the entire Sanders era. He's had four years. At 4-7, Colorado is guaranteed to finish with a losing record in three of them. Colorado has lost 10 straight on the road in conference play. If this were anyone but Coach Prime, we would already be speculating about who would replace him. But Sanders has generated so much attention and money for Colorado athletics that it will be difficult to cut the cord — particularly with a buyout in the $25 million range. It's a shame for college football that Sanders has become kind of your garden-variety Big 12 coach because the 2024 season was truly electric. But without Shedeur Sanders at quarterback and Travis Hunter doing everything on both sides of the ball, it's a pretty underwhelming and uninteresting product. The entire point of Deion Sanders was to be different, but a lot of coaches could lose seven or eight games a year at Colorado.

Colorado coach Deion Sanders didn't have many answers after his team's latest loss to Cincinnati. (Ian Johnson/Getty Images)

SEC: Though not surprising given the way LSU's season has gone, it is absolutely stunning in the big picture to look at the SEC standings and see the Tigers much closer to the bottom of the league than the top. College football is crazy right now, with most teams having a collection of good wins and bad losses that make it hard to pinpoint exactly what they are. LSU, however, has made it simple in Lane Kiffin's first year. They're the definition of mediocre. LSU has beaten Clemson, Louisiana Tech, Texas A&M, McNeese State, Mississippi State and Auburn — all opponents they can out-talent. They have lost to Ole Miss, Kentucky, Alabama, Texas and Tennessee — all opponents that have similar talent. Even though some of those games could have easily gone the other direction (not Saturday's 27-13 loss to Tennessee, which was fairly comprehensive), that particular trend has to be concerning to LSU fans. If Kiffin needs to have a clear talent advantage to contend for titles, it's not going to happen in the SEC regardless of how much money LSU is willing to spend on his roster. When you play this many quality opponents, you have to out-coach somebody at some point. For LSU to go an entire season and not pull out a single game where it was a coin flip at the beginning? That's a major indictment of Kiffin's first season in Baton Rouge.

Group of Six: South Florida thought it had wrapped up its coaching search a year ago with Texas A&M offensive coordinator Collin Klein. Then it became clear the Kansas State job was going to open, Klein's alma mater, and USF was forced to scramble at the last minute. It landed on Brian Hartline, who had been Ohio State's wide receivers coach since 2018 and had recruited a bunch of players out of Florida. So the hire made sense on paper, particularly after he called plays last year for the first time in his career. But in the real world, after seeing Hartline's first 11 games at South Florida, the school will need to think seriously about whether to give him a second season. That might be a harsh judgment on someone who is dealing with the learning curve of becoming a head coach. But this is arguably the best job in the American Conference given the geography and possibly the biggest roster budget in the league. Despite one of the most favorable schedules in college football, South Florida being 6-5 after a 35-14 thumping at Florida Atlantic is completely unacceptable, and the USF administration should look at itself in the mirror and ask itself if it did enough due diligence on Hartline to understand his strengths and weaknesses from the Ohio State perspective. Hint: It didn't.

Ryan Day: We are all going to have to get used to elite coaches having bad seasons in this volatile era, but it hits harder at a place like Ohio State that almost never experiences a true disaster. In fact, the last time the Buckeyes flopped like this was going 6-7 in 2011 amidst the chaos of Jim Tressel's ouster. (Then Urban Meyer came in and went 12-0 the following season.) Because Ohio State gets the most intense media coverage of any program in the country, this entire offseason will center around a grim autopsy of what went wrong. Most likely, the conversation will center on Day's decision to go NFL-heavy with his recent coordinator hires. Bringing in Matt Patricia to run the defense and Arthur Smith to be offensive coordinator may be fine decisions individually, but it does not feel like they have left Ohio State in a better spot than they were in with Chip Kelly and Jim Knowles in the coordinator suite. We'll see how Day responds, but he will surely do something because he's poised to start 2027 back in the same tenuous spot he was before winning the national title.

Matt Campbell: Of the many things that will rankle Penn State fans about this nightmare season, which stands at 6-5 with a game at Maryland left to finish up, let's focus on Campbell's hat. There's a sweet story behind why Campbell wears a worn-out cap with a deeply-bent brim on the sideline. It's a tribute to his grandfather, who worked two jobs and worked the graveyard shift at a Ford plant. It reminds Campbell of his blue-collar roots, so ever since he became a head coach at Toledo, he has worn one hat all season long, no matter how gross or faded it gets. Here's the problem, though. It's great to have a blue-collar mentality, but this is the big leagues of college football. Working hard isn't enough. If you're trying to win the way Penn State demands of its coaches, you need more five-star talent than five-star want-to. Does Campbell understand that? He should, especially now after a 25-24 loss at home to Rutgers that marks the absolute rock bottom for Campbell's first year. If your team isn't working hard enough to beat Rutgers (or Northwestern or Wisconsin), the blue-collar image you're trying to represent looks like fake hustle. And if your team isn't talented enough to win those games, then you've got the wrong person leading the program. We have to tell the truth. As annoying as James Franklin had become to Penn State's administration and fan base, particularly after a losing streak last season that sealed his fate, everything the school has done subsequently has made the decision to fire him look like a mistake.

Charles Huff: Memphis' coach has been a popular name in the Maryland coaching search, and Huff isn't doing much to knock down the speculation. At a recent press conference, in fact, Huff offered an eyebrow-raising quote about his future, saying he isn't worried about the chatter from a recruiting standpoint because "if I'm leaving Memphis, I ain't going to high school, I'm going to something bigger." Well, this weekend, Huff had an opportunity to make a good impression in the state of Maryland and let a massive opportunity slide by in a 38-34 loss at Navy. Memphis led this game 34-24 and had the ball before punting with about eight minutes left. Everything that happened after that point was a disaster, with Navy scoring on a 68-yard touchdown run, Memphis going three-and-out on the ensuing possession and the Midshipmen scoring the winning touchdown with 15 seconds left. This is a particularly egregious loss for Memphis because the Tigers haven't won the American Conference title since 2019 and beating Navy would have almost assured them of a spot in the championship game. They still have a good shot of making it via tiebreakers, but beating Navy would have kept Memphis alive for the CFP if Boise State stumbled somewhere. That's definitely off the table now.

Manny Diaz: Maybe it's a good thing that Diaz pulled out of the Florida State search earlier this week because Seminole fans would not have been happy campers if he was still in the mix coming off a terrible 20-10 home loss to Clemson. Yes, it says a lot about the job Diaz has done at Duke that losing to Clemson is now an upset. But, indeed, the Blue Devils were more than 10-point favorites facing a Clemson team down to its third-string quarterback. That was a juicy opportunity for Duke to get its eighth win and for the fan base to pound its chest because the Duke coach wasn't interested in leaving for Florida State. Instead, Clemson had the better gameplan by devoting all its resources to stopping star running back Nate Sheppard (19 carries, 116 yards) and forcing Duke to make some plays in the passing game. The Blue Devils couldn't do it, struggled to adjust and gained just 247 total yards. Duke will never complain about bowl eligibility given the program's history, but the whole point of Diaz sticking around for the long haul is to raise the floor and the expectations to a level where losing games like this is disappointing.

A Ramblin' Wreck of love: Things got so out of hand in Georgia Tech's 50-29 loss to Wake Forest that the ESPN broadcast turned away from the action on the field and focused on what appeared to be a young couple's argument in the stands with former Florida quarterback Jesse Palmer doing a play-by-play of the interaction. We sincerely feel for that couple, who surely did not attend to a college football game intending to become a viral internet meme. But when a game goes off the rails and announcers need to fill dead air, stuff happens. At least by the time the broadcast circled back, the couple was kissing and seemed to have made up. But that may be the last bit of love for Georgia Tech fans this season, who have watched their program spiral to 3-8, fire a bunch of assistant coaches and lose their last three home games by a combined 70 points. Head coach Brent Key will be under a lot of pressure to fix what broke this fall — not counting this couple's relationship issues.

Curtains for Curtis: This week could have been the big breakout for Vanderbilt freshman quarterback Jared Curtis, the Nashville native who switched his commitment from Georgia to his hometown school with a big NIL deal attached. Curtis had gotten the Commodores to within 27-24 at Florida and was driving inside the 25-yard line with a chance to take the lead and around seven minutes on the clock. Instead, Curtis took an awkward hit on second down, aggravating a wrist injury he's been dealing with and fumbling the ball. Though Vanderbilt retained possession, it was a tough spot to insert backup Whit Muschamp (yes, the son of Texas defensive coordinator Will Muschamp), who watched his first snap hit him in the chest for a 12-yard loss. Though Vandy tied the game on a 51-yard field goal, Florida scored on the next possession and won 34-27. We'll never know what could have happened if Curtis stayed in the game and gave Vanderbilt the lead.

Border-town blues: UTEP fired another coach this week, letting 37-year-old Scotty Walden go after winning just six of his 34 games. Nobody will blame the school for looking at the results and deciding it needs to spin the coaching roulette wheel once again. But is this the most difficult job in FBS? A 52-3 loss to Air Force in the first game post-Walden serves as a reminder of just how hard it's going to be for anyone to get this program going. Since 2000, UTEP has had five coaches. Dana Dimel went to one bowl game in six years. Sean Kugler went to one in five years. Mike Price managed to get bowl eligible three times in nine years. Gary Nord went 8-4 in his first season and then 6-30 over the next three. Look, any program can strike gold with a coach or a star quarterback and turn things around. New Mexico hasn't been a ton better historically than UTEP and is now on the precipice of winning the Mountain West. But between the program's awful track record, its isolated geography and inability to spend its way to relevance, UTEP needs a lot of luck with its next hire.
