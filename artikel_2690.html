Why Thanksgiving Week Is the Perfect Opportunity for Sports Radio to Develop New Talent

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

Thanksgiving week creates an interesting programming challenge for sports radio. While families prepare to gather around the table, airports fill with travelers and employees cash in their remaining vacation days, radio stations face a familiar reality: Some of their most dependable listeners aren't listening as often.

For programmers, that can become an excuse to take the easy route. Run syndicated programming, recycle some best-of segments and get through the holiday stretch with as little disruption as possible. But what if Thanksgiving week represented something more valuable than simply surviving a predictable dip in audience consumption? What if it became one of the most important weeks of the year for developing the future of your station?

When established personalities step away from the microphone, programmers have an opportunity. One that can introduce new voices, experiment with different combinations and give aspiring broadcasters something increasingly difficult to find in today's industry. A legitimate opportunity.

Sports radio is constantly aiming to attracting younger audiences and prepare for the next generation. But it often overlooks the people already working inside the building who would love nothing more than a chance to prove themselves.

Think about the part-time producer who handles weekend responsibilities. The board operator who dreams about hosting a show or the staffer who spends weekends calling play-by-play for free food and drinks. They aren't necessarily content with their current positions. Many have aspirations that extend well beyond the responsibilities they perform every day.

Every programmer has these staff members in their halls, but ask yourself this. Have you asked them about their desires for more? If you haven't, Thanksgiving week provides an excellent opportunity to start that conversation.

The economics of broadcasting have changed considerably across every company. Budget reductions, staffing consolidation and syndicated programming have reduced the number of available entry points for aspiring talent. Podcasting and digital platforms have created alternative paths, but they haven't eliminated the value of learning how to perform in a live broadcasting environment.

That's why programmers should view holiday scheduling differently.

Rather than treating the absence of established hosts as a programming inconvenience, consider it an opportunity to evaluate the people who could eventually become the station's next marquee personalities.

You don't need to hand someone the keys to your most important morning show. Start with a two-hour opportunity. Pair an aspiring host with an experienced producer. Give a promising part-time employee a chance to contribute alongside someone who understands the station's audience.

There are plenty of possibilities if programmers are willing to get creative.

Local podcasters deserve consideration, too. Who understands the market's teams, personalities and fan culture better than someone already creating content for that audience? A conversation, a direct message or an introduction through an existing employee could lead to a relationship that benefits both sides.

Sports writers and columnists are another resource. Albeit, their wing of the industry still has it's challenges. But, many already appear on radio shows regularly. They understand how to communicate with an audience, know the local sports landscape and appreciate the exposure that broadcasting provides.

The point isn't to find someone who can immediately replace your morning show host. It's to discover who might eventually deserve that opportunity down the road.

Don't let compensation become the reason you avoid experimenting. A small gift card, concert tickets or other station giveaways can provide a simple incentive for someone willing to contribute during a holiday week. More importantly, the opportunity itself carries value. For someone trying to establish a career in broadcasting, getting behind a microphone can mean considerably more than whatever they receive for showing up.

Of course, programming experimentation requires more than putting unfamiliar voices on the air and hoping something works.

Programmers need to listen. They need to evaluate performances, identify strengths and provide constructive feedback. Nobody expects an aspiring broadcaster to sound like a 20-year veteran during their first opportunity. That's precisely why coaching matters.

An investment in someone's development demonstrates that the station sees potential beyond their current job description. It also creates a relationship that encourages employees to communicate their ambitions rather than quietly pursue opportunities elsewhere. You don't always know what someone can become until you give them the opportunity to show you.

Thanksgiving also serves as a reminder of what makes sports radio special in the first place. It's an industry built around connection. Families gather to watch football, friends debate their favorite teams and listeners turn on their radios during crowded commutes or last-minute trips to the grocery store.

The format remains relevant because it creates relationships with people and communities. That connection doesn't disappear when a familiar personality takes vacation. However, maintaining it requires programmers to recognize that the next great voice might be sitting in their building, waiting for someone to open the door.

Thanksgiving week shouldn't be viewed as a scheduling problem. It should be treated as an audition period for the future. The next generation of sports radio talent won't necessarily arrive with an established audience, a recognizable name or years of experience. Sometimes, it starts with a producer getting two hours behind the microphone or a local voice receiving an invitation to join a broadcast.

Those opportunities don't guarantee success. But refusing to provide them guarantees that some potential will never be discovered.

Sports radio has spent years discussing how to evolve while protecting the qualities that made the format successful. Perhaps the answer isn't always another platform, another partnership or another expensive personality. Sometimes, it's simply giving someone a chance.

This Thanksgiving, be thankful for the people who keep your station running. More importantly, give them a reason to believe they could help lead it into the future.
