Unless you’re building a data center or have a side hustle, you’re significantly worse off than last year, according to the Fed

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Walk into a grocery store, park, or gas station, and you’ll notice prices across the U.S. keep climbing. On top of that, the Federal Reserve’s latest minutes suggest that unless you’re in two very niche groups of consumers, you’re probably markedly worse off in terms of income in 2026 compared to last year.By its very nature, inflation erodes spending power as prices rise. But in a balanced economy, when inflation is at its target of 2%, those increases are offset by income growth, so consumers can still afford goods and services without seeking additional income or eating into their savings.But in 2026, inflation has been well above the 2% target. The latest inflation readings from the Bureau of Labor Statistics came in at 3.7% over the past 12 months, through September 2026. However, in addition to prices rising above the central bank’s 2% target, Fed minutes from the latest Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) meeting showed wage growth for most industries lagged well behind price increases.

The affordability conundrum facing households is therefore twofold: Not only are prices rising quicker than consumers can reasonably expect, but any increases to their income are coming in well short of the new demands on their purse strings.

The Fed minutes from the meeting ending October 28 read that most participants “judged that aggregate nominal wage growth remained moderate and was broadly consistent with the committee’s 2% inflation objective.”

Some sectors are posting wage growth, the notes adding many members “observed that shortages of skilled workers needed for data center construction and related industries had contributed to sizable wage premiums in those industries, while a few noted that reduced labor supply in industries reliant on immigrant workers could add to sectoral wage pressures, although these developments did not appear to be having a material effect on aggregate wage measures.”

These factors are potentially good news for the Fed because they suggest the U.S. is not on the cusp of an inflationary wage-price spiral—making it easier for the FOMC to bring inflation back to its mandated 2% target. A wage-price spiral refers to a feedback loop in which higher labor costs push up production and consumer demand, leading workers to demand higher incomes again to offset the increased cost of living.

However, while it’s one less headache for the Fed, incomes lagging inflation by nearly 2% means consumers already stretched are being spread even thinner.

“Fed members acknowledged that wage growth is consistent with their inflation target—but inflation is not consistent with their target,” noted UBS’s Paul Donovan to clients this morning. “This is a subtle way of saying that wages are falling behind inflation.”

But in addition to the industries benefiting from the AI infrastructure boom, Donovan points out that the Fed’s notes convey that “consumer resilience continues to depend on lowering savings rates and engaging in side hustles. It implies labor cost pressures are not an inflation driver.”

The Fed has a few weeks and a handful of data releases before its next rate-setting meeting in December. However, the meeting notes state that the economy is “stable and close to maximum employment” while inflation is ahead of target; this suggests tensions in the Fed’s mandate may call for a rate hike.

Interest rate traders assign a 91% probability of a 25 basis point hike at the December meeting, per CME’s FedWatch. Fed Governor Christopher Waller has gone so far as to call for a 50 basis point increase, saying the central bank needs to “shock people.”

Higher borrowing rates and tighter financial conditions would put further pressure on consumers and the wider economy, a worry for Donovan. He noted that inflationary concerns “focus on oil prices (which the Fed cannot influence) and perhaps AI-related demand (largely beyond the Fed’s reach). The only way to offset those would be to create near-recessionary conditions in the parts of the economy that respond to Fed action.”
