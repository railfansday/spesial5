For Newcastle United these are now the steps that matter

There are Newcastle United seasons that announce themselves with the flourish of a fanfare, and there are seasons that arrive more quietly, almost tentatively, as if testing the ground beneath their feet.

This one, for Newcastle United, belongs firmly to the latter category.

Ten Premier League matches into the 2026-27 campaign, the Magpies sit in twelfth place with twelve points. Three victories, three draws, four defeats.

The numbers are neither catastrophic nor comforting. They are, instead, the arithmetic of transition.

After years of ascent under Eddie Howe—punctuated by European nights and the rare, precious taste of silverware—the club has entered a deliberate, if painful, period of reconstruction. Key figures have departed. Younger, less seasoned players have arrived. And into this unsettled landscape has stepped Matthias Jaissle, a thoughtful German coach still learning the particular cadences of the Premier League. Let’s pray he is precocious!

The style he favors—“vertical football”—is direct, urgent, and ambitious. Yet in these early weeks it has often looked unfinished, like a promising manuscript that still requires careful editing. Attacks break down before they fully form. The midfield sometimes lacks the necessary ballast. Set-pieces, once a source of both goals and defensive security, have lost their edge. Chance creation has been modest. The underlying numbers suggest a side that is still searching for its rhythm.

I would argue that the back half of last season under Eddie Howe, and with the “superstars” who moved on to their perceived “bigger” clubs, the performances were no better. I flew 3,500 miles to attend the Bournemouth match – at great expense may I add – to witness the worst Newcastle United performance in my near 50 years of following the club. The rot started last season (and I would venture to say in the summer of 2025).

None of this should surprise those who have followed the club’s longer story. Newcastle has always been a place where ambition and constraint exist in uneasy conversation. The Saudi ownership brought resources and expectation, but the financial rules of the modern game impose their own discipline, that I wish would be challenged in court as anti-competitive.

The past 2026 summer window reflected that reality: significant investment, yes, but also the departure of established leaders (good riddance to the rats!) and the calculated gamble on potential rather than proven readiness.

Still, there are concrete steps available this season—practical, unglamorous steps that can steady the ship without pretending the rebuild is already complete.

Matthias Jaissle must first allow himself the freedom of pragmatism. The purest version of his vertical approach has, at times, left the team exposed and the attack under-supplied. A measured adjustment—greater control in build-up when the high press is broken, tighter rest defence, a clearer structure for the attacking midfielder—need not abandon the philosophy. It would simply make it sustainable. Restoring set-piece competence would be another quiet victory. These are matters of coaching detail, not revolution.

The Newcastle United squad itself still holds untapped value. Joelinton’s physical presence and ball-winning capacity remain essential once fully available, but we have a young gem in Sean Steur who, whilst not as physically imposing as Big Joe, is poised, creative, and doesn’t mind sticking a foot in. Dan Burn’s leadership and aerial strength can stabilise both ends of the pitch. The younger summer arrivals require patience but also clear pathways and honest assessment. A stable selection, built around those who are ready rather than those who might be ready later, would help establish the consistency that has been missing.

Then there is January. The winter window is rarely the stage for transformative spending at a club managing financial ratios carefully. Yet it can be the stage for precise, intelligent reinforcement. The most glaring absence is a natural number ten—a player capable of receiving between the lines, linking midfield to attack, and providing the creative spark that current options have not consistently delivered.

One name that has already circulated is Bilal El Khannouss of Stuttgart. The Morocco international attacking midfielder was reportedly considered during the summer and is claimed to be available for a fee in the region of no more than£40 million, a figure that may prove more attainable than many alternatives. His profile—technical, progressive, comfortable in the half-spaces—aligns with what Jaissle appears to seek. Other options will be examined, of course. The market rarely offers perfect solutions in January. But the principle is clear: a ready-made creator who can accelerate the adaptation of the system would be more valuable than another project requiring time the club may not fully possess this season. Contract situations must also be addressed with clarity. Players entering the final year of their deals create both risk and opportunity. Selling at the right moment, rather than losing assets for nothing, can fund the necessary additions while maintaining squad balance.

None of this guarantees a return by Newcastle United to the higher reaches of the table overnight. That would be unrealistic. What it can provide is direction—a sense that the transition is being managed with purpose rather than simply endured. A top-half finish, or even a secure mid-table campaign accompanied by improved underlying performance, would represent genuine progress under the circumstances.

Football clubs, like the cities they represent, are defined as much by how they navigate difficulty as by how they celebrate success. Newcastle has known both extremes in recent years. The current chapter is quieter, more complicated, and less cinematic than the ones that preceded it. Yet it is no less consequential.

The work of improvement rarely arrives with trumpets. It arrives in adjustments of shape, in the careful acquisition of the right player at the right moment, and in the daily insistence that potential must eventually become performance. For Newcastle United this season, those are the steps that matter. The rest will follow, or it will not. But the path, at least, can be chosen with intention.
