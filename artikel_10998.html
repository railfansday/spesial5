Precise genomic integration of large DNA cargo remains a central challenge in genome engineering. CRISPR-associated transposases (CASTs)1,2 offer a promising solution by coupling RNA-guided DNA recognition to transposon-mediated integration. Because of their exceptional targeting fidelity, type I-F3 CASTs have been a major research focus1, yet the structural basis of their RNA-guided DNA integration has remained unclear. Here, using cryo-electron microscopy, computational protein design and functional assays, we report the licensing mechanisms that control DNA integration activity of Vibrio cholerae CAST (VchCAST). Structural determination was enabled by zero-shot computational redesign of TnsA, which enhanced integration activity by nearly two orders of magnitude without compromising specificity. The structure of the holo integration complex reveals the conformational licensing events that govern activation of the integration machinery at the on-target site. Multiple components (Cascade–TniQ, TnsC and TnsA–TnsB) undergo conformational changes upon assembly into the holo integration complex, and the catalytically competent state is stabilized only within the full assembly. Comparison with existing CAST integration complex structures reveals conserved architectural features, suggesting a common molecular basis for RNA-guided transposition across CASTs. Our findings establish a general mechanistic framework for RNA-guided DNA integration and demonstrate that protein design is a powerful strategy for engineering highly active CAST systems.

A long-standing challenge in genome engineering is how to achieve precise, programmable integration of large (≥1 kb) DNA cargo. CRISPR–Cas nucleases are capable of efficient DNA cleavage3, but their reliance on host DNA repair pathways limits both the efficiency and fidelity of large insertions4. CASTs offer an alternative strategy by coupling RNA-guided DNA recognition to transposon-mediated integration, without the need for DNA double-strand breaks or homologous recombination1,2. CASTs evolved from Tn7-like transposons through the acquisition of a CRISPR effector, with distinct effectors defining each of the major subfamilies: type V-K, I-F3, I-B and I-D5. All CASTs encode a conserved set of proteins: TniQ (bridging protein), TnsC (AAA+ regulator) and TnsA–TnsB (TnsA/B, transposase complex) (Fig. 1a). The CRISPR effector and TniQ form the targeting module, and TnsA/B and TnsC form the transposition machinery (Fig. 1a). Although all CASTs perform RNA-guided transposition, they can display a remarkable diversity of targeting behaviours. For example, in type I-B2 CASTs, the transposition machinery can associate with either the sequence-specific DNA-binding protein TnsD or an RNA-guided targeting complex6. Type V-K CASTs exhibit a robust off-target pathway, driven by the propensity of TniQ and TnsC to load at non-cognate sites7. Type I-F3 CASTs have a Cascade effector and exhibit near-exclusive on-target integration8, which makes them of particular interest for clinical9 and microbial genome-editing applications10.

Recent structural studies of CAST assemblies have begun to reveal how this conserved transposition machinery accommodates architecturally diverse CRISPR effectors. The V-K system from Scytonema hofmannii, ShCAST, is the best characterized to date; its holo integration complex (HIC) structure shows that the host factor S15 and TniQ associate with Cas12k and together strongly distort target DNA, an event that is required to recruit two helical turns of hexameric TnsC11,12, which in turn engages the transposase TnsB via its C-terminal hook13. A complete TnsABCD integration complex structure exists for type I-B2 CAST14, but structural information on other CAST assemblies is limited to isolated components or subcomplexes. TnsC organization is not fixed across CAST systems, as it forms a heptameric ring in other CAST systems, including I-F315 and I-B216, as well as in Tn717. Whether mechanistic insights from one system can be extended to other CAST systems is unclear.

Despite extensive biochemical, genetic and structural studies of individual components, the mechanisms by which type I-F3 CASTs achieve precise on-target integration remain unknown. Chromatin immunoprecipitation with sequencing (ChIP–seq) experiments have revealed a hierarchical assembly pathway in which Cascade–TniQ occupancy precedes TnsC recruitment, and stable TnsC accumulation depends on the transposase TnsA/B15. Cascade–TniQ binds DNA promiscuously and forms partial R-loops at many candidate sites15, but they cannot complete R-loop formation on their own18, suggesting that additional steps are required to license TnsC recruitment. Structures of TnsC15 have established the molecular details of its homo-oligomerization on DNA. Nonetheless, how all these components come together to form an integration-competent complex at the RNA-guided target site remains unknown. Thus, a structure of the fully assembled HIC is critical to establish the mechanistic basis for RNA-guided DNA integration and for rational engineering efforts to improve type I-F3 CAST integration efficiency for translational applications9,19.

Here, using a combination of computational protein design, single-particle cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) and directed in vivo mutagenesis, we uncover the mechanisms that regulate HIC assembly in the type I-F3 VchCAST system. The architectures of the strand-transfer complex (STC) and the full HIC reveal how RNA-guided target recognition, TnsC oligomerization and TnsA/B recruitment converge to organize a stable integration complex. Despite large structural differences across type V-K, I-F3 and I-B2 CASTs, conserved local features suggest universal mechanistic principles of RNA-guided DNA integration across CASTs. Our results reveal a context-dependent structural mechanism, whereby each of the aforementioned components undergoes significant conformational rearrangements relative to their isolated structures15,18,20 as a means to license RNA-guided integration complex assembly. Our work also demonstrates how structure-guided computational protein design can be leveraged as a highly effective strategy for improving integration activity without compromising specificity.

Because TnsA was refractory to purification (Methods), we hypothesized that stabilizing it in a product-bound conformation could both enhance activity and facilitate structure determination. We therefore modelled the VchCAST STC (comprising TnsA, TnsB and a strand-transfer DNA substrate) using AlphaFold 321 (Extended Data Fig. 1). We then redesigned the TnsA sequence using LigandMPNN22, with functionally important residues held fixed (32%; 74 out of 230 residues; Supplementary Fig. 1) and applied per-residue sampling biased towards TnsA homologues. Designs were grouped by sequence divergence from wild-type TnsA (TnsAWT), then selected for experimental screening on the basis of predicted confidence scores (Methods).

We screened 500 top-ranking designs, each containing between 2 and 87 substitutions (Supplementary Table 1), using our previously developed high-throughput Escherichia coli screen23. In brief, a kanamycin resistance cassette serves as the donor DNA, such that kanamycin selection reports overall integration activity. On-target genomic integration is enriched by dual kanamycin and arabinose selection, which selects for disruption of an arabinose-inducible ccdB toxin gene. Activity and specificity scores are calculated as the log2-transformed fold change in variant frequency compared to wild type23. Of the 500 designs tested, 67% showed higher activity than TnsAWT (Fig. 1b). The top-performing design (D1A; Fig. 1b) incorporates 27 mutations (Extended Data Fig. 2) and has a log2-transformed activity score of 6.37, with no significant change in specificity (specificity score = −0.27; Fig. 1b). This improvement translates in vivo to an approximately 100-fold increase in integration activity relative to TnsAWT (Fig. 1c) while maintaining high fidelity, with 98% of insertions occurring on target (Fig. 1d) and at the expected distance, 82 base pairs (bp) from the protospacer adjacent motif (PAM)1 (Fig. 1e).

TnsA is required for transposition, but its catalytic activity determines product identity: cleavage of the 5′ donor end generates simple insertions, whereas a failure to cleave generates co-integrates24. Long-read sequencing distinguishes these two products, establishing the extent of TnsA activity8. Using long-read sequencing, we found that D1A produced more co-integrates than wild-type TnsA (42% simple insertions versus 96.2%; Extended Data Fig. 3). This phenotype was attributable primarily to the Q73K substitution: reverting Q73 to the wild-type residue restored simple insertion frequency to near wild-type levels (92.4%) without compromising the transposition activity gains conferred by the broader D1A redesign (Extended Data Fig. 3), indicating that Q73K governs the simple insertion/co-integrate balance independently of those gains. This also does not appear to be a general consequence of the design campaign, as other top-scoring designs retain full catalytic activity (97–100% simple insertions; Extended Data Fig. 3). Finally, we examined the effect of redesign on the folded state of TnsA. Circular dichroism analysis revealed that TnsAWT has substantial random coil content, consistent with an unfolded protein, whereas D1A has a spectrum consistent with a well-folded, mostly α-helical protein (Supplementary Fig. 2). Together, these results demonstrate that zero-shot structure-based computational design can both improve CAST activity and facilitate structure determination, creating a framework for iterative structure-guided optimization.

We next determined the cryo-EM structure of the STC containing integration host factor (IHF), D1A (hereafter referred to as TnsA), TnsB and a strand-transfer DNA substrate (2.9 Å overall resolution; Fig. 1f,g and Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4). We found that K73 occupies a second-shell position relative to the TnsA active site, sufficiently close to form a hydrogen bond with catalytic residues, providing a likely explanation for why the Q73K substitution reduces the catalytic activity of TnsA (Supplementary Fig. 5). As this perturbation is confined to the local active site environment, the D1A structure presented here should therefore represent an otherwise catalytically competent state. IHF promotes CAST transposition 10-fold, although it is not absolutely required for activity25. In our structure, IHF binds LE DNA at a site between LE1 and LE2 (Supplementary Fig. 3), bending the DNA to permit occupancy of additional TnsB binding sites. This binding mode has been described in molecular detail in a related study that lacked TnsA and other components of the HIC20. Here, we instead focus on the novel insights we observe in the presence of the partner protein TnsA.

TnsA localizes to the 5′ end of the donor DNA, where it binds the flanking host DNA and forms a continuous β-sheet with TnsB, a feature highly similar to previous TnsA structures from the type I-B2 PmcCAST system (Supplementary Fig. 6). We also identified conformational flexibility in the STC ensemble, which was separable by 3D classification. The highest-resolution class, containing the majority of particles, corresponds to class 1 (Supplementary Fig. 7a), whereas a second, minor 3D class (27% of particles, 3.2 Å overall resolution) lacks observable density for target DNA and for TnsB beyond the C-terminal helix (CTH) domain (class 2; Supplementary Fig. 7b,c and Extended Data Table 1). Closer inspection of class 2 reveals that the catalytic DDE triad (D223, D308, and E344) is too distant (6.2–6.9 Å; Supplementary Fig. 8) to form a functional active site, and that the loop preceding E344 (residues 331–341) is disordered (Fig. 1i). By contrast, class 1 has the expected arrangement of catalytic residues, as well as two magnesium ions: a catalytic magnesium with the expected coordination geometry and a second structural magnesium positioned nearby that stabilizes a sharp bend at the target–donor DNA junction (Fig. 1j). The conformational transition from class 2 to class 1 would entail a ~20° rotation of the catalytic domain and target DNA-binding domain (TBD) of TnsB at the TnsA–TnsB interface (blue star, Fig. 1h), reorienting these domains to engage target DNA. Concurrently, a target DNA-binding loop in the catalytic domain of TnsB (residues 359–378) becomes structured, further priming the complex for target DNA binding (Fig. 1k). These structural observations show that the STC exhibits conformational heterogeneity; one conformation (class 2) appearing catalytically inactive, and the other (class 1) showing rearrangements consistent with active site organization and target DNA engagement. As TnsA/B requires additional transposon-encoded partner proteins (that is, TnsC) for integration activity1, these conformational changes may reflect a mechanism of activation for TnsA/B.

We next determined the cryo-EM structure of the HIC, comprising Cascade–TniQ, TnsA, TnsB and TnsC, assembled on a strand-transfer DNA substrate that mimics the in vivo integration product (Supplementary Fig. 3). Our cryo-EM data analysis reveals substantial compositional heterogeneity, with three identifiable assemblies (Extended Data Fig. 4): Cascade–TniQ (target-recognition complex (TRC); 3 Å overall resolution), STC–TnsC (4.3 Å overall resolution) and the HIC (4.5 Å overall resolution; Fig. 2a). In the HIC, local resolution is highest at the Cascade core (3.7 Å) and decreases towards the STC periphery (about 7 Å; Extended Data Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 9). Although IHF was included in the reconstitution, we did not observe IHF bound in the resulting structure, which may be attributable to the sample preparation method (Methods). Notably, the overall map was of sufficient quality to interpret different structural features without the need of a composite map stitched from subtracted focused-refined regions.

The HIC contains a Cascade effector, a TniQ dimer, a heptameric TnsC ring, a TnsB tetramer and two copies of TnsA (Fig. 2b), consistent with the stoichiometry established by structures of individual subcomplexes15,18,20. However, density for the seventh TnsC protomer (TnsC7) is noticeably weaker than for the other subunits (Supplementary Fig. 10), indicating substoichiometric occupancy or flexibility. The DNA-binding footprint accounts for the characteristic 82 bp integration distance from the PAM: 32 nucleotides occupied by the guide RNA spacer, followed by a 21, 14 and 15 bp footprint corresponding to TniQ, TnsC and TnsB, respectively, and terminating in a 5-nucleotide target-site duplication (Fig. 2c). Mutagenesis of the Cas8/5 PAM-interacting residues S127, R243, N246 and D461 substantially reduces integration activity (Supplementary Fig. 11), consistent with an important role for these residues in PAM recognition and transposition. Our structure of the VchCAST HIC reveals an arrangement wherein TnsC acts as a bifunctional adaptor, interacting with TniQ via its N-terminal face and with the STC via its C-terminal face (Fig. 2b). This architectural role of TnsC is similar to previous observations in ShCAST (type V-K)12 and PmcCAST (type I-B2) systems14,16. This modularity explains how CAST systems have diversified to co-opt a wide range of CRISPR effectors while preserving the core transposition mechanism.

A central question is how the targeting module is physically coupled to the core transposition machinery. In the HIC, TniQ2 serves as the primary physical bridge between the target recognition and transposition machinery (Fig. 3a), forming a predominantly hydrophobic interface with TnsC1 (Fig. 3b). Consistent with the functional importance of this interface, alanine substitutions at F2, L3 and F40 substantially reduced integration activity (Fig. 3c). TniQ forms a constitutive homodimer, but only the TniQ2 monomer interacts with TnsC, mirroring observations in the evolutionarily distant ShCAST and PmcCAST, in which a single TniQ subunit recruits TnsC12,16. Remarkably, despite the divergent overall architectures of the ShCAST (Protein Data Bank (PDB): 8EA3), PmcCAST (PDB: 8FF4) and VchCAST integration complexes, the TniQ–TnsC interface is notably conserved (Extended Data Fig. 5). In particular, the TnsC finger loop, previously shown to mediate critical TniQ interactions in ShCAST12, engages TniQ2 similarly in VchCAST (Extended Data Fig. 5). Consistent with this observation, truncations within the TnsC finger loop severely reduce transposition activity15. These findings identify the TniQ–TnsC interface as a shared docking point that may enable architecturally diverse CRISPR effectors to couple to common transposition machinery.

TnsB activity is strictly dependent on TnsC-mediated recruitment to the target site15, making these interactions central to understanding integration. We next examined how TnsB engages TnsC within the HIC, revealing multiple points of contact along the C-terminal face of TnsC, specifically at protomers TnsC2, TnsC3 and TnsC6 (Fig. 3d). Notably, the TBD of TnsB localizes to the TnsC2–TnsC3 interface (Fig. 3e), consistent with previous ShCAST and PmcCAST structures12,14 (Extended Data Fig. 6). Across Tn7-like transposons, ATP binding, but not hydrolysis, is required for integration activity26,27. AAA+ initiator clade regulators require ATP for oligomerization, as the ATP-binding pocket is formed at the inter-protomer interface28. This implies that transposition requires TnsC oligomerization on target DNA, although the mechanistic basis for this requirement has remained unclear. The conserved interaction between the TBD of TnsB and the TnsC inter-protomer interface now suggests that TnsB directly senses the oligomeric state of TnsC, providing a structural explanation for this long-standing observation.

Another conserved feature across CAST systems is the interaction between TnsC and the extreme C terminus of TnsB, known as the hook, which is critical for transposition13. The interchangeability of TnsB hooks between type I-F3 CASTs29 underscores the importance of these structured interactions within otherwise disordered regions. Consistent with this role, we observe continuous density connecting the CTH (residues 563–573) of TnsB to the surface of TnsC6 (Fig. 3f), although additional contacts are likely to remain undetected owing to the length and extensive disorder of this region. The TnsB hook is predicted by AlphaFold 321 to correspond to the last eight residues of TnsB, an assignment supported by cryo-EM density observed in the HIC (Extended Data Fig. 7). The hook binding site in VchTnsC aligns well with ShTnsC13 and PmcTnsC14 (Extended Data Fig. 7), indicating that although structural details may differ, the binding location is conserved.

Having identified the TnsB hook interaction with TnsC, we reasoned that the disordered N- and C-terminal regions in all components of the core transposition machinery (TnsABC; Supplementary Fig. 12) could mediate additional interactions. Consistent with this idea, we observed contacts between the N terminus of TnsB and TnsA, and between the C-terminal tail of TnsC and TnsB (Fig. 3g). Residues 3–10 of the disordered N terminus of TnsB occupy a hydrophobic pocket on TnsA (Fig. 3h) close to aromatic residues F6 and F9, consistent with AlphaFold 3 predictions21 (Extended Data Fig. 1) and sequence conservation analysis (Extended Data Fig. 8a). Although N-terminal truncation (Δ1–10) caused a significant reduction in transposition activity, the TnsB F9A mutation reproduced almost none of this effect, suggesting that residues other than F9 mediate the relevant contacts within the truncated region (Extended Data Fig. 8c). The C-terminal tail of TnsC (residues 321–330) is positioned against the CTH of TnsB (Fig. 3i and Extended Data Fig. 8b) at a hydrophobic interface, also supported by AlphaFold 3 predictions21 (Supplementary Fig. 13) and conservation analysis (Extended Data Fig. 8b). The TnsC F329A mutation almost fully abrogated transposition activity, similar to the effect of C-terminal truncation of TnsC (Extended Data Fig. 8c). Analogous interactions between the C terminus of TnsC and the STC occur in PmcCAST, albeit involving different subunits and interfaces14 (Extended Data Fig. 9). These findings uncover a previously unrecognized network of interactions involving the unstructured termini of TnsA, TnsB and TnsC, revealing how the core transposition machinery may maintain associations to facilitate integration-competent assembly.

A previous cryo-EM structure of VchTnsC solved in the presence of DNA revealed an open heptameric ring-like arrangement in which the bound DNA was poorly resolved (PDB: 7UFI)15. In our HIC structure, although we clearly resolved the path of target DNA, no specific TnsC–DNA interactions could be precisely mapped. Thus, it is likely that the TnsC ring primarily associates with target DNA via the complementary basic surface formed by its central pore (Supplementary Fig. 14). This is consistent with previous data showing that TnsC binds DNA relatively weakly15, suggesting that TnsC requires partner proteins such as TniQ to initiate oligomerization on target DNA. Notably, comparison with PDB 7UFI reveals that the heptameric ring is significantly more open in the HIC. Aligning on TnsC1 results in an 11 Å displacement at TnsC7 relative to the equivalent protomer in PDB 7UFI (Fig. 3j), the result of an average 0.8 Å greater rise per protomer (Supplementary Fig. 15). This indicates that partner proteins induce larger conformational changes than those observed in TnsC with DNA alone. Our observations suggest that the oligomeric state of TnsC is sensitive to the presence of the partner proteins TniQ and TnsB and is mediated through distinct interaction interfaces located on specific TnsC protomers.

We next investigated how TnsC is selectively recruited to the target site. The compositional heterogeneity of our reconstitution enabled us to resolve a free Cascade–TniQ complex (Extended Data Fig. 4) (TRC; 3.0 Å overall resolution; Fig. 4a) that closely resembles a prior structure of the target DNA-bound VchCAST Cascade–TniQ complex18 (Supplementary Fig. 16). This previously reported structure featured a partial R-loop and a disordered Cas8/5 helical bundle domain (HBD)18. By contrast, the Cascade–TniQ complex in the HIC (Fig. 4b) adopts a fully engaged conformation characterized by a complete R-loop and a structured Cas8/5 HBD. Comparison of Cascade–TniQ in the TRC and HIC structures reveals that R-loop completion is accompanied by a conformational change in TniQ2 to engage both target DNA and TnsC (Extended Data Fig. 10).

The non-target genomic strand (NTGS) is threaded through an electropositive channel at the tripartite TniQ1–TniQ2–Cas8/5 HBD interface, where it rejoins the target genomic strand (TGS) to reform the target DNA duplex downstream of the R-loop junction (Fig. 4c,d). The Cas8/5 HBD contributes to this channel through electrostatic contacts with the NTGS via R310, R311, K314 and R370 (Supplementary Fig. 17). Additionally, a previously uncharacterized loop within the Cas8/5 HBD (residues KSLIPEN), which we term the TniQ-interacting loop (TIL), interacts directly with TniQ1 (Fig. 4e). The functional importance of this interface is confirmed by alanine substitutions in the Cas8/5 HBD, TIL and at TniQ1 residues D361, S358 and W394, all of which substantially reduce integration activity (Figs. 3c and 4f).

Separately, TniQ2 engages target DNA through two distinct modes: hydrophobic packing against the R-loop junction (L326, L336, V362 and T363; Fig. 4g) and electrostatic interactions with the DNA backbone via basic and polar residues (R58, K65, R71, S104 and S106; Fig. 4h,i). Mutagenesis of residues at both interfaces substantially reduces integration activity, consistent with their functional importance (Fig. 3c). These observations establish that R-loop completion drives a conformational transition in the target recognition module that repositions TniQ to engage target DNA and license recruitment of the core transposition machinery.

We propose the following working model for RNA-guided transposition, based on our findings and incorporating prior observations on CASTs and other Tn7-like systems. Cascade–TniQ promiscuously samples genomic locations15, consistent with its ability to stably bind target DNA with only partial R-loop formation (Fig. 5). A cooperative conformational rearrangement in Cas8/5 and TniQ is required to stabilize the full R-loop and enable TnsC recruitment, with direct contacts of TniQ at the R-loop junction providing a structural readout of R-loop completion. This coupling is further supported by molecular dynamics simulations demonstrating correlated motions between Cas8/5 and TniQ30. We observe no other DNA distortions consistent with specific recognition of the target site by the transposon-encoded machinery (Supplementary Fig. 18), contrary to that observed in other Tn7-like systems14,31. Further insight into target-site selectivity comes from the observation that PAM-distal mismatches permit Cascade binding but abolish transposition15. The conformational rearrangements in the HIC occur near spacer nucleotide positions 25–32, raising the possibility that mismatches in this region could introduce geometric incompatibility that prevents stable docking of the HBD and subsequent TnsC recruitment (Fig. 5). TnsC oligomerization is required to recruit TnsA/B to the target site and may be sensed directly by TnsB through conserved interactions spanning TnsC protomers. Whether full heptamer formation is required prior to TnsA/B recruitment remains unknown and is not resolvable using our reconstitution approach; however, previous work on Tn7 suggests that oligomerization is completed prior to TnsA/B recruitment32.

TnsB also undergoes multiple layers of activation through association with partner proteins TnsA and TnsC. In isolation, the DDE catalytic triad is improperly arranged and too distant to coordinate magnesium ion binding, and target DNA remains disordered even when a strand-transfer substrate is provided20. Association with TnsA is insufficient to fully stabilize the catalytically competent conformation (Fig. 5). Only in the context of the HIC, in which both TnsA and TnsC are present, are the catalytic residues locked into an integration-competent state and target DNA stably accommodated. Although our reliance on a strand-transfer substrate does not allow us to establish the stepwise order of these conformations directly, the inactive-to-active transition that we observe is, to our knowledge, unreported in CAST systems despite extensive structural work from our group and others13,14,33. Notably, a conceptually related precedent exists in IS21, where the transposase IstA transitions from an inactive to an active conformation upon association with its obligate AAA+ partner IstB34, suggesting that conformational licensing may be an effective mechanism for ensuring selective activation of integration across Tn7 and related transposons. In summary, we propose that each component of the integration machinery undergoes conformational changes upon incorporation into the HIC, providing a structural basis for the exceptionally high on-target specificity characteristic of type I-F3 CAST systems. Both the targeting module Cascade–TniQ and the transposase TnsA/B serve to license RNA-guided DNA integration, ensuring that catalytic competence is only achieved upon assembly of the complete machinery at the RNA-guided target site.

Structured interactions embedded within intrinsically disordered regions of CAST proteins have critical roles in transposition, most notably through the C-terminal hook of TnsB12,29. Here, combining our cryo-EM structure with AlphaFold 3 predictions, we localize the C-terminal hook of TnsB and identify its binding site on TnsC. We also identify two previously unrecognized interactions: the N terminus of TnsB engages a pocket on TnsA, and the C terminus of TnsC contacts TnsB in close proximity to target DNA. These findings reveal an extensive network of structured interactions within otherwise disordered termini that physically connect the core transposition machinery prior to integration complex assembly. The mapping of these contacts has direct implications for the rational engineering of chimeric CAST systems and the design of improved variants.

The structural characterization of CAST systems has been largely motivated by the goal of engineering more efficient tools for genome editing; however, structure-guided efforts to improve CAST activity have had limited success29,35,36. Here we demonstrate that a high-throughput structure-based design approach can overcome these limitations, presenting a compelling and scalable alternative to directed evolution. We propose that thermodynamic stabilization of the integration-competent conformation is key to improve activity. This is consistent with the thermodynamic requirements of cut-and-paste transposition: the chemically isoenergetic reaction proceeds through states of increasing stability, and the product complex can be so stable that unfoldases (for example, ClpX) are required for its disassembly in vivo37. Consistent with this hypothesis, zero-shot structure-based protein design of a single protein, TnsA, enhanced integration activity by nearly two orders of magnitude in E. coli, approaching the gains achieved through seven rounds of directed evolution applied across the entire PseCAST system19. Notably, the redesigned variant not only improved integration activity but also enabled structural characterization of the HIC, illustrating how computational design and structural biology can be synergistic approaches.

The compromised catalytic activity of the top design variant D1A is attributable primarily to a lysine substitution at position 73, which is within range to form hydrogen-bonding interactions with the catalytic residue D91. We hypothesized that this substitution directly perturbs active site chemistry, which motivated our prioritization of the reversion mutant for testing; consistent with this hypothesis, reversion almost fully restored catalytic activity while maintaining substantial increases in activity. This trade-off reflects a known limitation of structure-based design methods: approaches such as ProteinMPNN can inadvertently stabilize protein structure at the expense of catalytic function38, as functional residues are often locally destabilizing to the protein fold39. Therefore, success of the design strategy proposed here depends on mechanistic knowledge and the availability of a high-quality model of the integration-competent complex, precisely what the structures presented here provide, and what has been lacking in previous engineering efforts.

The AlphaFold 3-generated TnsA–TnsB STC model was used as input for structure-based protein design. A total of 74 TnsA residue positions were fixed during design based on five structural criteria: (1) residues predicted to participate in primary or secondary interactions with the active site; (2) residues within contact distance of the DNA; (3) residues predicted to form protein–protein interfaces with TnsB; (4) residues predicted to contribute to a binding pocket engaging the TnsB or TnsC hook regions; and (5) N- and C-terminal residues with low predicted confidence (pLDDT), indicative of structural disorder. The full set of designable positions is listed in Supplementary Table 4.

Sequences were generated using three graph neural network-based protein design methods that sample amino acid probabilities from local structural context: LigandMPNN22, SolubleMPNN40 and HyperMPNN41. Because these models are trained on structural rather than functional information, and because design was performed on an AlphaFold 3 model rather than an experimentally determined structure, a bias towards TnsA sequences was introduced to restrict sampling to structurally favourable substitutions and improve the likelihood of recovering functional variants. Bias was applied by modifying per-residue logits prior to softmax normalization, such that increasing bias values progressively shifted the amino acid probability distribution towards the native identity without fully enforcing it. Bias strengths of 0, 1, 2, 3 and 4 were applied to each of the three models using the flag bias_AA_per_residue. In a parallel strategy, an additional sampling bias was introduced towards residues conserved in a multiple sequence alignment of TnsA homologues.

All designed sequences were folded as monomers and as part of the STC using Chai-142. Designed sequences were clustered using MMseqs243, and final designs were selected from each strategy based on threshold criteria across six metrics: backbone root mean square deviation (RMSD) of monomeric TnsA relative to input structure (<0.9–1.2 Å, depending on design strategy), backbone RMSD of predicted STC complex (<1.5–1.7 Å), predicted TM-score of the monomer (>0.918–0.928), predicted interface TM-score of the STC (>0.839–0.845), predicted local distance difference test (pLDDT) of the monomer (>0.893–0.910), and pLDDT of the STC (>0.834–0.840).

The DNA substrate used was designed based on in vivo genomic profiling experiments, synthesized as single-stranded DNA, and annealed to form a strand-transfer product substrate which was used for complex reconstitution, following our previously described strategy12,13. The left end spans LE1 through LE3 (positions 1–109) and the right end spans RE1 through RE3 (1–72), as shown in Supplementary Fig. 4. The native arrangement of left and right ends are identical up to position 30 (encompassing the 8 bp terminal sequence and LE1/ RE1). Past the first binding site, the placement and spacing of LE2–LE3 and RE2–RE3 differ, additionally there is a IHF binding site between LE1 and LE2 (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Two double-stranded DNA substrates were independently assembled for the STC and transpososome reconstitution: a LE fragment and a RE fragment, each designed to recapitulate the relevant structural elements of the VchCAST RNA-guided transposition product, including the transposon terminal repeat sequences, the 5 bp target site duplication, and flanking target and gDNA regions (Supplementary Table 6). A diagram of the substrates can be found in Supplementary Fig. 4.

The LE fragment was assembled from three oligonucleotides: LE–TGS, which contains the transposon LE and flanking gDNA, Target-RC, which completes the double-stranded target genomic region, and LE–HFR, which provides the complementary LE region to LE–TGS along with a host flanking region for TnsA binding.

The RE fragment was assembled from four oligonucleotides: RE–TGS, which contains the RE and the target strand for guide RNA binding, NTGS–Scramble, a non-target strand oligonucleotide contained a scrambled sequence to facilitate bubble formation, RE–HFR, an oligonucleotide that complements the RE sequence of RE–TGS and contains a flanking HFR region, and DTB–LUEGO, a desthiobiotinylated oligonucleotide incorporated at the 5′ end of the RE fragment to enable streptavidin-based affinity capture of the assembled complexes during pull-down experiments47.

Prior to annealing, all oligonucleotides were purified by HPLC using an Agilent 1260 Infinity II system equipped with an AdvanceBio Oligonucleotide column (Agilent Technologies) to ensure incorporation of only full-length species. The LE and RE fragments were each assembled independently by combining their respective oligonucleotides at equimolar concentrations in annealing buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 50 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA), heating to 95 °C for 5 min, and gradually cooling to 50 °C at 1 °C per min using a thermal cycler (Bio-Rad). The annealed fragments were stored separately until used in STC and transpososome reconstitutions.

STC assembly reactions were prepared using an annealed DNA substrate (0.5 µM in 100 µl) and containing TnsB together with D1A using the same 1:5:10 stoichiometry. Complex assembly was initially carried out under high-salt conditions in a buffer containing 26 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 5 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 20 mM KCl, 750 mM NaCl, 0.2 mM MgCl2, 15 mM magnesium acetate, 2% glycerol, 1 mM DTT and 0.1% Tween-20. Following assembly, samples were dialysed overnight at 4 °C from high-salt buffer into an identical low-salt buffer in which the NaCl concentration was reduced to 150 mM to promote stable complex formation.

For affinity capture of assembled complexes, Streptavidin Mag Sepharose magnetic beads (Cytiva) were washed 3 times with 500 μl of low-salt buffer prior to use. Dialysed samples were incubated with the equilibrated streptavidin beads for 1 h at room temperature with continuous rotation to allow binding of biotinylated DNA substrates and associated protein complexes.

Following incubation, the beads were washed 3 times with 500 μl of low-salt buffer to remove unbound proteins and nonspecific material. Bound complexes were subsequently eluted using low-salt buffer supplemented with 10 mM biotin. Elution was performed in 2 sequential rounds using 50 µl buffer per step, with each incubation carried out for 30 min at room temperature under rotation before collection of the eluate. Elution fractions were analysed by SDS–PAGE to confirm the presence of protein components within the assembled complexes. Sample quality, homogeneity and the presence of assembled STCs were further evaluated by negative-stain electron microscopy prior to cryo-EM grid preparation and vitrification.

Sequential on-bead assembly of the HIC was performed by first capturing the desthiobiotinylated RE substrate (0.5 µM in 100 µl) on Streptavidin Mag Sepharose magnetic beads (Cytiva). Binding was carried out for 1 h at room temperature with gentle rotation in binding buffer comprising 26 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 5 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 20 mM KCl, 750 mM NaCl, 0.2 mM MgCl2, 15 mM MgOAc, 2% glycerol, 1 mM ATP, 1 mM DTT, and 0.1% Tween-20, followed by 3 washes with 500 µl of the same buffer.

Cascade–TniQ was subsequently introduced at a 1:2 DNA:protein molar ratio and incubated for 1 h at room temperature, followed by 3 washes. The LE substrate (0.5 µM), pre-complexed with E. coli IHF at a 1:8 DNA-to-protein molar ratio, was then added alongside TnsB (1:5 DNA:protein) and designed TnsA (1:10 DNA:protein), and the assembly was allowed to proceed for 1 h at room temperature. Beads were washed 3 times with 500 µl binding buffer. In the final step, TnsC was introduced at a 1:10 DNA-to-protein ratio in binding buffer supplemented with equimolar concentrations of ATP and ATPγS (1 mM each) and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. Unbound proteins were removed by 3 washes with 500 µl of ATP/ATPγS-containing binding buffer.

The assembled HIC was released from the beads by two sequential elution steps, each using 50 µl of elution buffer (binding buffer supplemented with 10 mM biotin) incubated under rotation for 30 min. Eluted fractions were resolved by SDS–PAGE to verify the presence of all HIC components. Prior to cryo-EM grid preparation, samples were evaluated by negative-stain electron microscopy to confirm the presence of assembled HIC particles and to assess sample homogeneity.

Graphene sheets with a poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) support layer (ACS Material, TTG100BB,25 ×25 mm) were stored at 4 °C until use. Graphene transfer onto cryo-EM grids was performed based on a previously described protocol with minor modifications48. Ultrafoil grids (Au R1.2/1.3, Quantifoil) were placed on a 3D-printed grid transfer tool immersed in deionized water. Excess support material from the graphene pad was trimmed, and the PMMA/graphene pad was placed gently onto the water surface to avoid overflow onto the graphene layer. After positioning, the water level was gradually lowered by syringe withdrawal, allowing the graphene sheet to settle onto the grids. Grids were left overnight at room temperature to anneal, then baked at 150 °C for 30 min to promote firm adhesion. Grids were transferred to a Petri dish, and PMMA was removed by three successive acetone washes. Graphene-coated grids were individually transferred to a grid box and stored at room temperature until use.

STC samples were prepared for cryo-EM either directly from pull-down elution fractions or following two- to four-fold dilution in low-salt buffer. Graphene-coated grids were prepared as described above. Prior to sample application, grids were plasma-cleaned using a Solarus II Plasma Cleaner (Gatan) under an Ar/O2 atmosphere at 10 W for 10 s (s), with the graphene-coated surface oriented upward.

For vitrification, 4 µl of STC sample was applied to the carbon side of a graphene-coated grid and incubated for 60 s inside a Vitrobot Mark IV chamber (Thermo Fisher Scientific) maintained at 4 °C and 100% relative humidity. Grids were blotted for 3.5 s at a blot force of −7 and immediately plunge-frozen into liquid ethane cooled by liquid nitrogen.

HIC samples were prepared for cryo-EM from either undiluted pull-down eluates or two- to four-fold dilutions thereof. Graphene-coated grids were prepared as described above and plasma-cleaned using a Solarus II Plasma Cleaner (Gatan) under an Ar/O2 atmosphere at 10 W for 10 s with the graphene-coated face oriented upward.

For vitrification, 4 µl of HIC sample was applied to the carbon side of a graphene-coated grid and incubated for 60 s inside a Vitrobot Mark IV chamber at 4 °C and 100% relative humidity. Grids were blotted for 3.5 s at a blot force of −7 before plunge-freezing into liquid ethane cooled by liquid nitrogen.

Ni-NTA affinity grids were prepared as previously described49. Quantifoil Au R1.2/1.3 grids were coated with a Ni-NTA lipid monolayer composed of 20% 18:1 DGS-NTA(Ni) (Avanti Polar Lipids) diluted with DOPC (18:1; Avanti Polar Lipids). Grids were incubated with 3 µl of His-tagged HIC diluted in 2 µl of elution buffer (26 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 5 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 20 mM KCl, 750 mM NaCl, 0.2 mM MgCl2, 15 mM MgOAc, 2% glycerol, 1 mM ATP, 1 mM DTT, 0.1% Tween-20) for 1 h at 4 °C in a humidity chamber to allow affinity capture. Unbound material was removed by three brief washes with elution buffer, after which grids were blotted and plunge-frozen in liquid ethane using a Vitrobot operated at 4 °C and 100% relative humidity with a blot force of −7 and blot time of 3.5 s.

Vitrified STC samples were initially screened on a Talos Arctica transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operated with fringe-free illumination at 130,000× magnification (0.63 Å per pixel) and 200 kV, equipped with a Gatan K3 direct electron detector and a BioQuantum imaging filter (20 eV slit width). Data were collected using the EPU software package (Thermo Fisher Scientific). This screening step was used to assess grid quality and particle distribution prior to large-scale data collection. Grids of sufficient quality were subsequently imaged on a Titan Krios G3i transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operated with fringe-free illumination at 130,000× magnification (0.745 Å per pixel) and 300 kV, equipped with a Falcon 4i (4k × 4k) direct electron detector, a BioQuantum imaging filter, and a 20 eV energy-selecting slit. Data collection employed the aberration-free image shift and multi-shot-per-hole acquisition modes of EPU. For the STC dataset, 7,724 movies were collected using EPU with image shift, with nominal defocus ranging from −1.5 to −2.5 µm at 0.2 µm intervals. Each movie was acquired with a 3.1 s exposure and a total dose of 60 e− Å−2, fractionated into 60 frames. Frames were aligned using MotionCor250, and CTF estimation was performed in cryoSPARC v4.7.151. The full data processing workflow is presented in Supplementary Fig. 4.

Particles were initially picked using blob picker, and approximately 290,000 extracted particles were subjected to 2D classification. Classes representing the STC were identified and used as templates for a second round of template-based particle picking, yielding approximately 1,000,000 particles that were re-subjected to 2D classification. The resulting 22,000 particles were used to train a Topaz neural network picking model (v0.2.5)52. After confirming model performance, additional particles were extracted from all micrographs using the trained model. The new particle set underwent 2D classification, and selection of high-quality classes yielded 97,972 particles that were used for ab initio reconstruction. Heterogeneous refinement separated the dataset into two classes: 70,006 particles representing the STC complex and the remainder classified as non-particles. Non-uniform refinement of the STC class produced an initial map at 2.9 Å resolution. The particle stack was then subjected to 3D classification using principal component analysis with a 13 Å resolution cut-off, separating 18,831 particles lacking target DNA from 51,169 particles with target DNA bound. Both subsets were independently subjected to non-uniform refinement, yielding a 3.2 Å map for the STC without target DNA and a 2.9 Å map for the STC with target DNA. Both maps were further processed with DeepEMhancer v12132253 prior to model building.

Initial cryo-EM data collection on graphene grids revealed a strong preferred orientation bias, limiting the angular coverage of the dataset. To improve particle orientation distribution and enable higher-quality three-dimensional reconstructions, Ni-NTA lipid monolayer grids were subsequently prepared49.

A total of 144,294 movies were collected at a pixel size of 0.848 Å and processed using Patch Motion Correction and Patch CTF Estimation with a CTF-fit cut-off of less than 10 Å. Initial particle picking was performed by blob picking on approximately 2,600 micrographs, yielding ~570,000 extracted particles (binned 3×), which were subjected to two rounds of 2D classification to generate ~5,000 representative particles for training a Topaz neural network picking model (v0.2.5)52. The trained model was applied across all ~22,000 micrographs, extracting 2,722,447 particles (binned 3×). Following two rounds of 2D classification, 897,848 particles were retained for further analysis. The complete data processing workflow is described in Extended Data Fig. 4.

Single-particle analysis revealed substantial compositional heterogeneity within the reconstituted sample. Two-dimensional class averages resolved density for all expected components in multiple orientations (Extended Data Fig. 4). Heterogeneous refinement (n = 4) partitioned the dataset into 4 populations: 2 HIC classes (131,242 particles, 14.6%; and 462,255 particles, 54.8%), 1 STC–TnsC class (127,845 particles, 14.2%) and 1 Cascade class (146,705 particles, 16.4%). These populations enabled classification of the following assemblies: a Cascade–TniQ sub-complex (3.3 Å), a STC (2.9 Å) an STC–TnsC assembly (4.3 Å) and the complete HIC (4.5 Å) (Extended Data Fig. 4). Each population was further processed by sub-classification and homogeneous refinement. For the HIC, signal subtraction of the STC–TnsC region followed by 2D slice projection-guided particle sorting enabled focused local refinement, yielding a map at 4.5 Å resolution (Fourier shell correlation (FSC) = 0.143). Non-uniform refinement of the Cascade region within the full complex achieved 3.5 Å (FSC = 0.143). Free Cascade and Cascade–TniQ populations were resolved to 2.8 Å, 3.0 Å (closed) and 3.1 Å (open), respectively (FSC = 0.143). The HIC exhibits variable local resolution (3.7–9.0 Å, Supplementary Fig. 9), and focused refinements were combined into a composite map.

For the STC maps, PDB 9Y7B was used as a starting model. The structure was initially docked into the STC-without-target-DNA map and manually rebuilt in Coot (v0.9.8.96)54 where required. DNA sequences were modified manually and fitted into the density using ISOLDE as a ChimeraX plugin (v1.11.1). The TnsA subunit was modelled using AlphaFold 3 and docked into the corresponding density. This assembled STC model was subsequently used as the starting model for the STC-with-target-DNA map. Structural rearrangements in TnsB required individual domains to be docked independently into the density before rejoining in Coot.

For the Cascade–TniQ complex structures, individual Cas subunits were modelled using AlphaFold 3 and docked into the map. The DNA scaffold was derived from PDB 6PIJ and mutated to match the designed substrate sequence. The target recognition complex was assembled, and manual adjustments were performed in Coot.

Both the Cascade–TniQ and STC complex structures were used as starting models for modelling the HIC. TnsC protomers were individually modelled using AlphaFold 3 and docked into the density. The STC–TnsC portion of the HIC was used as the starting model for the off-pathway complex, with minor adjustments applied to the TnsC ring.

For all structures, real-space refinement of DNA substrates was performed in Phenix (v2.0rc1-5690)55 with base-pair and secondary structure restraints enforced. Model validation was carried out by computing map-model FSC at a 0.5 cut-off. The masked cross-correlation coefficient (CCmask) from refinement output was reported as the representative model-map cross-correlation. All validation statistics are summarized in Extended Data Table 1.

Helical rise and twist were measured from docked protomers in the HIC (PDB: 36AU) and VchTnsC structures (PDB: 7UFI) using a script from the Rosetta software suite: make_symmdef_file.pl56. Rise values (in Å) were extracted from the output and plotted using Python.
