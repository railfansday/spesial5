Who is standing for Fifa president – and can anyone finally topple Gianni Infantino?

Gianni Infantino remains in a strong position to secure a further four-year mandate as Fifa president, even after enduring significant pushback over his governance.

However, a number of challengers have stepped forward ahead of next March’s election. So, who is in the running to potentially oust Infantino, and do they realistically have a chance of beating him?

To be eligible, candidates require backing from at least five member associations and must demonstrate active involvement as a player or official within Fifa, a confederation or a national association for two of the preceding five years.

Every one of Fifa's 211 member associations holds a single vote, meaning national size or historical success carries no extra weight. Consequently, world champions Spain possess identical voting power to San Marino, who sit bottom of the world rankings.

When two or fewer contenders stand, victory requires a simple majority - exceeding 50 per cent - of the valid votes submitted.

In races with three or more candidates, a two-thirds majority of present and eligible member associations is needed in the initial round. Should subsequent ballots be required, the candidate receiving the fewest votes is knocked out in each round until two remain.

Formal nominations close on Wednesday, four months prior to the assembly. Voting will subsequently occur during the 77th Fifa Congress in Rabat on 18 March.

The Fifa general secretariat is required to inform member associations of the official candidates for president no later than one month before the Congress convenes.

Discontent mounted following proposals for the Fifa Forward Enterprise (FFE), an initiative designed to introduce private funding into a fresh entity overseeing commercial rights for premier Fifa tournaments, such as the World Cup.

Opponents targeted both the structural details of the initiative and the absence of consultation and openness with which it was introduced to Fifa's membership.

The backlash grew so severe that prominent administrators urged Infantino to step down, while multiple regional confederations formed a united front, prompting the president to eventually scrap the plan.

Having withdrawn the FFE plan following threats from European body Uefa to boycott Fifa fixtures, Infantino offered an apology to the Fifa Council regarding the management of the proposal.

While Uefa - among three confederations that had demanded his resignation - remained unmoved, the apology served to mend bridges with the Asian Football Confederation (AFC).

AFC head Sheikh Salman bin Ibrahim Al Khalifa, who had denounced the project as a "fundamental breach of trust", subsequently changed position to offer his endorsement to the president.

Elsewhere, South American governing body Conmebol declined to demand Infantino's departure, while the Confederation of African Football (CAF) maintained solid backing.

A pledge by Infantino to distribute $25m to every member association also seems to have been instrumental in smoothing over tensions.

Concacaf, representing North America, Central America and the Caribbean, originally aligned with Uefa and the AFC in voicing opposition. However, its delegation has since split, with Mexico alongside various Caribbean nations rallying behind Infantino.

As one of the most seasoned figures in the race, Tashima has outlined ambitions to reform Fifa's governance, rebuild institutional accountability and tackle what he views as an unhealthy centralisation of authority.

Possessing deep internal knowledge of the governing body's inner workings, the Japanese administrator offers a direct proposition: "I'll keep what's good and change what's bad."

Diallo entered the contest relatively late, gathering support from Uefa member associations sitting on both its executive committee and the Fifa Council.

In charge of the FFF since 2023, the Frenchman has consistently challenged Infantino's leadership. Nonetheless, Uefa has stopped short of confirming unanimous backing from its 55 member nations, leaving his exact level of support uncertain.

Vega grounds his platform in athletic credentials, commercial background and ten years of global campaigning, with the former defender stating he has travelled to over 140 nations to build support.

His bid is far from impulsive given his prior campaigns for office, yet three prior failures create doubts over whether his ambitions can translate into meaningful voting power.
