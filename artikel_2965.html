​Building A Data Foundation That Makes Enterprise AI Work

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Niraj Nagrani is Chief Data and AI Officer at Altimetrik.

After more than 30 years as an enterprise leader, I’ve been through many transformation journeys, from building hyperscale systems and training data for machine learning to migrating from legacy architectures to modern data platforms. Today, I see many organizations struggle to move from a successful AI pilot to a reliable production system​.

The problem is not with foundation models, which have improved exponentially over the past few years. In my experience, the problem almost always comes back to the underlying data architecture.

Many organizations are still working with data that isn’t ready for AI. Data quality and lineage may be governed inconsistently or not governed at all. And governance is often bolted on at the end rather than built in from the start.

I’ve worked with companies running over 100 siloed systems. When information is scattered that widely, it can be almost impossible to establish a consistent view of the business. Unstructured data like contracts, emails and support tickets can disappear into blind spots because they don’t fit neatly into traditional data structures. Another problem is the lack of a shared definition of business entities like revenue, customers or other important measures across systems. ​

Earlier machine learning learned patterns from a curated, labeled dataset to do one narrow task. Agents reason and act in real time across systems. At the moment of action, an agent has to know what a “customer” means, which policy applies, whether the data is current and what it is allowed to see. Without that context, you get a confident wrong action. Earlier machine learning needed clean training data; agents need living context.

The key first step of data modernization is what I call “business rule archaeology.” Over time, organizations accumulate business logic in stored procedures, SQL queries and their dependencies. Some of that logic is documented, but a lot of it isn’t, which makes it difficult to understand how the existing business processes actually work. Companies often underestimate the effort required to untangle these dependencies.​

Before planning for​ modernization, understand how the business uses its data and how its processes operate. From there, organizations can build an incremental migration plan rather than trying to move everything at once.​

​I think about modernization as four steps, and each one becomes a layer of the platform you end up with. First, unify the isolated systems in an open lakehouse built on object storage and open table formats such as Iceberg or Delta, holding one governed copy of structured and unstructured data. Feed it through streaming and change data capture, because agents need current information, not last night’s batch.

Second, define shared business definitions in a semantic layer, so “revenue” or “customer” means the same thing in every system. Third, capture how those entities relate in an ontology and knowledge graph. This is what gives an agent the context for its decisions. Fourth, put governed retrieval across structured and unstructured data, with governance as code providing access management, lineage and a replayable audit trail. Agents also need their own identities with least-privilege access, and cost controls belong in the design from the start, because the cost of running models decides what is affordable at scale.​

Data trust is crucial, but it’s especially challenging when different teams have different standards for what good data looks like. Trust is an interface problem you engineer: one definition, enforced in the pipeline.

One useful mechanism is a data contract, a machine-readable agreement covering schema, quality requirements and service-level expectations between producer and consumer. It functions like a stable API, making the relationship between those parties more predictable.

Many current operating systems and data warehouses were built to answer yesterday’s question for a human. An agent-ready platform has to serve decisions to a machine in real time, with context, semantics and governance built into its design. That is what matters most as we design the next generation of data platform architecture.​
