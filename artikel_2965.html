SpaceX Just Brought a Starship Back From Orbit and Caught It. Here's the Part of the Business That Gains the Most.

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

With the ship caught but the booster lost, why could Starlink gain the most?

SpaceX (SPCX -1.26%) got half its rocket home. On Saturday, Nov. 14, about 22.5 hours after Starship took off from Starbase, Texas, the launch tower's arms closed around the rocket's upper stage (the part SpaceX calls the ship). It was the first time SpaceX has caught a ship.

The lower half didn't make it. A day earlier, the Super Heavy booster's guidance system canceled its catch and steered it out over the Gulf of Mexico, where its flight termination system destroyed it after a landing burn.

Before it came home, the ship delivered 26 of Starlink's newer V3 satellites. And I think Starlink is the part of SpaceX that gains the most from a ship it can catch. After all, it uses a big chunk of SpaceX's launch capacity, and it makes most of the company's operating profit.

The growth stock finished near $139 on Friday, Nov. 13, the last session before Saturday's landing, about 38% under its post-IPO high of $225.64.

SpaceX has caught and reflown Super Heavy boosters before. The ship was the half it had never caught.

On this flight, though, the booster's boostback burn (which turns it back toward the launch site) stopped early after a low-fuel alarm, according to SpaceX, which is still looking into the root cause. So SpaceX still hasn't caught both halves of one rocket on the same flight.

Last month, I argued that the stock wouldn't get full credit for Starship until SpaceX could reuse the whole rocket. Saturday's catch covers half of that. And no caught ship has flown a second time yet. But CEO Elon Musk said on Sunday that this one could probably fly again after a few weeks of refurbishment.

SpaceX records no revenue for launching its own Starlink satellites. Instead, the launch cost is added to the satellites' value on its balance sheet and expensed over time as depreciation in the connectivity segment.

And it launches its own payloads often: 21 of its 33 Falcon launches in the third quarter were internal missions, and every Starship launch so far has been too.

Showing what this build-out costs, connectivity depreciation and amortization climbed 57% year over year to $986 million in the third quarter. This was up 22% from the second quarter, the same rate as the segment's revenue. In other words, the cost of the network is growing as fast as the sales it supports.

Starlink's already very profitable, of course. Connectivity operating income almost doubled year over year to around $2.4 billion. The segment's operating margin kept growing too, hitting around 45%, up from 38% a year earlier and 39% in the second quarter. CFO Bret Johnsen said the quarter had a one-time benefit, mostly from tariff refunds, worth about 3 percentage points of the segment's gross margin. Even without it, the operating margin widened.

This makes connectivity around 36% of SpaceX's third-quarter revenue but about 85% of the operating income from its three segments. Notably, the superintelligence segment, SpaceX's artificial intelligence (AI) business, pulled in about half of all revenue but earned just $431 million, held down by about $1.2 billion in stock-based compensation linked to acquisitions.

In short, Starlink is the profit engine. A cheaper launch might cut the cost of every satellite added to its books, and the depreciation charged against that profit for years to come.

"I think we'll need a few hundred V3 satellites for people to really feel the impact," SpaceX President Gwynne Shotwell said on the company's third-quarter earnings call in October. She thinks SpaceX can get there by the middle of next year.

Starship has delivered 52 V3 satellites on its two orbital flights, 26 apiece, so every 100 more takes about four flights. If each flight used up a new ship, every load of satellites would carry the cost of a whole upper stage. A ship that's caught and flown again spreads that cost over many launches.

Friday's close puts SpaceX at around $1.9 trillion. Using annualized third-quarter revenue, the stock trades at around 32 times sales. At that valuation, I think investors are already paying for a large share of the savings a reusable Starship could deliver.

Saturday's catch got SpaceX closer to those savings. But they won't show in Starlink's depreciation until caught ships fly again, and the booster still needs to come home on the same flight as the ship. I'd want to see both before paying this much for the shares.
