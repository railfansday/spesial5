Tackling The Trust Tax With Technology

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

A 30-year veteran in retail technology, Sensormatic Solutions President Tony D’Onofrio is leading the charge to redefine retail.

​Amid economic pressure and supply chain volatility, prices can only be controlled to a point. Shipping delays and short staffing can still happen if a snowstorm moves through, no matter how meticulous the planning. But what happens on the floor? That’s something retailers can still control with relative confidence.​

Retailers have embraced it, spending the past few years cutting friction from in-store operations at every turn and offering unstaffed checkouts and no-questions-asked returns in bids to win hearts when it matters most. Now, retailers are confronting the other side of the equation.​

Retailers’ investments in self-service touchpoints have yielded many of the customer experience benefits they promised. With shopper intentionality high and convenience still a top motivator, it’s no wonder self-checkouts (SCOs) are seen as a critical strategy for drawing in shoppers. Adjusted return policies have also become important, as nearly three-quarters (71%) of shoppers say they’re less likely to return to a retailer after a poor returns experience.​

However, other research highlights that these efforts to reduce friction at scale can come with unanticipated costs. It’s no surprise; as friction eases, retailers may unintentionally open the doors for exploitation. The expanded trust that must accompany self-service and relaxed policies has understandably been linked to increased losses primarily through increased crime activity.​

And it’s true that stores offering SCO touchpoints see losses grow rapidly. Locations with SCO options see losses 33% higher than stores without them, on average, according to a recent ECR study. The study also found that grocery stores that implemented SCOs saw year-over-year losses increase by 22% in the first year of operations.​

Further, every additional touchpoint can increase total losses across operations. Each transaction processed at an SCO carries a predictable and quantifiable loss. ECR found that a retailer’s total retail losses increase approximately 0.04% for every 1% of sales that occur at SCOs. This figure has quadrupled since 2018 (+0.01% for every 1%).​

These patterns can be attributed, at least in part, to increased malicious behavior. A recent IHL Group study (conducted in partnership with my company, Sensormatic Solutions) found that theft remains the highest single driver of retail loss among respondents. However, the report notes that external theft—which includes any losses related to malicious customer actions—only accounts for 25% of all retail losses.​​

The ECR study offers some additional color. The study identified non-scans at SCOs as a factor in up to 4.8% of transactions, but it stops short of attributing intent with any certainty. The authors offer a range of 6% to 80%, which is to say that they can’t separate crimes from accidents with any confidence.​

Of course, the trust tax reaches a peak around the holidays. More shoppers means more SCO use and more returns. A recent NRF report estimates that 15.8% of total annual sales end in returns; during the holiday period, this grows to 17% (download). As such, addressing the challenge and lowering the tax is at top of mind for retailers.​

Despite perceptions, retailers don’t have to decide between loss prevention and satisfaction. There’s a way to achieve the goal without touching the shopper experiences customers have come to expect. It starts with an adjustment to the leader’s understanding of the challenge at hand.​

The source of the trust tax isn’t too much trust; it’s too little insight. Most shoppers can, in fact, be trusted. The issue is that, with traditional deterrents, a few bad actors—or a few mistakes—end in broad strategies that treat every shopper as suspicious. The result is additional punitive strategies, like bag checks and tightened return windows, placed in the wrong touchpoints and aimed at the wrong targets.​

Investments in item-level inventory monitoring tools can help balance the scales. As items move, exploitation leaves a fingerprint; unintended errors leave their own. Product journey records that integrate shipping, POS, exit and return data is where these markers appear, and they’re far more reliable than human surveillance or standardized checks.​

Connecting these sources in real time can flag exceptions, such as an item leaving without a matching sale or a return without a matching purchase, so associates can act. They can also flag incidents at checkout that are more likely the result of error than theft.​

Getting to this point requires retailers to lay a foundation. Using RFID tags and sensors, retailers can track items throughout their supply chains, storage facilities, sales floors and exits. This enables retailers to connect the dots between POS logs, exit data and returns to get a clear picture of loss within and beyond their walls.​

Unified data can help retailers close that gap without touching the shopper—except in the places where increased friction serves them. These systems can surface mismatches in real time, so retailers can take steps to address what’s happening now and prevent it from happening later. This approach moves the friction from the shopper to the back end. The checkout and return stay as they are, while scrutiny goes to the exceptions the data flags.​

A decade of self-service shopping has shown just how quickly extending trust can become a cost center all its own. Addressing it ahead of the holiday rush has—for many retailers—meant deciding between satisfaction and shrink management.​

But data shows that it doesn’t have to. The trust tax is predictable; it rises with use, peaks with traffic and starts as often with an honest mistake as with a bad actor. It’s in retailers’ own data that they’ll find solutions that enable them to address self-service losses without touching experiences. This holiday season, retailers who invest in insight won’t have to choose.
