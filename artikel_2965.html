The Biggest Problem In Accounting Firms Isn’t Strategy; It’s Alignment

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

David Marquis is the Chief Executive Officer of Caseware.

Few professions are as steeped in tradition as accounting, and few are changing as fast. Technology, business models and client expectations are all shifting at once.

That can create tension, particularly for auditors. Their business is to manage risk, yet they are being asked to change at a pace that can feel at odds with that.

Across the industry, I see firms embracing change with vigor, firms that are more measured in their approach and everything in between. The good news is that nobody is alone in this. These pressures are happening across the profession and beyond it.

One pattern stands out, however: The firms that seem most confident and adaptable are rarely the ones that simply have the better strategy. More often, they are the ones that are aligned enough to act on it.

Accounting firms are often partnerships, and that structure creates its own challenges. The ownership model tends to make firms more consensus-driven by default. That is not necessarily a problem when the big decisions are clear. The difficulty appears in the day-to-day decisions. Smaller issues accumulate until a firm becomes divided across details. Partners may agree that standardization is necessary while reluctant to cede control over how their own teams function. Or, a firm may make a deliberate technology investment, but teams don’t use it consistently. In these scenarios, execution will start to slow.

I see a version of this in my own role as a CEO. Organizations may be aligned on the big things, but once you break those decisions down by functional silo, complexity or detail, alignment becomes harder. The more alignment you can create, the less you slow yourself down in terms of organizational execution.

For accounting firms, that challenge can be even more pronounced because it is not always clear who makes the judgment call on what. The gap is often less about the strategy and more about having the confidence to move with pace across the firm.

The fastest-moving firms don’t pretend the tension between individual partner autonomy and firm-wide consistency does not exist. They acknowledge it. That’s the first step.

From there, the question becomes one of clarity: determining what the firm’s call is and what the partners are individually responsible for. That might include client relationships, judgment calls on specific work items or how they manage their book of business.

Then there are the things, such as common processes, common technology decisions, common workflows and quality standards the firm needs to handle consistently. Knowing who is responsible for what, and what becomes a firm-level decision versus an individual partner decision, is incredibly important.

Judgment alone is valuable, but it doesn’t scale reliably. At some point, you need process. You need workflow. You need a system approach that holds the structure together as the organization grows. That is why I increasingly think about successful firms as systems rather than simply collections of individual partners.

This need for alignment is increasingly important because several forces are converging simultaneously.

AI is one. Some firms are experiencing and adopting it at speed. Some are just beginning to experiment. Accounting firms have good reasons to approach AI carefully and judiciously because the risk of being wrong can carry high stakes.

Talent is another pressure. Firms are dealing with fewer people entering the profession, higher expectations from those who do and less patience for antiquated ways of working.

Then there is private equity (PE), which is raising the bar on operational discipline across the profession. PE-backed firms are being pushed to improve how they serve clients, run internal operations and use technology. That creates a new reference point for what best practice can look like, even for firms that have no intention of pursuing private equity themselves. Not every firm will take the PE route, and none needs to. The operational discipline that makes PE-backed businesses attractive is available to anyone willing to build it.

The cumulative impact is more critical than any one of these trends. Firms increasingly have to be deliberate about how they operate and how systematically they can execute.

I sometimes hear firms say, “We’ll just wait this whole thing out.” I understand the impulse, but I think it can be the wrong one. That doesn’t mean chasing every trend. It means asking the harder questions earlier, even when the answers aren’t entirely clear.

Which aspects of the organization currently function effectively? Where do things work solely due to the presence of “human glue,” where one person holds it all together? In what areas does alignment fall apart among the partners? What has the organization been avoiding addressing due to too much friction in doing so?

Technology investment is one example. In a partnership, capital that might otherwise go toward technology may instead go toward profit sharing. That can limit the firm’s ability to invest in what will make it better over time.

Some of the strongest firms are intentional about setting that capital aside. They have a system for it. Everybody contributes, but it pays back over time.

Readiness isn’t always size-dependent. Ownership structure and leadership matter, but culture matters, too. I see firms where partners have agreed to invest in technology and systems not only for themselves but for the partners coming up behind them. They want to hand the next generation an effective legacy. They care about the brand and what the partnership means while other firms remain focused primarily on maximizing current profit.

The firms that ask the hard questions, invest for the future and build alignment now are the ones keeping their options open. Nobody knows exactly what the profession will look like several years from now, but the goal is to build a firm that can move with confidence and certainty when the answer becomes clear.​
