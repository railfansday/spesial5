Is Ken Paxton’s Senate Campaign in Texas the Bitter End of Trumpism?

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

At Coyote Crossing Saloon, a smoke-filled dive bar off Interstate 37, a 64-year-old Texas native named Tracey was showing me his teeth. He’s missing about half of them—a casualty, he said, of toothpicks soaked in horse liniment that serve as a potent stimulant for truckers looking to stay up late on long-haul drives. Tracey is a big fan of President Donald Trump. He voted for him three times and still supports him, even after all the excesses of his second term. But he can’t stomach Ken Paxton.

Dragging on a cigarette, he pointed to the Texas attorney general and Republican Senate candidate’s alleged corruption, his reputed infidelity, and the case of a plea deal his office offered to a Waco man charged with sexually abusing a young boy. “Just because you have all this money, how are you gonna just do as you please and nobody’s gonna say nothing?” Tracey remains a fan of the state’s Republican governor, Greg Abbott. “He’ll win, so I’m happy about that.” But Paxton? “Get him out of there.”

Texas attorney general Ken Paxton embarked on a bus tour of the Lone Star State as his Senate campaign draws to a close.

If Paxton has lost voters like Tracey, Republicans might be in trouble. Texas hasn’t sent a Democrat to the Senate since 1988, and this seat could determine who will control Congress for the final two years of Trump’s presidency. Yet the collapse of Trump’s approval across the country has put even red states like Texas in play for Democrats, creating a powerful undercurrent that Paxton’s opponent, James Talarico, has surfed to a narrow but steady lead in the polls. That’s turned Texas into a money pit: $500 million has been blown on advertising already, thanks in part to at least $168 million pumped into the state by Elon Musk, and it’s on track to be the most expensive Senate race in history.

It didn’t have to be this difficult. Since 2002, the seat has been occupied by Senator John Cornyn, who ran for reelection with the backing of the Republican establishment and a sizable war chest. But in the primary, the president backed Paxton, a scandal-plagued culture warrior who went above and beyond Trump’s loyalty tests, while Cornyn drew suspicion that he was fair-weather MAGA. Now Trump has placed a risky bet. These midterms are a referendum on the president, but this race, more than any other, is a gauge of his movement: Can a candidate with many of Trump’s failings but little of his charisma careen into the United States Senate?

Even in the ruby-red counties of West Texas, Trump’s support is slipping. Will Paxton pay for it?

A crowd in Sherman, Texas, settles down into movie theater recliners to watch Paxton speak.

This is the final sprint of the Paxton campaign, a frenetic bus odyssey dubbed the Come and Take It Tour. (There’s a cannon printed on the side of the bus.) The voyage has swept through the border towns of McAllen and Laredo, up through exurbs like Selma—just outside San Antonio—on to Austin and Waco and Dallas, then out through hundreds of miles of high plains to the West Texas towns of Amarillo and Lubbock, and then down to Corpus Christi for a rally with Trump himself.

“The person that is without sin, please cast the first stone,” said Pastor Ruben Mendez in his opening prayer at Paxton’s campaign event in Selma. The room was silent. “I don’t think so. I don’t think there’s anybody that can do that.” Mendez, the pastor of Iglesia Shalom, a small nondenominational church tucked down an alleyway in a suburb nearby, was speaking to a crowd crammed into the Blue Bonnet Palace, a country music venue in a corrugated iron barn just off the highway. A cardboard cutout of Brooks & Dunn hung above the stage alongside a motionless disco ball.

Paxton is locked in a close race with James Talarico, whom Trump has nicknamed “Talafreako.”

The pastor’s reminder that “King David was a flawed man” was met with scattered applause. If the subtext of this homily wasn’t clear enough, a protester soon made it explicit. “Why were you in Dubai?” a young man yelled as Paxton took the stage. He was swiftly bounced by security to chants of “USA!” from the faithful.

The protester was referencing the latest chapter of the biblical Paxton oppo file. The Wall Street Journal reported that Paxton spent New Year’s Eve 2024 partying in Dubai with Tracy Duhon, his rumored mistress, as well as two men who were then fugitives from US justice, one of whom had donated to Paxton. This indiscretion joins a long list of scandals that supporters of Paxton are willing to overlook to back the candidate (and, perhaps more importantly, keep Texas red). On the tour, they often invoked faith to make their case.

House Republican Darrell Issa, who served as chairman of the House Oversight Committee and worked with Paxton when he was a crusading state attorney general, stumped for the candidate on his swing from San Antonio to Georgetown. When I asked why he felt compelled to campaign for Paxton given the baggage, Issa echoed Pastor Mendez.

The line was lengthy to get into the rally in Corpus Christi.

“Is President Trump a man without flaws? No. But neither was Andy Jackson, neither was Bill Clinton, neither was Abraham Lincoln,” the congressman said. “So you judge somebody in elected office based on the honor that they bring to the office, the energy they bring, and the initiatives that they’re involved in, and that’s where [Paxton] excels. So am I going to say that this is somebody that, like our savior, was without sin? No. But I’m not somebody without sin, and neither are any of the voters.”

Some would argue that Paxton’s sins are more exotic than those of your ordinary Texan. He was charged with two counts of felony securities fraud by Texas prosecutors and in 2024 reached a deal to avoid trial that involved paying nearly $300,000 in restitution. Separately, the Texas House impeached him in 2023 on charges of bribery, corruption, and abusing his office. (Paxton has denied the allegations that led to his impeachment.) With state legislators under intense pressure from Trump, he was acquitted by the Senate. “Yes, it is true that my intervention through TRUTH SOCIAL saved Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton from going down,” Trump boasted afterward.

There’s also Paxton’s reputation for jet-setting: In his 11 years as attorney general, an Associated Press investigation found, Paxton spent an extraordinary amount of time traveling to plush locales including “California wine country, Caribbean resorts, and European tourism hot spots.” It cost the taxpayers at least $1.9 million to cover the expenses of his security detail. And then there was, of course, the 2013 surveillance video that caught Paxton pocketing a man’s $1,000 Montblanc pen. (His campaign called the incident a “mix-up,” and Paxton returned the pen after he was contacted by a sheriff’s deputy.)

Even some of the ads supporting him carry a bit of a shrug. Angela Paxton, who is suing her husband for divorce on “biblical grounds,” which is King James for “cheating,” appears in a spot urging Texans to vote Republican down the ticket. “Let’s talk about the elephant in the room,” she says at the start of the ad, which ends without any further mention of the elephant.

For many in the Republican establishment, these indiscretions have pushed Paxton beyond the pale, but that’s of little concern to the nominee’s supporters. “He may not have his life together,” said Janet, a Paxton backer from Corpus Christi, “but he’s better than the alternative.”

There was strong turnout for a Trump rally to support Paxton in Corpus Christi.

Kevin, a 62-year-old from the San Antonio suburbs, brought his wife to see Paxton speak in Selma. “I believe in this country there is a battle between good and evil,” he said. “I try to judge characters in a biblical sense.” Where does that leave Paxton, a man accused of violating quite a few of the commandments? “I’m okay with Ken Paxton,” Kevin said, waving a hand as I ran down the list of transgressions. “I believe Trump is the best president we’ve ever had. And we got to have people in there to support him.”

Jason Norton, 50, a bearded and tattooed pastor at King’s Trail Cowboy Church who delivered the prayer at Paxton’s stop north of Dallas, had a similarly permissive view of his alleged infidelity. “Christian does not mean perfect. Nobody would approve of that. And I’m sure he probably regrets it. But what he did hurt himself and his family. What Talarico’s doing will hurt the entire state.”

In a quaint historic town north of Fort Worth, I met Steve Bannon at the Texas headquarters of his War Room podcast. A longtime Paxton backer, Bannon has spent much of this campaign season in the state, broadcasting to the Trump base while helping to advise the Republican’s campaign. “We have not seen the data yet that shows part of our coalition actually coming out and voting against Trump,” Bannon told me. “I think most of them are staying home. That’s a huge win for us. If they come out and vote against us, we’re fucked.”

After Musk dumped $168 million into the race, Republican super PACs pumped out ads casting Talarico as too extreme for Texas. The Democrat’s disposition as a love-thy-trans-neighbor seminarian is a cover for something far more sinister, the argument goes. “Even the Bible says the devil comes as an angel of light,” Pastor Norton told me. “Just because somebody’s sweet doesn’t mean they’re right.”

It doesn’t help the Democrat that, like many in the party, he dabbled in the kind of heady progressive rhetoric trendy in the early 2020s, something anathema to red-state voters. Among the hits, of which Texans are regularly reminded in Paxton’s speeches, Trump’s posts, and endless campaign ads: Talarico’s remark in 2021 that “God is nonbinary”; his comment in the same year that there are “six really common biological sexes” and his description of himself as “a Christian who hates Christianity”; his 2022 run for reelection to the Texas House, which he said would be a vegan campaign, reasoning that reducing meat consumption was “necessary to fight climate change”; and his comment that the American flag is a “complicated symbol.”

Talarico has since walked back some of these lines, calling them “cringey” and “provocative.” But Musk’s money cannon ensures Texas voters are relentlessly confronted by them, and the race appears to have tightened in the final stretch of the campaign. For voters susceptible to these kinds of criticisms of Talarico, a Paxton rally provides a potent narrative. The notes from his stump speech that draw the loudest applause can drive the neutral observer to mild psychosis. We’re going to stop Sharia law from coming to Texas, goes one. The Pfizer vaccine was less than 1% effective, offers another. And of course: James Talarico wants to trans your kids.

Paxton was greeted by a sea of red hats as he arrived onstage.

That last bit is one of Paxton’s favorites, and it hit hard in some of the more incandescent-red counties we passed through. “I mean, this guy is really obsessed with transitions,” Paxton mused into the mic, winding up for the big delivery. “He wants to transition our kids, he wants to transition Texas off of the oil and gas industry.” (Wry pause.) “How about we transition him and his crazy ideas right out of Texas!” (Applause, cheers, hoots, and hollers as Paxton stepped back from the podium and smirked, almost bashfully, eyes darting back and forth as he soaked up the response.)

Some of the attacks on Talarico are more extreme. Paxton and a number of his campaign surrogates warned that the Democrat would help usher Sharia law into Texas. When I met with Bannon, he introduced me to Patriot Mobile cofounder Glenn Story, who’s active in Texas politics and counts Paxton as a friend. “I’m the one who told him to start talking about Sharia law,” Story said. “You go up to the Costco near me? Towelheads everywhere,” he said, using a slur for Muslims. “Come with me, I’ll show you.” Reluctant to spend my afternoon driving around looking for people who might appear Muslim, I declined.

At a stop in Sanger, a small town in a ruby-red county north of Dallas, the star of the warm-up acts was Brandon Gill, a young House Republican who has emerged as a favorite among the party’s hard-line base. He walked out to a roaring welcome, and one man shouted, “Brandon for president!” The heckle was deftly parried by Gill: “Let’s say Ken Paxton for Senate first.” As Gill ran through the list of well-worn criticisms of Talarico’s liberalism, Stacey, a woman in the crowd, shouted: “It’s demonic!”

“That’s right,” Gill replied. “It is demonic.”

Outside the venue, I spoke with Stacey and her friend Claire, two Paxton superfans from nearby who called themselves “MAHA moms.”

“He seems like a nice man,” Stacey said of Talarico. “He’s just misguided. If he was my neighbor, I’d vote for him to be on my HOA board. But not for the Senate.” They were confident that Paxton would keep the seat red. But Claire was concerned that her parents, both Republicans, had written in names in protest of Paxton’s candidacy.

On a good day, Ken Paxton is not a gifted orator. This was a bad day. It was Sunday night, the last stop of the weekend, and the candidate walked onto a stage at Schulman’s Movie Bowl Grille in Sherman, a highway town 60 miles north of Dallas, to speak to a few dozen supporters picking at tacos while leaning back in big pleather recliners.

By this point I had seen Paxton deliver his stump speech, a 15-minute case for his Senate candidacy, some half a dozen times. At Schulman’s Movie Bowl Grille, his delivery was pained. He seemed tired. He stumbled over his jokes, stopping and returning to the top after realizing he forgot the punch line. He struggled through a section meant to serve as a blistering indictment of his opponent, and he eventually spent several minutes looking down at the podium and listing Talarico’s crimes against Texas off a piece of paper as the crowd sat in silence.

At one point an elderly man with a walker had had enough.

“More enthusiasm!” he shouted from the audience.

Paxton looked up from his papers and out into the crowd. “What?”

“More enthusiasm!” the man shouted again.

“Yes,” Paxton said meekly. “We all need a little more enthusiasm to go to the polls.”

Trump never hesitates to remind voters at his rallies that Paxton “may not dress right, he may not talk perfectly, and he may not be the best-looking guy I’ve ever seen.” The candidate has a lazy eye, a casualty of a childhood accident in which a berry was thrown at his face, requiring multiple surgeries, and a subsequent college basketball injury. He presents as meek, almost sheepish, and speaks in a monotone that belies his reputation as a bare-knuckle radical. He slurs his words and stumbles over his lines.

Trump rallied the faithful for Paxton in Corpus Christi.

“Darrell,” he said at one stop, addressing Issa, “California arreddy has tt—you already have two senators, you d—we don’t need a third.” He’s less the guy you want to get a beer with, per the old campaign cliché, and more the guy you feel a bit sorry for when it’s his turn to address the room at a regional sales conference. At a Trump rally for Paxton in Lubbock, the president told the crowd that his praise of Jon Husted’s “very powerful ass” boosted the senator’s election campaign in Ohio. “Maybe I should do that with Ken Paxton,” he mused, “but I’m not sure your body is going to qualify.”

For Talarico and the Texas Democrats, who haven’t won a seat in the United States Senate since 1988, the Paxton campaign presents an extraordinary opportunity. “Billy Begala says: For the last 30 years, all you had to do to win in Texas is win the Republican primary and then go on vacation,” said veteran Democratic strategist Paul Begala, whose son Billy works in Texas politics. Paxton may have taken that truism a little too far. After announcing his candidacy for the Republican nomination in an appearance on Sean Hannity’s Fox News show, he “cleared his calendar and booked a hotel in Augusta, Georgia, during the storied Masters golf tournament,” according to the Associated Press, “a trip that cost Texas taxpayers at least $25,000.” Over July Fourth weekend, while Talarico hit the trail, Paxton was in Europe, sightseeing in London and Iceland with his rumored mistress. As Talarico raised tens of millions of dollars from small-dollar donations throughout the summer, Paxton struggled to keep up. A week before the general election, Talarico had raised $158 million to Paxton’s $39 million.

“Ken is a different type of candidate in modern times,” Bannon said. “He has a hardcore grassroots base, but he’s not a good fundraiser.” In the primary against Cornyn, Paxton “didn’t raise any serious money. This was primarily a grassroots-driven thing.”

Paxton, Bannon added, is “a populist fire-breather who is not particularly charismatic.”

The last-ditch operation to save the populist fire-breather who is not particularly charismatic came this week in the form of two Trump rallies. They may end up as the last major events of the campaign. On Thursday, the Paxton campaign announced it was canceling a rally in El Paso scheduled for Sunday, just two days before the election.

At the first of Trump’s appearances, in Corpus Christi, I watched from the press pen as Border Patrol union chief Paul Perez revved up the crowd with a question. “Is Texas Trump country?” he bellowed. A sea of bright red hats, bobbing in chaotic unison in a portside hangar, went ballistic. They had braved the rain and the dour mood spreading across the country to see a president in whom they very much still believed. But across Texas, the cracks in Trump’s support are deepening. Just two years after he beat Kamala Harris in the state by more than 1.5 million votes, The Economist’s poll tracker finds Trump’s approval in Texas is underwater by 18 points. This has fueled fears among the state’s Republicans that many of those who broke for Trump in 2024 could stay home this year or, God forbid, vote for the Democrat who thinks God is nonbinary.

Many rallygoers were decked out in full American flag regalia.

Another’s shirt echoed the Paxton campaign bus.

“Clearly, it has been a challenge for Ken to run during difficult personal times, but one of the reasons we admire him is that he is resilient,” said Matt Schlapp, the chairman of the Conservative Political Action Conference, who held a fundraiser for Paxton at his Virginia home last month. “He will win this race, but, like last time, there will be some fear and trembling along the way.”

Others in the coalition aren’t so sure. “Paxton is in deep shit,” Rich Baris, a pro-Trump pollster, said in a podcast appearance this week.

Trump may rally the base, but it’s hard to see how a historically unpopular president making the race about him helps with the broader electorate. After the president commanded Republicans to host a “Trumpapalooza” midterm convention that served more as a celebration of his presidency than as a typical party congress, Paxton privately told donors the event hurt his campaign. “When we did that convention—it dropped our numbers,” Paxton said, according to audio of his comments obtained by The New York Times. “Everybody’s numbers dropped. Right now, yeah, not good.”

At the rally in Corpus Christi, Trump spoke for more than an hour. He extolled the glories of his presidency and made little mention of Paxton. After airing a supercut video of Talarico’s cringiest comments, Trump turned to the crowd. “The only man standing between Texas and this raging lunatic is a man named President Donald J. Trump, thank you!” he said. As the crowd went wild, Trump added: “And I need the help of Paxton.”

On the Come and Take It bus tour, Paxton was less a glimpse of Trumpism’s future than a showcase of what might be left behind when Trump is gone. The familiar accommodations were still required: overlook the scandals, forgive the personal failings, keep faith with the fighter standing between you and the forces of darkness. But there was none of the showmanship that made those demands easier to accept in Trump. The Paxton cocktail is the controversy without the charisma—and a test of whether a record of pure allegiance to Trump is enough for a Republican to win Texas. Defeat may well announce the bitter end of pure Trumpism, or at least prove that Trump alone is the movement. When he leaves the White House in 2029, what will remain of it?

Paxton is not known for his oratory—or his skills as a fundraiser.
