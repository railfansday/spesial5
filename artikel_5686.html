What is holding back bidirectional EV charging as a grid resource?

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Let Utility Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Costs remain high and tech solutions are incomplete, but experts say careful utility program design and a new generation of vehicle charging solutions will bring value to both consumers and the grid.

Late last month, Ford confirmed that its midsize electric pickup, Fathom, will be able to deliver more than 10 kW of alternating-current power to a home when it hits the market next year.

Though it’s the sort of technical detail that may be lost on drivers more concerned with sticker price, styling or battery range, experts say it’s potentially game-changing for utilities hoping to harness the vast quantities of energy stored in hundreds of thousands of bidirectional-capable EVs on U.S. roads.

Drivers of EVs that can discharge AC power don’t need to install pricey, code-sensitive inverter equipment to back up their homes or send power back into the grid, said David Almeida, director of clean energy transportation at Pacific Gas & Electric Co. A Ford spokesperson told Utility Dive that the automaker’s pre-Fathom vehicle-to-home backup package retails for more than $5,000, not including professional installation. The Fathom’s package will cost up to about 60% less, they said.

“You take that cost from the customer [and reduce the] complexity of installation, and you make it a lot easier for them to take this option and … for the utility to tap into that,” Almeida said.

More accessible equipment is not the only precondition for bidirectional-capable EVs to realize their full potential, however. Utilities, regulators and automakers will also need to coordinate on enabling regulation, utility program design, product development and customer education, experts tell Utility Dive.

Automakers sell more than 20 bidirectional-capable light-duty EV models in the United States. Nearly all are “grid-parallel,” meaning they have the technical capability to both back up a home and export power to the grid, said Zach Woogen, executive director of the Vehicle-Grid Integration Council.

Some of these can be described as mass-market rides. The Chevrolet Equinox EV’s base trim retails for just over $35,000, about $16,000 less than the average cost of a new vehicle in 2026.

But nearly all require external inverters to convert their batteries’ direct-current power to AC flows that homes can use, Almeida said. Before this fall, the slow-selling Tesla Cybertruck — with a sticker price north of $80,000 — was the only AC-capable bidirectional EV on U.S. roads. Tesla began accepting orders in October for AC-capable versions of its more affordable Model 3 and Y vehicles, but drivers will still need a Powerwall home backup battery at an installed cost of around $12,000.

Low AC-capable vehicle stock is one reason why PG&E, which has more EVs than any other U.S. utility in its Northern California territory and aims to support 2 million bidirectional-capable vehicles by 2030, has seen limited uptake so far for its vehicle-to-everything program, Almeida said.

“We are still in the hundreds” of enrolled vehicles, including commercial vans, trucks and buses, he said.

PG&E provides up to $3,000 in upfront incentives for eligible residential customers to install bidirectional charging equipment at home, thousands less than the full cost of a DC-to-AC conversion package with installation.

Another reason is that, cost aside, installing a home DC-to-AC inverter is not as simple as mounting and wiring a new light fixture.

Some inverter models “take up quite a bit of space on the wall” and, at least in PG&E’s territory, must meet building codes requiring minimum setbacks from a home’s gas meter, Almeida said. That gives a homeowner with a tight garage or carport the difficult choice of moving their gas meter — if that’s even possible — or forgoing vehicle-to-home capability, he said.

“It’s not just that it’s costly,” Almeida said. “Any time you add more complexity, it’s going to make it that much less likely that the customer is going to do it.”

While disclaiming inside knowledge of automakers’ plans, Almeida said Tesla’s and Ford’s recent moves suggest other automakers are working on designs for AC-capable vehicles. New car designs can take five years or longer to move from concept to showroom, he said.

Ford and Tesla offer “a good signal of what the market is going to be,” he said. “I’d imagine that … other major automakers have [onboard AC capabilities] in the plan and they’re just not ready to share with us.”

At Ford’s invitation, PG&E in July teamed up with American Electric Power, BC Hydro, Consumers Energy and Exelon to develop what Dave McCreadie, Ford’s director of energy and grid services, called “scalable interconnection processes” ahead of Fathom’s 2027 release.

In an October blog post, McCreadie said the five utilities aligned on three key principles: clarifying when interconnection regulations should apply to bidirectional EVs, and specifically to require interconnection only for customers operating a vehicle in parallel with the grid rather than for charging or “isolated backup power;” updating interconnection procedures and/or regulations to accommodate newer standards, such as AC-capable vehicles; and maintaining collaboration between utilities, regulators and equipment manufacturers to reduce barriers to interconnection.

The working group was “a head start” for electric utilities in places where it expects the Fathom to sell well, Almeida said. After the midsize truck arrives, “it’s totally possible that we will see thousands of vehicles that have this kind of capability” on PG&E’s system, he added.

“We’ve been kind of in the ‘crawl’ stage. In the next year or two we’re going to be in the ‘walk’ stage before we run,” he said.

Bidirectional charging is one component of a portfolio of distributed resources that PG&E, with encouragement from state regulators, can use to get more value from an increasingly constrained grid, Almeida said.

“How can we tap into this technology to mitigate the need for us to build infrastructure to help save all of our customers money,” he said, paraphrasing a question PG&E has kicked around internally and in regulatory filings for years.

Electric vehicles play into other PG&E flexibility initiatives. Most customers participating in Flex Connect, its flexible interconnection program for larger distribution-connected loads, are commercial EV-charging sites, a PG&E manager responsible for the program told Utility Dive earlier this year. PG&E also serves more than 70 bidirectional-capable electric school buses in Oakland, California, which has one of the country’s biggest fleets today. It plans to add infrastructure for more than 230 electric buses over the next two years across three sites in San Francisco, a particularly constrained load pocket.

Utilities, regulators and state energy offices elsewhere see value in private and commercial EVs.

In New England, residential and business customers with enrolled thermostats or stationary batteries have for years taken advantage of the region’s ConnectedSolutions virtual power plant program, which pays battery owners upwards of $200/kW-year to participate in both traditional peak-shaving and more targeted local events. This year, the Massachusetts Clean Energy Center deployed dozens of fully subsidized bidirectional chargers to bring electric vehicles into the fold. One Highland Electric Fleets bus earned nearly $12,000 per summer in a two-year pilot earlier this decade, though “more like $6,000 per year is the norm” moving forward, company CEO Duncan McIntyre told Utility Dive.

Still, even after accounting for equipment installation costs, “that is not hard to make pencil — it is a lot of money if you’re able to show up” for called events, Woogen said.

Regional regulators keep upping the ante. Next month, the Connecticut Public Utilities Regulatory Authority is set to expand the state’s energy storage compensation framework to include commercial EV charging sites. The program would pay $325/kW-year for the first five years of enrollment and $175/kW-year for the next five.

The proposed decision would also clarify bidirectional EV interconnection standards, including for residential customers. But residential EVs aren’t yet eligible for demand-response compensation in Connecticut, highlighting the challenge of bringing regulation in line with technical capability, Woogen said.

In fact, only California and Maryland have adopted vehicle-to-grid interconnection standards for AC-capable EVs, according to Woogen’s group.

“In some cases, technology and customer interest are ahead of the market and regulatory capability to support [them]. That is the long pole in the tent — it’s very fragmented, and that tends to slow things down,” said Greg Guthridge, global power and utilities customer experience transformation leader for EY.

That said, utilities and their partners may also need to work on “consumer interest” through education, incentives and innovative program design, Guthridge said. Recent EY market research found that only 18% of U.S. customers have an “immediate interest” in energy-flexibility services, a broad category that includes bidirectional EV charging, he said.

“We have a bit of a challenge here. … We need to convince consumers that there’s value. Either they don't understand that or they're not buying into [it] just yet,” Guthridge said.

EY’s research suggests customers will be more receptive to “packaged” bidirectional charging programs that meet certain criteria, Guthridge said.

One is financial benefit: generous compensation for participation, ideally with an upfront component so customers feel they’re benefiting early. Control over or at least visibility into a program is another. Customers want to understand what’s happening in their home — and in this case, to their vehicle — and be looped into the process, Guthridge said. They also want participation that isn’t inconvenient or otherwise detrimental to their lifestyle or business activities, he said.

For inspiration, utilities and their automaker partners can look to companies like Rheem, which sells water heaters that play a significant role in some utilities’ distributed resource portfolios and has a reputation for “working with the utility closely to allow a seamless, effortless adoption process,” Guthridge said.

In that light, Ford seems to be making a shrewd move to get key utility partners onboard before Fathom hits the road.

“Automakers have an incentive to make the process as beneficial as possible for customers,” Guthridge said.
