Pat Cummins' remarkable 68-minute press conference took a clear-eyed and devastating aim at Usman Khawaja's integrity - Australia are in a civil war with no obvious resolution, writes LAWRENCE BOOTH

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Published: 05:20 EST, 13 November 2026 | Updated: 05:21 EST, 13 November 2026

Pat Cummins is either the greatest actor in world sport – or the claim that the whole Australian team knew about the sandpaper plot in South Africa eight years ago is a figment of Usman Khawaja’s imagination.

After Cummins’s remarkable press conference in Sydney on Friday morning, in which he called Khawaja’s various assertions ‘complete and utter nonsense’ and questioned the validity of their friendship, it was hard to discern any common ground, much less spot a middle way.

According to the blue corner, everyone was in on the scam, sandpaper was used not just at Cape Town but in the previous Test at Port Elizabeth, and there was a team meeting to discuss the question of collective responsibility.

According to the red, those who knew about the plan did not include the bowlers, nothing untoward happened at Port Elizabeth, and there was no meeting.

The only thing that can be said for certain after another tumultuous 24 hours for Australian cricket was that the two corners are now polar opposites, each eyeing the other with suspicion and incredulity. Barring an improbable climbdown, the twain can never meet.

Cummins, it must be said, eased into his work after an emotional start behind a lectern at the Cricket Central venue in Sydney’s Olympic Park. He began by stating his basic position, then invited questions, which for the next hour and a bit he handled with aplomb and a degree of statesmanship.

Pat Cummins held a remarkable press conference in Sydney to address the sandpaper claims

Speaking for more than an hour, Cummins dismissed Khawaja’s various assertions as ‘complete and utter nonsense’ and questioned the validity of their friendship

This will not please those who still find it impossible to believe that Cummins and his fellow seamers on that trip, Josh Hazlewood and Mitchell Starc, knew nothing of the scheme that led to bans for three batsmen – Steve Smith, David Warner and Cameron Bancroft.

After all, as any bowler will tell you, the idea that the condition of their most prized possession might be altered without their noticing is as absurd as trying to persuade a batsman to walk out without his favourite willow.

And yet. Cummins stuck to his guns, in contrast to Khawaja’s appearance on the ABC on Wednesday evening, when he repeatedly declined to name Cummins as one of the guilty party. And while Khawaja reacted to his TV grilling by cancelling media engagements, Cummins made it clear he would be answering every question that came his way.

Whichever side you believe, and whatever the doubts that remain, his press conference grabbed the momentum from Team Khawaja.

It also made the previous day’s appearance by Mitchell Marsh, Australia’s white-ball captain for the next three weeks against England, all the more bizarre. By refusing to engage with any of the questions raised by Khawaja’s autobiography, Marsh – himself a veteran of South Africa 2018 – did little to dispel the suspicion that Khawaja had a point.

And that in turn encouraged the view that Cummins, who called his own press conference without the knowledge of Cricket Australia, might be about to come clean.

Instead, he doubled down on the denial he and his bowling colleagues had issued in 2021, but now went further, taking a clear-eyed and devastating aim at Khawaja’s integrity.

‘Usman is a friend, but friends aren’t casual with each other’s lives and reputations,’ said Cummins. ‘He’s dealt with the way he’s presented his story in a very casual manner and it affects a lot of people. That’s what I’m grappling with at the moment, because I don’t think that’s OK. It’s not what friends do.’

Cummins plainly intended this to be his final word on the matter, but the problem facing Australian cricket is that the sore may never properly heal.

David Warner then hit back at Cummins and accused him of 'throwing Khawaja under the bus'

Only a couple of hours after Cummins finished, Warner – at Perth’s Optus Stadium as a TV pundit for the first ODI between Australia and England – was being asked for his view, and he didn’t hold back, repeating his belief that Khawaja’s version of events was ‘the truth’.

He added: ‘I really, really support him. I actually feel for him at the moment. I feel that the captain today threw Uzzy under the bus and I’m very, very disappointed about that.’

Disappointment. It’s mutual, and it’s everywhere. Cummins provided a brief distraction, but it doesn’t change the fact that this is a civil war with no obvious resolution.

Offered the chance to kick Australian cricket while it was down, England made humiliating contact with fresh air on the opening day of their eight-match white-ball trip.

Only hours after Pat Cummins had added a twist to the sandpaper saga by dismissing Usman Khawaja’s claims that the whole squad knew about the ball-tampering plans in South Africa eight years ago as ‘complete and utter nonsense’, Harry Brook’s side were blown away for 131 in the first ODI – having at one stage been 65 for eight.

Australia then romped home by seven wickets with more than 26 overs in hand to take a 1–0 lead into the second game of this three-match series in Adelaide on Sunday. For England, anyway, complete and utter nonsense felt about right.

It is not quite a year since they began their Ashes campaign here at the Optus Stadium by being bowled out in 32.5 overs in their first innings and 34.4 in their second – before stumbling to a 4–1 defeat.

Now, they obligingly split the difference, dismissed in 33.4 as Mahli Beardman, a 21-year-old fast bowler from Dunsborough, a couple of hours drive down the coast from Perth, picked up six for 34 – the best figures for Australia by an ODI debutant.

His first victim was Joe Root, caught behind for a painful 17 off 36 balls, and his second Brook, who had made only a single when he fidgeted fatally outside off stump.

That might have been enough to satisfy most newcomers, but Beardman had barely started. Jordan Cox made two before edging a poke, and Sam Curran top-edged a pull to fine leg for a duck.

When Liam Dawson, yet to score, squeezed a drive to backward point, Beardman had five wickets in 28 balls – not bad for a player whose only previous taste of international cricket had come in a T20 in Pakistan in January.

He then ended a lively last-wicket stand of 29 by removing Jofra Archer, who had just driven him down the ground for four. It was the only authentic boundary England managed off his bowling: the other, a streaky inside edge from Root, summed up the problems posed by his pace, bounce and accuracy. As bids for a place on next summer’s Ashes tour go, this was hard to ignore.

‘Laying in bed last night, that’s not what I had planned,’ said Beardman, who was taken under his wing by Australian fast-bowling legend Dennis Lillee after a mutual friend spotted him as a young teenager bowling at a bin down the side of a house. ‘It was an incredible game and it all happened so fast.’

If England had learned lessons from their collapses in the last year’s Test here, they showed little sign of being able to implement them. Players drove on the up, as they had so heedlessly a year earlier, and failed to cope with the Perth bounce.

England head coach Brendon McCullum was critical of his side's failure to adapt to the pitch, and called on them to learn from their mistakes going forward.

'It’s a bit like batting on a trampoline,' said McCullum. 'There are certain shots that are very difficult to execute. We talked about trying to put those away. We probably knew we were in for a low-scoring contest, but we expected to get a few more runs than 130.

'We're disappointed but there are some good lessons for us. If you look towards the World Cup in South Africa there’s going to be some pitches there that will test us from a bounce point of view.'

And all this after they had played a warm-up game at the WACA – a grievous omission before the Ashes – and trained with such intensity that white-ball mentor Kevin Pietersen had declared it the best practice session he had seen from an England team.

In the event, their top-scorer was Gus Atkinson, who hit an unbeaten 29 from No 10, including England’s only six.

Mitchell Marsh, stroke-less in the previous day’s press conference, put that into perspective by clearing the ropes four times during a 29-ball half-century, while Cooper Connolly – dropped by Tom Banton at midwicket on five – hit two sixes himself.

England’s only consolation had come at the start of Australia’s reply, when Atkinson bowled Travis Head first ball off the inside edge. Head it was who had taken care of Australia’s fourth-innings chase here a year ago, thrashing a 69-ball century that knocked the stuffing out of Ben Stokes’s attack.

Instead, it was Marsh who went for his shots, stopped only when he flicked Jamie Overton to Ben Duckett at deep midwicket, by which time Australia were already halfway there. For a few hours, at least, no one was talking about sandpaper.
