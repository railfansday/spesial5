The 13 Best London Museums to Visit

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

London’s numerous museums feature some of the best art, history, science, and cultural collections in the world. With many of these institutions free to enter, paying a visit to one or more can even be an inexpensive day out.

Many of the museums listed here have recently gone through major revamps, and over the coming year there will be even more destinations to visit, with the long-awaited London Museum coming to the historic Smithfield Market and the Museum of Shakespeare opening on the site of the Curtain Playhouse.

If you’re looking for extraordinary cultural objects from the dawn of civilization, head over to the venerable British Museum. There you’ll find such treasures as an impressive collection of ancient Mesopotamian items; the second-century BC Rosetta Stone, which provided the key to deciphering ancient Egyptian hieroglyphics; and the fifth-century BC ancient Greek Parthenon Marbles. Look up to see British starchitect Norman Foster’s amazing glass roof, completed in 2000. The British Museum holds more than eight million objects covering over two million years of human history, with about 80,000 objects on view at any given time. If you feel overwhelmed with the choice of what to see, book a tour and enjoy the luxury of being in the hands of a knowledgeable guide.

Right in the center of London on Trafalgar Square, the National Gallery offers artworks dating from 1260 right up to the 20th century—from Da Vinci to David, Canaletto to Constable, Michelangelo to Monet, Rousseau to Rubens, Cezanne to Seurat, Vermeer to Velazquez to Van Gogh, and that’s just a small fraction of its offerings. And it’s free to enter. For a fee, there are also larger exhibitions in the much acclaimed, recently renovated Sainsbury Wing. The Sainsbury Wing is quite the character itself, of course, ever since in 1984 King Charles III, while still Prince of Wales, described the initial proposed design for it as a “monstrous carbuncle on the face of a much-loved and elegant friend.” The plan was altered, and what opened in 1991 is what you see now: classical mannerism in a sensitively designed addition to the main gallery’s domed facade. After your visit, head to the St-Martin-in-the-Fields Crypt just over the road for a good-value hot canteen meal, and linger in this famous neoclassical church by 18th-century architect James Gibbs for one of its candlelight concerts.

The beating heart of the city, the Tower of London is steeped in so much history it’s dizzying—from the White Tower’s inception as a fortified palace by William the Conqueror on his invasion after the Battle of Hastings, in 1066, to its long-term use as a prison. King Henry VIII’s wife Anne Boleyn was imprisoned and executed there in 1536 after she was accused of adultery; Guy Fawkes, Robert Catesby, and the Gunpowder Plotters were kept in the Tower in 1605 after their failed plan to blow up the Houses of Parliament; and deputy Nazi Party leader Rudolf Hess was confined there in 1941. The Crown Jewels have been safeguarded at the Tower since 1661 and are immaculately displayed; other highlights include Anne Boleyn’s final resting place, an incredible collection of armor, twisted metal artifacts from the 1666 Great Fire of London, the Medieval Palace (in use until 1307) that recently opened to the public, and of course the Yeomen Warders, or Beefeaters, the retired military personnel who run the place. And if that isn’t enough, there’s also a fantastic immersive experience about the Gunpowder Plot in the old tunnels just outside the walls of the Tower, a superb addition that should be booked in advance.

A shrine to all things bright and beautiful and to creatures great and small, the Natural History Museum in South Kensington (around the corner from the Science Museum) was founded with proceeds from the Great Exhibition of 1851. Quite apart from what’s inside, this museum’s facade encapsulates its purpose: Stand in front of the building and see how many extinct and living species you can spot— there are saber-toothed cats, pterodactyls, ancient palaeotherium (stocky mammals with a beaklike snout), extinct and modern fish, lizards, goats, lions, and wolves. Opened in 1881, this museum was fast dubbed a “cathedral of nature,” and it still is, filled to the brim with flora and fauna, including a magnificent blue whale skeleton suspended from the ceiling. The museum holds an astonishing collection of specimens, 80 million in total, only a fraction of which can be displayed at any one time. Head to the all-time favorite Dinosaur galleries, complete with an animatronic Tyrannosaurus rex; then take your pick from volcanoes, fossils, human evolution, mammals, fish, amphibians, reptiles, minerals, and more. If the sheer amount of stuff is daunting, start with the Treasures gallery for the cream of the crop.

There is so much to see at the Victoria and Albert Museum in South Kensington that you just have to prioritize. With astonishing collections from Britain and across the world, the V&A holds examples of jewelry, design, fashion, art, and architecture, from medieval times to the modern era. One of the timeless highlights of this museum is the Cast Courts: The Victorians were mad about taking casts of classic sculptures, and we reap the rewards today. Built specifically to house the enormous reproductions of Trajan’s Column from ancient Rome, these monumental courts also hold an electrifying cast of Michelangelo’s David. Make sure you also see the Raphael Cartoons housed in their own gallery, and find out which William Morris works the museum currently has on show; his enduring Arts and Crafts textile and wallpaper designs are widely loved. And allow at least half an hour for a good browse of the V&A shop. It’s one of the best museum stores in existence.

Tate Modern has been a fixture of the art world since it was inaugurated in the year 2000 in an ex-power station, an ambitious undertaking by the Swiss starchitects Herzog and De Meuron. There’s a lot to see here; the best approach may be to concentrate on either the collections or the special exhibitions and not attempt to absorb it all in one sitting. Collection wise, don’t miss Surrealist Salvador Dalí’s 1938 Lobster Telephone; Unique Forms of Continuity in Space (1913, cast 1972) by Italian futurist Umberto Boccioni; Composition (1913) by British Vorticist Wyndham Lewis; the notorious Fountain by Marcel Duchamp (1917, replica 1964); the calming color fields of Mark Rothko’s Seagram murals (commissioned in 1958); and the classic Pop Art punch Whaam! (1963) by Roy Lichtenstein.

A treat for kids and adults alike, the Science Museum is always captivating. Wander into the Energy Hall for a hit of seductive steam-driven engines and then into the freshly reopened Space section to see space capsules from the 1960s to today. For those wanting a more interactive experience, there is the Wonderlab, for visitors 7 to 14 years old, and Power Up, a hands-on gaming experience for anyone over the age of 5, where guests can play on 160 consoles spanning five decades. (Be sure to book the interactive galleries ahead of time.) There’s an IMAX Cinema, too, with one of the biggest screens in Europe. The Science Museum has redone many of its galleries in recent years, including the excellent Energy Revolution, about the history of energy production, with examples of an antique electric car and offshore energy near the coast of Scotland; and the stunning Mathematics gallery, housed in a wickedly futuristic design by the late architect Zaha Hadid, showing objects such as intriguing handheld mathematical instruments, a 1929 experimental aircraft, early computers, and coding machines. There’s much more, covering such subjects as flight, clockmaking, science from 1550 to 1800, engineering, and medicine. And if you still want more, you can stay the night in the museum’s current season of Astronights, for space-obsessed children and their parents.

Founded by sugar magnate Henry Tate and opened in 1897, Tate Britain holds cornerstone works of the Victorian circle of Pre-Raphaelites, including Ophelia (1851–2) by John Everett Millais, John William Waterhouse’s 1888 portrayal of The Lady of Shalott, and lesser-known works by Dante Gabriel Rossetti, who exhumed his wife’s corpse just to recover an unpublished poetry manuscript with which she’d been buried. If you want to delve deeper into darkness, seek out the contorted forms of Francis Bacon’s 1944 work Three Studies for the Figures at the Base of a Crucifixion. Then recover by contemplating the gentle forms of British sculptor Henry Moore. Also in the collection are Torso in Metal from the Rock Drill (1913–15) by Jacob Epstein, a pivotal Vorticist work; Fall (1963) by Bridget Riley, one of her signature Op Art illusions; and the bright tones of A Bigger Splash (1967) by David Hockney.

Plunge into the decadent rococo interiors of the Wallace Collection with feel-good artworks and exquisitely crafted armor displayed in luxurious settings. Make sure you see The Swing (Les hasards heureux de l’escarpolette) (1767–68) by the Rococo hedonist Jean-Honoré Fragonard—it’s one of the sexiest paintings ever made—as well as the museum’s fabulously sumptuous collection of François Boucher paintings. The Laughing Cavalier (1624) by Dutch portraitist Frans Hals, enigmatic and engaging, also adorns the Wallace’s walls.

Entering the Courtauld’s permanent collection feels like getting together for a few beers with a bunch of old school friends—people you know, love, and will always relish the company of. With all the greats from the Renaissance masters to the Postimpressionists, the Courtauld shows you an old friend everywhere you look. There’s plenty to reacquaint yourself with, including Adam and Eve (1526) by Lucas Cranach the Elder, A Bar at the Folies-Bergère (1882) by Édouard Manet, Self-Portrait with Bandaged Ear (1889) by Vincent van Gogh, and a strong array of works by Paul Cezanne, Edgar Degas, Paul Gauguin, Claude Monet, Auguste Renoir, Georges Seurat, and many more.

Within the concrete jungle of the Southbank Centre nestles the Hayward Gallery, which consistently delivers excellent contemporary works in its novel 1960s concrete environment. There is not permanent collection and all temporary shows are ticketed. Pop over to the British Film Institute afterwards for a movie, or to the Royal Festival Hall for a meal or a performance.

From 1512 until the mid-18th century, Greenwich was the headquarters for the British navy. First established under King Henry VIII, the Royal Navy became key to Britain’s success as a nation in trade and warfare alike, and its base was always Greenwich, now a UNESCO World Heritage site. Among the noteworthy attractions is the Queen’s House, built for Anne of Denmark (1574–1619) and designed by the so-called father of British architecture, Inigo Jones. (And if you approach from the river, you’ll see the remarkable symmetry in the buildings that flank the house, one of Christopher Wren’s classic feats of architectural wonder.) Once a royal residence, the Queen’s House now holds an excellent collection of art, including the 1588 Armada Portrait of Elizabeth I sitting in regal splendor before scenes showing the ruined Spanish Armada and the victorious British fleet. You can also see 19th-century master J.M.W. Turner’s largest canvas, The Battle of Trafalgar (1822), only recently put on display.

But that’s only one small part of the Greenwich complex. Don’t miss England’s answer to the Sistine Chapel at the Old Royal Naval College: the Painted Hall (1707–26), James Thornhill’s masterpiece, reopened to the public in 2019 after major conservation. For a comprehensive history of Britain’s maritime exploits, from sailing the seven seas and polar exploration to warfare and global trade, visit the National Maritime Museum, whose highlights include the Trafalgar Coat, the uniform that Admiral Horatio Nelson wore on his deathbed in 1805. Then visit the Cutty Sark, the fastest sailing ship of its time. Built in Scotland in 1869, she traded tea from China with a typical round trip taking just seven to nine months. And then, to tie the whole site together, head to the iconic onion dome of the Royal Observatory, where time was first standardized, in 1884. Step on the prime meridian line, gratis, or buy advance tickets to see the Great Equatorial Telescope, installed in 1893.

Complete with its own ride through the old underground tunnels used to transport mail, the Postal Museum tells the story of the Royal Mail, which might not sound immediately enticing, but this museum does it so well, it’s worth a visit. Its manageable size, brilliant story, and ride on the Mail Rail, with history explained via engaging projections on the tunnel walls, make this museum a gem and superb for visitors of all ages.
