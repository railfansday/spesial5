Apache Kafka Tutorial: KRaft, Docker & Consumer Groups

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Posted on Nov 4 • Originally published at blog.naveedkumbhar.com

Originally published on Naveed Ahmed's Engineering Blog.

Apache Kafka is often described as a messaging system.

That's partly true, but understanding it as a distributed event log gives you a much clearer picture of how it works.

Producers write records to partitioned topics. Brokers store those records, and consumers read them at their own pace.

Unlike a traditional work queue, Kafka can retain events after they've been consumed, allowing different applications to process or replay them independently.

Let's walk through the key concepts and a small hands-on example.

A simplified architecture looks like this:

Each partition is an ordered sequence of records.

Kafka uses offsets to identify record positions within a partition.

If you have three partitions, you can have up to three actively assigned consumers within one consumer group for that topic.

Adding more consumers than partitions doesn't create additional partition-processing parallelism.

Modern Apache Kafka uses KRaft to manage cluster metadata without an external ZooKeeper deployment.

KRaft controllers form a quorum responsible for metadata decisions, while brokers handle client requests and partition storage.

This separation matters when you're investigating broker failures, partition leadership changes, or controller health.

This gives you a simple environment for learning partitions and offsets.

It isn't a highly available Kafka cluster.

You now have a local Kafka workflow using a producer, topic, and consumer.

Running one broker on a laptop is easy compared with managing a replicated cluster.

For Kubernetes, operators such as Strimzi can help manage cluster lifecycle, but they don't remove the need for careful storage and capacity planning.

Consumer lag tells you how far a consumer group is behind the latest available partition offsets.

The group must exist and have committed offsets for meaningful output.

If lag is increasing, check whether event production exceeds processing capacity.

Also investigate slow downstream dependencies, consumer exceptions, frequent rebalances, and partition counts.

Avoid resetting offsets without understanding the risk of skipping or replaying records.
