The Subpoenas Are Coming, the Subpoenas Are Coming: Tech Execs Brace for Congressional Crackdown, Say Lobbyists

Tanggal: 2026-11-11

On Thursday, I was on the phone with a source when he suddenly sounded like he’d gotten the wind knocked out of him. We were in the middle of talking about antitrust policy when he exhaled and said, “Wow. Worst one-day destruction of value in history. Just got the Wall Street Journal alert.”

The tech world—and the markets built on top of it—is still figuring out how to feel about last week’s momentous Democratic midterm sweep. My Republican sources, unsurprisingly, reported that the vibes in their ranks are “horrendous.” Anyone who works with data centers is just about ready to throw in the towel on planet Earth and take their infrastructure efforts to space, with voters having opposed data center construction via 40 different ballot measures. And then there’s the more general rebuke of the future the tech industry is building, and the possible implications for an economy that’s increasingly come to revolve around it.

Indeed, it seems that the debate over AI has really, truly broken through to the mainstream this year: According to an NBC exit poll, 56% of voters called AI and data centers a “crucial or major factor” in their votes for Congress. Amid the free-for-all of President Trump’s first two years back in office, AI companies have been allowed to develop their technology largely free of regulatory oversight, but they may now be forced to pump the brakes.

In a lengthy interview with Ross Douthat on CBS’s 60 Minutes, Trump said he was open to working with Democrats to implement some AI guardrails. Still, no one I spoke to this week told me they expect to see sweeping AI legislation—unless, as two different tech Democrats suggested, there is an AI-caused catastrophe that “scares the shit out of us.”

Democratic Senators like Bernie Sanders and Elizabeth Warren have already begun making plans to investigate tech corporations.

In the absence of such an event, “we expect [new AI laws] to be narrow and bipartisan. Child safety, AI agent liability, and predeployment testing, for example, all have Republican cosponsors,” said Christina Ho, an executive at the financial auditing firm Oath Verified.

Meanwhile, even more explicitly MAGA-coded industries could see more concrete crackdowns. Those in “the crypto world,” who spent the first year of Trump 2.0 flying high, now “realize they’re screwed,” said an executive at a conservative think tank—especially as famed crypto critic Sherrod Brown makes his big return to the Senate with, as one Democratic strategist put it, an “axe to grind.”

Defense-tech founders and investors may also find themselves toning down their rhetoric as the testosterone-fueled “Department of War” era comes to an end with the possible impeachment of Secretary Pete Hegseth. In recent years, “dual use” has become a bit of a “dirty word,” said Cody Huggins, a partner at Scout Ventures. He suspects that promoting the commercial applications of military technologies, from satellites to drones, “will come back to be a little bit more sexy than it has been.”

Perhaps the biggest winners of the post-midterms period will be the DC law firms brought on to help manage the flood of subpoenas promised by politicians like Bernie Sanders and Elizabeth Warren. “AI executives might be called to testify,” said Ho, adding, “Companies would be wise to prepare their executives for the witness table before they prepare for any new laws.”

A Democratic tech lobbyist told me that one of his clients has already received a list of questions from a congressional committee. “They’re not waiting to have gavels,” he said.

Luther Lowe, the head of public policy at the start-up accelerator Y Combinator, is hoping to see a return to the antitrust revolution once promised by former FTC chair Lina Khan. “I think some of that stuff could come back online,” he said, referring to antitrust legislation that then Senate majority leader Chuck Schumer declined to bring to a vote in 2022.

In the future, tech executives who went all in on MAGA (right after going all in on woke) may want to consider some age-old advice: just be yourself. “Companies need to stop thinking about political outcomes as permanent conditions. Regulations change, governments change, and priorities change. That’s not new,” said Andrew Peek, CEO of the investing platform Tilt. “What’s different now is the sheer amount of money being committed to technologies whose economics are still evolving. If your entire strategy only works under one political scenario, that’s a pretty big risk to be taking.”

Still, there was one midterm fight in which the tech world definitively won out: the one over Proposition 40, or California’s so-called billionaire tax, which was defeated. Some suspect that the hundreds of millions of dollars spent by Silicon Valley billionaires to defeat the measure could backfire. “There’s a growing wave of anger and resentment from the working class that is coming for them,” said the Democratic strategist. The irony of spending so much money to fight the tax, he said, is that if it had passed, it could have “temporarily removed some of the pressure in the pressure cooker.” All that money having been spent to shut down the tax, he warned, “is gonna make things much worse.”
