10 things to know about saunas, from stress relief to dehydration risks

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

A Cleveland Clinic family medicine doctor explains what sauna heat may do for stress, the heart, and pain, and when to skip a session.

Picture walking out of a grueling workout and into a small room filled with dry heat. Sweat beads on your skin within minutes. People have sought out rooms of this kind for centuries. A visitor may hope for more from the heat than damp skin. Anyone who climbs onto that hot bench has reason to ask what those minutes accomplish. A good sweat is easy to notice. Whether the heat does anything for the body or mind beyond that is harder to judge from inside a towel. The question matters most to the people who make the visit a regular habit, and to anyone weighing whether to start.

Warmth can harm as well as soothe, and the line between the two shifts from one sauna to the next. Each runs at its own temperature and uses a distinct style of heating. The surrounding environment adds a third variable. The three factors set the guidelines for a safe session, and anyone who ignores them invites overheating. A hopeful bather hears plenty about what a sauna might fix but has little way to tell which promises rest on research and which rest on hunches. Certain people also need a doctor's input before they sit down. A reliable guide has to separate what studies support from what they hint at, and it has to say who belongs in that group.

Cleveland Clinic, which describes itself as a nonprofit academic medical center, published a health explainer on saunas on Aug. 12, 2024, under its Health Essentials banner. Amy Zack, MD, a family medicine provider, supplies its medical commentary and outlines what time in the heat may offer and how to avoid trouble there. The article cites findings without naming papers, sample sizes, or numeric results, and it frames each benefit in hedged terms. Its scope covers eight possible benefits, the main types of saunas, the question of fat burning, the hazards involved, and three tips for users. The 10 items that follow draw on that material: the eight benefits, then the weight question and the precautions worth knowing before taking a seat.

Anxiety may ease for sauna users, and family medicine provider Amy Zack, MD, points to evidence that saunas help people under heavy stress at work or in their personal lives. In her reading, the sauna experience appears to lessen anxiety. Her remarks cover pressure from the job and pressure from life outside it. She treats both as situations where time in the heat may make a difference.

Experts have not settled why a small, heated room would calm a stressed mind. Dr. Zack notes that several theories compete to explain the effect. One she raises centers on the soothing atmosphere of the sauna itself. Her account brings the setting into the picture alongside the temperature, which suggests the relief may come in part from the surroundings a person sits in.

Sleep may improve with sauna use, studies indicate. A better night's rest would stretch the benefit past the minutes a person spends on the bench and into the hours that follow.

Quiet reflection carries its own weight in Dr. Zack's telling. "Giving yourself that kind of introspective time is extremely successful in helping to mitigate anxiety, depression and burnout," she says. Her claim reaches past anxiety to two other strains, depression and burnout, that she credits such a break with reducing. Taken together, her remarks suggest a sauna's mental payoff may depend as much on the pause it creates as on the hot air around it.

Sauna heat may strengthen the heart and help keep blood pressure within a healthy range, studies suggest. Both possible improvements flow from the body's response to temperature during a session. The person on the bench sets them in motion without lifting, running, or pedaling.

Dr. Zack traces the effect to a chain of physical reactions. Heat speeds the heart rate and sets off the sweating familiar to any sauna visitor. In her account, those reactions add up to a physiologic shift that resembles exercise. The same exposure, she explains, prompts the body to release anti-inflammatory agents.

Physical activity offers the clearest yardstick for what a sauna asks of the heart. UCLA Health reports that sitting in a sauna, along with the quicker heartbeat it brings, widens blood vessels and boosts circulation, much as a light or moderate workout does. The same outlet reports that findings gathered over several decades indicate sauna bathing produces a prompt, beneficial drop in blood pressure. On that view, a seated session and a gentle workout place comparable demands on the circulatory system.

Evidence for the heart claims remains incomplete, and Dr. Zack says so without hedging. She calls cardiovascular health an area that needs more research and leaves no doubt about that need. Her caveat follows her own account of how heat acts on the body. The placement marks the mechanism she describes as a working explanation awaiting confirmation.

Heart conditions change the picture for some would-be bathers. Dr. Zack advises skipping the sauna after a recent heart attack, and people with certain heart conditions belong on the list of those who should check with a provider before going in.

As things stand, a sauna session works as a possible complement to exercise for heart health, with too little proof to stand in for a workout.

Sauna sessions may relieve pain and stiffness for people who live with rheumatoid arthritis or ankylosing spondylitis, studies indicate. Ankylosing spondylitis is a form of arthritis that inflames the joints and ligaments of the spine. Its most common symptoms are pain and stiffness in the lower back, the hip, or both. Swelling and irritation in the spinal joints and surrounding tissue can leave the spine feeling rigid. Easing stiffness would target one of the two complaints most common among people with the condition.

Chronic pain outside those two diagnoses may respond to sauna heat as well. Dr. Zack points to some small studies that found such gains. She singles out dry and infrared saunas, which researchers believe push more blood into the muscles and calm muscle spasms. In her account, both types have shown results against chronic back pain. Her own description keeps the claim modest, and the benefit remains a possibility for any single patient.

Dry and infrared saunas deliver heat at different intensities. Traditional dry saunas burn wood or use electric heaters and run between 150 F and 195 F (65.55 C and 90.55 C). The majority keep humidity below 20%. Infrared models rely on lamps and settle at a cooler 110 F to 135 F (43.33 C to 57.22 C). Dr. Zack notes that infrared light leaves the surrounding air unheated and generates warmth inside the body. The gap between the two matters for anyone who struggles with intense heat. An infrared booth offers a cooler route to the same back-pain benefits she describes.

Ankylosing spondylitis has no cure. Its treatment options aim to control symptoms and may include exercise, physical or occupational therapy, and pain medication. A sauna's possible boost to muscle blood flow fits that symptom-focused approach and could give people with the condition another option to discuss with a provider.

Sore muscles tend to respond to heat, says family medicine provider Amy Zack, MD. Her wording describes a frequent pattern without making a promise, and it leaves room for aches that outlast a session. The article counts calmer muscles among four possible payoffs that studies link to sauna use, a group that also includes lower stress, a sturdier heart, and pain relief.

Brain-derived neurotrophic factor, or BDNF, may explain the effect, according to the article. The body releases this substance when it encounters heat, and a sauna supplies warmth in abundance. The explainer does not describe how BDNF acts on tired tissue. The link remains a working idea, and readers should treat it as one.

Evidence for muscle relief looks slimmer than the support behind the pain-relief benefit. Dr. Zack offers her point as a general observation, and the article attaches no study findings to it. Sauna use for chronic pain, by contrast, draws on studies of people with specific joint conditions. A bather who expects looser muscles from the heat is acting on a reasonable hunch with a plausible chemical explanation behind it.

Soreness that surfaces after a delay following strenuous exercise carries its own name, delayed onset muscle soreness, or DOMS. The condition is a familiar companion for people who push their muscles hard during exercise. Its late arrival shapes what a post-workout sauna can do. A visit that follows training comes before that kind of ache has set in.

Heat and cold split the work of treating DOMS. Warmth boosts blood flow and loosens stiffness, and cold therapy targets pain and inflammation. A sauna sits on the warm side of that divide. Its likeliest contribution for a sore body lies in easing tightness. Bathers whose main complaint is swelling or inflammation would find cold therapy a closer match for that goal.

Regular sauna use may lower the risk of neurocognitive diseases such as dementia and Alzheimer's disease, according to one study the article highlights. The finding concerns people who make the sauna a habit. It offers no verdict on the occasional session after a workout.

Dr. Zack cautions that this link needs more research before anyone treats it as settled. She attaches the same caution to claims about the heart, psoriasis, immunity, and weight loss.

She also raises a second, separate possibility. Sauna use may sharpen cognition, a matter of everyday thinking in the present. The study addresses the odds of developing a disease down the road. Each idea could hold up or fall apart on its own.

In her account, the physiological reasons behind any cognitive gain remain unknown. Her remarks on the heart and on muscle spasms name specific changes in the body. Her remarks on the brain name none. Without a known pathway, nobody can say how much sauna time a benefit would take, or whether it would last.

A lone piece of evidence and a missing mechanism limit how far the claim can stretch. They mark brain health as a field worth watching. Neither gives a reader a proven defense against dementia.

Humid sauna air may help people clear mucus from their airways, according to family medicine provider Amy Zack, MD. She links the effect to moisture. In her account, a wet sauna may hydrate the respiratory tract, and a well-watered airway lets a person cough up phlegm with less effort.

Her phrasing leaves room for doubt about the mechanism. Dr. Zack calls the hydration effect "likely," a word that signals probability and stops short of proof. The benefit also hinges on the person in the heat. Moisture eases the work, and a cough does the clearing. The sauna aids a reflex the body owns. A working cough becomes part of the bargain for anyone hoping to breathe easier.

Damp air separates the setting Dr. Zack describes from the dry room most people picture. She treats a steam room as its own option, distinct from a sauna, and notes that it runs on humidity. She finds no clear sign that wet heat beats dry heat, or the reverse. Her breathing explanation singles out wet saunas and depends on water reaching the respiratory passages. Any easier coughing she describes belongs to humid heat, even though neither style wins in general.

Sensitivity to temperature swings may keep some people from gaining anything at all. Dr. Zack warns that anyone who struggles with changes in heat and cold may not tolerate a sauna. She urges every patient to consult a primary care physician or a specialist before trying one. Her advice covers all patients, with no carve-out for people who feel healthy. Someone hoping a steamy room will loosen a congested chest faces an extra step before the first session. The same vapor that may ease a cough can overwhelm a body unable to cope with shifting temperatures. A breathing benefit, then, begins in a doctor's office before it reaches the steam.

Psoriasis flare-ups may come under better control with sauna use, and one study suggests the heat can shrink the spread of plaques and patches across the skin. Dr. Zack tempers the finding. She says the topic needs more investigation and adds that little to no research exists, with support limited to anecdotes from clinical practice. Until that work arrives, the sauna counts as a possible aid for flare-ups and nothing firmer.

Flare-ups give psoriasis its rhythm. Symptoms can cycle between active stretches of several weeks or months and calmer spells when they fade or vanish. Plaque psoriasis tends to last a lifetime, and infections, stress, medications, or trauma can spark its worse periods. Keeping triggers such as stress and skin injuries in check may help limit symptoms. Patients learn to steer clear of other flare triggers, among them streptococcal infections, heavy drinking, smoking, and some drugs such as lithium and beta-blockers. A sudden halt to systemic corticosteroids belongs on the same list. Sauna heat would work on those swings and leave the underlying disease in place.

Standard treatment scales with severity. Creams or ointments can handle mild psoriasis. Moderate and severe cases may require oral or injected medicines or phototherapy. Doctors encourage people with plaque psoriasis to get moderate, safe sunlight, and brief supervised ultraviolet sessions may improve their lesions. Sunburn remains off-limits. Against that lineup, sauna heat brings a slim record of its own.

Sweat creates its own hazard for one form of the disease. Rubbing and sweating can aggravate inverse psoriasis, which appears as smooth, red patches in skin folds. A sauna session works by making the body sweat. Dr. Zack advises anyone with a medical condition of concern to get clearance from a healthcare provider before going in. People with inverse psoriasis could find a session's sweat working against the skin they hoped to settle.

A stronger immune system appears on the list of eight possible sauna benefits, and the article says research shows saunas may help prevent the common cold and other viral infections. Dr. Zack keeps that promise at arm's length. She says more study must back up any claim that saunas improve immune function, and she attaches the same call for further research to her remarks on the heart, dementia risk, psoriasis, and weight loss. Of the five topics that draw her caution, immunity rests on the thinnest support.

The other entries bring more to stand on, whether a proposed mechanism, a study tied to a specific condition, or reports from clinical practice. Psoriasis can cite one study on flare-ups and anecdotal support from clinicians. The immune entry offers far less. It gives no explanation of how heat might help, no detail about the research behind the cold-prevention line, and no patient experience. Beyond that one sentence, its text amounts to a request for more evidence.

Immunity goes unmentioned in the article's subtitle, which names stress reduction, heart health, and pain relief as the draws of dry heat. The opening summary of what studies indicate covers stress, the heart, pain, and sore muscles, and it leaves immunity out of that rundown. The section's heading, by contrast, presents the improvement as fact. The text beneath it frames cold prevention as a possibility and holds the broader promise back until proof arrives. A reader skimming the headings could leave convinced of an advantage the body copy declines to confirm.

Sauna users who hope the heat will help them fend off illness have only limited research to go on, and Dr. Zack says more is needed before anyone can substantiate claims of better immune function. She urges anyone with questions about sauna use to talk with a healthcare provider, and a hoped-for immune boost makes a fair topic to raise. Her guidance calls for anyone with a medical condition of concern to get clearance before a session, whatever payoff that person seeks. Of the eight upsides on offer, immunity gives a bather the least reason to step into the heat.

Experts do not recommend a sauna as a weight loss tool, and Dr. Zack says no clear evidence shows that sauna heat burns fat. A couple of studies have looked at the question, she notes, yet none of them establishes that saunas help people slim down. She files the subject alongside several of the heat's other supposed perks as a topic awaiting more research.

The advice sets weight apart from stress relief, heart health, and pain, where studies point toward possible gains. On weight, experts advise against using the heat for that purpose at all.

Sweat drains water from the body during a visit, and a scale can register the drop. Someone who weighs in after a session may see a lower number for a short stretch. Dr. Zack calls the dip dehydration, a fluid deficit and nothing more. The number tracks water the heat has drawn off, and nothing in her account ties it to lost fat.

Proper rehydration erases the change. Dr. Zack says the lighter reading will not hold once a person replaces the fluids the heat pulled out. The pounds that seemed to vanish on the bench return with the water.

Timing creates the illusion. A scale reading from minutes after a session catches the body short on fluid, before drinking restores what sweat removed. A later reading, after the bather has drunk enough to replace the lost water, shows the gap closed. The two numbers measure hydration, and neither measures fat.

Chasing a lower number by staying longer in the heat would backfire. More minutes on the bench mean more sweat and a deeper fluid shortfall, with no clear proof of fat burned to show for it. Dehydration carries hazards of its own, and a bigger dip on the scale points to more of it.

A bather who reads a post-sauna drop as progress is reading a fluid debt the body must repay.

Dehydration stands among the largest hazards of a sauna session, and it shows itself through thirst, headaches, muscle cramps, and confusion. Sweat drains fluid from the body for as long as someone sits in the heat. The defense starts before the door opens, with water ahead of a session and more once it ends. Electrolyte drinks offer another way to replace what the body sheds. The Forest Service cautions against sugary drinks and alcohol in hot conditions. Both can feed dehydration.

Short visits keep the strain in check. Dr. Zack suggests about 15 to 20 minutes and notes that recommendations vary. Newcomers can begin with five minutes and add time on each visit. Dr. Zack tells anyone who feels unwell, dizzy, weak, short of breath, or uncomfortable to leave the sauna and seek medical help. The Forest Service warns that extreme heat can overwhelm the body's natural cooling and lists heat cramps, heat exhaustion, and heat stroke among the resulting illnesses. The agency's signs of heat exhaustion include heavy sweating, weakness, pale skin that feels cold and clammy, a fast and weak pulse, nausea or vomiting, and fainting.

Sauna use carries little danger for most people, yet a few situations call for skipping it. Dr. Zack advises staying out after a recent heart attack or stroke, or after drinking alcohol. A broader group should talk with a healthcare provider first. The group includes adults older than 65, anyone under 16, anyone pregnant or trying to get pregnant, people with neurologic conditions such as epilepsy, those taking medication, and anyone concerned about male infertility. The Forest Service flags elderly people, the very young, and anyone with long-term health problems as more prone to heat illness. The age cutoffs on the sauna checklist mark both ends of that same range.

Heart conditions earn their own place on the provider list, covering heart disease, severe aortic stenosis, heart failure, and high or low blood pressure. The CDC urges clinicians to help patients with cardiovascular disease spot when heat may be aggravating their condition. The agency's examples include greater shortness of breath, unusual fatigue during exertion, and chest pain. The CDC asks clinicians to remind people with heart failure or kidney disease to watch for both fluid overload and dehydration in hot weather. Dr. Zack wants anyone with a worrying medical condition to secure clearance before a session.

Most of these hazards answer to choices a sauna user controls: how much water goes down, how long a session lasts, and whether a provider weighs in before the heat does.
