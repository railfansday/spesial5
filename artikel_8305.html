At This Resort, the Owner is Driving a Snowcat, Turning Wrenches, and Fixing Broken Fences

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

I’d never ridden a ski lift with the person who owns the mountain below us before, so I’m not sure if this is common.

But as we chugged from the Ski Santa Fe’s base toward its 11,075-foot peak, Ben Abruzzo noted a chairlift returning to the base that sounded squeaky, a groomer that needed tending, and an orange safety fence that had fallen over. Abruzzo—whose day was charged with showing this writer around the mountain his family has owned since the mid-1980s—interrupted his responses to any of my questions on more than a few occasions, apologizing before speaking into a small walkie-talkie mounted on his chest. He instructed this person or that to address the various tiny issues that must pop up a million times a day on a mountain, acting more like a manager than the guy who owns the mountain.

Over the course of a single lift ride, it became clear how much Abruzzo not only cared about the experience of the roughly 250,000 visitors who enjoy his family’s mountain every year but also about the mountain itself. It makes sense once you realize that Ski Santa Fe is, in a way, woven into the very fabric of the person Abruzzo is.

“It’s all I’ve ever known,” said Abruzzo, as the lift neared Ski Santa Fe’s peak, the mountain he described as his “lifelong backyard.”

Owners and CEOs often love to say that their businesses are like a family. However, for the Abruzzos, Ski Santa Fe is not only their family business, but it’s their ancestral home.

Abruzzo is the third-generation owner of Ski Santa Fe, following in the footsteps of his grandfather, his father, and two of his uncles, who finalized the purchase of the mountain in 1985. It was hardly a lark, as the elder Abruzzo purchased the Sandia Peak Ski Area in nearby Albuquerque in the late 1950s.

Their familial sensibility permeates much of Abruzzo’s staff. His grooming manager has worked at the mountain for thirty years. His operations manager as well. The mountain’s ticketing manager bests them both, with 32 years of service.

“I think it’s because we treat our folks right,” Abruzzo said, pointing out that when he says ‘we,’ he doesn’t just mean his father, his late grandfather, his uncles, and any other Abruzzo who’s spent time working the mountain. “Our mountain manager, our operations manager, all of our full-time staff; they’re all invested because they’re all part of this community.”

Abruzzo started working on the mountain when he was 12, building mountain bike trails in the summer and working in the rental shop during the season. After commissioning as an infantry officer in 2001, Abruzzo deployed to Afghanistan with the Army’s 101st Airborne Division during the height of the Global War on Terror. Abruzzo returned home in 2005, working as an area manager at Sandia Peak. He relocated to Ski Santa Fe in 2010, eventually taking over the mountain shortly before the Covid-19 pandemic.

And even though he owns the ski area, Abruzzo still drives snowcats and still turns wrenches when chairlifts need tending. And he requires the same of all the mountain’s senior staff.

“We expect all of our managers to be well-versed in the blue-collar side of things,” he said. “And that includes me.”

Sitting high in the Sangre de Cristo Mountains, Ski Santa Fe is about a 30-minute drive from the heart of the famously artsy city in northern New Mexico. Of course, snowy days and weekends might impact that commute time, Abruzzo admits, despite the fact that the mountain has its own dedicated road crew charged with plowing the two-lane road leading up to Ski Santa Fe. Still, as Ski Santa Fe is the only ski area in the vicinity, it’s a drive that never feels out of reach for locals and visitors alike.

In a more corporate environment, that dearth of options could provide owners an excuse to rest on their proverbial laurels. However, Abruzzo sees that scarcity as an honor to uphold. If people only have one option, it’d better be a good one.

With 88 total trails, almost evenly split across blacks, blues, and greens, a trio of terrain parks, and 1,800 feet of vertical drop, all serviced by a double lift, two triple lifts, two quad lifts, two surface lifts, and a high-speed detachable lift, the mountain is far bigger and more diverse than you might think when you hear “family-owned ski area.”

But beyond all that, Ski Santa Fe is known by locals and regulars for two main reasons: first, its off-piste skiing. And second, its snowmaking capabilities.

On the day we spent together, snow fell from the sky in big, thick flakes. It was one of the 20 or so powder days Ski Santa Fe gets each season. The tops of tall pine trees, buried in some 200 inches of snow, poked out of the thick layers of powder. It was a relatively unfamiliar sensation to this lifelong East Coaster who is far better versed in riding ice than powder.

Still, Abruzzo assured, even when it’s human-made, Ski Santa Fe’s snow is some of the best and most consistent there is.

“We make very dry snow,” he said before pointing out how the mountain’s 42 snow guns and five-million-gallon storage tank allow them to cover most of the mountain in snow throughout the season. “It’s dry to the point where we have had snow gun companies write us a custom setting. And what you get is a much nicer surface to ski on that stays nice longer.”

A little shy of halfway down one of our day’s runs, Abruzzo cut from one groomer to another and, suddenly, a rustic, two-story shack stood surrounded by a small sea of skiers and snowboarders, each of whom seemed to have a beer, a cocktail, or a hot chocolate in hand.

At 10,500 feet, on-mountain restaurant Totemoff’s sits higher than the peaks of many ski areas in North America.

And while there was no entertainment beyond some music playing over the speakers, Abruzzo assured me that Totemoff’s often plays host to live music. Above the bar, apartments for Abruzzo’s family and staff really hammer home the idea of how deeply entwined the Abruzzo family is 0n this mountain.

As we sat chatting over a pair of light beers, the talk eventually came back to the Abruzzo family, their lifelong marriage to this mountain. I ask him what it’s been like having three generations of Abruzzos run a mountain together. He quickly corrects me.

“Both of my kids work here,” he said with a laugh. “So, it’s actually four generations.”
