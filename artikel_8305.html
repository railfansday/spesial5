On Ibuprofen Over Mather Pass — John Muir Trail Day 11

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

I awoke in the dark and went to get my bear cannister containing my breakfast.

Every day on the trail I have granola with dried milk and freeze-dried fruit.  The best version includes both strawberries and blueberries.  I am surprised at how quickly the fruit absorbs the water; unlike dinner, there is no waiting for breakfast.  I am also amazed at how tart and juicy the fruit becomes.  Even having the same thing day after day, I don’t grow tired of it.

I suddenly recalled seeing Sally, the hiker who set up near me after nightfall, earlier on the trail the day before.  She was descending slowly and gingerly, as if she were injured.  This morning she was already up and, when I asked her, she acknowledged having trouble with her ankle and being worried she might not be able to finish the John Muir Trail.

We chatted for quite a while.  She told me she was of Chinese origin, which I had guessed from her accent, but she now lived in Brisbane, Australia.  When she had her second child in China, she felt it was necessary to leave, because that was during the time of China’s one-child policy.  I did not ask her, but am pretty confident that “Sally” was not her birth name.

She brought her two children to Australia.  One was now a doctor, like her husband, and the other was an ophthalmologist.  Her husband supported her going on adventures and she had hiked all over the world.  Most amazing, she summited Aconcagua, which at an elevation of “only” 22,837 feet is the highest point in the Western Hemisphere!  “Quite a remarkable person,” I thought.

A situation like that is interesting.  We were both going north on the John Muir Trail headed for the same destination in Happy Valley, Yosemite.  With Serena and Evelyn, I was going north and they were going south.  It was a safe bet I would never see them again.  But with Sally, it is much harder to tell.  I might never see her again, or I might see her repeatedly, all the way to the finish line.  Time would tell.

I made a cathole on the hill above my tent the night before; then Sally arrived, so I was a little embarrassed how that might work in the morning.  But she was faster than I was and broke camp well before I did.  About the time she left, the sun was starting to creep down the mountainside on the other side of the lake.

Still in shadow on our side of Lake Marjorie; bright sunshine on the opposite shore.

In any case, I was able to go and do my business without worry and then head out.  Once again, I was late — it was already eight a.m. — even though I was up when it was still quite dark.

I am just a slow packer, what can I say?   But I expected a slightly easier day and 8:00 a,m. was bad, but not terrible.  I knew I would be starting downhill in similar fashion to the previous day but would only be dropping down to 10,000 feet before climbing again to Mather Pass.  With the prospect of going down, I took another Ibuprofen for my injured knee.

My knee did twinge from time to time, but it was functioning, so I kept descending and eventually reached the bottom.  A big river was located there, and I had read scary reports of people wading across with difficulty.  This late in the season, however, the levels were low enough that even in the big rivers, there were almost always places that you could cross by steppingstones.  With hiking poles for balance, those were usually not difficult.

Someone had placed a straight and deliberate line of rocks where the trail crosses the stream.

Here the rocks are more random and require a moment’s thought to discern the best route across.

There ensued a pretty level portion of trail that I enjoyed, having only a slight upward slope following the river.   At least ascending, my knee liked it better.  Apparently, my knee likes going uphill but doesn’t like going downhill.  I wish my lungs felt the same way, but when it gets steep, they start to wheeze and gasp.

Quite level, wih few trail obstacles — a trail like this invites a faster pace.

I kept chugging along, keeping one foot in front of the other as gradually the miles went by.  The trail eventually emerged from the forest, into a thinner type of forest, and finally into open landscapes.  The views of the mountains enclosing this valley filled me with awe.

In this valley, and all along the John Muir Trail, there were views like this in all directions.

Finally, I caught up to Sally.  She left camp almost an hour ahead of me, so I was making a faster pace.

I also saw Amy again, drying out her socks following a fall-in-the-creek mishap she suffered the day before.  It seemed that Amy and Sally were people I might continue to see from time to time.

It would not make sense for us to hike together, however, because they both have a very steady hiking speed, which is slower than mine.  Our similar net speed comes from my hiking fast, but then stopping frequently to admire the landscape, or take a photograph, or catch my breath.  It would frustrate both sides to try hiking together.

There was a little breeze, so despite the sun beating down, the zephyr kept it a slightly cooler.  As I got to the end of a sort of lunar landscape of boulders, it was not clear where on the ridge above me lay Mather Pass.  It turned out it was above a huge rockslide, but the trail was cut into the rock of the slide.  It ascended through many switchbacks.  At exactly one o’clock, five hours after starting, I stood on the top of Mather Pass.

While I ate lunch in the pass, Sally caught up and joined me for a time.  Then I started going down the other side, which everyone had said was rockier.  It was rockier.

I was really worried about my injured knee.  So, I descended carefully until suddenly, the trail just disappeared.   I was confused for a little while until I looked beyond a jumble of boulders and realized that the path continued on the other side of the rock fall.  The slide had completely obscured the trail.  I couldn’t say how long ago it happened, but trail workers maintain these popular trails, so I expect it was within the past year.  You would not have wanted to be on trail when these enormous boulders came rolling down the mountain!

How did I get through?  As everyone else did, I suppose, I devised my own route over the cascade of rocks and eventually found a way through to resume hiking the trail.  One key path-finding method on the John Muir Trail is to follow the trail of horse manure from the pack trains.

The Appalacian Trail has white blazes. The John Muir Trail has… this.

I really wondered how those animals managed to pick their way through the boulders with hoofs instead of hands to grip with!  On my way lower, I warned people going southbound toward Mather Pass of the obstacle that lay ahead for them.

At this point I thought, “Well, I made the high point of the pass at one o’clock and I’m only about two miles from my projected tent site.  Why don’t I try to log a little more distance today?  Then I won’t have as much pressure tomorrow and the day after.”  So, I continued on past the first Palisade Lake where I had planned to camp.

As I hiked, the sun grew more powerful until I finally put my umbrella up again.  I only used it for about two hours, but it definitely cooled me off a bit, and preserved some of my energy.

Amy and Sally and I had separately decided to press on further, but I came across Amy making camp quite soon after first Palisade Lake.  She said her legs just didn’t have it that day, so she had taken one of the tent spots by the trail.

Meanwhile, I continued on, now past the second Palisade Lake.  It was getting later in the afternoon, but a southbound hiker told me that down on the Staircase there were a number of individual tent sites.  At first I thought, “Well, maybe I’ll make it to the Staircase.”  This was a steep downhill stretch that, despite that hiker’s assurances, seemed unlikely to have much room for tent sites.

It was after four p.m., so then I reconsidered, “If I find a suitable site before the Staircase, I will play it safe and stay there.”

I looked at one spot that didn’t feel quite right, but then I saw another one on the other side of the trail.  It was nice and sandy and flat.

Seeing it in the bright sunshine, I assessed that it would work really well and I set up my tent.  Then I went and found a place to make a cathole, to prepare for the next morning.  Returning, I saw a couple of guys passing my tent site and realized it was not quite as private as I had believed.  But by then I was set up, and it was late afternoon, and I figured there would not be many more passersby that evening.

I made a lasagna for dinner.  Oh my God.  Was it a delicious lasagna?  Yes!  No, I’m joking, it was pretty awful.  However, I had learned enough to make it in the packet rather than create a gooey mess in my Nalgene container.  Cleaning that container had been a nightmare the last time I tried Italian food in it.

So, I ate the lasagna out of the packet, which worked okay, except that my spoon was not long enough to reach the bottom of the packet.  It made for messy eating.  I decided that for non-Italian dishes I would go back to my trusty Nalgene.

Back in the tent, I sent out my daily message on the Garmin, assuring people that I was okay.  In getting ready to turn in, I discovered an enormous blister on the second toe of my right foot.

I don’t know why, but the blister looked like it was going to explode nuclearly at any moment.  It was not really painful, but I looked in my first aid kit to see if there were any instructions.  Do you pop such a blister?  Do you leave it alone?  How do you treat it?  The directions were ambiguous, so I decided to leave it alone.

As for my knee, it seemed to have responded well to the Ibuprofen so far.  Just before sleep, I would take another ibuprofen, and another in the morning before the Staircase.  But then I might need to start cadging Ibuprofen from fellow hikers, because I would be running out.

The knee injury was two days old and it still felt tender.  My sense was that I faced serious risk if I incurred further injury; if there were none, however, I would be fine.

Camped on my rocky knoll, surrounded by mountains, it was a beautiful campsite.  Those mountains meant the sun departed early, but the sky remained blue for a long time after that.  I enjoyed the peaceful spot, and my slightly shorter day, and with my phone plugged into the charger block, I fell asleep.
