Fierce Healthcare is tracking workforce changes across healthcare in 2026. Stick with this tracker for the latest updates, and reach out to the team with any layoff news. Also, take a look back at our trackers for 2025 and 2024.

Healthcare generative AI company Abridge recently cut its workforce as part of an "organization-wide realignment," the company confirmed to Fierce Healthcare on Friday.

The company did not disclose how many staff were impacted by the layoffs. Abridge, founded in 2018, has raised more than $750 million in venture capital funding to date. The company works with more than 300 health systems and is projected to support more than 100 million clinical conversations this year.

"We’re in a rare moment in health care and AI, and we’re realigning from a position of strength to flatten our organization and build and deliver products faster. This realignment will enable us to continue to grow, hire in priority areas, and invest in new opportunities," the company said in a statement provided to Fierce Healthcare.

Abridge said it was supporting staff impacted by the cuts with "meaningful transition packages and outplacement transition support."

"Abridge is growing rapidly, reflected in both revenue growth and customer demand, and our pace of product development has accelerated significantly. As an AI-native company, we are realigning teams across the organization to further increase our pace of innovation. Abridge remains very well capitalized, and continues to hold more than a majority of the capital it has raised to date," the company said.

Healthcare startup Heidi reduced its headcount by 18%, the company's CEO and co-founderTom Kelly revealed in a LinkedIn post on Thursday.

The company did not disclose how many employees were impacted or what roles were cut. Heidi has more than 600 employees, so the cuts impacted about 108 staff.

The company, which first launched in Australia, initially started as an ambient AI medical scribe and then expanded into point-of-care clinical guidelines with Heidi Evidence and AI hardware (Remote) for clinical work as well as a voice-to-text feature, called Dictate. Heidi says it now supports around 2.8 million patient interactions each week in 110 languages from 190 countries.

"In the last few weeks, we reflected carefully on how we're structured, and we weren't putting our investment into the right teams and markets to make the most of our continuing growth. It's painful, but it's what we need to do now to continue growing quickly and deliver on our plans for next year," Kelly wrote in his LinkedIn post.

The company is providing severance and benefits packages that vary by country but "exceed statutory requirements in every location," he wrote.

"Heidi is growing rapidly because clinicians around the world need and want our technology. We are investing in expanding our products, markets and team, and now support three million patient interactions every week. Our team has helped make that possible," Kelly wrote in a note to staff posted to the company's website. "After reviewing our priorities in detail, we’ve made difficult choices about where to focus our team and investment to deliver the greatest impact for customers going forward. That means bringing more customer support into product, bringing R&D together in one roadmap and giving our new product businesses clearer ownership and dedicated teams."

Also this week, Kelly announced that two of Heidi’s three co-founders, Waleed Mussa and Lu Yui. are preparing to leave the startup. "Waleed and Yu built Heidi with me, and it’s going to be really hard for all of us to continue without them. They’re leaving for different reasons, with their transitions happening over the coming weeks and months," Kelly wrote in a LinkedIn post.

Mussa is leaving to spend more time with his family, while Yui will transition into an advisory role focused on models and hardware through the end of March next year, Kelly wrote.

In September, Heidi landed $340 million to fuel its push beyond clinical documentation into AI-powered workflow automation and agentic capabilities for clinicians. The company raised $100 million series C funding. Alongside the equity round, Heidi also secured a $240 million growth investment led by General Catalyst’s Customer Value Fund.

Children's Mercy, a pediatric health system based in Kansas City, Missouri, confirmed to press that it is cutting 115 positions, or about 1.2% of its total workforce.

The organization, in a statement, attributed the decision to "rising costs and reimbursement that has not kept pace." Among those affected, "most are non-clinical administrative, leadership and support roles," the statement reads.

Concurrent with the cuts was word that 473 of the system's revenue cycle and scheduling employees are shifting over to revenue cycle management vendor Ensemble, where they'll continue to work with Children's Mercy.

In April, the children's hospital announced plans to build a new acute patient tower at its flagship location in response to "increasing demand for highly specialized pediatric care as general hospitals nationwide reduce pediatric capacity." The project will expand total capacity by 25% to 30%, cost more than $1 billion and target a 2031 completion.

Rural Colorado nonprofit Montrose Regional Health, which includes a single 75-bed hospital and other clinical locations, announced this week a restructuring that eliminated 77 positions.

Of these, 43 were vacant and 34 were filled by current employees. Nearly half of the total positions were direct patient care roles, and 12 were leadership.

The system, in a release, said its volumes have grown about 10% over the past year while commercial reimbursement stayed flat and expenses rose. Its decision came after restricting hiring and capital spending, cost reduction efforts and other measures to reduce losses and limit outstanding insurance payments for care already delivered, and will save over $8 million annually.

The system said it does not have other workforce reductions planned at this time. The changes are not expected to affect "critical hospital-based services," though some service lines will be changing as part of the restructuring.

Specialty cancer care provider City of Hope filed a Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification outlining plans to permanently lay off 73 employees, effective Nov. 24.

The organization, in a statement to Becker's, said most of those affected work in business support and will receive support services like transition benefits and severance. The cuts will not directly affect patient care, they said.

Kaiser Permanente is laying off 147 workers across multiple Northern and Southern California locations, the country's largest nonprofit health system reported in Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification filings.

The reductions are effective on Nov. 20, and per the filing affect workers reporting to corporate and administrative offices.

The system, in statements to press, affirmed that the affected positions "do not provide direct patient care." The organization said some workers are looking at other openings within the system, though severance, career support and outplacement services are also being extended to others "where appropriate."

SAC Health, a San Bernadino nonprofit chain of clinics, laid off 52 more workers shortly after a prior round of 49 cuts.

The changes were reported in a Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification filing and, in a prior internal memo and more recent statement to press, described the decision as necessary to strengthen operations. Both rounds of cuts involved nonclinical workers, with the former closely following the closure of two clinics.

The organization said that patient care and services are continuing through the latest cuts without interruption, and, in an internal memo provided to employees, that no additional reorganization is planned.

Catholic Health is eliminating 40 positions across five of its locations and realigning 34 other roles, according to a Sept. 22 press release shared with Fierce Healthcare.

The layoffs affect both union and non-union positions at Catholic Health’s Administrative and Regional Training Center, Mercy Hospital of Buffalo, Mount St. Mary’s Hospital, Sisters of Charity Hospital and St. Joseph Campus.

The health system is working with affected employees to review potential placement opportunities in current openings, the announcement said.

Moreover, 34 positions within Mercy Hospital are being realigned as “volumes surge” in its emergency department. Impacted employees will be able to transition directly into current vacancies within the hospital.

“These are difficult decisions because they affect valued members of our Catholic Health family who have contributed greatly to our organization and the communities we serve,” said Joyce Markiewicz, Catholic Health president and CEO, in a statement. “We are committed to treating every affected associate with dignity, compassion, and respect while ensuring Catholic Health remains strong and positioned to fulfill our mission well into the future.”

PeaceHealth is planning to cut 150 positions across its facilities on Nov. 20, according to a notice filed with the Washington State Employment Security Department.

Bumping rights do not exist for any impacted positions, the Sept. 17 notice said.

Fierce Healthcare has reached out to PeaceHealth for additional comment.

Elevance Health is laying off 216 positions in Louisiana as it prepares for its contract expiration with Medicaid in the state.

The organization disclosed the plans in a Sept. 15 notice (PDF), in which it said the cuts would be effective Dec. 31. Louisiana’s Medicaid contract with Healthy Blue is set to expire the same day.

“We are focused on supporting our associates through this transition with clear communication, career resources and redeployment opportunities across Elevance Health wherever possible,” the company said in a statement to Fierce Healthcare. “We are grateful for their dedication to Louisiana Medicaid members since 2017.”

New Hampshire's Dartmouth Health said it notified 124 workers that their positions had been eliminated, while also shuttering 303 open roles at the Dartmouth Hitchcock Medical Center and Dartmouth Hitchcock Clinics Southern Region.

The adjustments, it said, will see executive and administrative leadership roles consolidated and "select programs" eliminated or affected, including Tele-ICU and Tele-ED, the New England Alliance for Health and some in-house employee training programs. The system said it does not expect the changes to harm patients.

“The employees affected by these changes have made meaningful contributions to Dartmouth Health, our patients and our communities,” Joanne M. Conroy, M.D., CEO and president of Dartmouth Health, said in a statement. “Some have spent many years serving this organization. The decision to eliminate a position or program does not diminish the value of the work these colleagues have done and the contributions they have made.”

Those affected by the cuts will be "offered resources, transition support and opportunities to pursue other roles within the health system," the system said.

Dartmouth Health noted that its operating expenses, particularly around labor, supply and drug costs, have outpaced patient revenues. Ahead of the cuts, it said it has already worked to reduce some expenses by reducing travel and conference participation, reducing staffing reliance on traveler positions and strategic capital deployment to expand capacity and meet demand.

The changes are a second round of layoffs for the academic system, when another restructuring saw 142 roles condensed into 66. Leadership attributed both to state mandates to reduce spending and other financial pressures.

Conifer Health Solutions, the revenue cycle business of for-profit Tenet Healthcare, plans to permanently lay off 1,037 employees as a result of its breakup with CommonSpirit Health, according to a mandatory notice filing with the Texas Workforce Commission.

The layoffs are set to begin on Nov. 2, according to the WARN filing, and involve workers who report to Tenet’s corporate headquarters in Dallas. Affected workers are not represented by a union and do not have any bumping rights. Read the full story here.

Hundreds of workers at nonprofit health system Trinity Health's Livonia, Michigan headquarters will be permanently laid off, according to a WARN filing with the state.

Trinity had signaled the staffing change back in June when it announced some in-house technology and information services work would be transitioned to an unspecified technology partner organization. That prior statement and the WARN filing outline positions that do not directly face patients.

"The pace and complexity of health care technology are accelerating," Trinity said in June. "Sustaining that pace is increasingly difficult for any single organization on its own. A partner whose core business is technology support gives us access to specialized expertise and innovation at the speed and scale health care now requires while strengthening service reliability.​"

The cuts are scheduled to begin Oct. 25 and run through Nov. 29. Trinity previously suggested that some of the affected workers would have "opportunities for new roles within Trinity Health and the partner organization along with transition support and resources.​"

For-profit hospital chain HCA Healthcare confirmed an unspecified number of layoffs in an emailed statement.

The system said the cuts represented "a small percentage of positions across our corporate office and support functions." It said the changes are a response to pressures like rising costs, shifting policy and increasing numbers of uninsured patients (HCA recently lowered its 2026 earnings forecast on worse-than-anticipated quarterly results).

The company said it would be providing "a range of resources including severance and recruitment and outplacement support" to those affected, and noted that it is still hiring for other roles across its national footprint.

Academic health system Stanford Health Care has disclosed plans to trim 95 employees, or around 0.5% of its more than 18,000-person workforce.

The organization disclosed the plans in an Aug. 28 California WARN filing, in which it said the cuts would be effective Nov. 2.

In a statement given to Fierce Healthcare, Stanford described the cuts as part of a regular review of its operations and patient needs.

None of the affected positions provide direct patient care, it said, and are located in its Technology and Digital Solutions department (79 workers, according to the WARN filing) as well as other administrative areas (16 workers).

"We are providing active support for redeployment within the organization where possible and opening new roles with the opportunity for impacted employees to apply," the statement reads.

Walnut Creek, California-based John Muir Health reported plans to lay off 78 workers, according to an Aug. 27 WARN filing .

The cuts will be effective Oct. 28 and span multiple sections of the nonprofit system. According to the filing, affected roles work in finance, human resources, marketing, IT, management and clinical care.

John Muir Health, in a statement, said that the layoffs impact about 1% of its workforce and "are mainly focused on leadership positions, employees in administrative services and employees who do not provide direct patient care."

The system noted national and state policy headwinds like the One Big Beautiful Bill Act and California's 2030 seismic mandate, respectively, for its decision to proactively tighten its headcount.

Impacted employees have been notified, and any who do not find another position within the organization will receive a severance package offer, the statement reads.

Minnesota insurer UCare will layoff 102 employees as it continues to wind down its business.

Per a Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification (WARN) posting on Aug. 3, the company will no longer support some of its Medicaid plans, which were sold to Medica, as of Oct. 1. As such, the layoffs are permanent.

Impacted employees include individuals working at its headquarters as well as workers who are primarily or exclusively remote.

Effected employees will work their last day in October, with one staying on until November, according to the notice. They will continue to be paid through the notice window.

Those affected were offered transition resources, such as benefits continuation and other internal job opportunities, with the notice highlighting more than 500 other positions that are currently open across the system.

Centra said the restructure is part of a broader initiative focused on spotting redundancies and efficiencies, "particularly in administrative functions," and will help "adapt to the significant pressures facing healthcare providers including reductions in government reimbursements, economic challenges, shifting demographics and evolving technologies."

Advanced Specialty Hospitals of Toledo's May 1 closure will see 116 workers permanently laid off, effective June 8, according to a Workers Adjustment and Retraining Notification filing.

The affected workers, according to the notice, include 45 registered nurses as well as nurse aides, pharmacy technicians, speech language pathologists and other clinical and non-clinical job roles.

Greenwood Leflore Hospital, a publicly owned hospital with 25 licensed acute care beds and 24/7 emergency department, said this week it would be laying off 86 employees, of whom 40 are part-time.

The decision is accompanied by immediate and planned service line closures including an after-hours clinic, wellness center, outpatient rehab and cardiac rehab, according to a statement given to press and other online notices. The hospital is left with about 425 employees plus 58 contracted workers.

The hospital, which serves an area of Mississippi with limited healthcare access, has been teetering financially for the past few years and had recently began exploring a deal to join the University of Mississippi Medical Center. This week's statement noted that the restructuring decisions were made "as the hospital prepares for either a transition of operations to a larger health system or a potential closure."

UCI Health announced a strategic restructuring that will lay off about 1% of its workforce, or roughly 150 people.

The six-hospital academic system said its effort will be combining position and better matching roles to its developing needs. Those affected "will receive comprehensive financial support, job placement services, and priority consideration for open positions across UCI Health as part of the organization’s realignment."

The California organization cited federal funding shifts and insurance reimbursement changes for its restructuring, as well as patients' different approaches to care access.

“We approach this moment with respect and care, feeling deeply grateful for our co-workers’ vital contributions to our community and those we serve," Chad Lefteris, president and CEO of UCI Health, said. "We recognize that change impacts all of our co-workers. But this realignment is vital to ensuring our ability to continue meeting the evolving needs of the communities we serve."

Bankrupt New York system North Star Health Alliance said in a release that it will be taking "additional operational restructuring actions" despite already conducting a prior round of layoffs near the top of the year affecting more than 100 people.

The system said the "workforce adjustments" will span the organization, and target greater operational efficiency while preserving essential care access. It did not disclose how many employees will be affected.

“This is a difficult but necessary step as we move through the Chapter 11 process and continue strengthening our organization for the future,” said CEO Andrew Manzer. “Our focus remains on strengthening the organization so we can continue delivering high-quality care close to home.”

Pharmacy giant Walgreens will eliminate jobs in two states as it adapts to cost pressures as a private company under new owner Sycamore Partners.

A spokesperson for Walgreens confirmed the layoffs in a statement to Fierce Healthcare. Bloomberg reported that notices submitted in Illinois, where the company is headquartered, include 469 job cuts. A further 159 positions will be eliminated in Texas, according to the report.

"We’re focused on becoming America’s best retail pharmacy, beginning with improving the in‑store experience for our customers and patients," the spokesperson said. "To do this, we’ve made the difficult decision to simplify our organization in both the support center and with our field leadership to speed decision making and improve the service that millions of customers rely on every day."

They added that the company will support employees in the transition.

Walgreens was purchased by Sycamore Partners last year after facing declining profitability.

Baystate Health, a five-hospital nonprofit system in Massachusetts, confirmed the reduction of 117 corporate positions, which it said is less than 1% of its total workforce.

The system, in a statement given to press, outlined "ever-increasing headwinds" ranging from rising supply expenses to expired Affordable Care Act subsidies to increased demand from patients covered by government plans.

Baystate did not outline specific affected roles, but said those affected by the job cuts have been "notified with compassion and provided with support resources." The statement also described a decision to outsource clinical engineering to medical equipment management organization TRIMEDX, with Baystate's relevant team set to become employees of TRIMEDX beginning in May.

Nine-hospital nonprofit PeaceHealth filed a notice Wednesday that it plans to permanently eliminate 94 positions across its Washington facilities.

The layoffs are expected to to be effective by April 12, according to the notice, and a list of affected roles include clinical and non-clinical job titles. Some of the employees have union representation.

In a statement to press, a representative noted the cuts affect less than 1% of the system's workforce and reflectthe changing needs of our growing communities, ongoing financial pressures to reduce costs and our commitment to advancing the health and well-being of those we serve.”

The reductions come just a few months after the system cut about 400 workers (2.5% of the workforce) and closed some open positions amid organizational adjustments. Prior to that it had cut 1% and instituted a partial hiring freeze in May.

Cigna is planning to eliminate about 2,000 jobs across its global footprint, accounting for about 3% of its total workforce.

Chatter across platforms like LinkedIn and Reddit have discussed the layoffs for several days. Benefits PRO reports that voluntary retirements were also offered to eligible employees.

It's unclear how many individuals are effected by the layoffs, and if any of the jobs were not filled.

“As we drive greater efficiency across our business, we have made the difficult decision to reduce roles in our workforce," a Cigna spokesperson told Fierce Healthcare. "This decision was made with deliberate care and focus, and we are providing a package that includes a variety of transition services for impacted colleagues.”

Unite Us, the tech company facilitating referrals to social services, laid off workers on Wednesday. The move affected about 20% of staff, or over 80 people, two people affected told Fierce Healthcare. The cuts primarily hit the product and customer support teams, they said.

The company did not confirm the number of people affected, but a spokesperson told Fierce Healthcare the move was a "targeted reduction." Unite Us is providing "career transition assistance" to those affected.

"We can confirm that Unite Us remains in a strong, stable financial position. The recent changes allow us to better align our teams and investments with our core offerings and deliver outcomes for our state and healthcare partners," the spokesperson said in an email.

Horizon Blue Cross Blue Shield will eliminate 242 positions, according to a WARN notice submitted this month in the Garden State.

A spokesperson for the insurer told Fierce Healthcare that the non-profit payer is staring down the same financial challenges that are impacting the industry writ large, and that it notified employees in November that it would conduct a restructuring in early 2026 that would lead to the elimination of about 300 positions.

The individuals impacted by the layoffs were notified on Monday, the spokesperson said. The insurer has trimmed its workforce by 8% accounting for this round of layoffs and other actions taken in 2025, they said.

"We do not take workforce reductions lightly," per the spokesperson. "Employees whose positions were eliminated were treated with fairness and respect and will receive a generous separation package."

New York's Erie County Medical Center said it is laying off about 150 employees, or about 3% of its team.

The cuts are primarily in non-clinical roles, though a local nurses' union said that some frontline clinicians were also affected. The hospital is also requiring one week of unpaid furlough in 2026 for its "management confidential employees."

The center's statement placed the blame on "inadequate reimbursements from both public and private payers," adding that "insurance companies continue to deny and delay payment for patient care services at an alarming rate. It stressed that it has not fully closed any of its programs, "but has made targeted reductions in services to meet patient demands and our financial realities."

ECU Health, in eastern North Carolina, will be laying off 31 positions within its Access East program, which helps deliver non-medical and other complex care-management services to Medicaid recipients.

The cuts stem from a decision by the state legislature not to fund the Healthy Opportunities Pilot program that supported its efforts. The system, in statements to press and a regulatory notice, said the terminations are set to go into effect on March 31, but that the organization is looking at other internal openings for those affected where possible.

Hennepin Healthcare, which operates HCMC in Minneapolis and a network of primary care clinics in Hennepin County, plans to cut 100 positions and shutter some services as it faces a massive budget shortfall.

The health system is making targeted service line adjustments as part of a broader, long-term effort to stabilize its finances and protect core clinical services and care capacity, the organization said in an announcement posted to its website on Monday.

Hennepin Healthcare will be closing or integrating some stand-alone clinics and services. The health system will close chiropractic and acupuncture services and refer patients to external providers and it is shuttering its sleep clinic. The system said it will continue to do sleep screening tests through primary care.

Senior and extended care also will be impacted as older adult patients will now be seen in primary care and nursing home services will be transitioned to other systems, the health system said. Patients needing pain treatments also will be transitioned to other providers. Medical and surgical services for weight management will be reduced and integrated into the Clinic and Specialty Center instead of operating as a stand-alone clinic.

Hennepin Healthcare runs Hennepin County Medical Center (HCMC), a Level I adult and pediatric trauma center and safety net hospital.

Earlier this month, the new co-leaders at Hennepin Healthcare sent a message to workers saying job cuts and service reductions would be needed soon because of a cash-flow crisis, the Minnesota Star Tribune reported.

During a press conference Monday, Dr. Kevin Croston, the co-interim administrator of Hennepin Healthcare, told reporters that the health system is currently facing a financial crisis due to budget challenges and health insurer UCare closing, among other factors, Minnesota Public Radio reported.

The health system is trying to close a $50 million budget hole by the end of March, several media outlets reported.

Hennepin Healthcare also is confronting a projected $100 million loss in uncompensated care and a $1.7 billion reduction in Medicaid revenue over the next 10 years, MPR reported.

Pomona Valley Hospital Medical Center shared plans to cuts 265 roles—128 via planned retirements and vacant position closures and the remaining 137 through layoffs and a reduction in hours. The layoffs will take effect by March 8, according to a WARN filing.

The 427-bed nonprofit, which employs about 4,000 people, cited federal and California reimbursement cuts for its decision as well as a budget shortfall of $40 million. The plan for job reductions and other measures was put together following a “thorough evaluation of operational requirements, organizational structure and redundancies in positions,” a spokesperson told press.

The layoffs will take effect by March 8, according to a WARN filing. Those affected will receive severance and other support.

Pomona Valley said the reductions affect management as well as clinical and nonclinical roles but that patient care would not be affected. A labor union representing some of the hospital’s nurses told membership it plans to enforce protections outlined in its collective bargaining agreement, including an option for laid-off nurses to apply for and be given other open positions for which they are qualified.

Blue Cross of Idaho said it will be laying off fewer than 90 workers, a move the group said will allow it “to remain a strong, Idaho-based company” and better serve its nearly 600,000 members.

The insurer did not give details on specific roles affected to a timeline for the cuts. It had already reduced its head count by 135 early last year when the state health department passed it over for a dual-eligible contract.

L.A. Care Health Plan will lay off 225 employees, about 3% of its workforce, on or before March 13.

The country’s largest publicly operated health plan said in a statement the decision stemmed from federal and state funding cuts, and that its restructure will strengthen critical capabilities and introduce new roles to help do so. It also said it will provide transition support and resources to affected workers.

Trinity Health said it will be laying off about a tenth of its revenue cycle management team, instead outsourcing the work to a vendor in a bid to reduce costs.

The 92-hospital nonprofit system did not disclose how many workers are affected but said the reductions affect workers in 15 states. The organization also did not share whether those losing their positions would receive a compensation package.

Alameda Health System will lay off about 250 employees across multiple locations.

A regulatory filing outlining most of these lists March 9 as the terminations’ date of effect. The California organization said its cuts will span several areas, and it is also offering voluntary resignations and retirements with financial incentives.

The reductions stem from impending funding cuts.

North Star Health Alliance announced layoffs affecting around 120 of its workers.

The New York system told press its cuts will span clinical, nonclinical and management employees and be split roughly equally across its two main campuses. Those affected did not receive severance payments outside of what was obligated under their employment contracts.

The cuts come in the wake of a payment dispute between North Star and New York’s Department of Health related to the restructuring of its hospital into a critical access hospital and a co-located inpatient acute psychiatric hospital.

Vibra Healthcare will be laying off 310 employees when it closes Specialty Hospital of Portland, the state’s only long-term acute care facility, due to financial challenges.

The decision was announced in December but will take effect on or before Feb. 1, per a WARN filing. Leadership, nonclinical workers and clinical positions such as nurses and therapists who worked at the 73-bed facility are affected.
