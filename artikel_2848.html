Film study: What is the Wizards’ offense missing?

Tanggal: 2026-11-15

Before Thursday night's barnburner of a loss to the Pistons in double overtime, the vibes around the Wizards, and their offense in particular, were nearing a free fall. The Wiz have the league's 26th-ranked offense, even after the onslaught against Detroit.

There were, to be sure, positive signals to glean. Alex Sarr played by far his best game of the season, knocking down four threes. Trae Young showed more scoring juice, shooting 17 free throws and hitting a buzzer-beater to force OT. Sarr and Anthony Davis connected on a rare lob in overtime.

But the underlying issues behind the bricktastic 4-9 start will take longer to solve. The Wizards barely shoot any threes, and miss most of the ones they do try. Only the Bulls have shot a worse percentage from deep so far. With poor spacing and clumsy jumbo lineups, the offense has tilted concerningly toward true Trae Young heliocentrism; Young leads the league in seconds per touch.

The Wizards use core actions that make a fair amount of sense for their personnel. They rely on side-to-side movement, hoping to launch Young downhill and create better angles for the pick and roll. That's an Iverson cut, intended to distract the defense and free up a Young-AD pick and roll. When Young starts going downhill, Tre Johnson flies up in tandem from the opposite corner. But the Pacers easily box Young toward the wing and funnel the ball away from the middle:

Young makes the pass to AJ Dybantsa, but Pascal Siakam closes him down, and Dybantsa, with no advantage, throws the ball away. This is not an uncommon sight for the Wizards — a Young pick and roll in tight confines near the sideline, putting minimal pressure on the opposing center.

The Wizards have to find a way to make plays like this impact the defense more. They're simply not getting enough out of their secondary actions. Above, for example, Johnson sprinting up from the corner is a good idea in theory. But nobody is screening for Johnson here. What if AD had turned at speed and set a pindown for him, just as the defense expects a screen for Young?

The best offenses pivot into second and third actions. These are built-in counters, depending on how the defense guards the initial one. Consider Sarr in this position, after running a pick and pop:

Bilal Coulibaly's cut through the paint is a scripted thing many teams with shooting bigs do. It's meant to drag away the help defender closest to Sarr — the guy whose job it would be to stunt toward Sarr and contest a three-pointer. But here, Sarr set the screen near half-court and barely made contact. Larry Nance Jr. recovered easily; no rotation needed.

This first action didn't yield much, which is normal. The next step for the Wiz is moving quickly and effectively into something else, and making the right reads from there. This is where the above play ended up:

Sarr dribbles into a hand-off with Johnson, who has sprinted into the space that Coulibaly vacated. This looks promising; George is spaced one pass away, and Johnson has his defender on his back. But Johnson doesn't see the pass to George in time, and Jalen Slawson blows up his dribble for a dunk the other way.

They have the right idea here. They're trying to use Sarr and Johnson in their optimal roles. But defenses see these kinds of actions every game. Without a higher level of precision — physical, well-timed screens, and snappy passing — these plays look ordinary and even aimless.

There are ways to loosen this up. Step one, of course, is to make some shots. (Even Thursday was a mixed bag in this regard, with Johnson shooting 1-of-9 from three.) Getting more consistent offense from wings other than George, who is leveling up before our eyes, would be a godsend. Most of all, the Wizards need Young to find more success probing around and behind bigs, rather than ending up in these situations:

The Wizards have been focused on running these "angle" pick and rolls near the sideline. They are useful for generating pocket passes to the bigs, while spacing guys on the other side. But too often, with Young struggling to separate off the dribble, opposing teams are using aggressive coverages and pinning Young to the sideline.

Tough games are looming against feisty, athletic Western Conference teams in Houston and Memphis. With a 4-9 record, they'll need to find a way to create more advantages in short order.
