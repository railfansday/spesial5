Airline pricing has come under more scrutiny in recent years after Delta disclosed it was using AI to price some fares and a lawsuit was filed against JetBlue, alleging that the company uses personal data to price airfares. The airline industry has said it uses no personal data to determine pricing.

Airline pricing is once again under scrutiny. Last week, the Federal Trade Commission ordered Delta Air Lines, United Airlines and JetBlue to provide data on how they set fares, as part of a study of whether companies use personal information to set prices.

The agency wants details on the companies’ pricing methods. The companies have 45 days to respond.

“We intend to scrutinize the opaque methodology that some companies in these industries may be using to determine how much consumers pay or how much gig workers are paid for the same product or service,” FTC Chairman Andrew Ferguson said in a statement.

The FTC sent similar orders to Lyft, DoorDash, Uber, Instacart, Target and Walmart, seeking data on pricing and, for the gig companies, on worker pay.

The Federal Trade Commission has ordered Delta Air Lines, United Airlines, and JetBlue to hand over data on how they set fares, part of a broader study into whether companies use personal information to price goods and services. The airlines have 45 days to respond. The same orders went to Lyft, DoorDash, Uber, Instacart, Target, and Walmart, with FTC Chairman Andrew Ferguson saying the agency intends to scrutinize "opaque" pricing methodology. The inquiry lands as fares have risen sharply this year, largely because of surging jet fuel costs, and as airlines expand their use of AI in pricing. Delta, which told investors in 2024 it was working with the Israeli startup Fetcherr and said last year it was pricing about 3% of domestic routes with AI with a goal of up to 25% by the end of 2025, said it does not use personal data to set fares and has never used, tested, or planned individualized pricing. United referred questions to Airlines for America, which said it welcomes the study and that its carriers do not engage in personalized pricing. JetBlue faces a class action filed in April alleging it uses trackers to set prices dynamically. Roughly a dozen states have proposed laws this year limiting how companies collect and use customer search data, with California, Arizona, Hawaii, Maryland, Pennsylvania, and Vermont proposing outright bans on surveillance pricing.
