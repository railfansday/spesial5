Attacked by Camino Cows

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

As we left Sarria at 7:30 AM, we immediately joined the throngs of new pilgrims embarking on a weekend hike. To earn a Compostela certificate, rules dictate that you must complete at least the final 100 kilometers to Santiago. Today’s trek cover 14 miles under overcast skies, with cool, comfortable temperatures hovering in the high 60s.

We began our morning by climbing a hill toward the oldest section of the city, where many albergues are concentrated. At the crest, the city ended abruptly. A quick descent brought us right back out into the open countryside.

From there, the trail climbed steeply along a beautifully wooded path. Lined with massive trees, it resembled an ancient road cut through a dense forest. A steady stream of pilgrims climbed alongside us in the misty morning air. Looking ahead across the open fields, walkers stretched as far as the eye could see. After Sarria, the Camino feels noticeably different–much more like a bustling tourist event.

In the small Hamlet of Vilei, I found Jesus! (the small plastic Jesus often left along trails). I tucked him into my pocket, and just like that, Jesus joined us for the journey.

The trail continued uphill towards the towns of Barbadelo and Serra, winding along dirt paths that cut through cow pastures. Along the way, we passed ancient trees with trunks spanning up to 15 feet in diameter. The lingering mist added a mysterious quality to the woods. For miles at a time, stone walls framed our route past quiet orchards and tree farms.

As we navigated the mostly flat terrain between small villages, short climbs and descents kept us moving forward. In several towns, local herders guided cattle through narrow medieval streets. These cows had impressive horns and felt intimidating as they brushed past just inches away. On multiple occasions, we had to use our trekking poles to gently nudge them away from our bodies.

Eventually, we crossed a peaceful creek via an old stone walking bridge. The sudden influx of weekend pilgrims was lively and loud, creating a sharp contrast to the quiet solitude of our earlier weeks. To manage the distraction, we turned inward and focused on our rhythm. When we checked our progress, we were shocked to realize we had covered 7 miles in just two hours!

Right past the creek stood a tree covered in memorials dedicated to loved ones. I stopped to scan the cards, viewing names, dates and faces–including a photo of a young man at just 20 years old. Taking a moment to pray and reflect, I felt immense gratitude not only for this journey, but for all the years that God has given me.

Throughout this stretch of the Camino, I noticed tall, narrow stone structures perched up on elevated stilts. At first, they looked like elaborate decorations, roughly the size of a coffin. After seeing dozens of them across various farming villages, my curiosity got the better of me. I snapped a photo and asked AI to identify them.

It turns out these structures are traditional granaries (horreos) us to store grain. They are raised five feet off the ground to keep rodents out, featured slated stone walls to allow airflow while keeping the crop dry. Many were surprisingly ornate, and we continued seeing them for weeks to come.

We stopped for coffee in the small hamlet of Morgade, where we ran into several pilgrims we haven’t seen in days. Many looked weary from the sudden crowd of weekend hikers. Soon after, our “tramily”, Robert and Jill, popped into the same café.

We ran into some familiar faces today! First, we bumped into Simon—our long-lost friend from the very beginning of this hike.

Better yet, we also crossed paths with Manuel, our new friend from Portugal. Manuel is a seasoned pilgrim who has hiked numerous Caminos. What makes Manuel unforgettable is the heavy collection of Camino trinkets he carries from every single journey he has completed.

Reunited, with our tramily, we set off together for the final push into Portomarin.

Emerging into an open clearing on a high ridge, we were surprised by the mountainous terrain still visible in the distance. Below us in the valley stretch the broad river, with now forms a massive reservoir lake.

To reach the water, we navigated a steep, narrow path eroded into the hillside and boxed in by 10-foot stone walls. The descent required careful footing across loose, large rocks. At the bottom, the trail met a paved road leading to a long, modern bridge spanning the river into Portomarin.

Portomarin was completely relocated to the hillside in the 1950s when the river was dammed o create the reservoir. As a result, the town lost much of its historic feel, even though several historic structures—including the main cathedral—were moved brick by brick.

Unlike medieval villages with narrow, winding alleys, new Portomarin features wide, straight streets line with uniform concrete paving. The layout gives the town a modern, resort-like feel, amplified by the crowds finishing their 100 kilometer leg.

We gathered with our tramily for food and drinks before retiring to our plain, modern albergue for the night. Reflecting on the day, we missed the quiet intimacy of the trail prior to Sarria, and we agreed that we wouldn’t repeat this final 100 kilometer section on future trips.
