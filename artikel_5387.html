13 Friday the 13th facts, from Jason's burlap sack to the calendar math

Friday the 13th trivia covering Jason's hockey mask, his mother's killing spree, Tom Savini's effects, Kane Hodder and why the date itself is considered unlucky

The most recognizable part of Jason Voorhees is something he did not have at the start. The white hockey mask now tied to the whole Friday the 13th series first appears in the third film. By then, the franchise had already gone through two movies, a different killer and a different disguise.

That gap between the image and its origin runs through much of the series. The first Friday the 13th opened on May 9, 1980, and Jason was not the one doing the killing. When an adult Jason finally arrived in the sequel, he hid his face behind something other than a hockey mask. The look most people picture took shape gradually, one film at a time.

The franchise grew to 12 films between 1980 and 2009. Across those releases, Jason drowned as a boy, returned as a grown man, was killed and came back again. He eventually shared the screen with another horror villain. More than one actor played him. One sequel opened in 3D, and more than one release landed on a Friday the 13th.

The title also stretched beyond the films. Friday the 13th: The Series ran on television from 1987 to 1990. Despite the shared name, it does not feature Jason. The show used the brand without the character.

This collection of Friday the 13th trivia covers 13 points. It runs from the burlap sack Jason wore before the mask to the sound cue built from a few repeated syllables. It also covers the effects artist behind the original film's deaths, the actor who played Jason in four films and a sequel twist that put a copycat behind the killings.

The final slide steps away from the movies and looks at the date itself. Fear of Friday the 13th has a formal name. The calendar math behind how often the date comes around is more exact than the folklore about why it is unlucky. Each slide stands on its own. Readers can start with the mask or skip ahead to the superstition.

Jason Voorhees first wears his hockey mask in Friday the 13th Part III, the 1982 sequel directed by Steve Miner. Before that film, Jason did not wear a hockey mask.

After Part III, the mask stayed. It became Jason's permanent look, and the character kept it in the sequels that followed Miner's film.

How the mask came to be used is less settled. Accounts of the production disagree on who chose it. They also disagree on how it reached the set. With no agreed version, no single person can be credited with the choice.

Jason Voorhees first appears as an adult in Friday the 13th Part 2, released in 1981. In that film, he wears a burlap sack over his head rather than a hockey mask.

Part 2 is the starting point for the grown-up version of the character. The original 1980 Friday the 13th never shows an adult Jason.

The burlap sack is the first face covering an adult Jason wears on screen in the series. Its 1981 release came one year after the original film.

The original Friday the 13th was filmed at Camp No-Be-Bo-Sco, a real camp in Hardwick Township, New Jersey. On screen, those grounds stand in for Camp Crystal Lake, the fictional summer camp at the center of the 1980 film.

The movie uses two names for one place. Camp Crystal Lake is the name viewers hear in the story. Camp No-Be-Bo-Sco is where the production shot its scenes.

Camp Crystal Lake is an invented name. A search for it on a map will not turn up the place seen in the film. The real location behind the fictional camp is Camp No-Be-Bo-Sco, in Hardwick Township, New Jersey.

The "ki ki ki, ma ma ma" sound in Friday the 13th is the work of composer Harry Manfredini. He built it from syllables drawn from the phrase "kill her, mommy."

Manfredini wrote the score for the original 1980 film. This cue is not a melody. It is a short string of clipped syllables.

Each half of the cue comes from one word. The "ki" is taken from "kill." The "ma" is taken from "mommy." Each syllable appears three times as the cue is written.

Not every word of the phrase survives in the sound. The "her" in "kill her, mommy" drops out entirely. What remains is the opening sound of the two words at either end of the phrase.

The phrase behind the sound points to the film's killer, Pamela Voorhees. Its final word, "mommy," fits a story in which a mother is the one committing the murders.

Corey Feldman plays Tommy Jarvis in Friday the 13th: The Final Chapter, released in 1984. Tommy is the character who kills Jason Voorhees in the film.

The Final Chapter reached theaters two years after Friday the 13th Part III. Counting from the original 1980 film, it is the fourth entry in the series.

The title presented the film as the end of the franchise. It was not. Friday the 13th: A New Beginning followed in 1985.

The ending puts Feldman's character at the center of the climax. Tommy, not Jason, is the one who ends the film's run of killings.

A lightning strike brings Jason Voorhees back from the dead in Jason Lives, the 1986 Friday the 13th sequel. Tom McLoughlin directed the film.

Jason had already died on screen before this film. The lightning bolt is the device that returns him, and it puts the series' central villain back at the center of the story.

The title spells out that premise. In Jason Lives, Jason is alive again.

Counting from the original 1980 release, Jason Lives is the sixth Friday the 13th film. It arrived one year after Friday the 13th: A New Beginning.

Jason Goes to Hell, released in 1993, ends with Freddy Krueger's glove pulling Jason's mask underground. Freddy vs. Jason followed in 2003. A reboot that reused the title Friday the 13th arrived in 2009.

The final image of Jason Goes to Hell puts Freddy's glove and Jason's mask in the same frame. It does not show the two characters fighting. The mask is pulled below ground, and the film ends there.

The scene points toward a meeting between the two villains without staging one. It leaves that matchup for a later film.

Freddy vs. Jason reached theaters 10 years after that ending. Ronny Yu directed the film. The crossover put on screen the pairing that the glove scene had only hinted at.

The casting marked a change. Ken Kirzinger played Jason in Freddy vs. Jason. Kane Hodder, who had played Jason in the films just before it, did not take the role for the crossover.

Jason Voorhees is not the killer in the original Friday the 13th. The murderer in the 1980 film is his mother, Pamela Voorhees, played by Betsy Palmer.

Sean S. Cunningham directed the film, and Victor Miller wrote the script. Their story is set at Camp Crystal Lake, the summer camp where the killings take place.

Jason's part in this first installment is small. In the story, he drowned as a boy at Camp Crystal Lake. His death belongs to the film's backstory, not to its run of murders.

He appears on screen only in a shock scene late in the film. That brief moment is the full extent of his role in the original.

The later sequels made Jason the franchise's central figure. In the 1980 film, the character whose name now defines the series is a drowned child, not the attacker.

The answer to "who is the killer in Friday the 13th?" depends on which film the question refers to. For the 1980 original, it is his mother.

Tom Savini created the special effects for the original 1980 Friday the 13th. His work includes the death of Kevin Bacon's character, who is killed by an arrow through the neck.

Bacon is part of the first film's cast. His character does not survive the story.

The arrow scene is one of the killings Savini's effects put on screen in the 1980 film.

Friday the 13th Part III opened in 3D on Aug. 13, 1982. That date fell on a Friday.

The timing put the third film in theaters on the exact day its title names. A series named for an unlucky date had one of its entries premiere on that date.

The 3D format was part of the release itself, not a later reissue. The film arrived in theaters in 3D on its opening date.

Jason Voorhees is not the killer in Friday the 13th: A New Beginning. The 1985 film reveals that a copycat, not Jason, committed its murders.

A New Beginning is the fifth film in the Friday the 13th series. It arrived one year after Friday the 13th: The Final Chapter.

The twist makes it the second film in the series in which Jason does not commit the killings. The first was the 1980 original.

The copycat setup did not carry into the next entry. Jason Lives, the sixth film, returned the real Jason to the center of the series.

Kane Hodder played Jason Voorhees in four Friday the 13th films, released between 1988 and 2001. His run covers The New Blood, Jason Takes Manhattan, Jason Goes to Hell and Jason X $TWTR.

Hodder first took on the role in The New Blood, released in 1988. He returned the following year for Jason Takes Manhattan, released in 1989. Those were his only back-to-back appearances as the character.

The gaps between his films then grew longer. Jason Goes to Hell followed in 1993, four years after Jason Takes Manhattan.

Hodder's final film as the character was Jason X, released in 2001. It arrived eight years after Jason Goes to Hell, the longest break in his run.

From The New Blood to Jason X, Hodder's tenure as Jason spans 13 years.

The 2009 reboot came six years after Freddy vs. Jason. Marcus Nispel directed it. The film carried no subtitle and no number.

It took its name directly from the original 1980 movie, Friday the 13th. The plain title marked it as a reboot rather than another numbered sequel.

The reboot opened on Feb. 13, 2009. That day was a Friday. The film's release date matched its own title.

No one knows for certain why Friday the 13th is considered unlucky. The calendar is more precise: every year has at least one Friday the 13th and no more than three.

The fear of the date has a formal name: paraskevidekatriaphobia. The superstition's origin is unclear. Several historical events are regularly tied to the date. None of them has been shown to be the source of the belief.

The Last Supper is one of the most frequently cited links. It turns up in many retellings of where the superstition began. That connection belongs to folklore, not to established history.

The arrests of the Knights Templar are another. They took place on Friday, Oct. 13, 1307. Popular accounts often point to that day as a starting point. That link is also folklore rather than an established cause.

A later event also shows up in the record. The composer Gioachino Rossini died on Friday, Nov. 13, 1868. His death is often cited in early printed references to the date. A date of death fixes when something happened. It does not explain why a belief began.

The Gregorian calendar repeats its pattern of dates and weekdays every 400 years. Across that cycle, the 13th of a month lands on a Friday 688 times. No other weekday reaches that total.

Friday's lead is slight. A 400-year cycle holds 4,800 months, which means 4,800 13ths. An even split across all seven weekdays would give each day about 686. Friday's 688 sits about two dates above that mark.

Spread over the full cycle, those 688 dates average 1.72 Friday the 13ths per year. No single year can match that average, because each year's count is a whole number. In practice, a given year has one, two or three.

The two halves of this story differ in certainty. The folklore has no confirmed starting point, while the calendar count can be checked to the exact date.
