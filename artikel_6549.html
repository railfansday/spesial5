Alexis Nikole Nelson, aka the Black Forager, Makes Nature Her Buffet

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

This is Amused Bouche, SAVEUR’s food questionnaire that explores the culinary curiosities of some of our favorite people. This interview series will dive deep into their food routines, including dinner party strategies, cherished cookbooks, and the memorable bites they’d hop on a flight for.

Alexis Nicole Nelson’s life mantra is simple: “Don’t put things in your face hole unless you know what they are!” This mostly applies to foraging wild foods, something she has been doing since she was a kid with her mom and has since turned into a flourishing career and community. She has showcased her adventures and recipes as @blackforager since 2018 (with millions of curious followers across Instagram, TikTok, and YouTube) and now in her debut cookbook, Happy Snacking, Don’t Die! This comprehensive guide to finding and cooking with ingredients from your backyard or nearby forests and fields is organized by season and anchored by Nelson’s vibrant personality and boundless energy, encouraging readers to get out of their comfort zones and educate themselves on edible plants hiding in plain sight.

Nelson’s journey to becoming the Black Forager started with an interest in vegetarianism as a 5-year-old, which, at the time, was discouraged by her family. “You have to finish growing first!” Nelson recalls her mom saying. Nelson ate meat products to keep from wasting food her parents prepared until she was 12, when she decided to start packing her own lunches. “It was a lot of undressed Boca burgers and French fries. It was a bleak time,” she admits with a laugh. She went fully vegan at 24 and hasn’t looked back.

I am not a very outdoorsy person, but I was captivated by the whimsical way Nelson goes about foraging and sharing online, and even more so after chatting with her on a recent Zoom call. When I asked for easy gateway foraging ideas, she immediately suggested field garlic or onion grass: “I am a biased party, because that is the first forageable that my mom taught me. Once you notice it, you see it everywhere, and it has a foolproof test, which is the smell test. If you break it and it doesn’t very fragrantly smell of onions and garlic, you have the wrong plant. Easy, easy!” She recommended looking for little purple flowers or round pink spiky balls, and on my next walk with my dogs, I spotted some. I haven’t picked and cooked with any yet, but now I’m on the lookout (and smell-out) for this abundant allium and plan on pairing it with the maitake bacon recipe from her book. Nelson suggested making a “BLT-adjacent sandwich with whatever mild greens are in season, a slice of tomato, and maitake bacon on toasty sourdough.”

Whether you’re a foraging beginner or an expert, Nelson’s edition of Amused Bouche will be equally eye-opening and mouthwatering. Read on for her adventures and misadventures in the fields and in her home kitchen.

If you could only eat one thing 24/7/365, what would it be?

I love a tofu scramble and mixing in whatever is in season, and I bring home mushrooms, greens, and aromatics. In the springtime, it’ll be ramp leaves and oyster mushrooms. In the summer, it’s chanterelle and bicolor bolete with some nodding onion flowers from my front yard. As we move into winter, I’m thankful that we get enoki mushrooms pretty consistently all throughout the season in the woods. I do a combo of firm silken and super-firm tofu because I want the best of both worlds. Eggs don’t just have one texture; the whites and yolks are different, and I always want to try to get as close to eggs as I can with my tofu textures. I’m perpetually on a search for a forageable legume that I can make my own tofu with. One of my friends, Jesse Akozbek (@feralforaging), makes tofu with black walnuts.

What’s the first thing you learned how to cook?

The first thing that I remember cooking is baking cakes with my dad—usually just a basic frosted vanilla cake. My mom used to be a cool head-honcho character at Procter & Gamble, and she would be gone for two or three weeks at a time for work. We’d bake the cake the night before [she got back], frost it, and then just before she came home, we’d leave the cake out and hide.

I broke out my food mill because I went a little overboard harvesting cornelian cherries with my fiancé this year. They’re fun to harvest, and we brought a step stool to reach more and ended up going home with a couple gallons of fruit. It’s all fun and games until you see them all on the counter and realize they’re gonna go bad so I gotta figure out what I’m doing with all of this. I thought of doing a glacé cornelian cherry like I’ve done with chestnuts before, but the water content of the cherries is so high that when I simmered them in a sugar syrup, they just disintegrated and disappeared. I tried to stir it carefully, but it all just turned into goo. I fished them out with my little spider, put them in my food mill, and ground it. They have big ol’ pits on the inside, and I am not trying to eat any of those. It was an applesauce consistency in the end, but like what if applesauce had dreams? What if applesauce was the star of a Studio Ghibli movie? I made cornelian cherry shortcakes with it, and my fiancé was like, “So I’ve had three…” and we realized that when you eat a lot of fruit and fiber at once, you remember it later. I also saved the flavored syrup that was created after straining out all of the goopy fruits, and I am saving it specifically for making Shirley Temples!

My favorite way to treat myself is getting dinner with my fiancé at Agni, an amazing Bengali American fusion restaurant here in Columbus, led by my friend, chef Avishar Barua! They have these french fries that take three days to make, and I—no joke—dream about them sometimes.

Marie Viljoen’s Forage, Harvest, Feast is the cookbook that showed me as a recent college graduate that foraging, the weird thing I’ve been doing since childhood, could become such elevated cuisine. It’s absolutely stunning, and she is a recipe and photography genius—she does it all! I remember checking that book out from the library for the first time and my jaw dropping at how she used foraged foods in ways I had never even let myself consider. She gets shouted out in my cookbook because I used a few of her recipes as a jumping-off point then over time I started changing things to make them my own. A decade later, it becomes something different but still has the heart of that original recipe, like my milkweed nectar is definitely the child of her milkweed cordial! Her book was eye-opening for me even before I knew this was what I was going to end up making my career, back when it was something I just did because I loved it and I was a poor recent grad. A really great way to spruce up a 59-cent pack of ramen is wild greens and mushrooms!

Is there a cooking disaster that made you swear off a dish forever?

I keep doing this thing where I start fermenting drinks and then travel, which is dumb. Don’t do that! My most explosive ferment was actually not mine, it was a gift from fellow foraging friend Gabrielle Cerberville (@chaoticforager). She very kindly brought me a bottle of her wild plum wine, and I did not put it straight into the fridge because I was not using my noggin. Three days later, I went to open it to pair with dinner, and it was like my world was in slow motion and it was just a geyser everywhere! Wine on the ceiling, wine on the walls, a lot of wine on the floor, a lot of wine on me. I cleaned it up sadly, shooing my pets away. No drunk pets! I also had a milkweed cordial explode, which meant I had to scrub sticky pink off every single thing in the refrigerator. Do not recommend it.

Which nostalgic foods from childhood bring you the most comfort?

I grew up in the Cincinnati area, so there will be a soft spot in my heart for Cincinnati chili forever. It’s just ingrained within me. Thankfully Skyline has vegan rice and beans using the same spices that you can also put onto pasta like a wild Ohioan. That will always taste like a Friday evening with my dad, because my mom is from New England and famously hates Cincinnati chili. I think calling it a chili was maybe its biggest downfall—when you learn about the history behind it and how it’s actually the child of a Greek meat sauce, suddenly the spice palette makes so much more sense. I think people would be so much cooler with that, but everyone’s expecting Tex-Mex chili, and that is not what you are signing up for!

When you’re playing dinner party DJ, what’s spinning?

There was a podcast I was obsessed with called The Dinner Party Download a decade ago, and I named my playlist after that. It has been evolving throughout the past 10 years and has Mitski, which I know feels sad for a dinner party but it’s mellow, some alt-J, Bill Withers, The Contours. A lot of Motown-era music, like The Supremes, Stevie Wonder, The Four Tops, Staple Singers. And then Janelle Monáe, Kate Nash, “Rhapsody in Blue” by George Gershwin is thrown in there. I go all over the place, but I think it creates a general buzzy atmosphere. I don’t want it to necessarily be as dancey as a dance party, but I also don’t want people to fall asleep, either.

What is your biggest entertaining flex to impress guests?

I really love plating things and cutting things into fun shapes and making them look pretty. I took a cooking class in Japan with Elizabeth Andoh, a very prolific cookbook writer, a couple of years ago, and after my fiancé and I walked out of it, we were just like, “Dang, our food’s real ugly!” A huge part of her class was how much presentation matters and how the first way that you are consuming the food is with your sight, with your eyes, so why not make it look cute?

Tell me about a meal so good you would hop on a flight to relive it.

While my fiancé and I were in Florence for a friend’s wedding, we knew that Italy, historically, was not the easiest place to be vegan. You can find things but maybe not that many things, and maybe not the things that look as good as the things everybody else is eating. So I was big sad the entire time until we stumbled into a tiny hole-in-the-wall vegan restaurant called Il Vegano. We found it on Google by searching “vegan restaurants near me,” and it had the best pasta with tomato sauce and tiramisù that either of us have ever had. Mind you, my fiancé is not vegan and very much could still eat real tiramisù and did on that trip. And the night ended with us essentially begging in broken Italian for the head chef to write a cookbook. We waddled back to our hotel saying it was the best meal we’ve ever had.
