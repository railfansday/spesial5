Review: CRKD Ult Pro Controller For Switch 1 & 2 - A Decent Pad Let Down By Awful Shoulder & Bumper Buttons

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

CRKD is the latest accessory manufacturer to produce a premium alternative to Nintendo’s own Pro Controller on Switch 2, but it’s one that needs a lot of refinement if it’s to become a legitimate competitor.

Priced at $69.99 / €79.99 / £64.99, the Ult Pro sits below the Pro Controller and roughly at par with Gulikit’s excellent TT line, price-wise. It comes feature-packed with TMR (Tunnel Magnetoresistance) analogue sticks, Hall Effect triggers, four back buttons, two extra bumper inputs, four profile modes, adjustable rumble, a charging stand, and more.

The analogue sticks feel good, featuring a more convex shape that you don’t often see these days. There’s a thin ridge near the edge of the grip which helps keep you aware of where your thumbs are sitting. It’s also worth mentioning that the grips can be removed for replacement designs, but these are sold separately – it would have been nice if at least one alternative were included in the box as standard. Otherwise, the TMR technology should keep stick-drift at bay.

The default D-pad design (again, you can swap it out if you want) is circular with a raised cross representing the four cardinal directions. I like this a lot: it feels quiet yet retains accuracy during gameplay, and I found it reliable for platformers like Super Mario World and action games like Contra.

The ABXY buttons unfortunately aren’t mechanical with a satisfying click, but they do the job well enough. Like the analogue sticks, they’re slightly convex in design and are spaced at just the right distance to allow easy, intuitive access to all of them. I also like the colours; CRKD avoided the exact same colour scheme as both the SNES and Xbox 360 pads, but it’s got the same vibe (you can also opt for an all-black controller, if you wish).

The shoulder and bumper buttons, sadly, let the side down. You can adjust the shoulder button travel distance, opting between a short, clicky input or a full, smooth trigger input. To be clear, the Switch 2 doesn’t support analogue triggers for anything except the Nintendo Switch Online GameCube pad, so your choice here is purely down to personal preference.

While the full-motion trigger feels pretty good, the clicky, tactile option is horrible. Rather than offering a satisfying click, they instead feel weak and spongy, almost as if the slightest bit of force will snap (or ‘CRK’, amirite) whatever mechanism is inside to produce the clicky response. I hated this, and CRKD should address it promptly for any future revisions.

As for the bumper inputs, the additional ‘L2’ and ‘R2’ inputs are far too close to the main ‘L1’ and ‘R1’ buttons on either side. So essentially, when resting your index finger across the bumpers, you’re covering both the standard button and the extra input, making it far too easy to accidentally press the wrong button. CRKD would have been wise to space these further apart and make the main bumpers larger (8BitDo’s Pro 3 is a good reference point), or just remove the extra inputs entirely.

Speaking of extra inputs, the four additional buttons on the rear are perfectly fine – I’ve got no major issues with these, and they’ll no doubt prove useful for anyone looking to gain the upper hand in Modern Warfare 4 (or perhaps even sprint while moving the camera in Ocarina of Time). The only downside is that they produce quite a loud click when pressed, though I think this is down to the plastic used for the exterior casing – it feels a tad cheap for the price.

You can map any input to the additional buttons — the four on the back and the two on top — by using the ‘P’ (Program) button underneath the D-pad. You also have four profiles if you want to swap between configurations. CRKD neglected to include decent instructions with the product, and the QR code for an online manual led me to a dead page, but it does at least have a video on YouTube if you need help remapping buttons and setting up profiles.

With the function buttons (i.e. Start, Select, Capture), CRKD bizarrely opted for two rocking inputs. With the left one, for example, you push it up for Select and down for Capture. It’s so weird – it works, but it’s weird. There’s no dedicated ‘C’ button; instead, there's an integrated macro to navigate to the Chat app if you push down on the right rocker.

You can connect the Ult Pro to your console via three different methods. Bluetooth is standard and works exactly how you’d expect: just hold down the large, black button until it flashes blue to enter pairing mode. You also have a USB dongle stored inside the charging dock, so plug this into your Switch 2 dock and you can pair that way instead. Finally, you can simply connect it via a USB-A to USB-C cable. The difference between these is, for me at least, not noticeable, but if you’re someone who’s particularly sensitive to lag, you might have a clear preference here.

Oh, and there’s no wake-up support for Switch 2. Sorry.

Finally, the Ult Pro lasts for around 15 hours on a single charge, and if you use the included charging dock, you’ll likely never need to worry about it dying on you. The dock design is simple and looks neat next to the TV when charging. You can also replace the rechargeable battery in the controller itself if need be, or simply use a pair of AA batteries.

There’s a lot to like about the Ult Pro, including its TMR sticks, the D-pad, the charging dock, and the lovely coloured ABXY inputs. Unfortunately, CRKD really dropped the ball with the shoulder and bumper buttons – I hate them, and they really ruin the whole thing for me.

With no wake-up support and a fairly cheap-feeling exterior case, this isn’t quite the Pro Controller competitor I was hoping for, but with some tweaks and refinements, I think the company could be onto something. Do I actually like those rocking function buttons, though? Honestly, I’m still not sure.

The sample used in this review was provided by CRKD.
