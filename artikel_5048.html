The 10 Best Cross-Training Shoes for Women, Tested by Editors

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

We put dozens of shoes through lifts, sprints, and lateral moves to find the most stable, versatile picks for every kind of gym workout.

OUR FITNESS EDITORS love to slip on cushy sneakers like the Hoka Clifton 11 for our 5Ks and hot girl walks. But on days when we're lifting heavy, doing a functional strength training workout, or have a gym sesh that switches between lifts and sprints, we turn to our favorite cross-training shoes. If you’re unfamiliar with the term, cross-training is combining different exercise modalities—like cardio, resistance training, and plyometric (rapid movements)—during a gym session. Think of these sneakers as multipurpose powerhouses. They keep your feet locked in, whether you’re deadlifting or speed walking on an under-desk treadmill.

“They’re designed to handle side-to-side movement, quick pivots, and changes in direction—not just forward motion,” says Melissa Lockwood, DPM, a podiatrist at Heartland Foot and Ankle Associates in Bloomington, Illinois. “This makes them more supportive for gym workouts, classes, or sports where your foot needs stability in multiple planes, like pickleball or tennis.” On the flip side, a walking or running shoe usually only supports you in straight-line motion, Lockwood says.

Wearing running sneakers for strength training workouts is generally a no-no (except for the caveat about the Brooks Adrenaline GTS 25 below). All the podiatrists I spoke with say it’s safer and beneficial to invest in cross-training shoes. That’s because they usually have flatter outsoles and thinner midsoles than running shoes to keep your feet locked in, says Richard H. Graves, DPM, a podiatrist at Sol Foot & Ankle Centers in Long Beach, California. That way, you won’t wobble during your deadlifts and box jumps. The material of cross-training shoes is also more durable than running shoes to resist wear and tear, and has thick heel counters to help prevent ankle injuries.

Whether you're a seasoned weightlifting junkie or a runner on a mission to integrate more training modalities into your routine, read on for the best cross-training shoes you can find.

After testing dozens of cross-training shoes this past year, the latest lululemon Chargefeel 3 stands out for its impeccable support and stability during speedy, lateral movements like ice skaters, kettlebell squats, and Zumba workouts. We’ve been fans of the Chargefeel line for years, with the previous edition—the Chargefeel 2—winning the best cross-training shoe for our 2025 WH Sneaker Awards. Lululemon has since made a few tweaks that make it even better. “Despite the fact that sneaker fit and feel is extremely subjective, everyone I know who has worked out in the Chargefeel—myself included—has loved its perfect balance of stability and flexibility,” says WH executive health and fitness director Jacqueline Andriakos, NASM-CPT.

It’s now 8 ounces instead of 9.1 ounces, and has a lower heel-to-toe drop—6 mm compared to 9.5 mm—to help alleviate stress on the ankles and feet. WH associate style and commerce editor Shannen Zitz loves the wider build for lifting and squatting. She says her feet feel locked in and extra secure. “My feet stay securely in place thanks to a forefoot-hugging band on the inside of the shoe and a tough plastic heel counter,” says Zitz. She also notes that the springy EVA foam cushioning makes it a workhorse for walking and running.

And post-workout, it’s a great leisure kick for traveling or running errands. “I recently wore these while traveling, and they kept my feet super happy power walking through the airport and standing on long security and customs lines,” Zitz adds. Andriakos agrees, stating, “The stylish silhouette looks good outside the gym.”

The fit is true to size, and the breathable knit upper doesn’t feel constricting when wearing thick socks (perfect for combating blisters!). Although wide size options are not available, the toes still have adequate room to spread with each step or lift. However, Zitz notes the wide outsole may feel too bulky during quick and dynamic HIIT workouts.

We wouldn’t recommend most running shoes for cross-training, but the Brooks Adrenaline GTS 25 is an exception. You can effortlessly transition from long runs to deadlifts, thanks to its balanced cushioning and moderate heel-to-toe drop.

Compared to the GTS 24, the latest version has slightly softer cushioning, with about 3 mm more foam in the forefoot and 1 mm in the heel, giving it a smoother, more comfortable feel underfoot. It also drops from 12 mm to 10 mm, creating a slightly more level feel. That can make it feel more stable during strength work than higher-drop running shoes.

At 9 ounces, it’s a lightweight gem, especially for those with overpronation or flat feet during agility drills. Its GuideRails technology—firm foam on both sides of the foot—keeps feet aligned during everything from Bulgarian split squats to lunges to speed walking. Plus, stiff heel padding keeps feet from sliding."These feel super comfortable and supportive for everyday life, traditional workouts, walks, easy runs—you name it," says Melissa Kendter, CPT, a functional strength coach. "I love that this shoe feels well cushioned but doesn't have a huge chunky sole, making it more appropriate for strength workouts than most running shoes."

Because the outsole is relatively smooth (it’s built for road miles, not side-to-side gym moves), we’d stick to non-slip floors for quick lateral work. If your workouts are heavy on lifting or plyometrics, a flatter cross-trainer like the Nike Metcon 10 may feel steadier and more secure underfoot.

We’ve been fans of the Nano series since 2018, and every year we notice Reebok levels up its cushioning and heel stability to keep our feet locked in and comfortable during even the most intense workouts. The midsole padding is firm for balancing during heavy lifts and lateral movements, yet is still bouncy for excellent rebound during HIIT workouts and explosive jumps. The mesh material upper is also firm, keeping you steady when lifting heavy.

MH digital designer Taryn Colbert used to prefer flat lifestyle shoes like Converse or Vans, but has since switched over to the Nano X5 for her cross-training sessions. The stiffer soles offer her better balance, while she notes that the heel counter is dense, meaning it doesn’t flex easily. However, the 7 mm heel-to-toe drop may be too steep for some weightlifters who prefer a ground-feel shoe. But it’s a pro if you like extra heel support.

The grippy outsole delivers superior traction when executing deadlifts or weighted squats, while the snug laces never budge. Colbert also notes that the breathable material helps combat sweaty feet. Just note that the fit runs tight (consider ordering at least a half-size up), and it weighs 12.1 ounces, making it one of the heaviest shoes in this guide.

If your workouts focus on dynamic moves like kettlebell swings or heavy lifting, the Nobull Drive is a gem. It’s crafted with a breathable mesh knit upper that feels sock-like and keeps feet cool during grueling reps.

The balance of flexibility and firmness makes it simple to transition from the squat rack to the HIIT studio. WH associate editor Olivia Luppino wears them during physical therapy to help maintain balance when doing Bosu ball squats, and then hits the gym to do deadlifts. She also notes the mesh isn’t scratchy—an issue she has had when wearing this material.

The subtle foam feels similar to a minimally-cushioned running shoe, while the low 4 mm drop keeps Luppino grounded when deadlifting. She also notes that a firm heel counter makes her feet feel secure without too much stiffness. And at 8.3 ounces, it’s the lightest shoe in this guide, which makes it an optimal choice for sprints and mixed-modality workouts.

Nike’s latest Metcon 10 is a top pick among our editors when it comes to pumping iron. Its main update compared to the last iteration is a smaller, stiffer plate strategically placed under the heel to help stabilize the feet when squatting heavy reps or doing deadlifts. It’s also lighter at 11.8 ounces (it was previously 13.3 ounces) and feels more responsive during jumps and HIIT when it’s not leg day.

Zitz was completely sold after her first wear. “I feel totally grounded with the 4 mm drop while squatting and lunging, and they never weigh me down while running,” says Zitz. “I also love how sleek and stylish they look (especially in the pink color!), and I've been wearing them with nearly every athleisure fit since I got them.”

The tough rubber traction is another highlight. It feels almost sticky, helping you stay put. Zitz adds that the grooves on the outsole are great for slick sidewalks or dynamic movements, like box jumps. The mesh upper also offers some breathability, but it’s not the most airy shoe in this guide.

Accardi says her movement feels “controlled, yet free” when these are on her feet. “While firmness is important in a cross-training shoe, many are too stiff, which limits power in workouts unrelated to lifting,” she says. “These feel nimble yet stable for agility drills and plyometric moves like box jumps.”

The moderate 6 mm drop and heel counter provide balance and sturdiness. The springy EVA midsole also alleviates joint impact during high-impact moves. And bonus: the cushioning is wrapped with rubber to help combat wear and tear, while the textured outsole provides perfect traction during lateral bounds and tuck jumps. “My footing feels nice and steady during transitions, whether I’m going fast or slow.”

Note that this kick is the heaviest option in this guide, weighing 12.8 ounces. It’s not the best option for long-distance runs, but it’s a top pick if you’re all about explosive movements.

Besides short sprints, it’s designed for workouts that blend cardio and strength—box jumps, sprints, step-ups, and ice skaters. Its signature CloudTec cushioning—compressive foam that absorbs impact—is ultra-bouncy and makes you feel like you’re flying during jumps and agility drills. It propels you forward whether you’re walking, running, or doing dynamic movements. One word that describes it perfectly is energetic. The foam also isn’t overly soft, which is great for support during fast transitions.

If you want a marshmallow-like feel in your cross-training kicks, the While On Earth Move Trainer is a star pick that’s plush minus the bulk. The spongy EVA foam is ideal for short or long runs, while a heel counter (hard plastic that wraps around the heel) prevents any budging during weight training. It’s also light and nimble at 10.6 ounces, making it a workhorse for cardio and strength work.

The Cordura nylon upper is abrasion-resistant and strong, while the rubber outsole is thick and sturdy to sustain repetitive force. Nike took the best traits of its iconic Free running shoe to create the Metcon 6—a trusty hybrid running and cross trainer. Whether you’re executing agility drills, speed work, or lunges, it delivers the perfect combination of comfort and stability, no matter what workout you have on deck. "This is perfect for my training workouts that consist of part speed run intervals and part strength training because of its combination of cushioning and stability," says Ashley Barnett, NASM-CPT. She adds that it’s great for jumping, strength routines, and mixed workouts. Accardi also loves wearing it during HIIT classes, highlighting its flexibility and responsiveness when transitioning from burpees to high knees.

Compared to the Metcon 10, it has a higher drop at 7.2 mm. While this is a pro in a running shoe, it may mean having less stability during heavy lifts. It’s also worth noting that it fits snug and narrow, so you may have to go up a half size. Accardi has bunions and had to size up to prevent the fabric from rubbing against the sides of her feet during runs and agility drills.

Everyone has a unique foot shape, and each brand can fit differently. So, finding the right fit can be complex. But as a general rule of thumb, your feet should feel supported, not suffocated, and you shouldn't be counting down the minutes until you can kick them off, says Mali Pelham, CPT, C.F.S.C., a personal trainer at Wattage in Chicago.

An easy way to measure if you have enough space is by being able to slide your finger back and forth at the back of the heel. Also, your shoes shouldn’t feel like they’re pinching the middle of your foot—this can lead to tingling and numbness if they’re too tight. If you’re completely unsure, it doesn’t hurt to get fitted at a shoe store. Referencing brands’ sizing charts can also be helpful. Also, pay attention to width, as different brands offer narrow and wide options.

Heel-to-toe drop refers to the height difference between the heel and the forefoot of the shoe.

Consider the type of workouts you'll be tackling in your new kicks. Each demands specific qualities in a shoe, though the best cross-training shoes for women are versatile enough to let you do all these workouts in them.
