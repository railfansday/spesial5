In this article, I will share my personal experience of getting in touch with and implementing MoCA (Multimedia over Coax Alliance), including why, how, which choices I made, and which mistakes to avoid. I hope that it can help anybody interested in starting on this path.

In 2024, I moved to an independent house built on three floors... plenty of room available and just an FTTC internet connection capped at more or less 70 Mbps. I did not have many exigencies in terms of devices and interconnectivity—just a couple of NAS units, a printer, a smart TV (at that time plugged directly into the router), some domestic appliances like smart bulbs, a pet feeder, and some video surveillance cameras. All these latter leaned on the 2.4 GHz SSID of my router. The most critical point was regarding my NAS units, which I decided to place on the upper floor along with the printer, and the best solution I found was just to set up a Wi-Fi mesh using an AP (TP-Link RE450). The Wi-Fi router was instead placed on the ground floor, thus the speed and the signal were quite poor, but with a slow FTTC, you almost don't notice it.

Finally, after a couple of years, FTTH was delivered to my city and I quickly decided to upgrade my slow internet connection; I was lucky anyway since a 2.5 Gbps line was available, to which I switched immediately.

And then... the situation became quite different. How can I take full advantage of this bandwidth and make it available in all my home? I suddenly discovered that at several points (especially on the upper floor) walls were obstructing the Wi-Fi signal so that I could not fully benefit from my router, which in turn is also capable of providing a Wi-Fi 7 signal; my internet provider released to my contract a ZTE ZXHN H6745 router which also features one 2.5 Gbps port and two Gbps ports, thus there is the room and the capability to distribute the signal in other ways than Wi-Fi.

After a quick check, I discovered that the Wi-Fi signal on the upper floor and in one bedroom located on an intermediate floor was decreased by 50% up to 85%, jumping from up to 1 Gbps in Wi-Fi 7 close to the router to 400-500 Mbps on the upper floor, while in the room with the NAS units I could just get barely 100-150 Mbps and even less in the aforementioned bedroom. This could be acceptable for many applications, but not, for example, when backing up my laptop or moving large files to/from the NAS units, and moreover, who knows about any future usages?

So, the first answer to this requirement must be obvious: set up a small LAN pulling cables up to your router, add Ethernet wall jacks whenever necessary, and a switch patched to your router which has, luckily, a port with proper speed. This was the first step and my first attempt.

First, I need to say that my home has several rooms, each of them equipped with phone and TV wall jacks. I counted in total 8 TV coax plugs (all working) and 7 RJ11 phone plugs (never tested and used, anyway they are there...) thus the first and less impactful option was to assess this spare infrastructure to enrich it with Cat.6 cables that could provide all the necessary speed and bandwidth. Fish-taping several times from all the wall jacks, I discovered that most of the plugs are connected to a daisy chain (see Picture 1), and this, of course, does not make things easier.

I could not locate the ending point for all the plugs since, in 3 cases, the electrician used the same conduits for the AC cables and the phone, and I preferred not to touch them. Given that, I could assess that probably (but for sure not easily) I could turn into Ethernet 4 out of 7 phone plugs using "normal" Cat 6 cables, thus not specific CEI-UNEL 36762 C-4 according to Italian regulations. Anyway, to make a cable arrive at the router, I had to pull it through three different plugs and this, although possible, is not a guarantee of success, not being an expert in this stuff.

Googling and posing questions to ChatGPT, I found out that even in cases in which standard cabling cannot be put in place, there are options that could work provided that other kinds of connectivity are available. In detail, there are MoCA and G.hn, which also use other media to make the Ethernet signal travel. Here below I'm reporting a synthetic prospect of the different solutions that can be chosen:

Among these, some recommendations must be pointed out which I learned directly on my skin:

Always use F-type connectors and be very careful when screwing them onto the coaxial cables, especially avoiding gaps between the dielectric and the connector, and using the appropriate size of the connectors. I was forced in some cases to use 5 mm connectors instead of 7 mm, since not all the cables in the conduits have the same section, unfortunately.

All the spare ports in the tap and splitters must be correctly terminated (I used Fracarro CA75F components) to avoid impedance mismatch and then diffraction of the MoCA signal.

Adding the POE filter between the antenna and the Fracarro DE4-12 also improved my signal, although not strictly necessary, having a dedicated and private TV aerial. The model I bought is quite common in the US and took a long time to be delivered: PPC 2520A GLP-1G70CWWS, which should cut the MoCA signal off by 70 dB, quite good for this. For this component, if you have (like myself) a power supply injecting DC voltage to the amplifier in the aerial, the position of this filter is quite important since its internal components do not allow DC voltage to pass. I have placed it cascaded to the OUT of the power supply.

TV signal first... I have three TVs in my home and they must keep working regardless of all the changes.

Last but not least: if you want to reach the max speed, everything should be compliant with that speed. For this reason, I had to replace the GE switch serving the NAS with a pretty good BrosTrend 2.5 Gbps switch with 8 ports. This seems obvious, but sometimes it could be a pitfall.
