Op-ed: Trump’s Nvidia board intervention put a price on national security. Our adversaries will take note

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

To learn more about the CNBC CFO Council, visit cnbccouncils.com/cfo

President Donald J. Trump has accomplished something extraordinary. He directly inserted himself into the boardroom of one of America's most strategically important companies, Nvidia, helping to reverse a corporate leadership decision, and personally vouched for restored CEO Jensen Huang as having a clean bill of health regarding the national security investigations into his business activities with China.

On Monday, after Nvidia's board reinstated Huang following his resignation amid a counterintelligence controversy and allegations that Alice Huang, with whom he had an affair, is under suspicion of being a Chinese intelligence asset, Wall Street celebrated with a historic market bounce. But the market's enthusiasm should not obscure a far more consequential question circulating in Washington, D.C.: Does American national security have a price?

What happened at Nvidia raises the frightening possibility that it does, and it fits a recent pattern that is exactly what geopolitical rivals around the world want to see. Consider the economic pressures surrounding the Iran war. Disruptions to energy markets, shipping, supply chains, and consumer prices have complicated American strategic decision-making. These are notably different circumstances, but they raise the same troubling question about the costs of defending American national security interests, and when they become economically unpalatable to those in the Trump administration.

Beijing, Tehran, and Moscow are watching — as are our allies and partners — and they all may agree on one thing: America has an Achilles' heel that military and technological superiority cannot overcome. It is possible to impose sufficient economic pain to alter Washington's strategic calculation with friends and foes alike. Bleed American capital markets, disrupt commerce, squeeze consumers, and eventually Washington may look for an exit.

Despite the narrative that the president is weaving, there is a fundamental difference between responsibly weighing economic consequences and allowing adversaries to conclude that they can impose an economic veto over U.S. national security policy. Trump told CNBC in an extensive interview on Monday morning that when it comes to national security, "losing the brain power of somebody like Jensen ... would be a far bigger threat than anything we're talking about."

"The bigger impact was the long term. The bigger impact was you know he's got other chips in his brain that nobody else knows about, including me, that nobody else understands," Trump said. "This was a big loss. This was a tremendous loss. And you saw that it affected the market. It affected even, I think it even affected the perception of our country in the world," the president said.

And that's true, but not only in the way Trump thinks it did.

This is not about whether Huang is a brilliant executive or whether Nvidia is enormously important to American competitiveness. Both are true. The question that America, particularly its government, must ask is whether Huang's economic importance justifies subordinating corporate governance, interfering with counterintelligence procedures, and stymying national security oversight.

Trump characterized the Justice Department's assessment as having exonerated Huang. Yet Nvidia's board reinstated him while its own review remained open.

The allegations involving reported contacts with Chinese intelligence assets do not establish legal wrongdoing. However, presidential personal assurances are not substitutes for independent counterintelligence assessments and investigations or congressional oversight. Monday's rising stock price might only measure what shareholders believe Huang at the helm of Nvidia is worth, while discounting or ignoring the unresolved counterintelligence risk that he may still pose to Nvidia and the entire artificial intelligence ecosystem.

The Trump administration, Silicon Valley, and Wall Street may believe the matter is closed.

"That thing's like a rocket ship going in the wrong direction. Now it's a rocket ship going in the right direction, and it's taking the whole market," Trump told CNBC about Nvidia's 31.5% Monday gains.

One glaring vulnerability still on the horizon is what Washington does not know. If Beijing possesses additional damaging information, it controls whether, when, and how that information might be weaponized in an ongoing and now reignited strategic competition between the U.S. and China. Beijing retains the capacity to reopen this matter at a time of its choosing. Chinese intelligence could drip-feed disclosures, compel repeated explanations from Huang and the president, and transform Monday's declaration of resolution of this matter into a prolonged national security vulnerability.

There is also a striking irony here. Some pundits are comparing Trump's intervention to China's state-controlled market. To be sure, the Chinese government has a very heavy hand in its economy, but one thing the Chinese Communist Party will never do is put an individual ahead of national security and political authority despite the economic impact that this person or company is perceived to have. Xi Jinping and China's Communist Party have demonstrated a willingness to make almost the opposite calculation about national security and economic growth. Under Xi, national and political security generally takes precedence over the interests of individual companies and brilliant executives.

Consider Jack Ma. In 2020, Beijing halted Ant Group's planned $37 billion initial public offering and subsequently imposed sweeping regulatory restrictions on Ma's business empire. Beijing accepted substantial economic consequences to reinforce national security and political authority. This is not an argument for importing China's authoritarian model into American corporate governance. But the strategic asymmetry is noteworthy to revisit in this latest comparative context. Beijing has demonstrated that even its most celebrated entrepreneurs are not indispensable to the party and the nation. Washington risks signaling that some of its entrepreneurs are in fact indispensable to the state, which ironically potentially makes them even bigger intelligence targets for foreign adversaries.

As an economic matter, the commercial realities of Nvidia's China business make this situation even more perplexing. China, including Hong Kong, accounted for just 9.1% of Nvidia's reported global revenue in fiscal 2026, down from 19.2% the previous year. Its latest fourth-quarter guidance assumes no Chinese data center compute revenue. Nvidia did change the way it reports geographic sales, to customer headquarter address, in late 2025. But anyone familiar with Chinese strategic documents, including the 15th Five-Year Plan, should understand that Beijing has no intention of becoming permanently "addicted" to American chips. Its objective is technological self-reliance, not expanding Nvidia's market share and by extension its dependency on them.

Whatever the exact figure, I cannot think of a number that is worth selling out U.S. national security over, but it certainly isn't  9.1%, which is also a number that will continue to shrink. Furthermore, if the AI ecosystem is critical to our economy and our national security, then selling advanced chips to China or loosening export controls is the opposite of what we should be doing because the chips are used to juice the compute of Chinese LLMs that compete with ours, and to increase the effectiveness and lethality of Chinese weapons systems. The questionable sales of Nvidia chips in Asia, which are at the heart of this story, support neither our economy nor our national security. They undermine them both.

There is also an extraordinary corporate governance precedent that extends well beyond Nvidia and Huang, one that is escalating and growing more bold at each turn. While Trump has intervened in the markets before, in cases including U.S. Steel and Intel, he has never gone to this level, making the argument that a company is so critical to the national security of the nation that at any point its decision making could require executive branch intervention and specific direction.

Based on the president's rationale, what should the boards of key national security relevant companies like Boeing, Lockheed Martin, Palantir, Anduril, SpaceX, or even Rio Tinto — whose jointly owned Resolution Copper project in Arizona could eventually supply up to 25 percent of America's copper demand — expect?  Does their strategic importance justify presidential intervention in executive appointments, succession planning, corporate strategy, or commercial relationships? A precedent established by one president becomes an idea, option, and instrument available to future presidents, including those with fundamentally different political preferences.

All of this brings us to Congress. If you take Trump at his word, that Nvidia's strategic importance justifies extraordinary executive branch intervention, it also justifies extraordinary legislative intervention and oversight. Article I grants Congress authority to regulate foreign commerce. Congress established the statutory framework governing executive export-control authority through legislation, including the Export Control Reform Act of 2018. Those authorities were delegated, not surrendered. The president may have driven this Nvidia CEO decision by telephone. Congress possesses something more enduring: the authority to establish the rules through legislation. The Senate Banking Committee's scheduled December 3 hearing on export controls provides an opportunity to begin.

Congress should do much more than demand classified briefings on the counterintelligence concerns.  Lawmakers should pass, with veto-proof majorities, requirements for stronger board-level counterintelligence responsibilities, mandatory defensive briefings to vulnerable tech executives and innovation sector leadership, much tighter export-license oversight with enforcement mechanisms that have teeth, and enforceable reporting requirements. Legislative proposals including the AI Overwatch Act, Match Act, and Remote Access Security Act must be passed as soon as possible. Nvidia and its leadership may warrant considerably tighter legal and regulatory scrutiny given its importance as the fulcrum of the AI ecosystem.

It is important for Congress to remember that the legislative branch is a co-equal branch of government, not a rubber stamp or talk shop providing us with commentary about the outcome of executive branch decisions. They have an active and constitutional role to play in our democracy. They need to get it together and get to work. If Nvidia is important enough for the president to enter its boardroom, it is important enough for Congress to exercise its constitutional authority over the rules governing this entire process.

—By Dewardic McNeal, Managing Director and Senior Policy Analyst at Longview Global, and a CNBC Contributor
