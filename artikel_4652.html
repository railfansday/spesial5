Why Are Addiction and Suicide Attempts Treated Separately?

This week I have a lot to be thankful for. Three years ago, I spent Thanksgiving in an alcohol and drug treatment center, both eager to leave and afraid to be discharged the next day. I was one month clean from a devastating, years-long addiction, and I was starting from scratch after leaving my career to save a family I wasn’t sure could forgive me. The urge to suicide, though acute when I entered treatment, had receded to arm's length.

Until last week, I hadn’t reflected on this desperation for some time. Then I got a heart-wrenching call from a friend in recovery. One of the men in our peer group had taken his own life. Like me, he’d endured many relapses, but the personal consequences of his latest setback reached beyond his ability to cope.

I had three reactions—two highly subjective and one objective. As a human, I was deeply saddened. A fellow traveler, in the prime of his life, was taken from his family, unable to disclose his suicidal urges to anyone who might have helped.

At the same time, I understood. As someone who’s attempted suicide twice during relapses in years past, I’d done the same thing as our friend, but I survived. I therefore grasped, at a level not everyone can, why he couldn’t do things differently.

Finally, as a psychologist who’s treated addiction and studied substance use disorders and suicide, I was reminded of our field’s slow progress in preventing and intervening with some of humanity’s most pressing personal ailments.

Twenty-five years ago, when I began teaching college courses on addiction, it was already apparent that people with alcohol and substance use disorders are at much higher risk of suicide than the general population. Studies had placed this risk at 10-fold or higher, and little has changed since. Then, like now, alcohol and drugs were implicated in nearly half of suicide deaths.

Given these statistics, it’s perplexing that research on and treatment of addiction and suicide are mostly separate in the U.S., something the Department of Health and Human Services lamented earlier this year. Journal articles on addiction usually don’t mention suicidal ideation or behavior, and journal articles on suicide seldom mention addiction. Why?

Professional training programs in addiction are highly specialized and often independent of more general training programs for mental illness. Moreover, most treatment facilities for addiction are private and operated outside the hospital-based systems of care where we treat other clinical syndromes, such as bipolar disorder, depression, and suicidality.

I’ve worked at and been a patient in both inpatient psychiatric hospitals and treatment centers. Patients who are fighting addiction often find themselves in psychiatric wards, not treatment centers, following suicide attempts. Understandably, staff prioritize suicide risk over addiction, often unaware that the former would never have happened without the latter.

When addiction’s role in suicidal behavior is obscured, referrals to addiction treatment are unlikely. This is especially so when patients under-report their alcohol and drug use, which is common in medical settings because patients fear stigma. Ironically, stigma runs deep in medical and mental health settings, something Nora Volkow, Director of the National Institute on Drug Abuse, has voiced significant concern about.

Treatment centers often show the opposite pattern as psychiatric hospitals, with stigma toward suicide suppressing its candid discussion, even though the numbers show us that 40 percent of clients have experienced suicidal thinking or attempts.

One might expect more openness to talking about suicide in peer support groups for addiction, but local group rules, customs, and personalities often discourage such disclosures. In my experience attending thousands of peer support meetings for addiction over the years, I recall less than a handful of suicide-related disclosures.

More recently, I’ve begun attending, as an expert observer, peer support groups for self-harm, suicidal behavior, and related mental health challenges. Although it’s early, I've heard no disclosures of substance use or addiction.

In the U.S., addiction and suicide are the two most stigmatized mental health conditions. My hunch is that peer support groups carry society’s stigma. Joining a peer support group for addiction—a difficult decision for most people—grants participants “permission” to talk about a specific mental health issue, which becomes destigmatized among members. Stigma toward other mental health issues, including suicide, remains unaffected.

The friend who called me last week recruited a suicide expert to address our peer support group. I hope it makes a difference by encouraging members to self-disclose. I, along with a colleague, have also initiated a peer support program that tackles both addiction and suicidality explicitly. I’ll report back another time to let you know how that goes.

Three years removed from my own mental health crisis, I have a lot to be grateful for this Thanksgiving. I hope you do as well.
