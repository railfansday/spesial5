The Body Shame Daughters Inherit From the Women They Love

A young girl watches as her mother stands in front of the mirror, sucking in her stomach. Later, at a family birthday celebration, the girl's grandmother declines a slice of cake, explaining that she has already eaten too much. The girl takes in everything she has seen and heard from two women she loves. Although neither has said anything about the girl's body, the message has been received loud and clear: She has learned that eating requires justification, that weight must be monitored, and that a woman's appearance is something she should continually evaluate.

These seemingly ordinary interactions can shape how girls experience their own bodies. Over time, these messages become so familiar they feel like personal convictions rather than beliefs learned from others.

Children learn about themselves by observing the people whose affection and approval matter most to them. When a mother expresses dissatisfaction with her appearance, her daughter observes how someone she admires evaluates herself.

A girl who repeatedly hears her mother criticize her weight may begin to believe that being thin is essential to feeling attractive or acceptable. Even when a mother tells her daughter she is beautiful, her own behavior can send a different message.

Research confirms the influence of these indirect messages. In a study of 50 mothers and their daughters aged 8 to 12 (Handford et al., 2018), girls whose mothers made self-critical comments about their own appearance and dieting reported lower body satisfaction and more problematic eating attitudes than girls whose mothers made no such comments.

The attitudes women pass to their daughters rarely begin with them but instead reflect the cultural messages they absorbed growing up. Despite a major push in recent years to change those messages, they have only changed form.

For centuries, the consistent message has been that a woman's appearance determines her worth. Restrictive dieting has been praised as discipline and self-control. As each new generation grows up, girls learn to connect appearance with how women are valued.

These messages become embedded in family culture. Comments about gaining weight are treated as expressions of concern. Compliments focus on thinness. Eating certain foods produces guilt, while resisting them earns praise.

A study of 242 grandmother-mother-daughter groups examined how disordered eating moves across generations (Arroyo et al., 2017). Maternal comments and modeling played important roles in these patterns. Disordered eating did not pass directly through all three generations, but indirect relationships emerged through how women communicated about food and weight.

A more recent study of 634 mothers (Ferrer et al., 2025) found that women who recalled weight-related comments in childhood were more likely to make similar comments to their own children. Those who remembered their parents dieting and commenting on their own weight were also more likely to repeat those behaviors.

These findings help explain why harmful messages sometimes persist within loving families. Messages about appearance become familiar through repetition, and women may pass them along without recognizing their influence. Family history influences later behavior, but it does not determine it. The patterns remain open to change.

Body shame reaches beyond appearance, influencing a woman's sense of worth and how she expects others to perceive her.

A girl who grows up watching the women around her struggle with their bodies may associate femininity with self-criticism. As she gets older, gaining weight feels like a personal failure. Her confidence comes to depend on how closely her appearance matches an ideal she never consciously chose.

These beliefs are particularly difficult to question when they develop within close family relationships. Children rely on caregivers to help them make sense of themselves and the world, which gives family messages an influence that can last well into adulthood. Eventually, a woman may hear her mother's criticism in her own thoughts. What began as an observation has then become part of her internal dialogue.

Many mothers and grandmothers are responding to expectations they have little power to change. Some genuinely believe that encouraging weight control will protect their daughters from social rejection. Others struggle with their own feelings of inadequacy and have never experienced a different way of relating to their bodies.

In my published clinical work on the intergenerational transmission of trauma (Franco, 2023), I have examined how experiences within one generation influence the emotional lives of those that follow. Body shame has many possible origins, and its presence alone does not establish a history of trauma. Still, family history can show how beliefs become embedded in relationships and persist across generations.

This perspective also makes room for compassion. Recognizing the harm of inherited messages does not require condemning the women who passed them along. Their intentions, circumstances, and limitations are all part of the story.

For many women, body shame feels inseparable from identity because it has been present for as long as they can remember. Viewing these beliefs in the context of family relationships and personal history can begin to loosen their hold.

A woman may recognize that the critical voice she experiences when looking in the mirror echoes attitudes she observed throughout childhood. Exploring those connections helps her distinguish what she genuinely believes from what she learned to accept as true.

This awareness creates opportunities for change. Women can begin questioning the assumption that their worth depends on appearance and develop a relationship with food that is less governed by guilt. Mothers who recognize these patterns in themselves can also become more intentional about the messages their daughters receive. This process takes time, particularly when body shame is connected to deeper experiences of rejection or emotional distress.

The women of previous generations often carried expectations they never had the opportunity to challenge. Recognizing those expectations offers their daughters and granddaughters the possibility of carrying something different forward.
