10 executives who left their companies and later returned as CEO

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Free daily briefing on global business news.

These 10 boomerang CEOs, from Steve Jobs at Apple to Pat Gelsinger at Intel, left the companies they once led or worked for and later returned to run them

When a board picks a new chief executive, it sometimes chooses someone who has already left the company once. That person knows the business, its products and many of its people. They also bring a record from their first stint, including whatever ended it.

Executives who take that route are often called boomerang CEOs. The term covers anyone who returns to run a company they founded, led or worked for earlier. The paths back vary, and each one reflects a different kind of succession decision.

Some returns involve founders who handed the CEO title to a successor and later took it back. Michael Dell $DELL gave up the role in 2004 but stayed on as chairman, then reclaimed it in January 2007. Larry Page passed Google $GOOGL's top job to Eric Schmidt in 2001 and resumed it in April 2011. Jack Dorsey, Charles Schwab $SCHW and Mark Pincus also returned to companies they had founded or co-founded after other executives ran them.

Other comebacks start with a board reversing its own succession plan. Disney $DIS's board brought Bob Iger back as CEO on November 20, 2022, replacing Bob Chapek, the executive who had succeeded him. Some executives return more than once. Howard Schultz took back the Starbucks $SBUX CEO job in January 2008, then came back again as interim CEO in April 2022.

A few routes run outside the company altogether. Steve Jobs left Apple $AAPL in 1985 and came back after Apple bought NeXT, the company he ran after leaving. He became interim CEO in September 1997. Pat Gelsinger joined Intel $INTC in 1979, left in 2009 for EMC and became CEO of VMware in 2012. He returned as Intel's CEO in February 2021. Michael Bloomberg stepped away from the company he founded to serve as mayor of New York City, then returned to run it in January 2014.

The 10 executives here span technology, consumer brands, entertainment, media and financial services. They are a selection, not a ranking or a complete list of CEO comebacks. Each slide sets out when the executive left, who held the job in between and how the return came about.

Steve Jobs became Apple $AAPL's interim CEO in September 1997, after Apple bought NeXT, the company he ran after his first exit. His route back to the top job ran through an acquisition rather than a direct appointment.

Jobs co-founded Apple in 1976. He left in 1985 after a power struggle with John Sculley, who was then Apple's CEO.

Jobs held the chairman's title at the time, not the chief executive's. The 1985 dispute ended with him giving up that seat and leaving the company altogether.

During his years away, Jobs ran NeXT. Apple bought that business in a deal that spanned 1996 and 1997. The purchase put Jobs back inside the company he had co-founded before any change at the top.

The CEO job opened when Gil Amelio resigned. Jobs stepped into the role on an interim basis in September 1997, 12 years after his 1985 exit.

That sequence set his return apart from a board hiring a former leader from outside. By the time the post came open, Jobs was already back at Apple through the NeXT deal.

He later dropped the interim label and led Apple as its permanent chief executive.

Apple released the iMac, the iPod and the iPhone during his second tenure. All three arrived after a return that began with the acquisition of NeXT.

Howard Schultz returned as CEO of Starbucks $SBUX in January 2008, then came back again as interim CEO in April 2022. He retook the top job at the coffee chain on two separate occasions.

Schultz held the chief executive title at Starbucks until 2000. His first return to the post came more than seven years after that exit.

In January 2008, he replaced Jim Donald as CEO. Starbucks was struggling at the time. The move put the company back under a leader who had run it before.

That tenure lasted until 2017, about nine years in all. When Schultz stepped down that year, Kevin Johnson took over as CEO.

The two returns opened up in different ways. In 2008, Schultz took the seat from a sitting chief executive. In 2022, the vacancy followed Johnson's retirement. One comeback displaced a successor, while the other filled a seat a successor had left.

The titles differed as well. His 2008 return carried the full CEO title. In April 2022, he stepped in as interim CEO, a label that set the arrangement up as temporary. It meant a permanent successor still had to be named.

That interim stint ran until March 2023, a span of less than a year. Laxman Narasimhan then succeeded him as Starbucks CEO. This exit came much sooner after his return than the 2017 one had.

In total, Schultz held the Starbucks CEO role in three distinct periods. The first ended in 2000. The second ran from 2008 to 2017, and the third covered 2022 to 2023. Jim Donald, Kevin Johnson and Laxman Narasimhan each held the job directly before or after one of those stints.

Michael Dell $DELL returned as CEO of Dell, the computer company he founded, in January 2007. He took the job back after Kevin Rollins $ROL resigned.

Michael Dell led the company as its founder and chief executive until 2004. That year, Rollins became CEO.

The founder did not leave the business when he gave up the top executive title. He stayed on as chairman.

The 2004 change split the two most senior posts between two people. Rollins held the chief executive title. Michael Dell kept the chairman's seat.

That structure shaped how his return worked. He did not come back from another employer or after time away from the company. He was still chairman when Rollins resigned in January 2007.

His route back was a move within the company's own leadership. The founder who had handed over the chief executive's title in 2004 resumed it while still serving as chairman.

Twitter $TWTR co-founder Jack Dorsey returned as the company's interim CEO in July 2015 and became its permanent CEO that October. The appointment put him back in a job he had held once before, as Twitter's first chief executive.

Dorsey's first run in the role ended in October 2008. He was pushed out, and Evan Williams became CEO in his place.

Nearly seven years passed before Dorsey came back. The opening came when Dick Costolo left the CEO post in July 2015. Dorsey stepped in that month, but under an interim title rather than a permanent one.

His return filled a vacancy. Costolo had already left. Dorsey did not displace a sitting chief executive to get the job back.

The interim label marked the appointment as temporary. That changed in October 2015, when Twitter made Dorsey its permanent CEO. About three months separated the interim appointment from the permanent one.

Dorsey did not give up his other job to take the role. He was running Square $SQ at the same time. He led the two companies in parallel during his second tenure at Twitter.

That arrangement set his return apart from a move between employers. He kept Square and added Twitter to his responsibilities, rather than resigning from one company to join another.

His second tenure lasted until November 2021, when he stepped down. Parag Agrawal succeeded him as CEO. Measured from the permanent appointment, the second stint ran a little over six years.

That put the length of his second run close to the length of his time away. Each exit also ended with a different executive taking over: Williams in 2008 and Agrawal in 2021.

Dorsey's path at Twitter ran through four stages. He was the first CEO, then a co-founder pushed out of the job, then an interim chief and finally the permanent chief executive again.

Bob Iger returned as CEO of the Walt Disney $DIS Company on November 20, 2022. Disney's board brought him back to replace Bob Chapek, the executive who had succeeded Iger in the top job.

Iger first held the Disney CEO post from 2005 to 2020. Chapek took over as chief executive when Iger gave up that role.

Iger stayed at the company after handing over the CEO title. He became executive chairman and held that position through 2021.

The succession unfolded in two stages. The CEO title passed to Chapek first. Iger's executive chairman role ended later.

The board's November 2022 decision reversed that plan. It replaced the executive it had chosen to follow Iger. It then gave the job back to the leader Chapek had succeeded.

Iger did not found Disney. His return rested on his earlier tenure in the same office.

The gap between his roles was short. His executive chairman term ran through 2021. The board called him back to the CEO post the following year.

Disney's CEO succession began and ended with the same executive. Chapek's tenure sits between Iger's two terms as chief executive.

Google $GOOGL co-founder Larry Page became the company's CEO again in April 2011, taking over from Eric Schmidt. Google announced the change in January 2011, roughly three months before it took effect.

Page had run Google before. He was its first CEO, holding the role from 1998 until 2001. About a decade separated the end of his first stint from the start of his second.

Schmidt became Google's chief executive in 2001. He was not one of the company's founders. His appointment put an executive from outside the founding team in charge of the company Page had co-founded. Schmidt held the CEO title for the entire stretch between Page's two tenures.

The 2011 change kept Schmidt at Google. He moved into the role of executive chairman. Page took the CEO title back.

That path differs from cases where a board removes a sitting chief executive. Google announced the handoff in advance. The outgoing CEO stayed on in a senior role. The top job also passed between the same two people in both directions. It went from Page to Schmidt in 2001, then from Schmidt back to Page in 2011.

The early announcement meant Google's next CEO was public knowledge while Schmidt still held the title. For that stretch, the company operated with a named successor already in place. That successor was also the executive who had held the job first.

Page's second tenure included a change to the corporate structure. In 2015, he led the creation of Alphabet, which became Google's parent company. Page then served as Alphabet's CEO.

Alphabet did not exist during Page's first stint as CEO. The 2015 restructuring gave him the top job at a holding company that sat above Google itself. By 2015, Page held a broader title than the Google role he had handed to Schmidt in 2001.

Charles Schwab $SCHW returned as CEO of Charles Schwab Corporation in 2004, after the company's board removed David Pottruck from the role. Schwab founded the brokerage, which carries his name.

Pottruck held the CEO job when the board acted. His removal left the top post open, and the board filled it with the firm's founder.

That sequence sets this return apart. It came through a board decision to remove a sitting chief executive. It was not a planned handoff or a successor's voluntary exit.

The move also meant the board did not run an outside search for Pottruck's replacement. It turned instead to the person who had built the company.

Schwab's second stint as CEO lasted until 2008. That year, Walt Bettinger took over the role, and the founder handed the top job to a new chief executive for a second time.

Pat Gelsinger returned to Intel $INTC as chief executive in February 2021, replacing Bob Swan. He had not founded Intel. He was a career employee who came back to take a job he had never held.

Gelsinger joined Intel in 1979. He stayed for 30 years and rose through the company to become its chief technology officer.

That title put him among Intel's senior technical leaders. It did not make him chief executive. Other executives held the top seat throughout his first run at the company.

His first stint ended in 2009, when he left Intel to join EMC. In 2012, he became CEO of VMware.

The VMware job gave him something his Intel career had not. It was his first experience running a company as its chief executive. He gained it outside Intel, at a different business.

His route back did not depend on a founder's title or a chairman's seat. He held neither at Intel. The company instead turned to a former employee who had spent the intervening years at other firms.

Intel brought him back 12 years after he left. He returned with two kinds of experience. One was his long technical career inside Intel. The other was his time leading VMware.

Michael Bloomberg came back to run Bloomberg LP in January 2014, after his time as mayor of New York City ended. He had left the company 12 years earlier, in January 2002, to take that office.

Bloomberg founded the business in 1981. It did not start out under his name. Its original name was Innovative Market Systems.

His departure came 21 years after that founding. The cause was specific. He stepped away from the company to become mayor of New York City.

That sets his exit apart from the usual routes out of a corporate top job. He was not leaving after a board decision about the company's leadership. He was leaving for elected office.

He served more than one term as mayor. His return to the company followed once those terms were over.

The result is a timeline set by public office rather than by a corporate succession process. The date he left and the date he came back both tracked his time at City Hall. Other returning CEOs came back after a successor resigned or a board reversed course. Bloomberg's gap in leadership was the length of his mayoralty.

Mark Pincus, the founder of social-games company Zynga, returned as its CEO in April 2015. He had stepped aside from the role in 2013, when Don Mattrick became chief executive.

Pincus started Zynga in 2007. He was CEO until the 2013 handover to Mattrick.

That handover put an outside hire in charge. Mattrick came to Zynga from outside the company rather than rising through its ranks. Pincus no longer held the CEO title.

In April 2015, Pincus took the title back. The founder again led the business he had started.

His return followed a specific sequence. He stepped aside for an executive hired from outside, then resumed the role himself. Zynga's leadership went from founder to outside CEO and back to founder.
