25% to 10%: how 18 tech companies cut their 2026 workforces

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Groupon, Canva, Oracle, Meta, and 14 more: a selection of 2026 tech layoffs sorted by the share of staff each company cut.

Picture opening an early-morning email and reading that today is your last working day. Tech workers across the industry have absorbed news like that throughout 2026 as companies reorganize to keep up with the swelling capabilities of artificial intelligence. Chief executives write memos about flatter structures, fewer management layers, and freeing cash for AI investment. Each of those decisions lands on people who depend on a paycheck to cover their bills. Nothing in the record suggests the wave has crested, and more reductions look likely before the year ends.

TrueUp finds that this year's job cuts appear to be arriving faster than last year's. Its count tops 200,000 tech layoffs so far in 2026, within reach of the more than 245,000 it tallied across all of 2025. Many reports place the U.S. at the center of the losses. Workers have started to push back. More than 4,500 Google $GOOGL employees signed a petition demanding buyouts before any mandatory layoffs, along with guaranteed severance. State government has taken notice too. In June, California Gov. Gavin Newsom unveiled a tool to track how AI affects the workforce and said the state was preparing for AI's advance through governance and fresh policy. Layoffs on this scale have turned private staffing decisions into a public concern.

Yahoo Tech keeps a running tracker of 2026 tech layoffs that moves month by month from March through November, with its latest update on Nov. 4. Its entries draw on news reports, company statements, and filings, and they cover far more companies than this list includes. The tracker itself does not sort companies by the share of staff they cut. This list pulls 18 entries that state a company-wide percentage and orders them from highest to lowest, giving the edge to the larger stated headcount when two companies post the same figure. Several of those percentages carry hedges such as "about" or "roughly," and some describe plans that will unfold over months or years. A share of workforce measures how far a cut reaches into a company, something a raw job count can obscure. A small firm and a global giant can report the same percentage while shedding headcounts that differ by thousands.

Groupon announced plans in May to eliminate roughly 25% of its workforce, about 400 jobs, the deepest proportional cut in this selection of 2026 layoffs. The company expects most of those positions to disappear by the end of the third quarter, based on a Securities and Exchange Commission filing, according to the Tribune. The schedule spreads the exits over several months instead of a single day of departures. Anyone staying through that window would work beside colleagues whose positions were about to vanish.

Financial filings put Groupon's worldwide headcount at 1,734 at the end of 2025, according to the Tribune. Measured against that base, the planned exits come to about 23% of staff, or nearly one in four employees. Many employers on the tracker have disclosed larger headcount losses, yet few have proposed removing so large a slice of their own payroll. The company has been shrinking for years. Earlier rounds of downsizing took it from more than 11,000 employees to fewer than 2,000 over the past decade, the Tribune reports. Cutting this deep into an already slimmed-down company reshapes the whole organization, unlike trims confined to one division or a thin layer of management.

Groupon tied the restructuring to one goal, rebuilding the business as an AI-native company. Its stated aim names AI as the destination, a direct connection that several other firms in the tracker sidestepped. Money figures in the plan as well. The Tribune reports that Groupon projects annualized savings of about $25 million and intends to put as much as half of this year's projected savings into AI infrastructure. The company also argued that the margins of scaling local commerce cannot support a "human-bandwidth solution." Read together, those statements make the layoffs both a cost cut and a funding source for the AI buildout Groupon now treats as its growth engine. The word "rebuild" signals an intent to remake Groupon from its foundations.

Canva is eliminating 22% of its staff, a reduction of just over 1,200 employees at the design software company. A cut of that size implies a starting headcount of roughly 5,500. The November announcement makes Canva one of the most recent names in a year crowded with tech job losses. A reduction this deep forces a company to decide which work continues and which stops, and Canva has signaled its answer through how it is reshaping those who remain.

Canva is folding its product, growth, and platform teams into one another as part of the same restructuring, according to reports. The merger redraws the company's internal map while Canva trims payroll. Three teams that once operated as separate units will run as a combined organization. Pairing that consolidation with the layoffs means employees confront new team boundaries and a thinner bench at the same moment.

Canva also aims to pare back "subspecialisation," the same coverage indicates. Fewer narrow specialties could hand those who stay a heavier load of responsibilities. Each worker left may cover ground that a bigger, more specialized roster once divided. The shift favors breadth over depth, so someone who once owned a single niche may end up juggling several.

No AI rationale surfaces in the descriptions of Canva's downsizing, which sets it apart from several 2026 entries that pin the exits on artificial intelligence or on rising AI spending. Canva's example shows that one of the steepest proportional downsizings in this selection came with no AI explanation attached. The changes described so far concern how Canva arranges the work itself. Combined divisions and broader roles point to a company that expects to run leaner, relying on generalists after this round. The accounts leave open which roles Canva will keep, but the direction tilts toward wider job descriptions spread across fewer hands.

ClickUp eliminated 22% of its workforce in May 2026 as the company turned toward AI to raise productivity. CEO Zeb Evans announced the layoffs himself, in a post on X $TWTR.

Evans denied that savings drove the decision. He said the layoff "wasn't about cutting costs," and he described the money it frees as flowing "directly back into the people who stay." His account treats the job losses as a reallocation, with payroll leaving one group of employees and landing with another. Read that way, ClickUp's total spending on people need not fall at all. The same dollars change hands.

Million-dollar salary bands give the remaining staff a concrete target. ClickUp introduced the bands for employees who "create outsized impact using AI," a standard that ties the company's highest pay to a single kind of contribution. Workers who stay now have a stated reason to measure their output against what AI lets them accomplish. The structure turns skill with AI tools from a useful asset into the clearest path toward the top of ClickUp's pay scale. It signals to job seekers what the company prizes before they apply.

Evans' reported remarks center on the people who remain and put no number on the departures. The 22% share stands as the sole gauge of how deep the reduction reached. More than one in five positions disappeared. Without a headcount, outsiders cannot tell how many people lost their jobs or how large a pool of money now flows toward the workers left behind. The gap leaves Evans' reinvestment pledge without a figure anyone can check against later results.

The disavowal of cost motives and the new compensation tier point to a company betting that a smaller staff, rewarded for what each person achieves with AI, can deliver more than the larger one it replaced.

PayPal $PYPL intends to eliminate around 20% of its staff, or nearly 4,800 employees, under a plan the Wall Street Journal reported in May. The reductions will roll out over the next few years. PayPal is the only company in this selection whose reported timeline stretches that far, a schedule that treats downsizing as a long project.

CEO Enrique Lores laid out the strategy for investors in two steps. The first strips duplication and extra layers from PayPal's organizational structure. The second speeds up the adoption of AI and automation across the company's operations.

Lores' ordering puts the cleanup ahead of the technology push. Several companies in this selection name AI as the main force behind their layoffs. PayPal's chief executive began with redundant roles and stacked hierarchy and turned to automation in his second step. His remarks cast AI as a tool to apply after the company sheds its excess management.

The multiyear horizon turns the 4,800 figure into a target. No milestones accompany it. Nothing in the reporting reveals how many positions disappear in any given year or which divisions absorb the first rounds.

Among the four companies in this selection trimming about one-fifth of their workforce, PayPal's number dwarfs the rest. Wix, Monday.com, and Patreon account for fewer than 1,800 roles combined, and PayPal's total runs nearly triple that sum. A cut of nearly 4,800 at around 20% implies a payroll of roughly 24,000 people, the largest base in that group.

Oracle $ORCL and Meta $META are the lone companies in this selection with larger job totals, and both are shedding a smaller slice of their staff. Oracle has carried out its cuts and Meta has begun its round, while PayPal's exits lie ahead. PayPal pairs a roughly 20% share with the third-largest headcount here, a match no other company in the selection can claim.

Wix, the website builder, is cutting around 20% of its staff, a reduction expected to touch roughly 1,000 roles. The 1,000 figure is an estimate based on the company's headcount. Working backward from those two numbers puts Wix's workforce near 5,000 people. The plan surfaced in May 2026, a month that also brought layoffs at Groupon, ClickUp, Intuit $INTU, Meta $META, PayPal $PYPL, and Coinbase.

Avishai Abrahami, Wix's chief executive, laid out the goal in a post on X $TWTR and boiled it down to three adjectives: "we need to become a faster, leaner, and flatter organization." Each word aims at a different target. Speed governs the pace of work, leanness governs size, and flatness points to fewer management tiers between employees and leadership. The job losses themselves deliver leanness, and the push for flatness suggests management layers will thin as well.

Wix also blames part of the decision on a force outside its software business. The company, based in Israel, points to the exchange rate between the Israeli shekel and the U.S. dollar. No other company among the 18 in this list names currency as a reason to downsize. The other companies' explanations range across AI, team structure, profitability, and market conditions.

Webflow, another company that builds and hosts websites, let people go in May in a round of unclear size. A developer told the San Francisco Chronicle the round outstripped Webflow's last major one, a 2024 reduction that affected 8% of its staff. One worker called the cuts "a bloodbath" in comments to the same paper. The tracker points to a shift toward AI as the likely cause. Webflow CEO Linda Tong argued that AI is redrawing the rules for how marketing teams build, test, and refine digital experiences. She added that "the companies that move decisively through moments like this are the ones that come out ahead."

Two website builders moved to shed staff in the same month, a pattern that hints at strain across the category. Wix carries a burden Webflow's chief did not cite, a home currency working against it.

Monday.com is laying off 20% of its workforce, about 630 people, as it pushes to turn its AI platform into a core offering. The two figures put the company's headcount before the July cuts near 3,150, with roughly 2,520 employees staying on. The reduction extends a product bet Monday.com placed several months earlier.

Monday.com built its main product earlier in 2026 around the belief that a growing number of business customers want their employees working alongside AI agents. The July round carries that belief past the software and into the organization that builds it. A company that sells agent-assisted work to clients will operate with a staff one-fifth smaller once the layoffs take effect.

Company leaders sounded confident about AI competition roughly five months before the job cuts. CNBC reported in February that co-CEO and co-founder Eran Zinman said Monday.com had felt no effect from AI companies. Zinman added that the company had reoriented its marketing and advertising around AI. On an earnings call, management told analysts that Monday.com held a strong market position. Executives cited new AI features, among them agents and a vibe feature, built to raise conversion and engagement.

Monday.com's 20% matches the share at PayPal $PYPL, Wix, and Patreon, yet its pace diverges from PayPal's. PayPal plans to spread nearly 4,800 job cuts across the next few years, per the Wall Street Journal. Monday.com's July layoffs come with no comparable multiyear timeline. In raw numbers, PayPal's plan reaches more than seven times as many jobs as Monday.com's. Wix's estimated 1,000 roles and Patreon's 93 jobs fall on either side of Monday.com's total.

By midyear, the AI push had reached Monday.com's marketing, its flagship software, and its payroll. Whatever pressure AI companies did or did not apply, Monday.com has chosen to compete on their ground, staking both its software and its staffing on AI.

Patreon cut 20% of its workforce in July, eliminating 93 jobs as it reworked what the company called "our organizational structure and how we work." Ninety-three positions at that share imply a staff of roughly 465 before the cuts. No other company in this selection that disclosed both a share and a headcount comes out smaller. Patreon shares the 20% tier with three far larger employers on this list, yet its job count amounts to a sliver of theirs. A layoff of fewer than 100 people still removed one worker in five at a company of that size.

Patreon's email announcing the cuts rejected AI as the motive, stating that "we are not making the above changes because we believe AI replaces humans." The same message conceded ground with a second line: "AI has fundamentally transformed the tech industry, though, including how we work." Side by side, the two statements deny that AI can stand in for people while admitting it has changed how the company functions day to day.

Jack Conte laid out the practical shape of the overhaul. He described a flatter hierarchy, with teams refocused on the company's top priorities. Conte also wrote that AI has altered how Patreon builds products and communicates, and that this change bears on how the business operates and arranges itself.

The disclaimer and the concession clash on the surface alone. Patreon rejected the notion that software could take over the work its people did. It named AI as a force behind the way it works, and Conte's account links that force to the company's internal design. The leaner hierarchy he described leaves surviving teams with fewer managerial layers above them and a narrower set of goals in front of them.

Patreon's explanation keeps AI in the background and the reshaped hierarchy in the foreground. The record suggests the technology changed how Patreon works first, and the 93 exits followed from the slimmer structure that emerged.

Squarespace is cutting 19% of its workforce, about 300 people, in a November move that the website-building company frames as a reset of how it operates.

Jeff Seedman, a Squarespace representative, told TechCrunch the changes aim to simplify the company's operations, sharpen its priorities, and let it move faster. He presented the overhaul as a way to ready Squarespace for the next stage of its growth. His explanation emphasizes speed and focus. It specifies no teams, offices, or product lines, and it never refers to AI.

AI-driven "vibe-coding" site builders have shaken Squarespace's industry as they gain popularity, according to the tracker. Seedman offered no such link himself. The connection between that new competition and the job cuts reflects the tracker's view of the market, and no company statement confirms it.

Webflow, a rival that builds and hosts websites, ran its own layoff round in May without disclosing the size. A developer told the San Francisco Chronicle the round outstripped Webflow's last major reduction, which hit 8% of its staff in 2024. An employee called the cuts "a bloodbath" in comments to the paper. The tracker identifies a shift toward AI as the apparent cause.

Webflow CEO Linda Tong argued that AI is rewriting how marketing teams build, test, and refine digital experiences. She added that companies that seize such moments come out ahead. Her framing puts AI at the center, a contrast with Squarespace's statement, which leaves the technology unmentioned.

Squarespace's 19% cutback runs more than twice as deep as Webflow's 2024 cut. Six months separate the Webflow round from Squarespace's November announcement. Across that stretch, two companies selling tools for building websites both shrank, yet only Webflow's chief executive pointed to AI as a force reshaping the work. In this record, Squarespace's silence on AI leaves the tracker's market reading as the only link between its cuts and AI-powered rivals.

Lucid $LCID, the electric vehicle maker, is cutting about 18% of its workforce in a June round that extends to the assembly line. Full-time employees, contract workers, and hourly manufacturing staff all fall within its scope. Including contract workers carries the impact past Lucid's own employees. Lucid is the lone automaker among the companies on this list, so its reduction affects people who build cars as well as those who work at desks.

Lucid's production side absorbs a concrete share of the loss. IBTimes UK reports that the company eliminated the second production shift at its Casa Grande, Arizona, factory. Dropping a shift removes a whole rotation of plant labor, which matches the hourly roles the layoffs include. An operation that runs one shift in place of two has less room to raise output if demand climbs.

Chief Operating Officer Marc Winterhoff is leaving the company alongside the layoffs. His departure takes away the executive whose title covers operations at the moment the company pares its factory staff. Leadership turnover and a shrinking payroll converge on the same part of the business.

Lucid ties the layoffs to money and markets, saying they aim to "boost profitability amid growing competition." The explanation centers on earnings and rivals and leaves AI out. Groupon, Monday.com, Intuit $INTU, and other companies on this list linked their job losses to AI, which sets the carmaker's rationale apart. Lucid's case shows that a company can arrive at a cut this steep through competitive and financial pressure, with technology absent from its stated motives.

Lucid's round landed in a June that brought 14,000 job cuts across companies such as game developer Bungie and trading platform Robinhood. A vanished shift and a departing COO point to a carmaker surrendering capacity and labor costs to secure the gains its statement seeks.

Intuit $INTU plans to lay off 3,000 employees, about 17% of its workforce, Reuters reported in May. No later report confirms the cuts as complete, and the figure still describes an intention.

CEO Sasan Goodarzi told employees in a memo that the layoffs would help the company focus on infusing AI across its services. His explanation links the job losses to the products Intuit offers its customers, with AI woven through them. The reduction becomes a way to redirect the company toward that goal.

Read together, the headcount and the percentage imply a company of roughly 18,000 employees before the reduction. A cut at this depth would remove about one in every six jobs at Intuit.

Intuit's planned total outstrips the raw job counts at several companies that sit higher in this list. Groupon, Wix, Monday.com, and Squarespace each post a steeper share while shedding fewer people. Groupon and Monday.com also framed their layoffs around AI, yet their combined 1,030 jobs amount to about a third of Intuit's plan.

The tracker's entry names no teams, offices, or regions, and it gives no completion date. Employees in every Intuit division face the same uncertainty until the company specifies where the cuts will land. Goodarzi's memo points to the destination, AI across Intuit's services, but leaves open which jobs the company will trade to get there.

Snap $SNAP is eliminating 1,000 workers, or 16% of its staff, according to Variety, and the company presents the smaller workforce as a bet on compact teams equipped with AI. The April 2026 announcement removes roughly one in six positions at Snap.

Investor pressure shaped the timing. Snap announced the layoffs weeks after Irenic Capital Management called on the company to make changes that would lift its performance and revenue. The short gap between Irenic's request and Snap's decision places the job losses inside a larger argument over how the company should improve its results. An outside shareholder's demand and an internal technology strategy point in the same direction, toward fewer people producing more.

CEO Evan Spiegel grounded his case in what he had observed inside Snap. "We have already witnessed small squads leveraging AI tools to drive meaningful progress across several important initiatives," he said. His remark treats existing teams as proof that the approach works. In his telling, the gains came before the cuts, which recasts the downsizing as an expansion of a method he believes Snap has tested. The quote names none of those initiatives and offers no measure of the progress. Outsiders have only his word for how much the squads achieved.

Snap is using AI to streamline its operations and to support the employees who remain. The math reveals the load they carry. A 1,000-person reduction equal to 16% implies a pre-layoff headcount near 6,250, leaving roughly 5,250 employees to absorb the duties of departed colleagues.

Snap's leadership is wagering that a staff that lean, armed with AI tools, can deliver the turnaround Irenic Capital sought. The bet ties an investor's financial demand to an AI thesis Snap has never proven at its new size. Snap controls its payroll. It cannot dictate revenue, which hinges on whether Spiegel's squads keep up the pace he described.

Rackspace cut 750 workers in June, around 15% of its staff, as the cloud computing services company turned its attention toward AI. Read together, the headcount and the percentage imply a workforce of roughly 5,000 before the layoffs. About one in seven employees lost a job.

Rackspace's filing names a strategic decision to give less weight to some older service delivery operations as the chief driver of the restructuring. Most of those operations belong to its Public Cloud unit. The filing describes steering the freed resources into building out the company's enterprise AI offerings.

The company frames the layoffs as a decision about where to spend. Rackspace chose which work to downgrade and which to grow, and the restructuring centers on older public cloud delivery. The jobs it eliminated and the products it wants to build sit in different corners of the business. Staff whose roles supported legacy operations carry the cost of a bet on a newer line of work.

Roughly 4,250 employees remain to carry out the shift. Rackspace now relies on a smaller workforce to keep the operations it retained running while it builds an enterprise AI business that its own filing presents as unfinished.

The cryptocurrency platform Coinbase is laying off 700 employees in a May round that removes around 14% of its staff. A reduction of 700 at that proportion implies a pre-layoff headcount of roughly 5,000. About one worker in seven is leaving the company.

CEO Brian Armstrong pinned the decision on two forces, the current market and AI changing the way Coinbase works. His account pairs an outside pressure with an internal one. Market conditions sit beyond the reach of any single firm. A shift in how work gets done reflects a choice about the shape Armstrong wants his organization to take.

The dual explanation separates the crypto firm from employers in this selection that tied their layoffs to AI alone. Armstrong did not present AI as the sole driver. By naming external conditions alongside it, he linked Coinbase's payroll to forces the company cannot steer. His framing leaves room for two readings: some lost roles track how the market behaves, and others disappear because the work itself has changed. Armstrong's public statement does not say how many positions each force claims.

Robinhood, another trading platform, said through its communications account on X $TWTR in June that it planned to let go of 10% of its workers, around 290 jobs. Whether AI shaped that move remains unclear. CEO Vlad Tenev said the company would be "utilizing frontier technologies to push our execution even further." His language points toward technology without any reference to external pressure. Two companies in adjacent corners of finance reached for different vocabulary to explain shrinking payrolls.

Coinbase's cut runs 4 percentage points deeper than Robinhood's and reaches more than twice as many people. Armstrong also offered the fuller public rationale. Of the two, the company asking more of its workforce is the one that named more reasons for doing so.

Oracle $ORCL eliminated 21,000 jobs over the past year, roughly 13% of its workforce, according to the filing it submitted June 23. The tracker credits Oracle with the heaviest layoff impact of 2026 so far. The filing's one-year window reaches back into 2025, so the figure does not line up with the calendar year. Even so, the headcount sits in a different category from most companies here, several of which shed a few hundred people or fewer.

Artificial intelligence appears in the document as a cause. Oracle wrote that adopting and deploying AI across its operations has already led to reductions in its workforce and may keep doing so. The second half of that admission carries the warning. By putting that possibility on the record, the company signaled that 21,000 might not be the final tally.

Spending on AI infrastructure drove the downsizing, according to reports that describe the goal as stopping a cash drain from Oracle's buildout. The company has reportedly sought to offset the money flowing into AI chips and data centers. In that trade-off, payroll gave way to hardware.

The purge began in March, when early projections put the coming cuts in the thousands without settling on a total. On the last day of the month, more than a dozen employees announced on LinkedIn that they had lost their jobs, according to Business Insider. The notices those workers received pointed to Oracle's current business needs and told them, "we have made the decision to eliminate your role as part of a broader organizational change." The wording framed each departure as one piece of a larger reorganization.

Another round followed in September, and its size remains unknown. Employees reportedly received early-morning emails on Monday, Sept. 14, telling them the day was their last on the job. The September round arrived less than three months after the June filing raised the prospect of further reductions. Oracle's cuts now run past the 21,000 it disclosed, by a margin no one outside the company has counted.

Etsy laid off about 220 people, or 12% of its workforce, in August to simplify its organizational structure. Most of the losses fell on product and engineering teams. Staff in those groups design and build the company's technology, and the layoffs struck that technical core harder than any other part of Etsy.

CEO Kruti Patel Goyal called the layoffs necessary to build "the organization we believe Etsy needs for the future." Her rationale looks ahead and treats the move as a redesign of how Etsy arranges its people. The reported reasoning names no revenue shortfall, cost target, or investor demand. The overhaul itself serves as the stated motive. Several other tech employers tied their 2026 reductions to freeing money for AI, among them Meta $META and Atlassian $TEAM.

An Etsy spokesperson told CNBC that AI adoption did not drive the cuts. A handful of other companies have issued similar denials this year. Epic Games said its March dismissal of more than 1,000 people had no connection to AI. LinkedIn's plan to let go around 875 employees drew a comparable disclaimer from one source, who said the decision was "not for artificial intelligence to replace jobs at LinkedIn." Etsy's denial carries the company's own voice, which gives it more weight than a single source's account.

Dropbox's October layoff of roughly 200 workers, about 9% of its staff, targeted many of the same functions, with engineering, product, design, and general and administrative teams bearing the bulk. Etsy removed a similar number of people at a higher share, a sign that Etsy started from a smaller headcount. Dropbox co-CEO Ashraf Alkarmi tied his company's layoffs to choices about where to invest and how to organize, a rationale close to Goyal's focus on structure. Engineers and product staff carried the heaviest load at both companies. Etsy's case shows that a double-digit cut to those ranks can proceed while the company itself rejects AI as the cause.

Meta $META began cutting 10% of its workforce in May, around 8,000 employees, and plans to close 6,000 open roles on top of those exits. The company tied the reduction to one goal: freeing more room for AI spending. Eliminating vacancies trims tomorrow's payroll in the same way dismissals shrink today's. Combined, the two moves would remove roughly 14,000 positions from Meta's staffing plans, counting openings never filled alongside people already on the job.

A far smaller March round came first. Meta started letting go of about 700 people on March 25, The New York Times reported, and the losses landed on Reality Labs, social media, and recruiting teams. The May layoffs reached more than 11 times that headcount. Shedding recruiters in March and scrapping thousands of openings two months later point the same way, toward a company that intends to hire less.

Reality Labs, the unit that runs Horizon Worlds for the Quest VR headset, absorbed part of the March blow as Meta steps back from building the metaverse. Thinning that division signals a company redirecting its people away from virtual worlds. The same wave reached social media teams too, which carried the reductions beyond the metaverse effort.

A Reuters report in March raised the prospect of a much deeper reduction, suggesting Meta could cut 20% of its staff because of rising AI investment. The March round fell well short of that estimate. The May layoffs reached half of it. Even at the lower share, Meta's stated reason for the spring cuts, making space for AI outlays, matched the motive the news service had described in March. The Reuters report overshot on scale but identified the same cause.

Between March and May, Meta moved staff away from its metaverse projects and toward AI, and closed vacancies would make up more than 40% of the positions it is removing from its books.

Uber $UBER announced in September that it would eliminate 3,300 jobs, about 10% of its workforce, in a restructuring built around the distance between rank-and-file staff and the top of the company. CEO Dara Khosrowshahi cast the overhaul as an effort to strip out management tiers and steer resources toward growth. "We are removing layers, simplifying team structures, refining our global location strategy, and focusing our people and investments against the biggest opportunities ahead of us," he said.

Uber pinned numeric targets to the reorganization. The company said it would shrink the number of employees who sit seven or more levels below the CEO by 20%. It intends to cut its count of micro-teams by almost 50%. The two goals measure the plan by organizational depth and fragmentation, and the job losses serve those aims.

The cuts reach managers and regular employees alike. Each management tier adds a rung between the CEO and the people doing the work. Sparing supervisors would leave the ladder as tall as before. By including both groups, Uber matched its layoffs to its stated goal of a flatter chain of command.

Khosrowshahi's memo left AI off its list of reasons, which sets Uber apart from several companies in this selection that blamed rising AI spending for their cuts. The memo instead invoked "the autonomous future" as the horizon Uber is working toward.

Uber's July round, a narrower cut, carried a different message. Bloomberg reported that month that Uber laid off 10% of its customer service division as it sought to "embrace artificial intelligence" and streamline its staffing. In May, the company had said it would slow hiring as it leaned more on AI inside its own operations. The two rounds point to a split in how Uber explains its cuts. Bloomberg's account tied the support-staff trims to AI, while the September memo grounds the companywide reset in management depth.

Atlassian $TEAM let go of roughly 1,600 employees on March 16, around 10% of its workers, and CEO Mike Cannon-Brookes presented the move as a way to bankroll the company's next bets. He said the reductions would "self-fund further investment in AI and enterprise sales, while strengthening our financial profile."

Cannon-Brookes's wording ties each eliminated job to spending that comes after it. He named two destinations for the freed money, AI and enterprise sales, and attached no dollar figure to either. His reference to the company's financial profile adds a third aim. Atlassian wanted the savings to firm up its books as well as pay for new work.

Cannon-Brookes made no attempt to separate the layoffs from money. His statement names savings, new investment, and the balance sheet in a single line, a plain admission that cost discipline drove the decision alongside strategy. Other chief executives in this selection have distanced their job losses from budget pressure, but Atlassian's leader built his explanation on it.

A new internal blueprint accompanied the layoffs. Cannon-Brookes said Atlassian is "changing the way we work and reorganising around our System of Work to move faster." His remark pairs a structural overhaul with a push for speed. The headcount cut arrived bundled with a redesign of how the remaining teams operate. Fewer people have to deliver faster results inside a reworked system, which raises the stakes for every employee who stayed.

Atlassian moved early in the year. Its mid-March round landed about two months ahead of the May wave that brought cuts at seven other companies on this list, from Groupon and ClickUp to Meta $META and PayPal $PYPL. Atlassian also matched the share of staff cut at Meta and Uber $UBER while losing fewer people than either. Under the self-funding approach, Atlassian's AI budget grows out of its payroll savings. Its technology push and its headcount move in opposite directions.
