Why the next Fed decision should not impact your house purchase

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

This analysis explains why the Fed funds rate and 30-year mortgage rates do not move in lockstep. It quantifies payment and interest differences across small rate changes, and frames decisions around affordability, time in the home and refinance costs.   AI Summary

A quarter point can dominate the housing conversation. It should not automatically dominate a household’s decision to buy.

The Federal Reserve held its benchmark rate at 3.75% to 4% on October 28. But the vote was hardly an all-clear for lower borrowing costs: two officials preferred a quarter-point increase. With inflation still elevated, another increase remains a possibility.

That uncertainty leaves prospective buyers asking whether they should wait. My view is that buying a reasonably priced home you can comfortably afford can still be a sound way to build wealth. A modest difference in the mortgage rate deserves a calculation before it becomes a reason to put that decision on hold.

First, the Fed does not set the rate on a 30-year mortgage. Mortgage pricing reflects longer-term borrowing costs, inflation expectations and conditions in mortgage markets. A quarter-point Fed increase does not automatically produce a quarter-point increase in a homebuyer’s quote. And once a fixed-rate mortgage closes, a later Fed increase does not change its interest rate.

Moving from 7% to 7.5% adds about $102 a month, roughly 5.1% to the principal-and-interest payment. That is real money. A buyer whose budget cannot absorb it should reconsider the price or timing. But for a financially prepared household, it may be manageable enough that other parts of the purchase deserve more attention.

A lower purchase price, fewer repairs or better loan terms can also change the economics. Buyers should evaluate the whole transaction instead of treating the interest rate as the sole measure of a good deal.

Freddie Mac’s mortgage-rate series reached 18.63% in October 1981. By the first week of January 2021, the average had fallen to a record low of 2.65%. Those extremes show how much the financing environment can change. They do not make today’s housing inexpensive: home prices, incomes and other ownership costs matter, too.

The payment math also shows why small changes should be distinguished from major shifts. On the same $300,000, 30-year loan, moving from 3% to 7% raises principal and interest by about $731 a month. Moving from 7% to 7.5% adds about $102. Both increases matter, but they present very different budget decisions.

It is easy to multiply that extra monthly payment by 360 and stop there. The half-point difference in our example adds approximately $36,625 over 30 years if the borrower keeps the original loan and makes every scheduled payment.

That is a valid lifetime comparison. It may not describe the buyer’s actual experience.

The National Association of Realtors’ 2025 Profile of Home Buyers and Sellers reported that the typical seller had owned the home for 11 years. That is a median among recent sellers, not an average for every homeowner or a prediction for a new buyer. Separately, Atlanta Fed researchers have described the average life of a mortgage as around seven to 10 years. Owning a home and retaining its original mortgage are different timelines.

Suppose our borrower sells after five years. At 7.5% rather than 7%, the borrower would have made about $6,104 in additional payments and would owe about $1,458 more at payoff. The additional interest cost is therefore about $7,562. Shorter ownership reduces the period of exposure; it does not erase the cost or the difference in remaining debt.

Refinancing can shorten that original loan’s life, sometimes substantially. ICE’s December 2024 Mortgage Monitor reported that rate-and-term refinancers in September and October had held their previous mortgages for an average of just 15 months. That was an unusually short period during a particular refinancing window, not a normal schedule every buyer can expect.

The decision should turn on the savings and costs. If a refinance costs $5,000 and reduces the payment by $200 a month, the simple break-even period is 25 months. Borrowers should also compare the remaining balances and repayment terms; restarting a 30-year clock can reduce the payment while increasing total interest.

The purchase needs to work at the rate available today. A future refinance can improve the outcome, but it should not be the condition that makes the home affordable.

Homeownership’s appeal extends beyond a future rate reduction. On our $300,000 mortgage at 7%, scheduled payments reduce principal by approximately $17,605 over five years and $48,687 over 11 years, without assuming any appreciation. That principal repayment builds equity, although it comes from the owner’s own cash payments and is not investment profit.

Appreciation can add to that equity, while falling prices can reduce it. Maintenance, transaction costs and the opportunity cost of the down payment also affect the result. Renting can be sensible for someone who needs flexibility or faces a substantially cheaper rental alternative.

For buyers with stable income, adequate reserves and plans to stay long enough to spread out transaction costs, ownership still offers a practical path to accumulating an asset while meeting a basic need for housing.

The Fed’s next move deserves attention. But a household’s ability to afford the home, maintain it and hold it through changing markets deserves more. A quarter point or half point should be part of that judgment. It should not make the judgment for them.

Author calculations use standard monthly amortization with 360 payments, a $300,000 initial balance and no points, financed fees, prepayments or tax benefits. Comparisons hold principal and term constant. The five-year interest comparison includes the difference in loan balances at payoff. Figures are calculated before rounding; rounded displayed amounts may differ by a cent when subtracted.
