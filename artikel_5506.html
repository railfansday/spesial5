Your technology wish list is only half the story

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

Lender feature requests are often symptoms, not the real problem. Share the business goal behind requests and evaluate vendors by what they build next, including unprompted releases that reduce steps and measurable cost per loan.   AI Summary

Every mortgage technology company keeps a list of lender feature requests. Most treat it as a roadmap. That habit is one reason lenders keep buying technology, even as the cost to originate a loan remains stubbornly high.

I have sat on both sides of that list. Early in my career, I was the lender buying and running point-of-sale systems, loan origination systems and CRMs. Later, I built customer success teams for lenders like the one I used to be. A feature request is a lender’s best guess at a solution. The real problem lies beneath it, and a technology partner that only builds to the request leaves that problem in place.

Here is my advice to lenders, from someone who now runs a technology company: Don’t stop at the wish list. Send the why along with it. Tell your technology partners what you’re trying to accomplish, and judge them by what they do next.Not every list is the same. Some items are requirements, such as a compliance rule, an investor guideline or a business process the technology must support. Any provider worth hiring should be flexible enough to handle those. Others are feature requests, usually tweaks to something that already exists. Then there’s the wish list: new ideas about what the technology could do. Wish lists are among the most valuable inputs a technology partner can receive. They just aren’t the whole story without the reason behind them.

Say your team asks its technology partner for a new field on the application. One provider adds the field and checks the box. Another asks why and learns you’re launching a new loan product. That provider adds the questions, then builds in logic so borrowers are prompted to upload the right documents based on their answers. Same request, very different result. The difference is context.Not every lender wants that conversation every time, and that’s fair. If you’re hungry and order a salad, you may not want the server asking why. Sometimes you just need the field. But when a request is tied to something bigger, such as a new product, a new market or a change in how loans move through your shop, a few extra minutes on the why can save a lot of rework later.

The best technology partners ask bigger questions than “What do you want from your point of sale?”

What do you expect from the industry over the next few years? Which loan products matter most to your growth? What should borrowers experience at each step? How are your loans performing after they close?

A partner that asks those questions is building for your business. One that only collects feature lists is building for its backlog.

The same dynamic shows up when lenders buy technology. For many banks, credit unions and larger lenders, a formal request for proposal (RFP) is required, and for good reason: Single-source procurement is bad practice. The RFP isn’t the issue. The format often is. A spreadsheet passed around without context and scored with checkmarks rewards the provider willing to say yes to every line. It reveals little about whether that provider understands how a loan moves from application to servicing, or whether it will still ship useful work two years after the contract is signed.

Lenders can maintain rigor and add context. Pair the spreadsheet with live working sessions, share the business goals behind the requirements and ask finalists how they would solve the problem behind a line item, not just whether they can check it.

A point-of-sale decision touches processing, underwriting, closing, secondary marketing and servicing. Choices made in the first minutes of an application ripple through the loan’s entire life. A provider that only sees its own screen will optimize that screen.

Shipping on a regular schedule is the bare minimum. What I look for in a partner, and what lenders should expect from us, is a release that solves a problem in a way the lender didn’t picture or never thought to request. You know you’ve seen one when the lender’s first reaction is some version of, “Wait, why didn’t we have this already?”

Getting there means a technology company has to say no to some requests. If you build whatever gets the most votes, you end up with a product designed for the average lender. I’ve never met a lender who thinks of themselves as average.

Cost is the pressure every lender feels most. In the second quarter of 2026, independent mortgage banks and bank-owned mortgage companies spent $10,936 to produce each loan, according to the Mortgage Bankers Association. The average, dating back to the second quarter of 2008, is $7,945.

Automation should take repetitive work off the desks of loan officers, processors and underwriters, not replace them, so those people can spend their time on judgment, problem-solving and the borrower relationships that build a business. Every technology company claims to deliver that. Few can prove it.

Make them show you. Before you sign anything, have the provider take a real loan file from application to closing and point to the specific steps its technology took off someone’s plate. If they can’t walk you through those steps, I’d be skeptical of any savings they’re promising.

Around 2016, one of the first things I did in that IT role was map the lender’s borrower journey. Many of our borrowers came through real estate agents. Imagine a couple finding their dream home online, with professionally shot photos that make everything feel possible. Then they go to apply for a mortgage and are sent back decades in time to a confusing PDF. Should they print it? Can they type into it? Do they check the box or circle it? Didn’t they already provide this information?

A decade later, point-of-sale technology is standard, and no one is going back to that PDF. But the industry has a new version of it: borrowers who upload a pay stub and then type the same numbers into a form. Borrowers notice. They can’t tell you what’s wrong. That is why lenders need partners who can.

Feature lists still matter. Every lender compares them, and they should. But a feature list only tells you what a partner has already built. The question I’d ask every technology partner, including us, is simpler: What did you build last year that no customer asked for?

If the answer is nothing, you’re buying a list. If the answer surprises you, you’ve found a partner.
