After looking at the data in SIMBA, I concede going back further doesn't change anything and your central conclusion that "the historical data set does not support any meaningful distinction between short bonds (two years) and intermediate bonds (up to 10 years)" is correct when applied to holding bonds at constant duration.

1. The Constant Maturity Premium (1870–Present)
Below is the SIMBA data showing the rolling premium for intermediate and long term treasuries compared to short term treasuries (all held at constant maturity). The -1% CAGR for 1980, for instance, would be the period 1950-1980.


Intermediate (ITT) showed an average premium of +4.1 basis points but a median premium of -1.1 basis points.
Long (LTT) showed an average premium of +13.7 basis points and a median premium of -3.9 basis points.

So most likely you lose by using duration in a constant maturity fund, but the payoff can be good. Call it a 'lottery' as you did then, I concede fair enough.

2. Efficient Frontiers: Ladder vs. Constant Maturity
I also looked at Damodaran's 10-year treasury data to compare holding at a constant maturity to holding a rolling ladder of bonds (to see the normal effect of pull-to-par). If the colors are difficult to see, just note there is little difference between 'fractional' (mathematical ideal if all interest was invested) and 'retail' (real world limitation you must accrue interest at the short rate until you can purchase a $1k bond). There is some difference at lower equity holding percents between 'lump sum' (where all money is invested the ten year maturity) and 'ladder' (where rungs are bought to have 1-10 year maturities) in terms of lower volatility for the ladder.

As you can see below, the ladder has higher returns with less volatility in the bear market and the opposite in the bull. This makes me suspect that a rolling ladder may behave differently as part of a 60/40 than holding at constant maturity.

4. Terminal Wealth Outcomes
Finally, I looked at the rolling terminal values from being 60/40 and holding a rolling 10-year ladder vs STT. The terminal value of the rolling 10-year ladder portfolio was typically $18,000 higher (per $100k invested). At its worst (1953-1983), the terminal value for the rolling 10-year ladder was $101,384 less compared to STT. At its best (1984-2014), the terminal value for the rolling 10-year ladder was $314,687 more than STT.



5. Portfolio Drawdown & Behavioral Risk
One reason to avoid duration is behavioral—the fear that it will introduce deeper portfolio drawdowns during rate spikes. However, evaluating a continuous $100,000 investment from 1937 through 2025 reveals that the 10-year ladder introduces little additional behavioral risk compared to short Treasuries (at least with the yearly data I have).

Overall Volatility: Over the full 89-year timeline, volatility is very similar (10.75% annualized for STT versus 10.83% for the Ladder).

Rolling 30-Year Volatility: Even when stress-testing every 30-year rolling window, the maximum volatility penalty for the ladder was a mere +0.58 percentage points (11.37% vs 10.79% in the window ending 1998). In their most volatile window (ending 2002), the STT portfolio hit 11.71% volatility while the ladder hit just 12.01%.

Maximum Drawdown (1973–74 Bear Market): -22.59% (STT) vs. -23.20% (Ladder). Both portfolios peaked in 1972, troughed in 1974, and fully recovered by 1976.

Longest Time Underwater: Both portfolios spent the exact same 6 years underwater to start the simulation (1937–1942), clearing their original $100,000 balance in 1943.

Lowest Absolute Value: The ladder actually provided a slightly higher absolute floor during the 1937 year-end trough ($79,740 for the ladder vs. $79,266 for STT).

In short, the 10-year retail ladder seems to capture the terminal wealth upside shown in Section 4 without penalizing the investor with greater overall volatility or deeper drawdowns during market panics.

Conclusion
While I do agree the duration looks like a crapshoot if using constant maturity, I am not convinced that conclusion holds to bonds in general or to rolling ladders in particular. Since the 10-year ladder delivered a higher CAGR (8.67% vs 8.41%) on nearly identical volatility (10.83% vs 10.75%) which resulted in a superior Sharpe Ratio (0.46 vs 0.44) when compared to STT, I would argue bond investors did get rewarded for using duration with a rolling 10-year ladder in the historical data.

Discussion & Limitations
To be fully transparent, simulating a historical bond ladder using annual data requires some approximations that are worth noting:

Yield Curve Interpolation: Damodaran's dataset provides the 10-year yield and the T-bill rate, but lacks intermediate yields (3-year, 5-year, etc.). To price the interim rungs for annual 60/40 rebalancing, the simulation estimates off-the-run pricing. While this does not perfectly recreate historical yield curve inversions or "belly" fluctuations, the impact is muted because the ladder inherently holds to maturity—interim mark-to-market pricing only affects the small fraction of bonds sold to rebalance into equities.

Dataset Splicing: Comparing Damodaran's 10-year data against Simba's short-term (STT) and equity (TSM) data is not perfectly seamless. However, both the STT baseline and the Ladder portfolio used the exact same equity series (TSM) to isolate the fixed-income mechanics.

Academic Constant-Maturity vs. Real-World Bond Funds: Academic constant-maturity data assumes the bond is traded out entirely every single period to maintain an exact duration. Real-world bond index funds (like VGIT) operate more like a rolling ladder, allowing bonds to pull to par before dropping them at a 1-year threshold. Therefore, a real-world intermediate index fund likely captures some of the "shock absorber" effect seen in my ladder simulation, placing its actual historical performance somewhere between the blue (constant maturity) and green (ladder) lines on the charts above.

Annual Data Masks Intra-Year Drawdowns: The maximum drawdown and volatility metrics in Section 5 are calculated using year-end portfolio snapshots. Real-world behavioral risk is often driven by intra-year volatility (e.g., a mid-year market crash or rate spike that partially recovers by December). While the true maximum peak-to-trough drawdowns for both portfolios would certainly be deeper if measured daily or monthly, this limitation applies equally to both the STT baseline and the ladder, preserving the validity of their relative comparison.

It would be really wonderful if someone with better data could confirm or refute these findings!
Thanks for this post--impressive effort and a valuable addition to the thread. My big takeaway: rolling ladders are different, and deserve their own analysis, which I have not done. I can imagine lower volatility, and better returns to duration--up to a point. Because: the yield curve has a positive slope further out the maturity line then the annualized returns with which we were concerned.

I can imagine a 10-year ladder outperforming a 5-year constant maturity--and also a 7-year ladder. But it is not at all clear to me that a 15-year ladder would outperform a 10-year ladder by more than a de minimis amount. The yield curve flattens out too.

And all these ladder analyses assume Odysseus tied to the mast with wax in his ears, able never to mark to market the ladder, serenely holding until maturity. Unlikely from a behavioral standpoint. the more so for longer ladders.

Next, a few cautionary notes.
1. there are no meaningful Treasury returns from about 1900 to 1918. Certainly no differentiation by maturity. Inconvenient, but a fact. There are Treasury returns before 1900, but all the major sources (Shiller, Siegel) use a single index of municipal bond yields instead. Simba uses a simulation.
2. Damodaran, at annual frequency, lacks resolution. You need at least the Ibbotson or Shiller data.
3. If you want to pursue bond history, there really is no substitute for the CRSP Treasury file--everything, every bond, note and bill, back through 1926, monthly returns. I use my university library's subscription to Wharton Data Services to access.
4. There is a robust history for corporate (railroad) bonds before 1919, back to the 1860s or a little before. But theory chokes and gags on these: NOT RISK-FREE GOVERMENT BONDS. Verboten for theoretical work.
