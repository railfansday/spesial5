Day 35: Samaín: Halloween in Galicia

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

Unable to sleep until my alarm, I was up and starting the long day ahead before 5:30. Triacastela had transformed overnight into a spooky Halloween town. Okay, not really, but the misty cloud that had sunk into the valley made me think so.

With a lot of kilometers on the agenda, I took the planned route I discussed with my brothers and walked into the forest.

There was a quiet stillness permeating throughout.

Most might’ve called it eerie, but because of the comfort I learned to find in the dark, I savored it.

Today was Halloween in the US, Día de Los Muertos in Mexico, and Samaín here in Galicia.

In many traditions around the world, this time of year was believed to be when the veil between worlds was the thinnest. Today, I planned to lean into that energy and see what happened.

The sounds in the trees around me set the feeling of the day.

Birds I’d never heard before cawed and crowed.

Water droplets of cloud condensation pitter-pattered on the trees.

As the sun came up, I descended into another valley of clouds.

It was a town. As I moseyed along the streets through the dense fog, I came upon an old brick wall with rocks adorned with quotes. The phrases were wise and gave me something to contemplate. While reading them, I felt something brush my leg. I looked down and saw a puppy, ribs poking out a bit, and shaking. I bent down to pet it, and it jumped up, as if asking to be picked up. And so I did.

After setting it down and walking a few steps further, I found the source of the quotes and the dog: Terra de Luz (Land of Light). It was an old blacksmith property now open for pilgrims to simply stop, rest, and reflect. They had a table of food, a permaculture garden, and signs offering yoga, meditation, and integration. It felt like a place I could spend my whole day, if I didn’t have the biggest day of walking ahead of me. For the five minutes I was there, though, I definitely felt a wave of peace.

Though the environment looked eerie, the energy I felt was light.

All different types of dogs approaching me for pets.

Giant slugs crawling their way along the trail.

Newly born calves staring at me with their tongues out.

A field full of giant pumpkins ready for harvest.

As I walked, I researched how the local traditions celebrated the holiday of Samaín.

With roots in Celtic tradition, Samaín marked the end of the harvest. Roasted chestnuts (magostos) were eaten. Pumpkins and gourds were carved. And whispers of lore included witches (meigas) and mischievous spirits (trasnos).

The theme of the holiday naturally continued as I caught up to my Italian brother. We picked apples from a tree along the trail and noticed the many flowers adorning the gravestones for the day of the saints.

When we got into the forest again, we came upon a magnificent tree that looked to be older than the country of Spain itself. Call me a tree hugger, but as I touched its branches and took in its magnitude, I could feel its wisdom.

For the next portion of the walk, I chose to do it alone. I finished the last chapters of The Pilgrimage as I strolled under the many chestnut trees.

When I finished this book the first time, I felt it had an anticlimactic ending. Now that I’ve walked the road he recounted, the ending of the book felt impactful, like I’d learned the same lessons that he concluded at the end of his journey.

My journey wasn’t over, and I knew I had more to glean, but I definitely knew I’d found what I had come looking for.

And with those thoughts, I entered the most pilgrim-concentrated part of the Camino. Many people who didn’t have the freedom to do the whole route only had to walk the last 100 kilometers to receive a Compostela. This stretch would be busy, and I did my best to mentally prepare.

Well, that didn’t go super great. Many new pilgrims I didn’t know, combined with not getting a lot of alone time, weighed on my mood.

I made it to the 100-kilometer mark from Santiago. It didn’t feel real. Didn’t I just start walking?

When I arrived in Portomarín, I was completely exhausted, both mentally and physically.

Then the brothers and I ended the night with a new tradition: Uno. We were all exhausted and delirious, though, so the fun didn’t last long.

Early to bed for another day closer to Santiago.
