Fatbergs, pigeons and Pankhurst: rebranded London Museum opens new £437m home

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

The London sign mounted on a former awning structure that bridges between the former General Market and Market Annexe at Smithfield Market © Hufton + Crow

The new London Museum based at the historic Smithfield Market in Farringdon—one of the biggest museum developments in the UK capital in recent years—will open to the public this week with exhibits including a “fatberg” from the sewers, the Suffragette Emmeline Pankhurst’s hunger strike medal and Roman writing tablets.

Emmeline Pankhurst’s hunger strike medal © London Museum

Based at London Wall near the Barbican Centre until it closed in December 2022, the institution was previously called the Museum of London. The new complex, one of Europe’s largest cultural infrastructure projects, is designed by Stanton Williams and Asif Khan in partnership with the conservation architects Julian Harrap.

The museum caused a stir in 2024 when it unveiled a rebrand centred around the image of a defecating pigeon. Sharon Ament, the museum director, tells The Art Newspaper: “It’s not often a museum brand gets into the news. I think that’s because people saw, in our pigeon, the city and their experience: a place full of contrasts. It’s beautiful, it’s fun, it’s also a bit scruffy! I don’t think there’s a better representation of us than a pigeon.”

The first phase of the London Museum will open in a Victorian part of the Smithfield Market © Secchi Smith

The new museum site will open in two phases: the formerly derelict Victorian General Market dating from the 1880s, home to the permanent galleries, will open on 28 November, while the 1960s Poultry Market, which houses a learning centre, temporary exhibition spaces and on-site interactive collection store, will launch in 2028.

“We are using time as an organising principle, so our major spaces all behave differently with their own emphasis, objects and vibe,” says Ament. The General Market houses the Past Time and Our Time sections, plus Night Time. Past Time spans 2,000 years, telling the story of London via landmark events like the Great Fire in 1666 and the 2012 Olympics.

A chunk of the Whitechapel Fatberg © London Museum

“Stars of the collection” like the Cheapside Hoard of Elizabethan and Jacobean jewels; the medal given to Pankhurst to commemorate her 1912 hunger strike; and a sample of the fatberg—a 250-metre long mass of oil and wet wipes—will also feature in Past Time.

“We have two significant but very contrasting buildings in the Victorian General Market and the listed 1960s Poultry Market, representing different moments in London’s history. We think about them as two of the biggest objects in our collection and they lend themselves to different experiences,” says Ament.

Our Time in the Linbury Hall is a social hub at the heart of London Museum, set across the former trading floor of the General Market. Around its perimeter, a restaurant, shop and show space for families and schools sits alongside 13 large installations exploring the ways Londoners have come together © Hufton + Crow

Asked if the museum is just for Londoners, she says that the city’s residents will discover new stories from the city’s vast history, and be part of telling its story today, while for international visitors, “this is a great first place to start their immersion in London”. The museum has an ambitious visitor figure target of over two million annually.

There are 18 people in the curatorial team but the museum is also supported by an academic advisory panel and “some 100,000 people from London and beyond” who have helped shaped the museum. These include various focus groups, community and school groups, and advisory panels such as the museum’s Access Panel and Family Panel

View into part of London Museum’s "London Tastes" display, exploring the changing appetites of a global food city © Hufton + Crow

The cost of the new museum has risen to £437m in total (the budget was set at £337m in 2019). The Greater London Authority, under mayor Sadiq Khan, has given £95m, while the City of London Corporation is contributing £222m. A range of private foundations and sponsors including Bloomberg Philanthropies, the Goldsmiths’ Foundation, the Linbury Trust and the National Lottery Heritage Fund are also backing the project.

As for running costs, statutory funding comes from the City of London Corporation and the Mayor of London, Ament says.

“We also want to find ways to stand on our own feet more,” she says. “That means support from commercial partnerships and activities, like retail, ticket sales, our ‘fan club’ [the museum’s membership] and venue hire. We’re also leasing spaces, known as the museum ‘Houses’, that sit around the perimeter of the General Market, so now we are landlords as well.” She says the sites are sustainable through the use of solar panels and smart technology.
