Florence Pugh Won’t Let Fear Hold Her Back

Tanggal: 2026-11-11

Every item on this page was chosen by an ELLE editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

The daring East of Eden star on her banner year, speaking out, and not standing for bullshit.

Florence Pugh says she “tries to be brave at least once a day.” Whether she’s raising her voice, taking on complex roles, wearing a sheer dress and an I-don’t-give-a-fuck attitude on the red carpet, or even—gasp!—showing cellulite onscreen, she is consistent in her courageousness. When we meet in late September, it has been two days since she made a powerful statement on Instagram about the lawsuit alleging gang rape at Cornell University, and then posted again when she learned her original post had been blocked by Meta. “No one fucking censors me,” she tells me. “Don’t even try. That’s just going to add fuel to the fire.”

It’s not so much that Pugh is fearless (she is still going through life as a woman, after all), it’s that she was raised with steel in her spine. “My dad has always hated bullies, and he’s always told me that if you ever see anything, you have to do the right thing. Otherwise you are just the same as them,” she says. “And I think that’s where that instinct of ‘I must speak out on this injustice’ comes from. It’s always important to stand up for people who don’t have the voice that I have. I have the ability, and I have to use it. I don’t know who I would be if I didn’t.”

Pugh is in New York City in the days ahead of the premiere of East of Eden, her latest project in perhaps the biggest year of her career to date, between the Netflix limited series and roles in three blockbusters: Spider-Man: Brand New Day, Dune: Part Three, and Avengers: Doomsday.

When she arrives for our lunch at the Greenwich Hotel, her aura precedes her. Before she’s in sight, I hear her thanking waiters, busboys, and a random guy who had the fortune of holding the door for her, purring “darling” and “love” at them as she floats by, her charming, deep British drawl on full display. When she steps into the courtyard, she waves at me from the other side as if we’re old friends.

As we tuck into some bigeye tuna and a green Caesar (she is happy to share), the conversation turns serious quickly. Pugh is arguably the highest-profile celebrity to post about the heinous accusations at Cornell, and I ask her why she felt compelled to do so. “Because I’m a woman who cares about women. I care about women I know, and I care about women I don’t know. And if we allow for stories like that to become in any way normal, then we are allowing ourselves to continue toward that destiny of abuse and rape being accepted as a norm,” she says. “Rape can never ever be a conversation that we move on from. It has to be something that we look at, face-on, and we demand justice, accountability, and for the rapist to see what they have done to someone’s life. Because if we don’t, we’re accepting it. And I don’t ever, ever, ever want to live in a world where we move on from it easily.”

I tell her I know it might seem odd to talk about Cornell in an interview that’s meant to be about her acting career, but she breaks into a smile and says, “It’s actually perfect.” You see, to her it’s all related, because she tries to be just as brave onscreen as she is off it. “I’m terrified of every single role that I’ve played. Like, ‘How am I going to do that? I don’t know if I can.’ But that’s also the thing that really thrills me, because it means that I get to come face-to-face with that feeling of fear again and override it. And the same can be said of things in life that are intimidating or are scary or require me to be brave. It’s like, ‘If I stay in this, I’m going to live in this fear. And if I punch through it, then I can overcome it.’”

Her characters demand a lot of her. They are complicated, nuanced, and often find themselves clawing out of their circumstances by tooth, nail, and, more than once, poison. At just 19 years old, and without any formal acting training, Pugh’s award-winning breakout role in Lady Macbeth (not that one) required her to embody a bride in 1865, so miserable in her cruel marriage that she murdered her way to freedom. In 2019, she played both a grieving twentysomething-turned-cult “May Queen” in Midsommar and the youngest sister in Greta Gerwig’s Little Women, managing to imbue the latter character with such humanity that she had audiences feeling empathy for Amy March (of all people), and also received her first Oscar nomination. And since her debut as Yelena Belova in 2021’s Black Widow, she has done many of her own stunts in the Marvel films, once jumping off a 2,227-foot-tall building nine times to get the shot.

Her range and repertoire are why Zoe Kazan, writer, executive producer, and co-showrunner of this year’s buzzy East of Eden adaptation, has said Pugh was the “only person” she envisioned for the role of the notorious Cathy Ames. “I thought it was the most gripping, sensational script, and I immediately started saying the lines out loud, so I was like, ‘Oh fuck, well, I’m Cathy,’” Pugh tells me. “I loved how she was a full human who does awful things, and has awful things done to her, and makes mistakes. I loved the question marks and the complexities to her, because that’s real to me.”

In John Steinbeck’s telling, Cathy is pure evil, and it’s unclear what made her that way (he himself seems not to know). Leave it to a woman to add depth to a female character. Kazan’s Cathy is just as ruthless, but she has a backstory that is used to add context, but never to justify her heinous acts. She’s fully formed, messy—okay, yes, likely sociopathic—and exactly the type of woman Pugh loves to take on. “I don’t know how to describe it other than I play characters that ask a lot of audience members,” Pugh says. “I think there’s something that excites, and also troubles, people about the way that I find humanity in even some of the most evil characters. I think it fascinates people.” As we take our final bites, I say people should just let women be, both in real life and onscreen. “And let us be big and beautiful,” Pugh adds. “I know that’s intimidating, but please let us, because it only makes great things.”

There’s so much scrutiny of how we look, how we dress, and how we age. And then when we do those things, they’re like, “Oh my God, she looks like this, or she’s done too much of that, or she’s gained weight, or she’s lost too much weight.” You can never get any footing right. I feel like the best work that I’ve ever done is when I truly feel ugly. When I’ve got shit makeup on and I’m either sick or ugly or having a hangover or a breakdown. I love that type of makeup because it allows me to be free.

What I love about acting is, I don’t have the typical self-consciousness of just being a woman. When I’m acting, I get to be comfortable in the body that I’m in. If the camera picks up that I have a stomach roll when I’m sitting down, then I have a stomach roll when I’m sitting down. And if the camera picks up that I’ve got cellulite on my thighs, then yeah, I’ve got cellulite on my thighs. I just give it over to the art. So performing is very freeing for me, and it always has been.

It is so crucial to be okay with how you are and who you are, because people are going to attack you regardless. They’re going to say really shitty things, no matter if you’re too small, too big, too wrinkly, too spotty, too loud, too quiet, too opinionated. They’re going to find issue with you, because that’s just the nature of the internet and people and opinions. So it’s really important to find peace with what you want to represent and who you want to be. Because then you can just be like, “Ugh, those dickheads.”

The moment I got that nomination, it’s like the pressure just vanished—there was nothing breathing down my neck anymore. I felt the energy of how people wanted to work with me change. It was not only just a pat on the back that I could feel, but it was a pat on the back that I felt like everybody could see. But it also made me even harder on myself to get another one. It’s like, I know that I’ve achieved this before. I can achieve this again.

I learned how to drive on the Warner Brothers lot when I was 19, for this pilot that I was doing called Studio City. And so I learned with the transpo captain, going around the Warner Brothers lot in a car, and I remember at the time thinking, This is so hysterical, this is so movie world. I just thought it was hysterical that I was constantly learning how to drive all of these amazing cars for so many projects, but I never had my license. [She has one now.]

The thing that I’m getting better and better at is protecting myself. Every time I work on a movie, I get more confident in the way that I can protect myself and the way that I can lead a film set, and just not stand for bullshit anymore. I’m not doing it. I’m not standing for bullshit behavior. I’m not standing for bullshit attitude. I’m not allowing for people to be rude to the crew. You just get bolder and bigger and more confident with time, and you want to be around and work with the right people. And the moment someone is making that vibe feel off, everybody feels it. I don’t want to deal with messy. It’s just about surrounding yourself with professionals who have the same way of working in mind: Show up on time, don’t be rude, don’t be a misogynist.

There’s something so magical about doing those big, stonkingly large movies with budgets and costumes and sets, because those are the films that I remember watching when I was a kid. But I’m not one thing. I also love doing really small things. I love how they both feed my soul.

I would like to have lunch with Cathy. No, actually I’d like to have lunch with Kate [what Cathy calls herself in later episodes]. But I wouldn’t be trying to fuss on her. I’d love to have dinner with Kate just because I’d be really interested to see how she talks to people outside of me. And then I’d love to have dinner with Amy March, because she’d take me to some fabulous place in Paris and I know she’d be wearing fantastic clothing. And then there’s poor Dani [in Midsommar]. I’d need to have a whole weekend with her. She’s so broken. I’d need to feed her soul food. I’d need to cook for her. I’d need to put her in front of the fire and wrap a blanket around her and just have hugs.
