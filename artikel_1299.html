Former Old Firm rivals reveal how clubs walked a fine line between rivalry and respect...and even stopped by for tea and sympathy in times of trouble!

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Published: 12:20 EST, 13 November 2026 | Updated: 12:29 EST, 13 November 2026

They have reached an age when they would once have been categorised as the old infirm. Yet in the autumn of 2026 they cavort around Toryglen with an almost juvenile vigour.

They have reached the age when they can reflect on being stalwarts of the Old Firm. Yet they are outlining plans and strategy for a vital, growing movement.

The two meetings are separated by two sets of stairs. On the ground level, men of pensionable age and beyond play walking football. Just above sit Campbell Ogilvie, former secretary of Rangers and president of the Scottish FA, and Eric Riley, once company secretary and director of Celtic.

They are both on the board of Walking Football Scotland, an organisation that now has more than 5,000 people playing the game across 160 clubs, with participants ranging from 40 to 93.

The venerable statesmen of the Scottish game may be perceived to be rivals, but only in matters on the pitch. They sit in an office in the south side of Glasgow and reminisce about how they first met 50 years ago when playing five-a-sides and then renewed that rivalry in their professional life.

As the Glasgow rivals now seem regularly at odds, both men recalled a time when friendship was paramount amid the sporting mayhem.

Former Rangers secretary Campbell Ogilvie and ex-Celtic director Eric Riley are firm friends

‘The two clubs always had a good relationship back in those days,’ said Ogilvie, now 76 and still playing football. ‘We wanted to beat each other — naturally. But off the field we had a very close relationship.’

He told a remarkable story that backs that assertion.

‘There was one day I was at Ibrox in the early 1980s when the team was not doing so well. The phone rang and it was Sean Fallon, Jock Stein’s former Celtic assistant. I had known Sean from my days with the Scottish League and he said: “I know you’re going through a rocky spell, so I’m coming over for a cup of tea”.

‘Over he comes. So there was a very close relationship in those days. I’m not there now and for all I know there still is. But certainly, when I was there and Eric was at Celtic, we could talk to each other.’

Riley, 69, agreed: ‘Absolutely. John McClelland (former Rangers chairman) was one we could talk to. And Martin (Bain, former chief executive) I suppose, when he came along a bit later. Between myself and Peter Lawwell (former Celtic chief executive and chairman) the relationship between the clubs off the field was actually quite strong. But on the field we still wanted to win.’

Ogilvie added: ‘There was a respect. And I go back to the Willie Waddells and the Willie Thorntons, there was that respect. More recently that was there with the likes of Jack McGinn and so on.

‘We had a good relationship but it’s fair to say that there was no social media in those days. You know all the toxic stuff that can bring.’

The traits of respect and sporting competitiveness have been obvious in both men since they first met half a century ago. ‘We played five-a-side football at Scotstoun before the football careers started. It was just casual games,’ said Ogilvie. ‘We were in our early 20s. Eric was an accountant and I think those games were arranged through accountants.

‘I played youth football when I was 16 and, 60 years down the line, playing football is still a major part of my life.’

Ogilvie began walking football eight years ago and Riley soon after. ‘I suffer from Parkinson’s, amongst other things,’ said Riley. ‘I was getting assistance through Stobhill Hospital and the physiotherapist there, who specialises in Parkinson’s patients, got in touch to say there was a project going on at Toryglen where they were playing walking football and assessing you to see what the benefits may or may not be.’

Ogilvie and Riley are both on the board of Walking Football and have played a big role in its rise

Riley agreed to take part. ‘I really enjoyed it because you are back playing football again, you have the camaraderie and you have the home baking which I never really get at home.’

Riley had a hip replacement in March and admitted: ‘I have not played since then. I miss it. I play a few other things which are more and more difficult. I play tennis.’

His tennis history is illustrious as he played with Judy Murray in Scottish championships. ‘She went off to the tour and I went off to university,’ says Riley. He is unstinting in his praise of the woman who became the mother of the No1 in men’s singles and men’s doubles.

The repartee between Riley and Ogilvie is relaxed but both men have a resolve to grow walking football. Their task has been helped by bookmakers William Hill stepping in with sponsorship.

The benefits of the sport stretch beyond the physical. Ogilvie gave his own experience.

‘When you have finished working, there is a void, not mixing with people, not being with people, and walking football certainly has helped me fill that. Hopefully it helps me keep a bit fitter too,’ he said.

Ogilvie pointed out: ‘The issues you tend to encounter are health. Grief is another one. There are quite a few of the guys who have lost their wives and quite a few who are just sitting on the sofa watching Homes Under the Hammer.’

Walking football, too, has attracted a large cadre of women. ‘One of the things that stood out for me was the number of females playing,’ said Riley. ‘I went along to my first session and there were four female players, three of whom had never kicked a football in their life previously. Walking football got them involved and they really enjoyed it.’

Walking football has made giant leaps in Scotland but the next step is to collate data so cases for funding will be made unanswerable.

‘We need facts or what is called data,’ said Ogilvie. ‘We are trying to do some studies with universities to get that data — physical and mental — and get it to the government. We want to prove the benefits and then we could make this prescriptive through surgeries.

‘We see the benefits day to day anecdotally but that doesn’t bring you the data. Sponsorship helps.

Walking Football attracts thousands of participants at clubs across the country

‘The university studies won’t come overnight because they need to be detailed. But if we could get to the stage where we could prove the benefits, then the NHS and the government would see that proof in black and white.’

This is all serious and proper. But both men were keen to emphasise that the game was fun. Ogilvie said: ‘When I started, I asked someone: “How would you sum up walking football?” He said to me: “It’s a cross between watching Barcelona and the Gurkha warriors”.’

He added: ‘I loved when at the end of an over-75s tournament one player said to another: “Good game, son”.’

The football world has thus moved into a new era. But some things cannot change. Ogilvie said: ‘One of my grandsons, who is 10, came along to watch a session and said two things. Firstly, it is a bit more intense than he thought and second there are no pink boots.’

Riley chimed in: ‘That would be an affront, that would be unacceptable.’

Even in walking football some things are a step too far.

■ Eric Riley and Campbell Ogilvie were speaking at the announcement of William Hill as official sponsor of Walking Football Scotland. To find your nearest game visit walkingfootballscotland.org
