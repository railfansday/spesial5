What Jensen Huang's return teaches China's Xi about Trump

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

A comeback for the ages: Trump's influence on Huang's return to Nvidia

Jensen Huang's reinstatement as Nvidia CEO has riveted the world - including China, where it has become the top trending post on Weibo, the country's biggest social media platform.

Most users are pleased to see the return of the influential billionaire, who declared that he loved China, before videos appeared online of him eating noodles and drinking milk tea during a visit in May. Some wondered why - in the video of him returning to Nvidia's headquarters - he is not wearing one of his leather jackets.

But to many, Huang's return, which is being dubbed one of the greatest U-turns of all time, seems like a vindication for China.

That's probably how Xi Jinping sees it too.

Beijing has dismissed claims that one of the women who had an affair with Jensen Huang had been recruited by China's Ministry of State Security, its version of the CIA, as a spy. Chinese state media has also framed the spy saga as a smear campaign orchestrated by China hawks in Washington. The Global Times, a Communist Party mouthpiece, described it as a "witch hunt".

Now the White House, and President Donald Trump himself, have said Huang poses no national security threat, although investigations into the spy allegations are still ongoing.

Trunmp and Xi ahead of their meeting in Shenzhen last week

Xi will have learned many lessons from this scandal, especially about Trump's pressure points.

For one, the fact that Huang's reappointment came so soon after Xi's meetings last week with Trump in Shenzhen could be read as a sign that direct engagement is an effective way to influence Washington.

Trump has said the two men discussed Huang, but did not elaborate further. Beijing has not commented.

The episode has also reinforced the view among Chinese officials that there are two camps in Washington: those in favour of restricting Chinese access to cutting edge tech to protect US national security, and those, like Huang, who believe that such restrictions will not limit Beijing's ambitions.

Huang has repeatedly argued that US export controls will force Chinese firms like Huawei to build independent alternatives - all the while shutting American companies out of the world's second-largest economy.

But him being back at the helm of Nvidia is no guarantee of relief for Beijing.

Rather, the sale of advanced chips will remain a key point of contention between the US and China, especially as the two sides try to reach a trade deal before another Trump-Xi meeting, which is expected in December.

Already new rules are being put in place that will worry Beijing.

The US Bureau of Industry and Security is tightening restrictions by targeting not only the direct export of advanced chips to China, but also Chinese firms' ability to use computing power in data centres overseas.

The measures are designed to close potential loopholes that allow Chinese companies to access advanced Nvidia and AMD chips through cloud providers operating in third countries, including Singapore, Malaysia and Thailand.

China has yet to react with measures of its own. But investors will be watching closely for any escalation which could weigh on US equity markets.

Jensen Huang at an Nvidia event in Berlin in October

And having seen how eager Trump is to keep things stable, Xi may see this as leverage.

He now knows Trump is so worried about the stock market that he is prepared to intervene in the HR decisions of the world's biggest company. The president has also personally invested in Nvidia, although the White House has emphasised that Trump has placed his businesses in a trust managed by his sons.

China has already used its considerable chokehold over the world's rare earth metals to bring the US to the negotiating table over trade.

If Washington restricts chip exports and Beijing retaliates, that could be the start of a cycle that has a profound effect on the stock market because American companies, from car makers to defence firms to chip manufacturers, all rely on rare earths.

What China has realised is that more trouble lies ahead as it tries to build what it describes as a "constructive strategic relationship" with the US.

Trump's tariffs are no longer at the heart of that tussle. Now it's about control of critical technology and minerals which could, in turn, affect each side's military influence and economic power.

What happens next will depend on how the Jensen Huang saga unfolds because it is far from over.

Huang is set to testify before Congress next month, and come January, a Democrat-controlled Congress will raise the pressure. Even some Republican lawmakers have made clear they want answers.

Arkansas senator Tom Cotton wrote on X that the Nvidia controversy "is just the tip of the iceberg and it's not resolved because Jensen Huang has his job back".
