10 Best Meal Delivery Services, Tested by an Ex-Restaurant Critic

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

The best meal delivery services and meal kits make your home life easier—and maybe make dinner something to look forward to rather than dread. Our lead tester for this guide, former WIRED Reviews writer Matthew Korfhage, didn’t expect to appreciate meal kits as much as he did, especially since he likes to cook and worked as a food critic on both coasts for more than a decade.

The best meal kits help you climb out of a cooking rut of turning to the same dishes—and the same flavors—over and over. After testing every major meal delivery service over the past two years, we’ve narrowed the field to just a few of the best options for each family and diet. Quite simply, the best meals our testers have eaten from a meal kit come from Marley Spoon, which offers a path to genuinely good home-cooked meals on nights when you might otherwise feel uninspired.

But depending on your priorities, other meal delivery services might be a better option. Home Chef offers the greatest flexibility for busy families while keeping prices affordable. EveryPlate is the best meal kit for people on a tight budget. For a healthful, tailored diet (including an option for true gluten-free meals), we recommend Green Chef. If you’d rather not cook at all, CookUnity is the best prepared-meal delivery service we’ve ever had the pleasure to warm up.

We assessed each meal plan’s recipes, variety, sourcing, ease of preparation, convenience, organization, packaging, ingredient quality, and suitability for different dietary restrictions and needs. We also tested the best new and innovative meal kits for those looking for alternatives to Blue Apron's or HelloFresh’s long-standing meal delivery services. And not for nothing, we sorted out some often maddeningly nontransparent pricing.

Be sure to check out our investigation into whether meal kits are sometimes cheaper than groceries when trying out new recipes, and WIRED's related guides, including Best Plant-Based Meal Kits, Best Food Gifts You Can Buy Online, Best Coffee Subscriptions, and Best Delivery Chocolate Boxes.

Updated November 2026: We’re retesting the kits in this guide, including special holiday offerings from meal kit makers like Marley Spoon. We’ve added some notes reflecting this in relevant sections and checked for accuracy throughout.

In the decade since it was introduced on American shores, the strength of the Martha Stewart–endorsed Marley Spoon meal delivery service has remained steadfast: It’s the cooking.

The best individual meals we’ve cooked from the 20 or so kits we’ve tested over the past two years have come from Marley Spoon. A hearty, Hungarian-influenced creamy mushroom chicken; a boldly Cajun-spiced whitefish; or a delicate eggplant parm. Those meals are still on the menu.

But Marley Spoon has also been evolving of late. Over the past year or two, the German-founded meal kit has added more menu items, more cosmopolitan meals with global flavors, and a greater variety of preparation times that now include a lot of 15-minute meals. It’s done this without raising the price of the meal kit this year—no mean feat amid rising food and shipping costs.

The classic “Martha’s Best” recipes are now augmented by meals that emphasize convenience and global flavors. A third of the menu is now prepared, ready-to-heat meals. There are more 15-minute dishes and tray oven bakes. Marley Spoon also seems to have broadened its international fare, with takes on a Moroccan-spiced apricot and beef tagine and Indian-influenced keema matar that’s as easy to make as garam masala sloppy Joe. You can even get special holiday kits—we can confirm that the Thanksgiving feasts are excellent, and more specialty meal options abound for December holidays.

Still, more than other meal kits, Marley remains devoted to classical cooking technique—the sort they teach in culinary school and on Martha Stewart’s TV shows. This is no accident. When the German-founded meal kit arrived in the US, it did so with the cooking maven’s endorsement and a lot of her recipes and techniques. Recipes often call for light seasoning throughout the process—not all at once. A deeply savory kale and sausage soup might be balanced with acid from a surprising splash of red wine vinegar.

Marley Spoon is more honest than most meal kits about the time it actually takes to cook a recipe. While your 15-minute meal might acccctually still require 20 minutes, it definitely won’t take 30 or 40. There is one point of inconvenience with Marley, one that might help keep the company’s prices from climbing: Unlike other top-line kits, Marley sends your ingredients jumbled in a box, which reduces packaging waste but also leaves you hunting for the right spice mix or dried ingredient. The meat sourcing also isn’t as transparent as in some kits.

Like Blue Apron and others, Marley Spoon has recently partnered with food delivery service Misfits Market after its previous US partner, FreshRealm, filed for Chapter 11 protection. We'll be testing and monitoring to make sure Marley keeps earning its spot at the top of this list. While maintaining value, Marley Spoon offers genuinely good cooking and acts as a little school in culinary techniques. Some other meal kits on this list might offer more flexibility. Marley offers a firm backbone.

Home Chef lets you do pretty much whatever you want. Do you want two servings of two recipes, but eight servings of a third? No problem. Did you want to add an oven-ready steak meal for six to this week’s order, and only this week’s order? Also not a problem. Have a large family? Home Chef offers a cheaper family plan for batched family meals. Want fewer meals this week than your typical order? Each meal will cost a little more, but you can still cut back your order on the fly without canceling.

More than any other meal kit we’ve tested, Home Chef is optimized for convenience, down to the transparent plastic bags that house each meal’s ingredients. The recipes are finely detailed, with easy-to-follow instructions. Nutrition and allergens are listed in even finer detail online. Each recipe is classed according to difficulty, and even the theoretically highfalutin’ “culinary collection” meals aren’t hard to make or dauntingly culinary—mayhaps a stuffed chicken breast, perhaps some gnocchi.

It’s no surprise that WIRED previously listed Home Chef as the best meal kit for beginner chefs: the recipe instructions are full of little hand-holds and extra advice. That said, the meals we tried weren’t quite as sophisticated or as tasty as those from our top pick, Marley Spoon, nor as varied and interesting as HelloFresh. One “culinary” meal was basically a stuffed burger with seasoned fries.

But convenience, flexibility, and good administration count for a lot in this world. So do relatively low prices: $10 a meal on most standard plans and $8 a plate on the kid-friendly family plan. If you’ve got a family life with meal demands that change from week to week—which is what a lot of people looking to meal kits are trying to solve—Home Chef may be the meal kit you need.

CookUnity is the only prepared-meal delivery service we’ve tried that has ever delivered a meal better than local restaurant takeout. Specifically, from the Seattle kitchen run by chef Gaurav Raj, former WIRED reviewer Matthew Korfhage tried multiple Indian gravies, from butter chicken to chicken lababdar, that tasted better than what he could get at the Punjabi restaurant down the street.

Prepared meal delivery services promise a hunger-squelching holy grail: fresh meals, delivered to your door in a box that’s ready to heat and eat, minus all the effort of actually cooking it. But the results from many other prepared-meal delivery services have been mixed. Indeed, some struggle to better the TV dinners they resemble. The reason CookUnity has been a big standout is its unique business model.

Most prepared meal plans come from a single big corporate kitchen. But CookUnity is more like a cross between a meal service and DoorDash. CookUnity enlists restaurant chefs, often big names like Jose Garces or Rick Bayless or Iron Chef contestants, to design recipes and hire cooks who make your meals out of regional commissary kitchens, and ship them never-frozen down the street. This model leads not just to quality but also to diversity of food.

Over two years of testing, we’ve tried multiple well-composed salads, a surprisingly wonderful truffled beef stroganoff, credible Filipino adobo, and Japanese-style katsu with curry that crisped up tantalizingly in an air fryer.

Menus, chefs, and (probably) results may vary depending on your region and your distance from the cooking hub. Hubs now include New York, Austin, Miami, Atlanta, Los Angeles, Seattle, Chicago, and Toronto. (See the delivery map to check whether CookUnity services your area.) Korfhage had good results from the small Seattle hub, and WIRED reviewer Louryn Strampe said the adobo flank steak and rigatoni alla vodka she scored from the Chicago hub straight-up blew her mind.

Prep tends to happen one of two ways: either you nuke it, or you follow the chef’s ideal stovetop or oven instructions to create optimal texture and flavor. After reheating, you might also stretch burrata cheese over your pasta or ladle cumin-forward sauce on your salmon. The meals are filling, the textures are spot-on, and the packaging is compostable and recyclable. Each meal lasts in the fridge between four and seven days from the date of delivery, through the miracle of modified atmosphere packaging.

After retesting CookUnity this year during warmer weather in Oregon, we found that meals arrived at food-safe temperatures—no small feat, given that some meals drove in from four hours away.

HelloFresh is pretty much synonymous with “meal kits.” It’s the biggest and one of the oldest services. It’s become a global brand, available in 18 countries. Chances are, if you’ve tried a meal kit, it’s one of the first you tried. What’s surprised us, though, is how much HelloFresh has improved since we first tried it: better sourcing and more cosmopolitan, modern recipes.

The brand has become a logistics powerhouse. These days, you get seasonal or even regional ingredients, depending on where you live. Proteins are sourced from multigenerational, family-run meat purveyors. Each subscription box is also well-organized, with each recipe’s ingredients portioned into labeled bags. All you need are pots, pans, a stove, and basic oil, salt, and butter.

Especially over the past year, HelloFresh has undergone a near-astonishing expansion of its menu. Where Korfhage lives, more than 400 menu items are available each week, spanning a truly global range of cuisines. One day, he could find himself deep in a Gambian-inspired peanut stew. The next it’s an aspirational Hainanese chicken feast, a simplified Thai-style green curry shrimp, Tex-Mex enchiladas slathered in cheese and ranchero sauce, or Guamanian-inflected chicken kelaguen. Or he can just make himself a salad, wrap, or sandwich.

This summer, HelloFresh has even offered single-order grill boxes in partnership with meat delivery service Good Chop, with 32 servings comprising burgers (six), dogs (eight), steaks (four), sausages (eight), and bacon (eight strips) for $80. (According to Korfhage, the steaks and bacon were the two best-selling points.)

Where possible, HelloFresh has even begun dabbling in seasonal ingredients, like conehead cabbage Korfhage charred on his grill last summer. In some areas, HelloFresh has even added regional dishes and ingredients. It’s an exercise in logistics that makes the home chefs a little giddy. After criticism, the company added more rice to the rice meals last year. That said, shipping prices are up, and the per-serving price has also hiked around 50 cents this year—a tribute to the rising gas and food prices that have plagued 2026.

The global offerings don’t make much claim to authenticity, mind you. Also, HelloFresh’s 20-minute meals sometimes take as long as 40 minutes to make. But HelloFresh’s offerings look a lot like how people eat these days. They are bright, light, and lightly internationalized: with pan-Latin steak and rice bowls, beef stir-fried with ponzu and plum, Dutch-inspired orange Dijon chicken, Thai-style curries, and maybe some mango salsa atop a vaguely Southwest-y pork roast.

Green Chef is first and foremost the organic arm of HelloFresh, assiduous enough that they sent an apology note when they had to sub in nonorganic Roma tomatoes. In a recent August test, Green Chef’s fresh ingredients and sourcing impressed as much as any meal kit we’ve tasted, including fresh herbs, seasonal fare, wild-caught sockeye salmon, and delicious (farmed) steelhead trout.

The healthiest diet is the one that’s attentive to your specific needs, and this is where Green Chef shines. The meal program has a strong vegetarian and dairy-free focus and at least 10 keto meals a week. Green Chef is also among the few meal delivery services that prepare its gluten-free meals in separate facilities—backed by independent validation from the Seattle-area Gluten Intolerance Group. As of August, more than half of Green Chef's meals are gluten-free, mostly dishes like rice or quinoa that don’t need gluten rather than pale imitations of gluteny things. Green Chef even offers free phone consultation with a dietitian. (Korfhage’s counseled him on protein and fiber and directed me to the meals marked as consistent with the Mediterranean diet.)

Meals arrive well-organized, in mostly recyclable packaging. Recipes are easy to follow, though the instructions are a bit minimalist. (Read ahead, and your prep times will go down.) The proteins are flavorful. And options are international and sophisticated, from shrimp spiced with Yemeni schug to cumin-ancho meat dishes. Even in August, the most difficult time to ship perishable food, herbs and veggies had nary a wilted leaf—a difficult and rare achievement, especially for fresh herbs like the parsley in the gremolata for a Turkish-spiced steelhead trout. Overall, there are fewer options than you’ll find from HelloFresh’s flagship kit, and Green Chef is among the more expensive meal kits. But the quality and variety of ingredients reflect that.

Hungryroot has among the lowest prep times of any meal kit we’ve tested that isn’t actually pre-prepped—perfect for the friend who “doesn’t have time to cook” and instead finds their DoorDash budget ballooning. Hungryroot meals tend to take about the same amount of time to cook as to eat, 10 to 20 minutes for most meals we’ve tried.

Each meal tends to be assembled from a small number of components, with a lot of the prep already done. An organic sauce or salsa or dressing, some version of pre-mixed salad, a sprig of broccoli, a pack of sous-vide chicken or smoked pulled pork. It’s a bit like running a personal fast-casual restaurant out of your home kitchen.

It’s also nice that you can customize Hungryroot to suit your needs and dietary preferences. The big idea behind Hungryroot is to be a full-service, AI-assisted food concierge. First, Hungryroot logs all your dietary, lifestyle, and budget preferences. (Hate a mushroom? Follow a low-salt diet? Don’t like masala spices, specifically? Don’t worry.) Then it suggests a whole world of ingredients and recipes, which you can sub out from a seemingly endless list each week, depending on local ingredient availability. Usually, there’s room left over in the plan to add some fresh fruit or a sweet treat. One detriment of this ultra-customized system is that you’ve got to get pretty far through the ordering process (and enter your credit card) before seeing your personal menu. Here’s the online “cookbook” of potential menus, for those who’d like an early glimpse.

This is a bit of a game-changer for meal kits, frankly, though the scenario I just described would end up a bit expensive ($10 a serving, plus $10 shipping). And so Blue Apron also has a DoorDash-style loyalty model: Blue Apron Plus. You pay $10 a month—the price of shipping on a single order—and then shipping is free for the month. This means that if you order more than once a month, you save money by signing up. Price per meal has fanned out since the reboot, ranging from $8 a serving for lunchtime salads and wraps to $13 for hearty bakes and seafood plates. This lets you choose meals based on your budget.

This is an ambitious turnaround for a meal kit that WIRED reviewers had loved in the past before going through some bumps in the road under new ownership beginning in 2023. So far we’ve had pretty good results. Our most recent test included fun, internationalized recipes like crisp, lightly spicy bang bang chicken; bao bun burgers with shishito peppers; za’atar-spiced salmon; and an excellent, hearty shrimp and feta bake. Prep for most recipes hovered around 30 minutes. And on each, you could sub out a main protein. WIRED reviewer Kat Merck tried Blue Apron’s low-effort, assemble-and-bake meals and came away mostly impressed by the sourcing and ingredient quality—but also wondered whether the focus on simplicity led to compromises in some recipes. She couldn’t bring herself to bake raw meat for a harissa beef dish, per instructions, without first draining the grease. And so she cooked it in a skillet first.

There’d been a few ingredient hiccups in 2025 as the new plan rolled out, but this summer Blue Apron partnered with grocery delivery service Misfits Market as a new fulfillment and delivery partner—theoretically leading to more regional options and faster delivery. Blue Apron has also changed its packaging to be more eco-friendly. As this goes fully online, we’ll retest Blue Apron to see the results. Note that beyond main proteins, allergens and dietary restrictions can be a little difficult to navigate on Blue Apron’s site.

With most plans at $7 a serving plus shipping, HelloFresh’s budget option, EveryPlate, remains the lowest-cost meal kit we've tested. And yet, this budget meal kit still uses the same proteins from local purveyors as HelloFresh’s much more expensive plan.

The secret is not skimping on calories or portions, but rather simplicity. EveryPlate’s meals derive their flavors from a small set of ingredients, and a single sauce or herb or spice mix—or perhaps some citrus cross-utilized as both juice and zest. Ingredients also tend to be cross-utilized quite a bit, which doubles as good cooking.

In a test this year, Korfhage made himself a fun Tex-Mex-style “quesabirria” with a chili-style spice paste; a hearty plate of pork chops with thyme and shallots; and a ground-turkey rice bowl that relied on ginger, garlic, and a mix of soy glaze and citric ponzu sauce.

HelloFresh’s low price makes it the only meal plan we’ve tried that wouldn’t require any particular adjustment in a grocery budget—at least for two people. And yet, this budget meal plan from HelloFresh somehow offers dinners that even your judgy mom-in-law would recognize as balanced.

This year’s pork chop meal, in particular, is the sort of meat-and-two supper you probably took for granted as a child. Pan-seared chops. Crispy-leafed Brussels sprouts baked in the oven. Fresh mashed potatoes. But what elevated the meal from basic staple to weekday extravagance was a thoughtful shallot-garlic-cream pan sauce, cooked with fresh thyme in the juices from the pork after finishing searing. The sauce was rich, complex, and aromatic.

The laundry list of staples you’re expected to have in your fridge for EveryPlate is a little longer than with more expensive kits, which includes flour and eggs. The box of ingredients is not as organized as HelloFresh’s. The menu is significantly shorter than HelloFresh’s at about 35 options, but still competitive with many meal delivery services. While the meals skew more basic than adventurous, EveryPlate impressed us with its simple, good taste and economy, at a price that wouldn’t stretch many household budgets.

WIRED reviewer Molly Higgins, who eats no meat, had a good experience with EveryPlate's shorter menu of vegetarian options this past spring and secured a week’s worth of food for less than she ends up spending on groceries in New York City. Sure, you may want to fill out a meal by adding your own rice to supplement Korean-inflected beef or pork with squash and carrots. (A great rice cooker makes this easy.) It still adds up to an economical meal, one you might appreciate on a Tuesday when you’re out of other ideas.

Best budget meal delivery service for large families: Marley Spoon’s lower-cost meal kit, Dinnerly, was WIRED’s former budget pick. It’s still a good, affordable pick for small families, if not quite as low-cost as EveryPlate. But for large families (six portions), it’s almost certainly the best budget option at just $6 a meal (for six servings), with a much larger recipe selection. Compared to Korfhage’s testing last year, Dinnerly’s recipes have grown lighter, more sophisticated, and maybe a little smarter: a za'atar chicken recipe here, a Mediterranean-inflected pork tenderloin or lemon-butter shrimp there. Often it feels like you’re getting our top-pick kit, Marley Spoon, at a discount. The difference shows up mostly in Dinnerly’s simpler ingredient list, usually featuring a single herb or spice accent rather than a complicated medley of flavors.

Of all the plant-based meal kits WIRED reviewer Molly Higgins has tried, Purple Carrot took the, uh, carrot. Purple Carrot offers boxes filled with premade, frozen, oven-ready meals—with adventurous dishes like mole enchiladas or an artichoke-stuffed ravioli that needed a little extra seasoning. But where Purple Carrot truly shines is with its fresh-made recipes. Unlike some meal kits that make you hunt through a box, the ingredients for each of Purple's recipes arrive well organized in individual plastic bags, accompanied by beautifully designed recipe cards. It’s also a flexible plan: Order what you’d like each week, with a minimum $50 order.

Purple’s recipes are devoted to the joy of cooking—sometimes to a fault, as with some hour-long recipe preps. (Advertised shorter prep times, as is often the way with meal kit recipes, were bald-faced lies.) Higgins, a natural cook, loved the time spent sautéeing, searing, mixing, and spicing. She especially enjoyed the results. A lovely Mediterranean rice salad with stuffed grape leaves took just a half hour to make, with some simple oven-roasted radishes, grape tomatoes, and zucchini.

The results are a parade of textures as tasty as one would expect at a restaurant, but with relatively little effort, Higgins says. Another meal of squash dumplings required boiling the squash and individually stuffing the dumplings, which took more than an hour. It ended up being Higgins’s favorite meal of the week, a case where effort meets reward. But the effort was nonetheless real. Purple Carrot bursts with texture, flavor, fun international ingredients, and the spirit of invention. If you’re the sort who likes to take time in the kitchen, there’s vegan gold at the end of that rainbow. Lately, Purple Carrot has also branched out into special meals, like a box designed in collaboration with fitness influencer Robin Arzón.

According to everyone we talked to who’s taking GLP-1 medication for weight loss or diabetes management, the drive to seek out delicious food just isn’t what it used to be. What has not changed is the utilitarian need to get enough protein, in a reasonable form, balanced against other health considerations. Does this not sound romantic? It mostly isn’t. But if hunger doesn’t move you like it used to, it’s even more important that your food tastes good enough to actually want to eat it.

Enter Tempo by Home Chef, a never-frozen, prepared meal service that offers about 30 meals each week. As many as two-thirds of these meals are marked “protein-packed,” with more than 30 grams of protein. And at least 10 of these are GLP-1-balanced, per a dietitian. What you’ll get: sufficient protein, not too many carbs, no other funny stuff.

Tempo’s proteins are well-managed, if unexciting. But the meals were easy, nutritious, balanced, and comforting, without relying on a pile of unhealthy sodium and carbs to be palatable. At $12 a meal, Tempo’s pricing is about par for the course for prepared meals.

WIRED reviewer Kat Merck, who tested the GLP-1-balanced meals, found the portions appropriate for those on the medication and appreciated that the meals offered her the proteins she needs to maintain muscle mass even while chemically un-hungry. Just note: You may end up with a lot of chicken and a lot of fish. Both Merck and Korfhage were happy to see the occasional chicken thigh, and not just breast. Thighs are better tasting, for the record.

For pure protein-loading and macro management, a no-nonsense option is Fuel Meals (see below), which has almost no sodium and more than 40 grams of protein per meal.

These meal kits former WIRED reviewer and lead tester Matthew Korhage tried didn’t make his final cut, but he still recommends them. Here are his thoughts on what he liked and what held them back.

Sunbasket (~$16 per serving): Sunbasket focuses heavily on fresh, organic ingredients and offers a whole lot of variety in its menus. Its recipes are attentive to saucing and to basic good cooking techniques such as deglazing. Like Hungryroot, it also offers breakfasts and snacks to supplement meal options with little extras such as coconut yogurt and sous-vide egg bites. The meal kit also lets you filter out allergen-containing items. WIRED reviewer Louryn Strampe loved the flexibility and add-ons. During a recent test, I enjoyed an excellent Greek chicken and orzo salad dish—and, wonder of wonders, the advertised prep time was actually the actual prep time (about 30 minutes). The focus on organic ingredients does make Sunbasket one of the more expensive meal kit options, and the annual Thanksgiving meal kit has remained a lovely and welcome extravagance at $200.

Gardencup ($11 to $15 a serving): Gardencup is a little like having the whole menu of Sweetgreen sent to your house or office, except in cylindrical form. Basically, you pick out premade salads, soups, and snacks, and they come in little cups—fresh, lovely, crisp, and ready to eat. I found it to be a great way to keep myself eating healthy when there’s no time to cook at the office (or the home office), which is typically when DoorDash or freezer-meal temptations run wild. It remains among the healthiest prepared food delivery options I know. I even tested Gardencup as a salad option during a cyclospora parasite scare, in part because I missed salad and because Gardencup could attest that it did not use lettuce from any of the suspected sources of the outbreak. The salads remained delicious and delivered a shocking amount of protein in the meat options. But variety of options is not a strong suit. A slackened focus on local sourcing, plus limited transparency on sourcing—Gardencup will disclose only that it sources its greens in the salad belt from Salinas, California, to Yuma, Arizona—has kept Gardencup in my honorable mentions.

Factor ($13 to $17 a serving): Factor is a prepared-meal delivery plan run by HelloFresh, with ready-to-eat meals that look a lot like TV dinners. But there’s a twist: The trays have never been frozen. They were made fresh in a commissary kitchen and shipped with cold packs, so they taste kind of like restaurant leftovers. Consistently, Factor ranks among the best-tasting prepared meal plans I’ve tested, especially when I shrug off the directions and reheat my meals in an air fryer instead of a microwave. Proteins and high-protein meals are a strong suit. Proteins maintain their texture quite well, especially a truffled filet mignon I couldn’t believe I microwaved. Still, some meals, especially carb-avoidant or keto meals, are oddly mushy, while meals centered on proteins and whole starches, like potatoes or rice, alongside veggies like green beans or Brussels sprouts, tended to fare quite well. So did stir-fry-style meals. My gluten-free colleague, Scott Gilbertson, wrote that he had the best luck with Factor’s Mexican fare. I do wish Factor would shed its reliance on the microwave: When I went off-script and used a Ninja Crispi air fryer or convection oven, I had much better results than with the nuker. But non-air-fryer ovens do not seem to offer the same improvement. Like many ready-to-eat meals, it’s a bit more expensive than the kits you cook yourself. Oh, and as a bonus? Factor has a really, truly delicious selection of nutrient-amped juice bottles and elderberry “shots.” I could pretty much subsist on their probiotic cucumber-pineapple green juice.

Fuel Meals ($14 to $15 a serving): Fuel Meals are a no-nonsense, no-fluff, no-added-ingredients, pure-protein-packed nutritive meal plan, with many meals paleo-friendly. Some of Fuel’s meals had as few as five ingredients, essentially the macronutrients plus a modicum of oil and salt. A large percentage of meals are marked gluten-free or dairy-free. Does all this mean less flavor? It can. The meals are also often not beautiful, dominated by a large and no-fuss serving of protein. But Fuel’s meal service offers an admirable focus, especially for those bulking up or watching carbs. Meals are substantive, usually topping 600 calories and 40 grams of protein, with no added sugar, fatty dairy, or carb-heavy filler. As a baby gym rat, I appreciate how clear the macros are, but some meals are high in sodium and often aren’t deficit-friendly.

Wildgrain ($13 to $17 per loaf or box of pastries): This is less a meal delivery service than a way to step up a home meal. Wildgrain is a monthly bread-box delivery: You receive par-baked bread and pastries from small bakeries across the country. This is pretty much the same process that likely happens at local restaurants when your warm bread basket comes out: It’s not quite as high-quality as you’d get directly from an artisan bakery, if you live in places with artisan bakeries. But it’s also as fresh as it gets, and I had a great experience testing the box in October 2025, especially the sourdough breads, which are perfect for large meals with houseguests. You finish baking at home, so you end up with ultra-fresh bread, biscuits, doughnuts, or scones that are still warm and crisp from the oven. A Wildgrain subscription arrives as a monthly box filled with four, six, or 12 items, ranging from a full sourdough loaf or fresh-made pasta to a pack of six doughnuts or four large croissants. Basically, you build your own box each month, choosing from healthy sourdough or pasta and decadent pastries.

Thistle ($12 to $15 per meal): A prior top pick for solo diners with individually prepared dishes that require little to no prep, Thistle is mostly a plant-based meal kit—but you can add sustainable meats to any otherwise vegan meal for $3. It’s also so local and seasonal that the West and East Coasts have different menus, and the whole middle of the country, except Chicago, gets none. (You can check your zip code here to see if you can get delivery.) Former WIRED reviewer Adrienne So used Thistle to get herself to eat more vegetables and thus avoid a life of rickets and/or scurvy. Portions are generous enough to split among meals, and in a nice turn for those who hate disposing of boxes, Thistle's drivers will pick up the cooler bag that held last week’s meal and replace it with a new one full of food. WIRED reviewer Molly Higgins’s favorite meals from Thistle were a whirlwind of textures, including a Mexican-inspired corn and poblano chile salad with adobo pinto beans and a chilled, lemongrass-accented rice noodle bowl that mixed spice, tang, crisp veggies, and deep umami from mushrooms and seaweed. She still dreams about it sometimes. Breakfasts are available at a lower cost, as low as $9.

Tovala (~$9 to $15 a serving): It’s not every day you get to try something that feels super new. Tovala offers perhaps the most ambitious ready-to-heat and prepared-meal delivery solution I’ve seen: the meal kits come with an oven! Unlike the sogginess of many prepared meals, Tovala's recipes come in little foil pans with recipes custom-designed for a small steam oven. The results are often delicious—as was the case during my testing with a sweet chili–glazed salmon with pickled veg and noodles—and the QR code scanning function makes each recipe seamless to cook. Stick with the meal plan for six weeks, and you get a quite affordable and powerful little convection oven, toaster, and steamer. A previous flaw was that Tovala only offered single-serve meals. But as of early 2026, at least four or five meals a week offer two to four servings, making the meal delivery service much more useful to families and couples—and much more economical per serving. But note that it’s also one of the most sodium-filled meal services I’ve tested.

Gobble ($17 a serving): Formerly the top pick for fast-cooked meals, Gobble previously wowed with speed-demon dishes that also offered interesting and worldly flavors. Indeed, the most recent test included Caribbean rondon, an Indonesian peanut curry stir-fry, and steak vierge. But while the flavors have stayed interesting, the focus on fast cooking appears to have waned since my colleague Louryn Strampe tested Gobble. Cook time estimates aren’t printed on the recipe cards, but meals took as long as 30 minutes. For now, Hungryroot has taken the fast-cooking crown. For small households, Gobble is also among the most expensive kits. At most portions, prices approach $17 a serving plus shipping.

Nurture Life ($6 to $7 per serving): Nurture Life is like a restaurant kids’ menu in ready-to-eat meal-kit form. We loved the idea behind this fresh-made, never-frozen delivery meal plan when we tested it a few years back: toddler- and slightly bigger kid-friendly meals, from mac and cheese to spaghetti and meatballs to myriad variations on the chicken nugget. Meal prices have dipped to reasonable levels lately, so it's likely due for a retest—and each plate includes vegetables alongside the greatest hits.

Veestro ($7 to $14 per serving): WIRED reviewer Louryn Strampe enjoyed Veestro as a ready-to-eat vegan option, with premade meals delivered fresh and freezable for extra meals on hand in a pinch. The service offers a number of filters for other dietary requirements, and satisfying taste and texture—which is not always a guarantee on ready-to-eat meals. Veestro has updated its menu and offerings since the last time WIRED tested, including several snacky soup offerings. The online menu interface, which lets you sort by ingredients and filter out unwanted ones, is among the best I’ve seen.

Splendid Spoon ($9 to $13 per serving): Splendid Spoon is a nutrition delivery kit that offers plant-based smoothies, soups, bowls, noodles, and shots. Everything here is natural and free of gluten or GMOs, including spaghetti and plant-based “meatballs.” WIRED reviewer Louryn Strampe has a big yen for the smoothies in particular ($10 apiece), but wasn’t quite prepared for the intensity of a lemon juice shot that comes as part of a five-pack of dense 3-ounce superfoods.

ModifyHealth’s Braised Beef (top) and Paprika Salmon (bottom)

ModifyHealth ($10 to $13 per serving): The idea behind ModifyHealth is that food can be medicine. Sometimes, I’ll admit, it tasted like it was designed purely for nutrition rather than flavor. ModifyHealth is a prepared meal delivery service tailored to people who need a heart-healthy, low-carb, or low-FODMAP diet to avoid dire digestive or health consequences. GLP-1 weight-loss plans are also available for people with diabetes or others. Gluten-free meals are attested to be made in an entirely gluten-free facility. The meals were simple, benign, and low-sodium but also sometimes a little soggy—a common problem with prepared meals. The plastic top of the packaging was also difficult to disengage from the base of each meal tray—a problem when the plastic is hot after a turn in the microwave. But here’s what I do like: The meals are carefully tailored with consultation from dietitians to help people for whom food can be a source of fear or pain. Many insurance plans cover an additional service: one-on-one dietitian consultation. ModifyHealth also offers a free consultation for those just trying to figure out which diet plan is right for them. For those with IBS in particular, this remains the most focused food plan I’ve seen.

Daily Harvest (prices vary): Daily Harvest is another ready-to-eat meal delivery service specializing in dietary restrictions: plant-based, plus gluten- and dairy-free. Smoothies are a highlight, as are harvest bowls, pastas, and grains. Calories are low. Ingredients are often inventive. The meal’s a lifesaver for the solo vegan eater without time to prep a meal, and WIRED’s Molly Higgins appreciated that the meals mostly relied on the natural flavors of the vegetables themselves, accented with flavors like curry and lemongrass. As with many frozen meals, however, texture wasn't a strong suit. The ready-to-blend smoothies are great, though.

Sakara Life ($30+ per serving): Sakara Life offers plant-based weekly menus in fresh, prepared portions, with greens, flavorful sauces, all-organic ingredients, and textural add-ons like seeds or berries. But it’s among the most expensive meal plans we’ve tested, and neither WIRED reviewer who tried it really cottoned to it. Louryn Strampe questioned the science behind detox and cleanse health claims, calling Sakara “egregiously expensive” and full of “bitter veggies and tart fruits.” Molly Higgins, meanwhile, said Sakara Life’s tinctures and metabolism supplements didn’t agree with her system, and that the mostly raw-food plan made her long for “human food.”

If you're talking raw materials by the pound—meat, zucchini, rice, noodles—meal kits will, of course, cost more than buying food at grocery stores. It's a service, after all, with added value above simple ingredient cost. Unless you've got quite expensive taste, you'll easily be able to make delicious meals at home for less than the $7 to $14 a serving that a meal kit will cost. That said, this doesn't necessarily mean that meal kits are expensive for what they offer. We conducted an experiment, trying to re-create four different meal-kit meals by going to my local grocery store—buying every ingredient provided by the meal kit. Turns out, if you don't have the right sauces and spices at home already, it's very difficult to re-create these tasty meals at grocery stores for less than they cost from a meal kit, in part because you'll most likely have to buy full containers of sauces and spices instead of preportioned ingredients,

So, is HelloFresh worth it compared to a grocery store? Caveats are in order: For staple ingredients and spices you'll use in multiple recipes, the grocery store is, of course, cheaper. Once you buy a container of paprika for an individual recipe, it will also be there for future recipes, whereas meal-kit spices are portioned for the meal. So the real answer is that meal kits can be a quite economical way of trying out a new recipe, or a new style of cooking, without larding up your fridge with condiments you won't use again. For ingredients you'd use less commonly, a meal kit can reduce waste and spoilage, and maybe even compete on price for an individual meal.

If your comparison point is takeout, well, the best meal delivery services on this list will almost certainly be cheaper and more nutritious. We've found that a meal kit in the fridge tends to be a good motivator to cook a nutritious meal—and thus can save both the money and the cholesterol.

To really save on cost, some people like to keep testing out the trial offers and discounts. Much like mattress-in-a-box companies, meal kit companies usually have a running promotion. Usually this takes the form of a trial discount price that'll drop your cost by half or more on the first box, in hopes you'll like the service enough to keep it on at full price.

A meal kit a few times a week ends up balancing out well: It's a motivating factor to eat better, and it means that when you do go to the grocery store, you can do so less mindlessly and more purposefully, given that you've got a few meals' worth of ingredients in the fridge. It's also had the side effect of broadening your culinary toolkit, keeping you from getting stuck in the same ruts.

That said, it's a set grocery expense and not necessarily a small one. Tossing or recycling cold packs and boxes gets tiring. And depending on the time of year, you may prefer shopping in person for what's seasonal and local, when produce is at its peak—an experience you don't get from a meal kit, or from grocery delivery, for that matter. If you're cooking for a bigger household, meal kits can also lose their utility quite quickly. A convenient option for two can become a much larger expense for a family of four or six.

Can I Pause a Meal Kit Service When I Go on Vacation?

Pretty much every meal kit tested has an option to pause subscriptions—and there's no particular limit to how often you can do this. The main thing is to be sure that you've canceled with enough lead time. Some services let you cancel or pause delivery as late as the Friday before a Monday delivery. HelloFresh requires five days' notice. Marley Spoon requires six days, based on one of our testers’ home locations. Some, like Hungryroot, may lock in next week's order as early as the previous Monday, depending on where you live. Read your terms of service, and act accordingly.

For those with more variable schedules, the service we'd probably recommend is Blue Apron, which changed its model last year and no longer locks in delivery each week. You'll have to remember to order a box each week, which can be done where I am with four days' notice.

Don't order too many meals per week: You know the old John Lennon line: Life is what happens when you're busy, out eating a random burrito, then thinking guiltily about the meal kit at home in your fridge. Aspirations are great, but don't order more meals than you're likely to make, or you'll be sad. Err on the side of caution. Order just enough meals per week that making yourself a recipe from your HelloFresh or Home Chef box is still a delight and a convenience and an overall boon to your life—not an obligation. If you're somewhat improvisational and impulsive, three meals a week is the sweet spot. The prospect of a few easy meals usually saves me from an impulse weeknight DoorDash.

Make room in your fridge: Meal kits take the place of a lot of grocery shopping. But they're also a lot of food, and a lot to keep organized. Clear a tall enough space in the fridge to put the whole meal kit box in, after pulling out the cold packs: This way, you're not left worrying about which groceries belong to the meal kit, and you won't lose any ingredients. You can just pull the whole box out when you want to make a meal. That said, some plans like Home Chef, HelloFresh, and Green Chef are very good at organizing each meal into its own separate bag. An added bonus from these more organized plans is that you'll be able to use less space in your fridge. Over time, this will matter.

Check the recipe cards to make sure you have everything you need to make a recipe: Most meal kits expect that you'll have certain staple ingredients in your home, usually including oil and butter. Recipes also have requirements for cookware. Check this before you start a recipe. Nothing worse than realizing you need a stick of butter on step 5, with carrots already browning in the oven.

Remember, you owe nothing to the recipe: Meal kit services hire recipe developers, and on the best meal kits, these chefs have spent a lot of time optimizing each recipe. But you owe them nothing—nothing! Add spices, change steps, season food when you want to season it. Meal kits can teach you a lot about how to make a good meal and shake you out of tired culinary routines. But it's your meal. Make it how you like. Have fun.

Chances are, whatever time of the year, a WIRED reviewer is testing a meal kit. We constantly cycle through various meal kits, testing and retesting each of our top picks at least once a year—and often multiple times, especially when each meal delivery service changes its format or expands to new offerings.

When possible, we order at least four meals a week from each meal delivery service. We prepare meals according to instructions and see how well they go. If we deviate from instructions out of horror or curiosity or a simple sense of culinary propriety, we note this and tell you why.

We check prep times against what's advertised (rarely an exercise in honesty!) and take note of any inconsistencies, vagueness, or frustration in the recipe card instructions. Convenience matters: If a meal kit needlessly recommends a nonstick pan, we like it less—especially if the recipe says to heat that pan before adding food, or later mentions browned fond. Nonstick isn’t cast iron or carbon; there’s no fond. If rice portions are too small, we'll flag that.

The quality and freshness of the produce are important, and the same goes for the meat. Where possible, we look into where the meat was sourced, and check on the reputation, safety, and standards of the meat suppliers. If a meal kit swears it's gluten-free, we verify this—calling certifying organizations where relevant.

We usually try to order as varied a menu as possible. We might check in on gluten-free meals, a seafood item, a vegetarian item, and white and dark meat items. We might also test a Midwestern-style hot dish or Southern meat-and-two alongside meals that draw (or attempt to draw) from global inspirations. Sometimes we test the same meal kit multiple times for different dietary needs, or call in multiple testers to do so. WIRED's vegan tester, Molly Higgins, often tests the same meal kit as lead tester Matthew Korfhate does, but with a different focus.
