Nearly 3 in 4 Gamers Are More Likely to Wait for Sales, Newzoo and G2A Find

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

A Montreal-based Game enthusiast who has been writing about the industry since 2013.

Price and gaming has taken on an entirely new meaning over the last year. The idea of waiting for a sale became a borderline meme over a decade ago, when Steam started running infamous deep-discount sales on digital games. A cursory search for “Gabe” or “Steam Sale” can be a long timetraveling road through years of in-jokes from the enthusiast community. These goofs and gags barely hold up all these years later, but they do harken back to a simpler time in the world of games pricing.

Today, conversations around discounts, sales, and pricing will launch just about anyone from the wide-world of gaming into a tirade about a whole host of criticism of the game industry: what do we do about physical games, do we even own digital games, is Mario Kart World worth X number of dollars. The list goes on. In the market of 2026, between the cost of individual games, the increasing price of hardware, live-service games, and monthly subscriptions, there are more factors than ever that influence player spending habits. It’s a fascinating time to look into how pricing dynamics are influencing the industry.

A report from Newzoo and G2A.COM found that 73% of surveyed players say they are more likely to wait for sales these days. The survey included 6,166 active PC or console players aged 18–55 across six countries, all of whom had purchased or played at least one AAA or AA game in the previous year. The study, “Game purchasing behavior in an era of choice” investigates how modern PC and console players are choosing to spend their hard-earned money in 2026. And while, yes, most players are happy to let the release date come and go so they can get a good deal on a given game, that doesn’t mean that players are entirely content to wait particularly long for a discount.

Wardogs lead developer Joe Brammer took to X (formerly Twitter) to offer another perspective on why studios are struggling to stay afloat.

Looking back at their purchases over the past three years, respondents reported buying just 42% of games before or during release month: 17% through pre-orders and 25% during the month itself. So how does this break down outside of the launch window?

In summary, of the purchases respondents recalled making over the past three years, 82% happened before release or within a game’s first year, including pre-orders. The remaining 18% happened more than a year after release. Crucially, it’s worth bearing in mind that this describes the timing of reported purchases. Still, it highlights how important it is for publishers keep a game top-of-mind after launch, and make sure players are able to take advantage of discounts and other offers post-launch.

Among players who buy beyond release month, the average expected wait for a discount worth considering is 4.9 months. And naturally, how deep the discount cuts plays a big role in purchasing behaviour.

On average, respondents consider a game reasonably priced at 29% off, and Newzoo notes that a 45% discount represents a ‘good deal.’ In their sample, audiences considered a 70% cut a potential red flag to a game’s quality, which is an interesting note that’s hard to unsee when you browse Steam.

One of the other notable data points from the study is that despite players being happy to wait for discounts, they also consider games a very high-value medium. In fact, 69% consider it better value than entertainment such as television, films and music, while 62% agree that game prices reflect rising development costs and complexity. Of course, that doesn’t mean audiences aren’t always still looking for a deal if they can take advantage of one.

“With nearly 60% of purchases occurring later than release month, publishers need long-term monetisation strategies that go well beyond the first week or month,” Newzoo research director Alexis Pamboris said in the press release.

Where people shop is another piece of the puzzle. Official digital storefronts (PSN, Steam, etc.) are expectedly dominant, but 24% of subjects in the study claim to use third-party marketplaces to purchase or research a title. G2A, which co-authored the report and runs its own marketplace, states that the alternate purchasing channels give players more choice. As stated by G2A chief business officer Katarzyna Jakubiec, “different purchasing channels serve different consumer needs.”

Most players, especially those with a Steam backlog, can probably relate to some of Newzoo and G2A’s findings. Waiting for a sale is a familiar part of deciding what to buy, from a box of cereal to a piping-hot copy of Grand Theft Auto VI. What’s interesting is how clearly that patience shows up in the numbers. Players still want new games, but many are willing to let release day pass until the price feels right. For publishers, the challenge is giving them a reason to finally move something from the wishlist to the library.
