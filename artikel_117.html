The US pastor who wants to see women's right to vote overturned

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Watch: Doug Wilson talks about DC outpost of church where Hegseth, other members of Trump administration worship

"Patriarchy is in accordance with the way God made the world." That is the view of Pastor Doug Wilson, a conservative minister who the US Secretary of Defence Pete Hegseth calls his mentor.

Speaking to the BBC from his base in Moscow, Idaho, Wilson explained his view that a society led by men is "a very great blessing". It's a perspective Christians who disagree with him say is based on a fundamentalist understanding of the Bible, which promotes the subjugation of women.

A self-described Christian nationalist, Wilson believes the US should be a theocracy, with gay sex criminalised, abortion outlawed, and people of other faiths unable to hold public office.

In the past he has delivered a sermon on "The Lost Virtue of Sexism," arguing that the Bible says women should be excluded from leadership of the church, and certain roles in civic affairs. And as part of his views on the division between the sexes, he would like the 19th Amendment of the US constitution, which granted women the right to vote, to be repealed, something his detractors say is anti-democratic.

The vast majority of Christians support women's right to vote, and dismiss his message as misogynistic and extreme.

Rev. Canon Dana Colley Corsello, the vicar at the Washington National Cathedral, rejects Wilson's understanding of what the Bible says about women's roles.

"In my mind, there is no biblical precedent or standing for what [Wilson has] proposed," she told the BBC.

"I'm married, I have a husband, I have children. My husband and I are complete equals in everything we do."

But while Wilson still represents a fringe perspective amongst Christians in the US, some of his views have been gaining support.

The denomination he co-founded, the Communion of Reformed Evangelical Churches (CREC), has around 150 churches in the US. It's a drop in the ocean compared to the 350,000 or so other Christian congregations the US. But Wilson says membership continues to rise.

His influence has grown in part because of his connection to Pete Hegseth. The Secretary of Defence is a "proud member" of a CREC affiliated church, a Pentagon spokesperson told the BBC, adding that the "secretary very much appreciates many of Mr. Wilson's writings and teachings".

Wilson says the two have "a warm relationship", although he stresses it is "largely a pastoral one, not a political one". Wilson has led prayer services at the Pentagon, including one in mid-November.

Joining Wilson on stage, Pete Hegseth said "thank you for your leadership, for your mentorship, for the things you've started, the truth you've told, your willingness to be bold."

Hegseth worships at the Washington, DC branch of Wilson's network of churches. And there are other members of the current administration that worship there too, according to Wilson.

Of all his views, it is his statements on women's suffrage that have garnered the most headlines.

Wilson favours "household voting", where the head of the home â usually a man â votes on behalf of their family. It's the system used for elections within his church, although the women in Wilson's family do vote in civic elections in order to counter votes of liberals, he says.

He says only in "odd" circumstances, such as divorce or bereavement or "where the husband was a quadriplegic," should women assume the role of head of household. Just 7% of votes in Wilson's church polity are cast by women.

When challenged by the BBC, he admitted it was a system that gave men "outsized influence".

Wilson says that repealing the 19th Amendment is "low" on the list of priorities for him and his congregation. But, he adds, "we do think it".

Julie Ingersoll, a professor of religious studies at the University of North Florida, says Wilson's beliefs are misogynistic and extreme - part of a world view that says women should be in complete submission to men in every part of their lives.

It is extremely unlikely that the 19th Amendment would be repealed - it would require either a proposal from two-thirds of the House and Senate or two-thirds of the state legislatures. Three quarters of states would then need to ratify it.

Still, says Ingersoll, the threat to women's rights should be taken seriously.

"It's not unlike the battle that they waged against Roe versus Wade," she says, referencing the Supreme Court decision that granted women the right to an abortion. It was overturned in 2022, after years of legal battles that restricted women's access to abortion.

"Thirty years ago, people would have said, oh, they're not gonna undo abortion rights.

"But they chipped away and they kept chipping away until they were able to overturn it. And I see this as directly parallel."

While Wilson says overturning the 19th Amendment could take a century or more, there are conservative Christians who are pushing for women to have their right to vote removed more immediately.

Last February, pastor Dale Partridge posted on Instagram saying "We will repeal the 19th Amendment within ten years".

Partridge, who has around 900k followers across social media, credits Wilson as being someone who "broke the ice" for "strong politics and theology".

Partridge has said that "the majority of women are not capable of responsible voting" because too many of them are liberal.

He also frequently collaborates on podcasts with another conservative pastor named Joel Webbon.

"Those guys have overshot by a long way," says Wilson of the two younger pastors. "They are disruptors and basically rage baiting."

So what might be driving those who support the effort?

Kelly Marino, an associate professor at Sacred Heart University who studies women's suffrage, says that the last decade has seen big wins for the women's rights movement, and "liberal political ideology" in general.

But, she argues, "any time where there was sort of this peak of feminism, there've been people who respond aggressively and try to push back".

Added to that are the high levels of uncertainty and conflict that have characterised the post-Covid period.

"I think there's been this craving for tradition and things that felt safe," Marino says.

That, she says, can be seen in the popularity of notions such as being a "tradwife" or "soft girl", who reject career goals in favour of traditionally female roles of wife and mother.

Marino says that while she doesn't think the constitution could be changed in the immediate future, the conversation around repealing the 19th Amendment is shaping the way people think about women's roles in general.

Canon Corsello, the Vicar at Washington Cathedral, sees the midterm election results as a repudiation of the push against women's rights.

"Cultural issues did not translate in this election", she says. "When people are preaching love, empathy, inclusion... that actually resonated with people".

Wilson's views have, however, been gaining ground beyond the US. The CREC has more than 30 churches internationally, including in Poland, Japan and Brazil.

The latter country has seen its own movement of right-wing influencers pressing for the removal of women's suffrage, with some leaning on religious language to make their point.

Wilson wants to continue expanding his brand of Christianity around the world, and has recently accepted a church in England into his network.

While CREC churches in each country have their own distinct characteristics, Wilson says their core beliefs are the same.

When it comes to the US, he says his ideal scenario would be for his congregations to "to practice what we believe in the church and for outsiders to look at that and envy it and say, man, that seems like a good system".
