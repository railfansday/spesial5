RTO means women risk doing the office housework all over again

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Women have already been disproportionately affected by return-to-office mandates, being expected to juggle work, childcare, household responsibilities, and doing the mental gymnastics to stay on top of it all. Now, old-fashioned assumptions about the unpaid work women are expected to take on in the office risk adding to that burden.

Usually, the conversation about mental load centers on household chores, such as who is responsible for doing the laundry, planning meals, and buying the groceries. And it’s women who are more likely to do more domestic labor even when both partners are employed full time. One 2024 study of 3,000 US parents found that mothers took primary responsibility for 71% of household tasks requiring mental effort, compared with 45% for fathers. 

It happens at work, too, where it’s known as “office housework.” These are the unpaid, often unrecognized tasks that keep an organization running but rarely help the people doing them advance their careers. Women therefore can end up doing more work without getting any credit for it.

And RTO might be making things worse.

A study of 1,091 professional women in the US found that 31% reported experiencing everyday gender discrimination when working on-site, compared with 17% when working remotely. This included performing tasks that weren’t their responsibility. The results suggest that being physically present can expose women to more everyday gender discrimination and create more opportunities for these assumptions to surface.

The problem is also evident in the UK. In a new survey of 2,000 working women and men by advocacy group Co-operatives UK, 22% of women said they’d been asked to do paperwork outside their role, 21% had been asked to clean or tidy the office, and 20% had been asked to make coffee for senior leaders. Overall, nearly half (49%) of women said they’d been asked more than once to do something outside their role, such as making tea or booking a meeting room.

Women have been highlighting the problem on social media, sharing their experiences and how they push back against the assumption that they’ll take on these tasks.

One content creator, Alex Schudy, said she had noticed colleagues using what she described as “weaponized incompetence” to try to get her to take notes in meetings, often by throwing her a compliment to disguise it. In her video on TikTok, she said she let the “silence linger” when one colleague suggested she do it because her handwriting was neat and she was “organized.” She politely declined, explaining that she needed to focus on presenting her part of the meeting. Someone else was designated the notetaker instead.

Another creator, Anna Kennedy, described in a video how much office housework she had come to realise women were doing — from keeping track of action items and chasing colleagues to making sure feedback was acted upon.

“Things that keep the machine moving fundamentally, but are not rewarded or seen in a performance review,” she said. “I’m never, I swear to God, never seeing men doing this kind of work.”

As more employees settle back into office life, many hope there have been changes for the better, particularly around work-life balance and flexibility. But some of the oldest workplace habits seem stubbornly resistant to change, and it’s time women stopped being expected to pick up the slack. 

Ultimately, they shouldn’t have to spend their working days doing the jobs that keep everyone else organized, only to be left behind as everyone else speeds ahead.
