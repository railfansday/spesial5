Warning For Families Traveling To California: New Booster Seat Law Covers Kids Through Age 15

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

I have a California trip coming up with my family. If I hadn’t seen this on Twitter, it wouldn’t have occurred to me that my 8-year-old daughter might need a booster for the ride from the airport. We live in Texas, where she’s aged out of the child seat requirement. Starting January 1, 2027, California will insist on measuring how the belt fits her in the car we actually wind up with from National, Avis or Uber. She could fit the belt in our car at home and need a booster in the SUV we get assigned to, but she’s already aged out of the California law that requires the rental company to have a child seat for her. We might have to bring one with us just in case. New Booster Seat Rules Go Into Effect January 1 Gavin Newsom signed Assembly Bill 435 in October 2025. Through the end of this year, a child 8 or older isn’t required to use a car seat or booster seat. Starting January 1, 2027 the rule becomes that a 15 year old requires a booster seat unless all five of these conditions are met: Their back is all the way against the vehicle seat. Their knees bend over the front edge of the cushion. The shoulder belt sits snugly across the middle of their chest and shoulder, clear of their neck. The lap belt sits as low as it can, touching their thighs. They can keep that position for the whole ride. An 8-year old who meets this can ride with just a belt, while a 15-year old who fails to meet one of these conditions needs a booster. Here’s a demonstration of the fit test: . What Happens At The Rental Counter And The Uber Pickup California’s rental car law requires agencies to have a compliant seat available for rent on request for children under 8. This new law doesn’t change that. I don’t count on rental companies having a car seat for my young son, even where it’s required and I’ve reserved it. If they’re out of seats, or the buckle’s broken in the one they have, my wife or I has to wait at the airport while the other one goes to Target. I wrote about a mother at the Atlanta airport who said the seats Avis rented her for her babies didn’t work. Avis says airport availability can be limited. At participating Hertz locations, the AAA rate includes a child seat with an advance reservation, subject to availability. ‘Availability’ is doing a lot of work here. Meanwhile, this also has a huge effect on rideshare (and taxis). They’re not required to have a booster. And if I don’t have one, I’m responsible for violations and the driver can decline the ride without it. Los Angeles and San Francisco do have Uber Car Seat. You reserve it in advance, pay a $10 surcharge and get one. It tops out at 49 inches and 65 pounds. A 50-inch child is too tall for the seat Uber supplies and can still need a booster for that ride. And a family with two kids who need restraints still has to supply the second one (or split up and take two Ubers). California is extreme here, although there are various outlier rules already. The U.K. requires boosters until age 12 or 135 centimeters while Germany is age 12 or 150 centimeters. Is This Really A Safety Issue? When a kid scoots forward because their knees don’t bend over a deep seat, the lap belt can ride up over their belly. The theory of the booster seat is that it helps put the force of a crash on the bones of the pelvis. In a study of 388 children and five vehicles, at least 90% of kids 4 feet 9 or taller fit the belt in the compact car and small SUV. Only 77% fit in the large SUV. A study of Washington state crash records found that over a 13 year period looking at 8-12 year olds, 11% in boosters were injured while 15% were injured wearing belts alone. Researchers calculated a 19% reduction in injury risk with a booster, while 0.3% in each group had an incapacitating injury. The study couldn’t establish a difference between booster and no booster for those serious outcomes. There’s a claim of a 45% reduction in injury risk but that’s from a study of 4- to 8-year olds, and even there it seems there’s no statistically significant reduction in injuries. Probably the best we can say is that there’s a real benefit in crash fatality rates for young children but there’s no data suggesting any benefit for 13- to 15-year-olds, and there’s not even any estimate for California preventing injuries with this new law. Who Wanted This Law? Would it surprise you to learn that this bill was supported by the Juvenile Products Manufacturers Association, which is a lobby group for companies that sell booser seats? I’m not sure how a police officer at a traffic stop is supposed to judge whether a child can hold the belt fit position throughout the entire ride. The base fine for violations is $100 the first time and $250 thereafter plus assessments. And 60% of the fines collected go to local health departments to help buy more booster seats. In Car Seats as Contraception, researchers estimate that women with two children still covered by their state’s car seat mandate were 0.73 percentage points less likely to have a third child that year. Typical nonsensical California overregulation. A true nanny state we have here.

Now there’s going to be a teen tourism lobby, e.g. theme parks, in California.

Nonsensical? It really is a safety issue. If a child does not fit the seat properly, they can be seriously injured. It is based on evidence-based recommendations and bioengineering recommendations. There is literature from Washington State (see below) showing a 19% reduction in overall injury rates, and another study showing a 10% absolute reduction in serious injury when boosters are used. Studies on this question are somewhat limited because the research is technically challenging, but what we do have supports a significant benefit.

Car manufacturers state that a child must be able to sit with their knees bent over the front of the seat, their back firmly against the backrest, and the seatbelt across the chest, not the neck. If they can’t, they need a booster. Plenty of kids who are 8 years old are too short or small to be safely restrained by the car’s restraint system, and a booster improves their chances of making it through a collision without serious injury. My own daughter (I live in Texas) was in a booster until she was nearly 12 because of her height.

Other states have similar, albeit with some variation, laws: Colorado, Washington, Louisiana and Minnesota. This law simply ensures people use their cars safely.

While many laws, regardless of jurisdiction, are silly, this one is based on best practices to improve children’s safety.

So, overall, while I know this is inconvenient, it is far from an overreach by a “nanny state.” A big failing of this law, it seems, is that rental car agencies are not required to support their customer’s compliance by providing a booster for older children.

I jerryrig seatbelt positions so they do not chafe against my neck. I would imagine everyone who is smaller shares a similar experience.
