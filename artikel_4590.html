As I approach 90, balance changes are occurring. As time passes, several brain and body changes make walking less stable.

Falls are the leading cause of injury for adults aged 65 and older, so everyone 65+ should develop increased falling awareness. Let’s think through it together.

As we pass 65, there is a natural decline in the specific senses that send signals to the brain for mental and physical coordination. They begin to decrease. Walking requires brain-triggered nerve signals to and from muscles and joints, so as one ages, the brain’s ability to process these nerve signals diminishes. For example, with aging, gait slows, walking changes, and it becomes harder for the brain to sense body position in the surrounding space.

With aging, the brain's sensorimotor networks slowly diminish. It becomes more difficult to respond to changes in surroundings, and physical instability increases. By 90, using my own case as an example, step length has shortened, and my movements are less smooth than in the past. Muscle weakness in calves and hips compounds my brain changes.

With age, the brain's ability to integrate sensory changes makes walking less stable, increasing the risk of a fall. For some, the changes are subtle; for others, more pronounced. However, changes are normal, and they are real.

This happens to each of us at some point; the rate of decline is different for each of us. Yet, as noted, the CDC has identified 65 as a milestone year for particular attention.

In my case, I have fallen twice, and each fall caused a fracture. Statistically, women fall more than men, and a first fall doubles the risk of a second. So, my advice is to be realistic and make a plan to help avoid falling.

Common sense says that willingness to think about falling in the future is important as we age, and the best kind of falls are those we avoid by staying alert and paying attention.
