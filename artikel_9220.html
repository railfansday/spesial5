A primary beneficiary is the person or entity you designate to receive an asset — such as a life insurance policy, 401(k), IRA, or bank account — when you die. If you name multiple primary beneficiaries, they split the asset according to the percentages you specify. A contingent beneficiary (also called a secondary or backup beneficiary) receives the asset only if all primary beneficiaries are unable to inherit, either because they predeceased you, disclaimed the inheritance, or cannot be located.

This layered system exists because life is unpredictable. If you name your spouse as primary beneficiary on your life insurance and you both die in the same accident, the contingent beneficiary ensures the proceeds go where you intended rather than into your general estate where they become subject to probate.

You can name individuals, trusts, charities, or even your estate as a beneficiary. However, naming your estate is generally a poor choice because it forces the asset through probate, exposing it to creditors, delays, and court costs. The entire purpose of a beneficiary designation is to transfer assets outside of probate — naming your estate defeats that advantage.

When naming multiple beneficiaries, you must choose a distribution method that determines what happens if one beneficiary dies before you. This choice is critical and frequently misunderstood.

Per stirpes (Latin for "by the branch") means each branch of your family tree receives its designated share. If your son predeceases you, his share passes down to his children (your grandchildren) in equal portions. The family branch stays intact.

Per capita (Latin for "by the head") means assets are divided equally among surviving beneficiaries only. If your son predeceases you, his share is redistributed among your other surviving beneficiaries — his children receive nothing.

For most families, per stirpes is the safer default because it prevents accidental disinheritance of grandchildren. If you want surviving beneficiaries to absorb the shares of those who predecease you, per capita achieves that — but make sure that outcome aligns with your actual wishes.

Some financial institutions also offer per capita at each generation, which distributes equally among the nearest living generation first, then passes remaining shares equally to the next generation. This hybrid approach is less common but available at some custodians.

Beneficiary designations are deceptively simple forms that create binding legal consequences. The most expensive mistakes happen not from malice but from neglect.

In most states, a divorce does not automatically remove your ex-spouse from beneficiary designations on retirement accounts or life insurance policies. Federal law (ERISA) governs employer-sponsored plans like 401(k)s, and ERISA does not recognize state divorce revocation statutes. If you divorce and never update your 401(k) beneficiary, your ex-spouse can legally claim the entire balance — even if your will leaves everything to your new spouse.

Minor children cannot legally receive life insurance proceeds or retirement account distributions. If you name a 5-year-old as beneficiary, the insurance company will not release funds until a court appoints a guardian or conservator of the child's property. This process costs $2,000 to $5,000 in legal fees, takes months, and requires ongoing court reporting. Instead, name a trust for the benefit of the child or use your state's Uniform Transfers to Minors Act (UTMA) custodial designation.

If you leave the beneficiary field blank or all named beneficiaries predecease you without a contingent, the asset typically defaults to your estate. This triggers probate, adds 6 to 18 months of delay, and exposes the asset to estate creditors. Always name at least one contingent beneficiary.

Your beneficiary designations and your will can contradict each other. The beneficiary designation always wins. If your will says your daughter gets your IRA but the IRA beneficiary form still names your brother, your brother gets the IRA. Review both documents together annually.

Set a recurring calendar reminder to review every beneficiary designation once per year — ideally alongside your tax preparation. Pull statements from your 401(k), IRA, life insurance, bank accounts, and brokerage accounts. Verify that primary and contingent designations match your current wishes. This 30-minute annual review prevents the kind of outdated-beneficiary disasters that lead to costly litigation and assets going to unintended recipients.

For state-law-governed accounts like IRAs and life insurance, some states have revocation-upon-divorce statutes — but not all. For ERISA-governed accounts like employer 401(k)s, federal law does not recognize state divorce revocation. You must manually update the beneficiary form after divorce to be safe.

Naming a trust provides control over how and when assets are distributed — especially useful for minor children, spendthrift beneficiaries, or blended families. However, trusts named as IRA beneficiaries must meet specific IRS requirements to qualify for stretch distributions under the SECURE Act. Consult an estate planning attorney before naming a trust as retirement account beneficiary.

A POD designation on a bank account functions exactly like a beneficiary designation. The named person receives the account balance upon your death without going through probate. You maintain full control of the account during your lifetime and can change the POD beneficiary at any time.

Primary and contingent beneficiary designations are among the most powerful — and most overlooked — estate planning tools. They transfer assets instantly upon death, bypass probate entirely, and override your will in every state. The 30 minutes it takes to review and update your designations after every major life event can save your family months of legal proceedings, thousands in attorney fees, and the heartbreak of assets going to unintended recipients.
