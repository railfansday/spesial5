Good morning and happy Tuesday! Today’s newsletter includes good news for international students, bad news for the makers of signs proclaiming “zero tolerance” for violence in hospitals, and mixed news for children with rare brain disorders. Read on!

No branch of the NIH has faced more scrutiny than the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases. For nearly four decades, the institute was run by Anthony Fauci; it’s since become a major focus of Republicans looking to re-litigate the Covid pandemic.

The institute has been without a permanent director since Jeanne Marrazzo was forced out in 2025 for objecting to grant terminations. Now officials have announced that acting director John Powers III will take the lead role.

Powers is a physician-scientist who’s worked at NIAID in several clinical research positions. He has run the institute in an acting capacity since June. Earlier this year, he co-authored a paper with NIH director Jay Bhattacharya that argued NIAID should move away from its focus on HIV and pandemic preparedness, instead prioritizing broader categories of infectious disease and immunology.

Read more from STAT’s Anil Oza, including Powers’ response to questions about health secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr.’s position on vaccines.

One of the key tenets of Alcoholics Anonymous is surrender to a higher power. There, and in other organizations, faith-based addiction recovery can serve an important role in supporting people trying to stay sober.

But experts warn the Trump administration’s full-throated embrace of these programs will result in federal grants to centers that don’t always provide evidence-based treatment. As STAT’s Theresa Gaffney explains, some faith-based addiction centers don’t allow participants to use drugs like methadone, which is approved by the Food and Drug Administration for opioid use disorder.

Kennedy also has expressed support for unconventional (and scientifically dubious) approaches to treatment, including “wellness farms,” where people reside in nature and engage in manual labor. Read more about the tricky balance experts hope Kennedy can strike.

Last year, researchers thought they’d had a breakthrough — they finally engineered a virus to ferry gene therapy to the brain. Experts were hopeful this innovation could cure Alzheimer’s, Parkinson’s and slew of rare diseases.

Then the first person to undergo treatment with this new technique — a 3 year old — died of brain swelling 65 hours later, sending fear and uncertainty through the industry. Nearly a year later, researchers have unveiled how the process can be shored up for the future, with ideas for new tests and trials to ensure safety. Read more here.

A proposal by the Trump administration to cap the amount of time foreign students and scholars can spend in the country has been struck down in court. In a decision Monday, a federal judge said the rule was “arbitrary” and “capricious,” writing that the government did not sufficiently consider the ramifications of the new policy.

In his story on the ruling, Anil Oza breaks down why this matters: “International researchers make up nearly a quarter of life science Ph.D. graduates and around 60% of postdoctoral researchers. About 40% of American Nobel laureates in the sciences are immigrants.” Read more.

Violence against doctors and nurses has jumped in recent years, due to an ugly swirl of understaffing, overcrowding, and pandemic-era stress that hasn’t quite faded. In response, many hospitals have started to hang signs declaring “zero tolerance” for violence or other bad behavior from patients.

The problem, according to three professors, is that these signs don’t actually help protect workers. Instead, they signal to families that care is something that can be withheld. The signs often include long lists about things that patients shouldn’t do, without a similar standards for nurses and doctors.

“The content of the signs sends clear messages to patients and families about their tenuous status and value in the institution,” they write. Read more.
