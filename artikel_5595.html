The Fine Print on Our National Bet

Tanggal: 2026-11-15

— As online sports betting becomes routine, its mental health costs are becoming harder to dismiss

Nothing is more absurd than a direct-to-consumer pharmaceutical commercial, or an online sports betting commercial.

At least the pharmaceutical ad has the decency to be honest about its absurdity. A smiling couple strolls along a beach as a voice-over races through a pharmacologic inventory of calamity: nausea, insomnia, rash, suicidal thoughts, spontaneous combustion -- well, perhaps not that last one, but give the announcer time. Across the bottom of the screen, safety information scrolls at the speed of a stock ticker during a market crash. The viewer is left with a vague sense that the medication may cure a minor inconvenience while replacing it with several more interesting problems.

Sports-betting commercials have adopted the same visual grammar, with a stranger moral logic. The smiling young men on screen aren't walking on a beach; they are shouting at televisions, tapping phones, and celebrating a three-point shot as if they had personally discovered penicillin. Then, below the spectacle: "If you or someone you know has a gambling problem, call the National Problem Gambling Helpline."

It is the public health equivalent of putting a fire extinguisher on a flamethrower and calling it responsible.

The disclaimer acknowledges what the advertisement otherwise works hard to conceal: sports gambling is not merely entertainment. It is a commercial product designed to encourage repeated use, normalize risk, and convert attention into revenue. The app is in the pocket. The odds are refreshed constantly. The promotion is personalized. The game no longer ends when the final whistle blows because there is always another game, another parlay, another "risk-free" offer that is neither risk-free nor especially free.

We have taken an activity once associated with casinos, racetracks, and back rooms and made it as frictionless as ordering dinner. Then we seem surprised that some people order too much.

A recent JAMA Psychiatry study adds empirical support to what clinicians have observed in less formal settings. The authors found that legalization of online sports betting was associated with increases in the probability of antidepressant and anti-anxiety medication use, as well as increased outpatient visits for depression and anxiety among adults with employer-sponsored insurance.

Most people who bet on football will not need psychotherapy. But why have we built a system that increases exposure to a potentially addictive activity, then treats the resulting distress as an unfortunate side effect rather than a predictable cost of doing business? The answer, of course, is money. It is almost always money, dressed in a blazer and labeled innovation.

Sports leagues, media companies, betting platforms, and states profit from tax revenues. Influencers profit from sponsorships, affiliate links, paid subscriptions, and online engagement when they market supposedly certain betting picks as "locks," even when their followers lose money on those bets. Meanwhile, the gambler absorbs the losses, and families may absorb the debt, secrecy, conflict, and despair. Clinicians and behavioral health systems absorb the aftermath. This arrangement is often sold as individual freedom, a useful phrase whenever someone else is making the money.

This type of "freedom" is not unique to gambling. Debates over cannabis legalization, drug decriminalization, alcohol policy, tobacco, and other forms of consumption repeatedly reveal the same ethical tension. However, legalizing or decriminalizing illicit drugs does not reduce overall use; in many cases, it is associated with increased consumption or with use that remains relatively stable, depending on the substance and region. The relevant question is not simply, "Should this be legal?" It is, "What system of incentives are we creating, and who bears the consequences?"

Government often acts as though revenue is a moral anesthetic. People describe gambling tax proceeds, whether from betting or the lottery, as funding for schools, roads, or public services, as if the money's destination negates the circumstances of its collection. A dollar is a dollar, except when it is earned through the slow accumulation of losses among people least able to afford them. We would do well to ask whether a state budget should rely, even in part, on gambling-tax revenue when that revenue depends on bettors losing money.

Elected officials like revenue, voters like convenience, and professional sports has become an entertainment-industrial complex that is too large, too beloved, and too well sponsored to criticize without seeming humorless. Nobody wants to be the person who interrupts the playoff party to ask whether the game has become a delivery system for gambling products.

But we can ask: What is the role of clinicians in a system that profits from predictable harm? Should we attack the entertainment-industrial complex as we once did the medical-industrial complex -- and lose? I don't think clinicians should become scolds, prohibitionists, or cultural referees. We should become witnesses. We see the patient who cannot sleep because of debt. We see the spouse who discovers hidden accounts. We see anxiety worsened by financial instability, depression complicated by shame, and crises that appear in the chart as insomnia, panic, alcohol misuse, or suicidal thinking rather than "sports betting." We recognize that an individual need not show up with a casino chip to prove they are addicted.

Our voices should be present in legislative hearings, hospital community-benefit plans, professional society statements, public health campaigns, and medical education. Screening for gambling-related harm should become more routine where clinically appropriate, especially when patients present with abrupt financial distress, escalating anxiety, depression, substance use, or relationship conflict. We should also insist that prevention not be outsourced to a phone number scrolling beneath a commercial that makes restraint look unfashionable.

The National Problem Gambling Helpline is necessary, but it should not serve as the ethical fig leaf for an industry that profits when people keep betting.

A society reveals its values not only by what it prohibits but also by what it promotes, subsidizes, glamorizes, and calls entertainment. If we are going to place a sportsbook on every smartphone, we should at least be honest about the wager. The house may not always win in the short term, but it has already convinced us to keep playing.
