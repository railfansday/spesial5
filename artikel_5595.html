Our Patients May Already Be Using Peptides. Counseling Matters.

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

In October, HHS issued a policy under which licensed pharmacies can prepare compounded medications using six unapproved peptides: BPC-157, Epitalon, KPV, MOTS-c, Semax, and TB-500. Social media has been abuzz ever since, as these products are increasingly marketed for injury recovery, metabolism, cognitive performance, and longevity. However, there's one major problem: these peptides have not been deemed safe or effective and are not approved by FDA. Under the interim policy, HHS will refrain from enforcing certain restrictions while the FDA evaluates their safety and suitability for compounding.

The policy follows the July 2026 meeting of the FDA's Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee. Just before the meeting, FDA scientists recommended against adding any of the seven peptides under consideration, citing insufficient evidence for safety and effectiveness and concerns about peptide identity, characterization, and quality. Despite these concerns, the committee voted to recommend six of the seven peptides be added to the list of substances eligible for compounding -- without undergoing the premarket safety, efficacy, and quality review required for approved drugs. This creates a different regulatory pathway from the one followed by the many other FDA-approved peptide-based therapeutics, and the composition of the committee raised questions about potential conflicts of interest.

News of the announcement quickly spread, prompting some to wrongly interpret the decision as a sign that FDA approved these peptides. To be clear, this policy bypasses premarket safety, efficacy, and quality review required under FDA.

Drawing on our respective backgrounds in research and clinical medicine, we recently came together to discuss these concerns. Our complementary perspectives underscore an important challenge for physicians: understanding what the scientific evidence tells us about these substances while navigating the questions and misconceptions patients may bring into the exam room.

Peptide-based medicines are already used to treat a range of conditions, such as diabetes and weight management. But all peptide-based medications are different, and the success of approved treatments (such as certain GLP-1s) doesn't mean other peptides are safe or effective. Many of these "wellness" substances are deceptively marketed as "natural alternatives," but that doesn't make them natural or harmless, as they are still pharmaceuticals with unknown human dosing, safety, or efficacy data to guide prescribing.

Consider BPC-157, widely promoted for injury recovery and tissue repair. It has been marketed as a "natural" peptide found in our bodies, yet it has never actually been identified in a human body. Much of the enthusiasm stems from laboratory and animal research suggesting potential health benefits. But these preliminary findings cannot establish whether BPC-157 improves recovery in humans or what risks it may pose. Researchers have raised questions about its ability to simulate the growth of new blood vessels, a process that can support tumor growth and spreading. Encouraging people to take BPC-157 when we are lacking critical preclinical and clinical data is both irresponsible and potentially dangerous.

Even claims of clinical research can create a false sense of reassurance. Consider a TB-500 study listed on ClinicalTrials.gov that explicitly described itself as "fictional" up until November 7, 2026. (Note we're currently investigating why a trial with a "fictional" designation would be published on ClinicalTrials.gov.) There are, in fact, no human clinical trials on TB-500. There are some studies of the parent peptide thymosin beta-4 but you cannot extrapolate biology from a peptide fragment to its parent peptide.

The same scrutiny must apply to safety. FDA scientists have flagged serious concerns as have the American Medical Association, the Economic Cycle Research Institute, and the Institute for Safe Medication Practices. Drug development involves more than identifying a potentially promising biological effect. Researchers must identify the target of the molecule, understand the biological pathways it promotes, establish appropriate dosing, investigate toxicity, and understand the consequences of repeated exposure all in preclinical studies before even considering human testing, let alone concluding that it is safe and effective in humans.

Some potential harms may not become apparent for months or years. Without adequate long-term studies or any tracking systems for those using these drugs now, or planning to obtain them through compounding, even identifying a connection between a later health problem and earlier peptide exposure may prove difficult.

FDA-approved treatments backed by rigorous evidence should always be the standard for patient care. Physicians have a responsibility to thoroughly counsel patients about the risks and limitations of unapproved treatments, including peptides, ensuring they have the information needed to make informed decisions. For patients interested in experimental treatments, legitimate clinical trials offer a pathway under appropriate safeguards.

But clinicians must also recognize that patients may already be using these substances, often obtained outside their regular medical care and, in some cases, without any physician involvement. During routine exams and medication reviews, physicians should ask directly about peptides and other unapproved drugs, particularly those obtained through wellness clinics or online providers. Understanding what patients are taking, where they obtained it, and at what doses is essential to identifying potential adverse effects and drug interactions.

For patients already using these products, clinicians should document their use, evaluate possible adverse effects, and report suspected reactions through FDA's MedWatch program. For BPC-157 and many other experimental peptides, there are no adequate studies establishing how these products interact with commonly prescribed medications, and their long-term risks remain largely unknown, particularly for older patients managing multiple chronic conditions.

There are also plausible, largely unstudied concerns about peptides that stimulate growth-related pathways or promote new blood vessel formation in patients with active or prior cancer. Compounded and gray-market products introduce further uncertainty, including contamination, inconsistent potency, and, particularly in illicit products, substituted ingredients. Clinicians may not even know what substance a patient has taken, making potential drug interactions harder to identify. Until better evidence is available, clinicians should routinely ask about peptide use, review these products alongside each patient's medical conditions and medications, and report suspected adverse reactions to FDA's MedWatch, even when causality is uncertain.

The HHS policy has brought renewed attention to a much larger problem: the growing promotion and use of unapproved and untested products before their benefits and risks are understood. Physicians should not recommend these unknown substances as established treatments or allow the politicization of policy to be mistaken for scientific endorsement. Our responsibility is to uphold the standards of evidence that protect patients, not normalize the use of treatments that have yet to meet them.
