Follow the Smart Money: This Billionaire Tech Investor Has Been Loading Up On Shares of These 3 AI Chip Stocks

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Chase Coleman's Tiger Global has been adding to its stakes in Nvidia, Cerebras, and Intel.

By Geoffrey Seiler – Nov 24, 2026 at 10:28AM EST

Chase Coleman's Tiger Global is one of the preeminent technology-focused hedge funds in the world, with many investors who have worked under him eventually starting their own successful funds. In the third quarter, the billionaire tech investor was busy trimming many of his larger stakes. However, one area he was buying was artificial intelligence (AI) semiconductor stocks, increasing his positions in Nvidia (NVDA -8.73%), Cerebras (CBRS -12.08%), and Intel (INTC -10.17%).

Let's look at what Coleman may find attractive in these AI stocks.

At the end of Q2, Nvidia was only Tiger Global's third-largest holding, but three months later, after Coleman bought more shares, Nvidia was its largest holding, representing over 13.5% of its portfolio. While the stock has been on a roller-coaster ride in the past two months, there is still a lot to like about it.

Nvidia continues to see incredible growth despite its size, with its revenue more than doubling last quarter and the company forecasting 75% revenue growth next fiscal year. Despite that, the stock trades at a forward P/E of just 10 times fiscal 2028 (ending January 2028) analyst estimates.

While Nvidia is facing intensifying competition in the data center segment, it still holds distinct advantages. The company has transformed itself into a complete AI infrastructure player and, as such, is starting to capture a larger share of the revenue pie. With its Grace Hopper architecture, it was getting about $18 billion per gigawatt of capacity, while with Grace Blackwell, it captured around $25 billion per gigawatt. With the addition of more chips to its platforms, such as language processing units (LPUs) for inference, and an expanded software stack, it is now getting $40 billion per gigawatt with Vera Rubin. Meanwhile, Nvidia has locked up important fab capacity and access to scarce high bandwidth memory (HBM) that will help it maintain its leadership in the data center segment.

Cerebras is one of the most interesting AI chip stocks, in my view, and may have the most upside potential of any. The company's chips are the fastest for inference, but due to their unusually large size and their unique power management and cooling requirements, they are also expensive. This has made its systems more of a niche solution, and the company often rents out capacity on them to clients.

However, Cerebras has a major commitment from OpenAI and has secured deals with Amazon, Oracle, and Microsoft. The company has also teamed up with Advanced Micro Devices for a cheaper disaggregated solution that uses Cerebras systems for the ultra-fast decode phase of inference and AMD's GPUs for the pre-fill phase. Analysts at Morgan Stanley have projected that the total addressable market for fast inference will surge from around $4 billion this year to $550 billion by 2030. That's a huge opportunity. Tiger Management added about 1.45 million shares to its position in Q3, increasing its share count by about 48%.

Intel is the third chip stock that Coleman bought in Q3. His timing wasn't the best, and investors can now grab the stock at a much lower price than Tiger Global was paying, as the company issued weak guidance in late October amid a weakening PC market. High memory prices have pushed up computer costs, decreasing demand, and Intel still has considerable ties to the PC market.

Intel's central processing unit (CPU) server opportunity and the potential to turn around its money-losing foundry business are what likely attracted Coleman to the stock. Data center CPU demand is currently through the roof, given the emergence of AI agents, which require far more of them than prior iterations of AI. In servers built for AI model training, the ratio of GPUs to CPUs is 8 to 1, but for those being used to handle inference workloads — the actual use of the models — the ratio dropped to 4 to 1. For agentic AI, which involves less of the heavy parallel processing that GPUs provide, and more computing tasks that must be handled sequentially (which CPUs are built for), the ratio is heading toward 1 to 1.

Meanwhile, Intel has spent a boatload of money over the last several years building new fabs in the hopes of competing more effectively with the world’s leading foundry, Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing. Intel hit a major milestone earlier this month, announcing that it had signed its first external customer deal for its 18A chip fabrication technology. If Intel's foundry operation can become a viable alternative to TSMC for high-end chipmakers, that would be a huge boost to the company.
