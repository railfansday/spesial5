What does space radiation do to the brain’s immune cells?

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

Simulated space radiation altered gene activity in the brain’s immune cells, with different effects in male and female mice.

Earth’s atmosphere and magnetic field shield people on the ground from much of the radiation that astronauts encounter on missions to the Moon or Mars.

Galactic cosmic rays are highly energetic particles found in space. According to NOAA, they carry enough energy to damage spacecraft electronics.

NASA wants to know what they do to human brains.

A University of Rochester lab has spent more than 15 years on this question. Professor of Neuroscience M. Kerry O’Banion, MD, PhD, has led the work.

In earlier research, his team found that simulated space radiation accelerated Alzheimer’s-related changes in mice.

“We know cognitive changes from exposure to radiation in space are a real risk,” O’Banion said.

“It’s important we understand how brain cells are changed. Knowing which mechanisms are impacted and how may help the development of countermeasures to protect or lessen an astronaut’s exposure to space radiation.”

In mice, simulated space radiation altered gene activity in the brain’s immune cells differently in males and females.

The researchers detected no short-term behavioral or cognitive changes in either sex. The team is still looking at the long-term effects.

“Additional studies examining other time points and effects in other experimental systems will be required to understand this further,” O’Banion said.

The experiments ran at the NASA Space Radiation Laboratory at Brookhaven National Laboratory on Long Island. Researchers used simulated space radiation, produced by particle accelerators.

At the time, Brookhaven was the only facility in the world with that technology. There are now two.

Neuroscience graduate student Mark Osabutey led the project. He used single-cell RNA sequencing to examine nearly 16,000 microglia.

These are the brain’s immune cells. They came from male and female mice exposed to simulated galactic cosmic radiation.

“Surprisingly, despite clear molecular changes, we found no widespread inflammatory activation or measurable radiation-associated behavioral impairment at the time we examined,” Osabutey said.

He suggested the molecular responses may happen earlier. Or they may not be tied to detectable functional impairment at all.

Male and female microglia showed distinct responses.

In the male brain, changes were linked to cellular stress adaptation and metabolism. In the female brain, microglia showed broader reductions in immune signaling pathways.

It’s unclear whether these changes are protective, harmful, or temporary.

Researchers did find one shared change. Both sexes had more of a protein called FKBP5 in two brain regions, the cortex and the dentate gyrus. FKBP5 helps regulate how cells respond to stress.

“We now need to understand whether FKBP5 plays a protective or harmful role, and how different radiation exposures and other spaceflight stressors influence long-term brain health,” Osabutey explained.

“Our findings highlight the importance of considering biological sex when assessing neurological risks during spaceflight and identify potential pathways for developing protective strategies.”

Osabutey has worked on NASA-funded research since joining the O’Banion lab.

In 2023, he received a predoctoral grant augmentation award from NASA. This grant funded his study of population-level changes in microglia after galactic cosmic radiation exposure.

“Receiving NASA support has allowed me to contribute to research addressing questions of national and international importance,” Osabutey said.

“As a physician-scientist in training, I could not have asked for a better environment in which to train, and the experience has taught me how to pursue meaningful scientific questions through rigorous basic/translational investigation and collaboration, skills that I will carry forward in my future efforts to better understand brain injury and neurological disease.”

“Graduate students and postdoctoral fellows have been an essential part of our NASA research effort,” O’Banion added.

“It’s comprised of a large team that includes the outstanding organizational skills of our long-time senior research technician, Lee Trojanczyk, as well as support personnel at Brookhaven National Labs. Over time, nearly a dozen University of Rochester trainees have contributed to this work.”

Long trips to deep space are not just an engineering problem. They are a biology problem.

This study adds an important detail: the same dose of radiation may not land the same way in every brain.

Male and female brains showed different molecular responses. That doesn’t settle whether one sex is more vulnerable.

It does suggest that future risk assessments should account for sex. It also points to a specific protein, FKBP5, worth watching.

The researchers detected no behavioral or cognitive impairment in the mice at the time they tested them. That leaves open whether effects emerge later.

For now, the finding is a step in understanding how space travel changes the brain. It won’t keep astronauts home, but it may help keep them safe when they go.

The study is published in the journal Scientific Reports.
