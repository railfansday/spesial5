Scientists find a new way to study some of the ocean’s most elusive microbes

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

A new study sheds light on the largely unexplored microbial world beneath the ocean and its potential ecological significance.

Most of the microbes living on the deep seafloor have never grown in a lab dish. On the way to the surface, a sample loses the high pressure and steady cold its microbes are adapted to.

The sudden change can kill the microbes before anyone gets to study them.

A robotic system called DISCUSS keeps both conditions in place, from the seafloor all the way to the lab bench where single microbes are picked out. With it, a team grew a bacterium that barely grows at ordinary air pressure.

The work was led by Jing-Chun Feng of Guangdong University of Technology in China. Zhengguang Liu, a researcher in the University of Manchester Department of Chemical Engineering, was one of the paper’s corresponding authors.

“Scientists have long struggled to grow many microorganisms found in nature under laboratory conditions, limiting our understanding of how they live and function,” said Liu.

The deep sea is home to a vast diversity of microbes, many of which have adapted to extreme pressure and near-freezing temperatures.

Some deep-sea microbes even produce enzymes and other molecules.

The molecules that deep-sea microbes create can be useful for medicines, new materials, food and energy.

Scientists say that less than one percent of deep-sea microbes can be grown with normal lab techniques.

“Deep-sea microbes are particularly fascinating because they have evolved to survive extreme pressure, cold and unusual chemical conditions,” Liu said.

The testing of this technology was carried out in the South China Sea at a depth of 4,300 feet (1,300 meters) in a cold seep location.

Cold seeps are areas of the seafloor where methane and other compounds emerge at temperatures similar to those of the surrounding water.

At this depth, the water pressure would be 1,900 pounds per square inch (13 megapascals), which is about 130 times the atmospheric pressure at sea level, and water temperature would be 39°F (4°C).

Before carrying out any sampling procedure, sensors detected the levels of methane, carbon dioxide, oxygen, acidity, temperature, and pressure on the seafloor.

After evaluation by the software, only those locations with scores related to methanotrophic microbes were sampled.

The sealed samplers kept more than 98% of the seafloor pressure on the way up.

In the lab, the system built a virtual copy of the seafloor conditions from the sensor readings. Researchers call that kind of copy a digital twin, and the machine used it as the target to hold.

When a sample moved from one chamber to the next, one pump pushed while another pulled at a matched rate. That kept the pressure from jumping during the handoff.

To grow one kind of microbe on its own, a microbiologist spreads cells thinly across a soft gel. Each cell that survives grows into a small dot called a colony.

DISCUSS performs this task inside a sealed, high‑pressure chamber. A robot arm spreads the cells with a needle, and a camera watches through a sapphire window.

A force sensor in the arm lets it press lightly enough not to tear the gel. In tests, the camera software recognized colonies with 94.6% accuracy.

The needle hit its target within 0.002 inches of the intended spot. Between tasks, the system sterilized the needle using light, keeping the pressure seal intact.

“From an engineering perspective, what excites me most is how intelligent sensing, modeling and automation can help us explore biological systems that were previously difficult to study,” said Liu.

The team recovered 34 microbes and grew each one on its own.

In 11 of them, a gene that scientists use to tell microbes apart differed enough from known reference strains that they may belong to groups not yet described.

The team took a close look at two of the 34. Both turned out to be strains of known species.

One belongs to Pseudomonas songnenensis, and the other, named HP_SR10, belongs to Bacillus cereus.

To test how much each strain depended on pressure, the researchers grew both for 11 days at five pressures, from ordinary air pressure to a little above the seafloor’s.

The Pseudomonas strain grew about as well at ordinary air pressure as it did at seafloor pressure. HP_SR10 didn’t. It grew best at the same pressure as the seep it came from, and poorly at ordinary air pressure.

The authors wrote that this strain can’t be recovered with conventional methods, which grow microbes at surface pressure.

The system also kept a whole community of microbes alive and working for 180 days.

At its center was a partnership between methane-eating archaea, single-celled microbes distinct from bacteria, and bacteria that use sulfate from seawater.

Pumps fed the community methane and nutrients and flushed out waste. That kept sulfide, a byproduct that holds these microbes back at high levels, below 4 millimoles per liter.

Around day 110, both partners became noticeably more common.

The authors wrote that communities like this one typically collapse in closed lab containers with no fresh supply.

Carbon made near the ocean surface can sink to the deep ocean, and scientists still don’t fully understand how deep-sea microbes change it.

Growing a wider range of these microbes under conditions close to home could help scientists study that process.

Only two of the 34 microbes were studied in detail. The study doesn’t describe what the 11 possibly new microbes do, or whether any make useful molecules.

All of the samples also came from a single seep. The authors noted that the design could be adapted for deeper water and for very hot places such as hydrothermal vents.

Next, the team plans to run many plates at once under different conditions and to track dissolved chemicals more closely as they change.

The researchers also plan to automate growing microbes in liquid instead of on gel.

The full study was published in the journal Nature Sensors.
