The Real Problem With Memecoins Is the Way They’re Traded

Memecoins don't need to be banned, tamed or ignored but simply need a new trading structure or vehicle, one that does not feel flimsy, to say the least. In this regard, vaults have become a tool that can give this asset class a future beyond their current casino like existence.

Proof that the category is growing came in September, when a trader turned $2,972 into just over $2.1 million in under 12 hours, although most of it is still held as the MEME token and subject to volatility. The MEME token, an asset on the controversial Robinhood Chain that rallied more than 3,100x in a day, was born as a jab at AMC chief executive Adam Aron, who had called Robinhood's tokenized AMC shares "contemptible."

From the outside looking in, the entire episode looked absurd, but at the end of the day it took place on rails built by a publicly listed US broker, and that detail in itself is hard to ignore.

It is no secret that, so far, most professional money entering digital assets has moved through crypto ETFs (particularly Bitcoin-related ones). CoinMarketCap's head of research, Alice Liu, summed up this trend, noting:

"Institutions are buying Bitcoin through ETFs. Retail is trading everything else through a phone."

And while such regulated doorways into memecoins also exist, they have barely been used. For instance, US Dogecoin ETFs spent August logging inflows of just hundreds of thousands of dollars as opposed to the billions flowing into Bitcoin, Ethereum, Solana and XRP.

In fairness, the caution isn't misplaced given that major meme tokens routinely fall double digits in value over the course of a few days. Plus, there is also the threat of manipulation, with CertiK tallying $488 million in September losses alone ($387 million of which was tied to exploits) in relation to crypto assets.

Lastly, retail isn't only trading memecoins onchain, but trading stocks there too, as tokenized equities topped 4 billion in assets on chain in September. To put it quite simply, the line between "crypto" and "traditional" assets is fading on the same venues, in the same wallets, and often in the same liquidity pools.

Amidst all of this, crypto vaults have come in and put forth a unique proposition, acting as smart contract wrappers around a defined strategy such that what goes in, how they're weighted, where the yield comes from, and when capital can be withdrawn are all set out upfront. The model is already being used to bring private credit, tokenized equities and commodities onchain, and there is no obvious reason it can't carry riskier assets as well. In practice, that could take a few forms, such as:

Position limits, audits and redemption terms can be written into the product itself rather than left to a trader's discipline, though each one does a different job.

For instance, position limits cap how much of the vault can sit in any single asset, so with rebalancing a memecoin sleeve set at, say, 5% stays at 5% whether the token doubles or crashes.  An audit, in this regard, checks the smart contract code for bugs and known attack paths. It lowers the odds of an exploit but doesn't guarantee one won't happen, and it says nothing about whether the assets inside are a good bet.

Redemption terms set out how and when depositors can take money out, and they only mean something if they match how easily the underlying assets can actually be sold. A vault that promises instant withdrawals while holding thinly traded memecoins is making a promise the market may not let it keep. The USP (of such a framework) is such that some of the biggest names in finance have also arrived at the same conclusion.

Most recently, Ondo Finance launched Ondo Intelligent Portfolios, a product line that packages BlackRock-designed model strategies into single transferable tokens. The first three (BLKHIon, BLKDIGon and BLKGRWon) target high income, diversified growth and high growth, with constituents, weights and every rebalance visible onchain and rebalancing handled at the smart contract level.

DeFi-native builders too have approached the same idea but with a twist. Valdora, for instance, runs vaults and liquid staking on ZIGChain, curated by risk profile across private credit, quant strategies and tokenized equities. The firm’s setup illustrates what investors should expect from any vault, i.e. how each strategy works, where its yield comes from and what the risks are (all laid out before a deposit is made, with the underlying infrastructure going through independent security audits).

None of this, however, means that pension funds need to start buying dog-themed tokens but it does entail the start of a new, more mature conversation. Memecoins are, among other things, a real-time read on retail risk appetite. On Sept. 10, for example, the sector traded roughly 12% of its $28.8 billion market cap in a single day. That's one snapshot rather than a daily norm, and heavy trading alone doesn't make a market fit for institutions, but it does show how much retail money is moving through the space.

Furthermore, given that tokenized stocks are already doing billions on Uniswap (with tokenized gold trading at a pace that dwarfed last year's metrics easily), the pieces seem to already be onchain and in motion. In all of this, what's still missing is a structure sturdy enough to hold them responsibly, and a vault only fits that bill if it gets a few things right.

For starters, it has to cap memecoin exposure at a size the rest of the portfolio can absorb, publish its holdings and rebalances onchain, set withdrawal terms that match how quickly its assets can really be sold, and spell out the risks in plain language before anyone deposits. Audited code is part of that, not the whole of it. Even a vault that ticks every box won't make memecoins safe, but it can make the risk visible, sized and honest, which is more than the current casino setup offers.
