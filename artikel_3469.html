We Officially Ranked Every James Bond Actor

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

James Bond is one of cinema’s great characters, and one that will never fall out of fashion. Although other franchises have attempted to invent or expand upon new continuities, the Bond series has adapted to the times by reframing its story to fit the cultural, social, and political trends of the era. Bond is unabashed entertainment representing pure heroism, but he’s drawn from a darker character imagined by Ian Fleming as a ruthless assassin who wears his license to kill as a badge of honor. Each actor to take on the role has had to contend with the pressures of being a smoldering hero, a fantasy of charisma, a loyal patriot, and a womanizing charmer, resulting in very different performances over time.

The speculation as to who could be the next Bond always illuminates conversation, as it's more than just a role that can be recycled in the vein of Batman, Spider-Man, or even more classical characters like Robin Hood or King Arthur. The Bond films screen for the Royal Family and become historical artifacts that attract attention and analysis for years on end, making it all the more important to make the right choice in selecting the person to play him. Denis Villeneuve is currently underway on casting the next actor to take on the role, meaning that either Robert Pattinson, Nicholas Hoult, Josh O’Connor, Callum Turner, or Jack Lowden will have massive shoes to fill. Each Bond actor has value in one way or another, but time has painted a fairly evident hierarchy.

It’s often forgotten that there were two Casino Royale adaptations before the 2006 film that launched the most recent iteration of the series. The first was a television episode of an anthology show from the ‘50s, but the first cinematic version was in a feature length parody film released by Warner Bros. as one of two non-EON entries in the series. Although it’s a spoof that gathers an entire cast to play different versions of Bond, the main star is David Niven, who had ironically enough been Fleming’s first choice during the casting of Dr. No.

Niven is a charismatic actor who can deliver the one-liners needed to play a slick version of Bond, but he’s trapped within an insufferable parody film that wasn’t all that funny in 1967, and has aged even worse today. Bond becomes a caricature and Niven is encouraged to be a stickler for British protocol in a manner that doesn’t fit with the edgier persona that Fleming had created. It is nonetheless interesting to consider what Niven could have done with the role had he been given the chance to make a “proper” Bond film; the best idea of what his 007 would have looked like is in the World War II classic The Guns of Navarone, where he plays a sharp British commando whose bravery and intelligence feel quite Bond-ian.

On Her Majesty’s Secret Service is one of the best Bond films ever made, and it would contend Skyfall and Goldfinger to be the top pick if it weren’t for a mediocre performance by George Lazenby. Lazenby does a decent enough job, all things considered; he has virtually no acting experience before being cast, and attracted a fair amount of backlash for doing something different with the character. On Her Majesty’s Secret Service plays Bond as being a more wholesome, romantic character with a wry sense of whimsy, and Lazenby both gives a charming performance without ever feeling totally believable as a secret agent.

Lazenby lacked the physical prowess needed to be an action hero and can come off as rather stiff in some of the dialogue exchanges, although to be fair there’s no telling what more he could have done with the part had he agreed to star in Diamonds Are Forever. That Lazenby refused to return is partially due to the pressure he was facing from EON and the fan backlash, but it also might simply be because he never felt completely comfortable in the part. While he’s not necessarily a great Bond, the chemistry Lazenby has with Diana Rigg makes for one of cinema’s greatest love stories.

Roger Moore is a divisive Bond who is widely misunderstood by some contemporary Bond viewers who don’t understand that his version of the character was intentionally campy and completely in-on-the-joke. Moore was an older version of Bond who developed a more bemused perspective on the potentially world-ending scenarios he often found himself in, and the fact that his emotions rarely rise above mild concern is part of the charm of his films. With that being said, each Bond can only be judged by the totality of their time as the character, and Moore’s output in the role was quite mixed.

Live and Let Die is a fantastic debut that showed Moore could be suave and swanky in a way that was different from Connery, as he felt both decidedly modern and classically gentlemanly. The Spy Who Loved Me is a similarly great film that offers Moore the chance to show the most range because it unlocks a darker side of the character; a moment where he reveals a shocking secret to Barbara Bach’s Agent XXX is among the greatest moments of acting in the franchise’s entire history. Yet, Moore often felt secondary to the spectacle in some of the sillier installments, and even admitted by the end of his run that he was far too old to be playing the character.

Timothy Dalton is perhaps the hardest Bond to judge, even more so than Lazenby, because his run as the character was cut short prematurely. While On Her Majesty’s Secret Service felt like a complete statement about Bond as a character, Dalton had only really started to encompass the role by the time that his two entries in the series were up. The Living Daylights is a good movie, but the script was clearly styled in Moore’s sensibilities and didn’t totally unlock the more mature take that Dalton had. License to Kill was a swing in the completely different direction that turned Bond into a vengeance-seeking vigilante, and it’s not until the end of the film that he returns to MI6 to become a more classical version of the character.

Dalton is nonetheless one of the few Bond actors to show an actual interest in Fleming’s books and clearly knew the text well enough to purposefully distinguish himself from his cinematic predecessors.

Pierce Brosnan is in a similar place to Moore in his run as Bond being somewhat uneven, but the difference is that he was locked into his performance the entire time. Brosnan’s debut in Goldeneye didn’t just save the franchise as a whole, but split the difference in terms of what every film since should aim for; it retained all the classical elements of the series, but felt hip and modern, showing self-awareness without being too cheeky. Brosnan was believable as a killer and a master of spycraft, but he was also the most handsome and charming actor to play Bond as a romantic foil.

Brosnan’s strength in Bond is made clear by how much he elevates his films, as he was able to successfully bring a darker component to The World Is Not Enough in spite of a ridiculous script with some truly terrible lines. Although Brosnan has admitted to being disappointed that he did not get to make a fifth film, Amazon and MGM would make a lot of fans happy if they brought him back for a one-off legacy sequel.

Sean Connery is one of the greatest movie stars of all-time and is responsible for defining what Bond became. There is no question that he is the reason that the character remains a popular culture titan and not an obscure character from Cold War-era spy novels, as Connery created a larger-than-life hero who still had one foot planted in reality. Connery had the dexterity as an actor to lean into the Hitchcockian elements of From Russia With Love or the spectacular audacity of Goldfinger, but he was so naturally compelling that he turned more routine Bond adventures like Thunderball and You Only Live Twice into great entertainment.

Connery found something very unique within Bond that cut through some of the character’s inherent contradictions. He’s an unabashed servant of the crown who doesn’t feel prickly, a womanizer who isn’t toxic, and a killer who can still deliver a one-liner. Bond is more than just an action hero, and Connery is still who most people think of when they imagine the character.

Daniel Craig is responsible for revamping Bond for the modern age and turning him into a truly complex character while also delivering the most authentic interpretation of what Fleming imagined him to be. Casino Royale wasn’t just the technically impressive and gritty installment in the series, but a truly harrowing drama that saw Craig making a vulnerable character stooped in tragedy. Skyfall placed Craig’s Bond in a place where he had to advocate for his own existence, arguing on behalf of the franchise itself, and it was so convincing that it became the highest-grossing entry in the series to date with over $1 billion.

Craig retained the dry sensibilities of Connery while showing the intensity of Dalton, and also took Brosnan’s tips of doing extensive training to ensure that his stunts would look real. One of the reasons the next era of the franchise faces so much pressure is the intimidation of following Craig.
