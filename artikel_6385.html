Hubble spots a ghostly trail that may lead back to two shattered worlds

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Hubble’s first visible-light view of the Cat’s Tail near Beta Pictoris offers clues to a possible collision between two large icy worlds.

Near the young star Beta Pictoris, a long streak of dust reflects so little starlight that it had escaped detection in visible light, the kind our eyes can see.

Now the Hubble Space Telescope has caught it for the first time. Its faintness fits dark, carbon-rich dust left over from a crash between two worlds at least about the size of Pluto’s moon Charon.

Beta Pictoris is about 65 light-years from Earth and roughly 18.5 million years old.

It’s still surrounded by rubble left over from building its planets, and astronomers nicknamed the streak the Cat’s Tail when they first spotted it in 2024.

The James Webb Space Telescope first revealed the tail in infrared light emitted by its warm dust.

Arin Avsar, a doctoral student at the Lunar and Planetary Laboratory of the University of Arizona, led the new study.

“The Cat’s Tail is thought to be the aftermath of a collision between large bodies – think something like Pluto,” Avsar said.

“Oddly, this feature had previously only been seen with the James Webb Space Telescope, which detects infrared light, but remained invisible at shorter wavelengths, which has puzzled the community for a while.”

Avsar’s team stacked Hubble images taken in six rounds between 2012 and 2025. The result is the deepest Hubble picture of the system so far, but the signal is still weak.

Near its tip, the tail is barely brighter than the surrounding dust.

In visible light, the Cat’s Tail is roughly 200 to 1,000 times fainter than it looks to Webb in infrared.

“We think the Cat’s Tail has eluded our observations in visible light until now because of what it’s made of,” Avsar said.

“It consists almost entirely of what we call organic refractory material, which is heavily carbon-based. These long chains of carbon atoms absorb visible light, so they’re quite black.”

The team tested that idea with computer models of dust grains made from different mixtures. Rocky grains are made of the same kinds of minerals found in Earth’s crust.

A tail made mostly of them would have been about 100 to 10,000 times brighter in visible light than what Hubble recorded.

Dust that was 85% to 99% carbon-rich material by weight, with a little rock mixed in, matched best. Webb’s earlier infrared measurements had found the same thing.

Avsar’s team also checked that no single round of observations had created the tail by mistake. They left out one round at a time and rebuilt the image.

The tail showed up every time, though it got noisier near the tip.

The dust isn’t only dark. The models also showed that each grain is extremely porous, with somewhere between 55% and 93% of its volume taken up by empty space.

Dark, fluffy dust grains this tiny are an odd combination.

“How you can get dust grains that small that are as porous as we see here, no one really understands, and that’s part of why we study this feature,” Avsar said.

What turned a young planetary body into fluffy dust is still a matter of speculation, Avsar added.

Kevin Wagner, a co-author and a NASA Sagan Postdoctoral Fellow at the university’s Steward Observatory, offered one possibility.

“It’s possible that the velocities involved in the collisions were so high that the carbon grains vaporized, turned into gas, and then recondensed as they cooled, and that’s how they became so porous,” Wagner said.

The Webb team that found the tail in 2024 estimated that the crash behind it happened roughly 100 to 200 years ago. That team also calculated how much carbon-rich dust the tail holds.

Avsar’s team then asked how big the colliding bodies had to be to supply that much carbon.

Using measurements of a comet, the dwarf planet Haumea, and the dust that drifts between stars, they settled on a range of 14% to 38% carbon-rich material by weight.

Depending on the assumed carbon content, the team calculated a minimum diameter of roughly 750 to 1,000 miles (1,200 to 1,600 kilometers) for each body.

That’s about the size of Charon or Makemake, two icy worlds in the Kuiper Belt, the region beyond Neptune where Pluto orbits.

These are minimum sizes, rather than estimates of the bodies’ most likely sizes.

The team assumed the debris was equally dense at every size, but the tail’s fluffy grains are much less dense than solid chunks, so the real bodies were likely bigger.

The carbon most likely came from deep inside them.

Pluto’s reddish surface is coated with similar material, but that coating holds far too little of it to build a tail like this one.

Webb’s pictures are flat, like any photo, so they can’t show how far the tail stretches toward or away from us.

Astronomers measure distances like this in astronomical units, the average distance between Earth and the sun, about 93 million miles (150 million kilometers).

Visible light helps here. Dust scatters visible light differently depending on the angle between the star, the dust, and the telescope.

The heat it gives off looks the same from every angle. Comparing the two telescopes’ views let Avsar’s team estimate that angle.

“We find that instead of extending for 1,000 astronomical units behind the star, the Cat’s Tail may extend straight sideways relative to us, and there is no third dimension to it,” Avsar said.

“It’s essentially in the same plane of the sky as the star and possibly extends a little bit behind it.”

The team’s models can’t settle it completely. They lean slightly toward a tail that extends somewhat behind the star. But they can’t rule out a tail in the same plane as the star, as seen from Earth.

A faint cloud of fine dust, called a halo, surrounds the disk above and below its flat middle.

With the new images, Hubble traced the disk out to about 500 astronomical units from the star, roughly 46 billion miles (75 billion kilometers), for the first time.

Telescopes on the ground had mapped that halo in 1995, but Hubble’s view is about 20 times sharper.

The sharper images show the halo is lopsided and warped. On one side it bulges outward above the disk, and on the other side it bulges below.

The disk’s main layer has a similar warp, and earlier studies traced it to Beta Pictoris b, a giant planet whose orbit is tilted compared with the disk.

Avsar’s team suggested that the same planet, or possibly the recently found planet Beta Pictoris d, may shape the halo too.

“This gives us another line of evidence into how giant planets influence comets through their immense gravitational force,” Avsar said.

“It’s a testament of how much these massive planets really influence their surrounding dust and icy bodies out to hundreds of astronomical units.”

Confirming that will take computer models that include all three planets and the push of starlight on small grains, the authors wrote.

By one estimate, icy moons and dwarf planets in our solar system are about 45% to 70% rock by weight. Much of their carbon-rich material is mixed into their rocky cores.

A crash that smashed two of them apart should have released plenty of rock along with the carbon, yet the Cat’s Tail has almost none.

Starlight may have separated the debris. It pushes carbon-rich grains harder than rocky ones, so the two kinds could have ended up in different places.

The rock might be in other dusty clumps that Webb has already seen in the disk.

Hubble also picked up a faint smudge near a second, tilted disk around the star, too weak to tell whether it’s real dust or a flaw in the image.

Deeper Hubble observations planned for the system should settle that.

With more detailed models of how these grains scatter light, astronomers could also tell where the tail really points.

The full study was published in the journal The Astrophysical Journal Letters.
