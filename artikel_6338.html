Physicists break through a major speed barrier in quantum computing

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Physicists achieved exceptionally fast and accurate quantum operations by correcting an overlooked source of error in superconducting qubits.

Inside a quantum computer, speed and accuracy are supposed to pull against each other.

Flip a quantum bit faster and you have to hit it harder, and a hard hit tends to knock it into states it was never meant to occupy.

A team in Germany has now flipped a qubit in 6.8 nanoseconds and got it right more than 99.98% of the time.

Such operations usually take several tens of nanoseconds, and light travels only about 6.5 feet (2 meters) in 6.8 of them.

The gain came from an error that earlier correction methods had overlooked, and it was measured on a single isolated qubit, so larger processors are still untested.

A qubit holds its fragile quantum state for only a limited time.

Every basic operation, or gate, has to fit inside that window, and complex algorithms and error correction need a great many of them.

So the shorter each gate, the more useful work a quantum computer can do before its qubits forget.

The catch is physics. A gate on a superconducting qubit is a pulse of microwaves, and a shorter pulse has to be stronger to do the same job.

Physicists at Forschungszentrum Jülich, working with colleagues at RWTH Aachen University and the University of Cologne, set out to find how far that could be pushed.

Yuan Gao and Rami Barends ran the experiments at the Institute for Functional Quantum Systems, and Felix Motzoi’s group at the Institute for Quantum Control supplied the theory.

The type of superconducting qubit used in this study isn’t a true two-state system. It has a ladder of energy levels, and a computer uses only the bottom two.

Think of an elevator meant to run between the ground floor and the first floor. Push it too hard and it overshoots.

Engineers have known since 2009 that a strong pulse can carry a qubit one level too far.

The standard fix, a pulse-shaping method called DRAG, was built to stop that. Motzoi was a co-author of the 2009 paper that introduced it.

What the new work showed is that a strong, fast pulse can also lift the qubit two levels at once.

Those double jumps come from absorbing two packets of microwave energy together, and at very short gate times they turned out to be the main source of error.

They’re also hard to undo, the authors wrote, because a qubit stuck on a high level can stay there through many steps and corrupt error correction.

The team’s solution, called R2D, applies the DRAG correction twice to suppress the microwave frequencies that cause the qubit to jump two energy levels at once.

The researchers adjusted just three parameters, testing the qubit repeatedly to find the optimal settings.

The team also needed a new way to measure the result, because leakage this small is hard to see.

So they built a sequence that repeats a gate hundreds of times with a twist of phase between repeats, which makes the stray population pile up instead of washing out.

With it, they could measure leakage down to about one part in a million, where the standard method starts to blur.

The new sequence gave a clear leakage signal within 400 operations, where the standard method needed 800 gates for a weaker one.

Compared with the standard DRAG correction on its own, the new pulse shape cut leakage by more than a factor of 20 at the same gate length.

The researchers ran the test on a single transmon qubit, built at Jülich’s Helmholtz Nano Facility and run at 5.323 gigahertz.

They tried pulses from 6.6 to 9 nanoseconds long, with 1 nanosecond of padding after each to absorb distortion.

Shorter pulses made the math unstable, and longer ones overflowed the memory of the control electronics.

At 6.8 nanoseconds, the two basic gates, a full flip of the qubit and a half flip, came out with accuracies of 99.981% and 99.989%.

The chance of the qubit escaping to a higher level stayed below 0.002% per gate. So the researchers achieved exceptionally high accuracy even at this short gate time.

Pushed to 6.6 nanoseconds, leakage to the third level took over, which the team’s simulation had predicted.

For the full flip, control error also grew as pulses got shorter, which the team attributed to distortion of the microwave pulse on its way in.

Most of the remaining error came from decoherence, the qubit losing its state on its own, at about one part in 10,000 per gate.

The authors called the pulse strength, measured against the spacing of the qubit’s levels, a record for single-qubit gates on superconducting hardware.

Everything here happened on one qubit sitting by itself. Whether the pulse shape works as well inside a larger processor, and during gates that act on two qubits at once, hasn’t been tested.

The authors wrote that the same approach should carry over to two-qubit gates and to other kinds of qubits.

The next step is the hardware. With longer-lived qubits and a tighter handle on pulse distortion, the team wrote, the method could reach gates that are right 99.999% of the time.

That is one more nine than this experiment managed.

The full study was published in the journal Nature Communications.
