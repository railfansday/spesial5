The 7 Best Electric Stoves to Upgrade Your Kitchen

These expert-picked models cook cleaner than gas, and offer features like convection and air fry.

Electric stoves burn cleaner and more efficiently than gas—in fact, per the Department of Energy, they’re about three times more energy-efficient than their gas counterparts. They’re also more straightforward to install—no gas line needed—and often feature flat, easy-to-clean, glass-ceramic cooktops. Plus, their ovens preheat quickly and provide even and consistent heat, which is ideal for anyone who loves to bake. No wonder electric stoves have become the go-to for the majority of households in the U.S.

Electric stoves and ranges come in a wide range of sizes and styles, and many models even have high-tech features such as convection baking, air frying with more room for food than countertop models, and Wi-Fi connectivity. These stoves make a great addition to any kitchen as they’re versatile, reliable, and often quite affordable, too. Plus, some even qualify for a tax rebate.

We rounded up the best of the best, so when you're ready to replace your existing electric stove—or switch from gas—you’ll know just what you need.

Size can be a significant limiting factor, so measure your stove space first. The standard width for ranges is 30 inches, but if you’re outfitting an apartment kitchen, tiny home, or RV, you’ll want to focus on the few narrower models available.

Next, choose your style: Freestanding stoves have finished sides and back guards, allowing them to stand alone. Slide-in models are designed to sit between cabinets for a seamless look; their stovetops typically sit flush to the back wall, which may require a backsplash update. Drop-in ranges need a custom cabinet base for installation.

Most electric cooktops feature smooth surfaces with heating elements housed beneath tempered glass or ceramic, though traditional coil-style options still exist and tend to be much cheaper. Smooth-top stoves are easier to clean, while coil burners are simpler to replace if one fails.

Standard electric ranges typically come with five burners (four on narrower models). One is often a warming burner, useful for timing multiple dishes. Some models also offer bridge elements—also referred to as "synchronized burners"—which link two burners, handy for griddles or oversized pots. If you frequently boil water, look for one with an express boil burner, designed to heat up faster.

Conventional ovens rely on stationary heating elements—one at the bottom for baking, one at the top for broiling—leading to natural heat rise and possible temperature variations. Standard convection improves on this by adding a fan to circulate hot air for more even cooking. European convection (also called “true” convection) goes a step further, with a third heating element behind the fan for uniform heat distribution across multiple racks.

For everyday cooking, conventional or standard convection works fine. But if you want more precise, even baking and roasting, true convection is worth considering. Be prepared for a slight learning curve if switching to convection, as most recipes are designed for conventional ovens. A good rule of thumb: reduce the temperature by 25°F (e.g., 450°F to 425°F, or 375°F to 350°F), or keep the same temperature but cut the cooking time by about 25 percent. Either way, start checking for doneness earlier than the recipe suggests. A meat thermometer can also help ensure accuracy.

Control panel placement affects both aesthetics and functionality. Backguard controls, found on most freestanding stoves, keep buttons and knobs safely out of reach of children but may be less visually streamlined. Front-control stoves, common in slide-in models, offer a sleek, built-in look and easier access to settings but can be a hazard if you have small kids or frequently lean against the stove while cooking. Many models now include touchscreen controls, which allow for precise adjustments and smart features but may require more care to keep clean.

Many newer electric stoves come with high-tech features designed to make cooking more efficient and hands-free. Wi-Fi-enabled models let you monitor and control your oven remotely via an app—preheating, adjusting temperatures, and setting timers from anywhere. Some even integrate with voice assistants like Alexa or Google Assistant for hands-free commands.

Touchscreen controls and digital displays offer a sleek, modern look, but keep in mind that they can be more finicky than traditional knobs, especially when wet or smudged. If you prefer a more tactile experience, look for models with physical dials or a hybrid control panel.

Some ovens now include built-in air fry functions, which circulate superheated air for crispy, fried-like results without the oil. It’s a great feature for quickly roasting veggies, crisping up frozen snacks, or making healthier versions of fried favorites—no extra countertop appliance needed.

Beyond the sticker price, you’ll want to factor in some extra costs. For one, power cords aren’t usually included in the price, and you can’t reuse your old one. Lowe’s, for example, charges $39.98 to install—which includes a new power cord. Some retailers also charge a separate delivery fee. And, if you’re replacing an existing stove, you should expect to pay around $50 to have your old one hauled away and disposed of. Finally, while most stoves come with a one-year limited warranty (which typically covers manufacturing defects and basic repairs), some retailers offer extended protection plans (for an additional fee) at checkout.
