The Best New Restaurants in NYC Come in All Cuisines and Sizes

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

New York City has a new restaurant flinging its doors open seemingly every hour—and that's on top of the time-worn institutions, from old-school steakhouses and delis to bagel shops run by the same family for decades.

When it comes to the new, we're spoiled for choice: One week, it's a fabulous Korean small-plates bar with a commendable natural wine list downtown; the next it's an imported taverna from Argentina in the Village where the steaks are juicy as hell and, well, there's even more great wine to work your way through. To help you parse through all the opening news and find the buzzy spots worth fighting for a reservation at, our editors are eating out night after night, trying new Mexican mariscos joints and modern Indian fine-dining spots, to report back on the ones that stand out.

This is our Fall 2026 status report on the best of the new—the places to try your luck walking into, or commit to a Friday night dinner at, whether you're a NYC local or a visitor in town for just the weekend. These are the best new restaurants in New York right now.

The best new restaurants in New York City stretch across Manhattan, Brooklyn, Queens, the Bronx, and Staten Island. Every business on this list has been selected independently by our editors and written by a Condé Nast Traveler journalist who knows the destination and has personally tried the restaurant in question. When choosing new restaurants, our editors consider both high-end and affordable eateries that offer an authentic and insider experience of the city. We’re always looking for standout dishes, a great location, and warm service—as well as serious sustainability credentials. In terms of how we define ‘new,’ we've chosen to focus on restaurants that have opened within a year of the publish date. When you work your way through the list, check back in: We'll update this guide regularly as new restaurants open in New York City.

Neighborhood: Williamsburg, BrooklynGo for: Martini hour and Shrimp Voodoo

After around seven years of living in Williamsburg, I’ve finally found the moody, cozy refuge this pocket of the neighborhood was missing. Booth’s Extra Fine, which just opened on October 9, feels like a sexy little secret. Hidden behind discreet oxblood curtains, the space simulates a vintage 20th-century supper club with dark walnut, rich leather booths, and deep, amber, brain-massage-like lighting. The heart of the restaurant stems from chef Nick Pfannerstill’s grandfather, Booth—an effortlessly stylish Milwaukee car salesman whose 1920s-50s photos line the walls.

Lean into the nostalgia and splurge on the porterhouse steak for two (or three—it's massive), but don't skip the Caviar Royale or the chef’s-kiss Shrimp Voodoo appetizer, cooked and swimming in Creole garlic butter. Personally? I can't wait to return and try the dry-aged Extra Fine Burger and a nice martini. Speaking of martinis, look out for Booth's "martini hour" launching soon, putting $12 cocktails—gin or vodka—at your disposal. —Jessica Chapel, commerce writer

Neighborhood: Hudson Yards, ManhattanGo for: A standout egg curry

Indienne, a tasting-menu-driven Indian restaurant, comes from chef Sujan Sarkar, who has worked in kitchens across Mumbai, New York, and San Francisco and runs a Michelin-starred restaurant of the same name in Chicago. Sarkar’s shtick? In his words: he reimagines Indian cuisine by “blending classical Indian flavors with refined European technique.” Think: deconstructed and then reconstructed. It takes a couple of courses to get a feel for what Sarkar is doing. The dhokla, for instance, isn’t spongy; it’s cubed, dehydrated, crackly, and tinged with white chocolate. The zingy passionfruit pani puri arrives in the shape of an oyster shell over reindeer moss. But once you get past the initial surprises, things really pick up. The “egg curry”—a whole steamed egg sitting on top of curry foam encrusted with potato sev and topped with caviar, served with buttery babka pao and house-churned butter—is an absolute standout. Things get a little more traditional with the chicken makhani that my husband got: chicken, three ways (chicken breast topped with spiced chicken mince wrapped over malai mousseline), served with buttery dal and even butterier naan. I didn’t miss the meat for a second, being on the vegetarian tasting menu myself, because my celeriac kebab was smoky and melt-in-your-mouth. And then came another surprise: the ghewar, a traditional Indian sweet that arrived like a mini layer cake with edible gold leaf (there’s a lot of this) and a bouncy coconut foam. A Penicillin-adjacent drink made with apricot whisky, mead, and ginger kept me company along the way. As we left, we were shown Sarkar’s other venture in the same premises, the British-Indian chophouse Elder, that was busy on a Tuesday night. —Arati Menon, global digital director

Neighborhood: The West Village, ManhattanGo for: ribeye among many other things

From the man (Pablo Rivero) behind one of Buenos Aires's most iconic steakhouses (Don Julio) comes Graciela—an Argentine tavern on a cozy corner in the West Village, where the wine list is long and commendable, the food is fire-cooked, and the room is star-studded in that quiet, in-the-know way. All the hype made me suspicious, but if you're expecting a Corner Store or Carbone experience, this is not that. It's just fantastic cooking that is elevated and pristinely executed enough to be covetable, with an experience that stays focused on the food. And the space, with dried herbs hanging from the ceiling and heavy wooden furniture and warm, low light, feels both moody and inviting; cool yet welcoming.

It's easy to rack up a major bill here, because every corner of the menu is worth exploring—from a red pepper dip with wood-fired oven-made bread, to the sliced bondiola coppa with lemon zest and olive oil (a very true must), to, of course, the salty, sizzling steaks. (The iconic whole tomato starter has been lost to the turning of the seasons, but I am certain something autumnal will replace it.) Some flavors are more complex, but much of what you'll find are crowd-pleasers, like the milanesa neapolitana which our serve aptly described as being "like a pizza milanesa" which is impossible to complain about. —Megan Spurrell, senior features editor

Neighborhood: The West Village, ManhattanGo for: Roti, actually, to accompany the beef cheek panang curry

The preponderance of new restaurants in the West Village specifically should be studied—sometimes it feels like we could dedicate a whole list to that neighborhood alone. The best of them, like Hommali, take the cuisine of choice and present it in an eminently stylish, upscale dining room. Every time I asked general manager Kritayanee “Air” Promsarin where something—the stemware, any number of the vividly colorful paintings on the walls—had come from, the answer was JJ Market, or Chatuchak Weekend Market, in Bangkok. It just looks good in there, I can't stress it enough. And then there's the matter of the food, which is buoyant, delightful, and oftentimes a wonderful introduction to Thai flavors for many Americans. I'm talking about a cold tom yum seafood cocktail, a heavily-spiced beef tartare on tripe cracker, and tao jiew lon, a fermented soybean-coconut dip scooped up with fried veggies. The mains are enormous and well-priced, and the best bite of the evening was not the beef cheek panang curry (although that was great on its own), but rather the piece of delicate roti used to scrape up the last remnants of sauce. —Charlie Hobbs, associate editor

Neighborhood: SoHo, ManhattanGo for: sashimi and sake pairing

For this toro-obsessed tuna lover, Noury’s torotaku tartare topped with uni was love at first bite. As I greedily piled the pale-pink tuna belly, pillowing orange uni, and pickled yellow daikon radish with a dash of sushi rice onto a sheet of toasted nori to make a DIY handroll, I knew at once that I had reached appetizer ambrosia. And the saké pairing? Divine nectar.

I had high expectations for chef-and-sommelier duo Alex Chang and Lina Goujjane, who opened Kiko together in 2024, which I believe remains one of the city’s most underrated restaurants simply because of its isolated locale in so-called Hudson Square. Now, with Noury the pair finally has an address to match their ambitions in prime Soho, on Sullivan Street between West Houston and Prince.

While Kiko is cozy and chic, Noury is stylish and sexy. The restaurant is modeled after a Japanese “izakaya,” relaxed dining establishments that serve shareable plates and drinkable tipples. As such, small bites, raw seafood, and saké are the star, but the vibe isn’t so puritan as an omakase counter. In addition to the daily changing sashimi and futomaki, there are also non-fish entrees like the steak haché (not my favorite bite, but a popular crowd pleaser) and pork and cheese mille feuille katsu; plus a surprisingly addictive chrysanthemum salad. To pull it all together, Chef Alex tapped Joji Miwa as Noury’s chef de cuisine, whose career spans award winning restaurants like Sushi Sanshin in Osaka and Sushi Noz in New York. That’s where he first met Lina, the mastermind behind the beverage program, which includes both extremely rare and accessible sakes and a selection of shochu cocktails and refined spirit-free options. Like at Kiko, the service at Noury is knowledgeable and their hospitality kind. Come. Eat. Drink. Be merry. —Hannah Towey, news editor

Neighborhood: The West Village, ManhattanGo for: ox tongue

Somssi, like Arthur, is new but doesn't feel it. It's a West Village restaurant with no flash or bombast, just a short and clever menu that jumps off from operator Ahris Kim's Korean origins and a casual, dimly-lit couple of dining rooms with lace accoutrements and eclectic hanging art giving the most comfortable feeling that you're at a friend's supper club rather than a sterling food and beverage operation. There's the semi-viral "Potato, Potato, Potato and Caviar" croquette, which is what it sounds like and will appeal to all, but more fun is to be had with things like the Grilled Ox Tongue, which comes undisguised as such on the plate with its uneven triangular shape atop romesco. I only gagged once while eating it, when my teeth came too close to my own tongue and I thought too hard about what I was eating, but then quickly continued my feast for love of the novel and tender dish. —Charlie Hobbs, associate editor

Neighborhood: Nolita, ManhattanGo for: crudo spotted with stracciatella and a late night

It was fitting that I ended up at Tashca just a few days after returning from Portugal, because I could fully appreciate how plucked-from-Lisbon this special spot in the LES is. Open only on Thursdays (the space is available for private events on other nights), this breezy little restaurant feels like being at a friend’s slightly chaotic but very endearing dinner party. Your table may or may not be ready when it’s supposed to be (we were given sparkling wine when ours took 30 minutes longer than planned, which is a trade I would have willingly made), and on that note, all reservations are made by Instagram DM (and you will need one; I saw several walk-in hopefuls turned away). But when you do flit through the open doors into the dining room, you won’t want to leave. Candles burn low throughout the night, and flowers hang from light fixtures—and the food, most importantly, sings, toeing the line between beauty and comfort, with distinctly Portuguese dishes that feature great seafood, bright produce, and a long list of tasty wines to pair. On the night I visited, there was a $95 prix-fixe made in partnership with visiting chef Joao Magalhaes Correia (who is usually in the kitchen at his Lisbon spots like Bar Alimentar). It featured a couscous bathed in squid ink and topped with a perfectly charred skewer of grilled octopus; melon draped in presunto ham; and a crudo topped with globs of stracciatella that I never could have imagined would work as well as it did. Come early, and plan to stay late. —Megan Spurrell, senior features editor

Neighborhood: NoMad, ManhattanGo for: fried chicken, actually!

When the Jungsik team closed sleek offshoot Sea in NoMad in May, it wasn't to abandon ship. They worked with commendable quickness to open a new concept, Mūje, by July. Like Jungsik proper, Mūje serves a tasting menu. Unlike Jungsik proper, Mūje is casual and focused on Asian food more generally rather than Korean exclusively. It's also more affordable at $150 for eight courses (Jungsik will cost you $335 for 10). There's a lot of fun to be had here with such a premise, like the tender short rib that comes with four different wraps nodding to Chinese, Japanese, Korean, and Vietnamese approaches, respectively. The best bite by a mile is the chicken wing, which is insane. It comes deboned and stuffed with pork, a Chinese technique, but is fried with the Korean Prinkle seasoning (a combination of cheese and onion and other good stuff) to create something really special. You only get one, so savor it. —Charlie Hobbs, associate editor

Neighborhood: Greenpoint, BrooklynGo for: an immaculate and affordable champagne list, plus the half chicken of the moment

I loved being at Arthur. With its lively and long dining room, buoyant soundtrack of soul music, and warm service, it's a place that feels like it's been around for a long time despite having opened in April. Food and drink are reasons to visit, in equal measure. Charlotte Mirzoeff's wine list is extensive and counts the French along with the Corsican and Portuguese, with a Champagne list 30-plus bottles deep (Mirzoeff is obsessed with the stuff, wants us all to be drinking more of it, and has two options for less than $100 to help make that happen.) Kevin Finch's food is rustic and comforting, the type you want to eat on the regular, but never boring—escargot comes grilled on a skewer beneath a bed of chives so ample it looks like a wig, for example. Two favorites: the kohlrabi with anchovy, herbs aplenty, and hazelnut (sop up the sauce with the pillows of housemade brioche) and the sasso chicken, with its crispy skin served atop crispier rice. Burgerheads take note: they've got an excellent one, with dijon, blue cheese, caramelized shallots, and a thick brisket patty between a sesame seed bun, but it's only available on Wednesdays. —Charlie Hobbs, associate editor

Neighborhood: Dumbo, BrooklynGo for: oysters broiled with bone marrow butter

Mexican food is back in the former Gran Electrica space on Front Street, a stretch of Dumbo real estate bustling with tourists moving to and from the Brooklyn Bridge vantage point. This time, the operation is run by Alan Delgado, the chef behind Fort Greene burrito spot Los Burritos Juarez, with drinks from bartender Ivy Mix. The great backyard garden patio remains, although I enjoyed my meal in the front dining room, which manages a moody mise en scène with its terracotta walls and glowing light despite lovely, huge windows overlooking the street and its passersby. Mix's beverages are stellar and inventive, in particular the rotating micheladas and frozen beverages that are tart and perfect. On the food front, the must-order comes first in the oysters, which are broiled a delicious brown color in bone marrow, and arrive bubbling. It's the perfectly decadent start. People talk about the fish tacos in particular, but I preferred the suadero, a confit brisket with guac on a corn tortillas rather than the flour Delgado is known for (and which can be purchased plain on the side, something I recommend.) There are flashier entrees, but the Chile Colorado—a braised pork shoulder with pinto beans and pickled onions that takes on a stew-like consistency, is subtly smoky, extremely filling, and something I'll be returning for. —Charlie Hobbs, associate editor

Neighborhood: Williamsburg, BrooklynGo for: potato bread, celery salad, and a breezy dining room

In the location of the former Llama Inn, a beloved modern Peruvian eatery in Williamsburg, the new Cafe Bar J.F. has arrived. Despite a name that feels designed to be confused and/or forgotten (both of which I keep experiencing), the space inside—and the food they serve—is an honorable replacement for Llama Inn. The concept, which comes from the Llama Inn team and is spearheaded by chef Francisco Castillo, is a “South American Tavern”. To me? It feels like eating at one of the many breezy, design-eyed restaurants I love to splurge at in Lima or Buenos Aires, with dishes that build on beloved foundations like ceviche and asado. Madrid-based Plantea Estudio has elevated the room from the plant-filled corner it once was to a more demure combo of dark-wood and metal furniture (and while the rooftop remains open, the interior is so lovely I chose to stay indoors). The prices will get ya if you don’t pay attention, but certain items—the potato bread and the celery salad, two surprising ones—really lingered. Go for a date night, or a celebratory dinner with a friend, and take the excuse to work through the satisfying cocktail menu. —Megan Spurrell, senior features editor

Neighborhood: The West Village, ManhattanGo for: Guinness, oysters, and whatever seafood special is on the chalkboard

My friend Sarah put it best. There we were—me leaning back in a booth, she perched on a stool—at Dean's, the lively new pub from King team leaders Jess Shadbolt and Annie Shi, the windows flung open in celebration of one of spring's first sunny days, and she says, “This feels like what the Hamptons is supposed to be like.” The parts that make it so: a location in the Village and the according clientele, nautical stylings (lots of dark wood and blues of the navy variety), abundant seafood done the British way. All to say that, somehow, we felt as though we'd stumbled into something of a seafood shack. The halcyon weather won't be a constant, of course, but the place will do just as well comforting the cold in winter with its pies and beers—the stargazy pie, a hopefully perennial special, is strikingly fish-forward when it appears on your plate, head and tail of a milky-eyed mackerel protruding. Whether or not you eat its eyeballs, the rest of the dish beneath the buttery crust is basically chowder, not at all intimidating but very much delicious. It's also rather heavy, so if you want to try as many dishes as possible while also downing excellent Guinness pour after excellent Guinness pour, perhaps stick to such happy hits as the oysters, fish and chips, and scallops in malt vinegar. —Charlie Hobbs, associate editor
