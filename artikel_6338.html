An Egyptian villager working near his house discovered the buried remains of two colossal pharaoh statues

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

A villager's discovery in Egypt led archaeologists to two colossal royal statues, including a confirmed Ramesses II figure once about 28 feet tall.

A resident of Behbeit El-Hagar, a village in Egypt’s Gharbia Governorate, was working a plot he owns in front of his house. There he found a strange inscription on a stone block, and he reported it right away.

From there, a rescue excavation by Egypt’s Supreme Council of Antiquities (SCA) uncovered the remains of two colossal royal statues. Egypt’s Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities announced the finds on October 13, 2026.

One statue is confirmed as Ramesses II. Its head alone stands about 5.2 feet (1.6 meters) high.

Behbeit El-Hagar lies in the central Nile Delta. The village holds the ruins of a major temple to the goddess Isis, according to the Metropolitan Museum of Art.

The resident alerted the local antiquities inspectorate. Inspectors examined the stone and confirmed that it was an ancient artifact, and the SCA sent a specialist team to excavate the spot.

Tourism and Antiquities Minister Sherif Fathy praised the resident for reporting the stone immediately. He said public involvement is key to protecting Egypt’s heritage for future generations.

Fathy called the discovery an important addition to Egypt’s recent finds. In his view, it sheds further light on the history and archaeological significance of Behbeit El-Hagar.

The block that started the search is pink granite, about 4.3 feet (1.3 meters) long. Its hieroglyphs preserve two royal titles of Ramesses II.

One is Nesu-bity, or “King of Upper and Lower Egypt.” The other is Sa-Ra, or “Son of Ra.”

Dr. Hisham El-Leithy, secretary-general of the SCA, described the first statue. The king wears the Double Crown, which symbolized the unification of Upper and Lower Egypt.

The upper part of the statue’s back pillar also survives. It is inscribed with the king’s Horus name, Hor-ka-nakht-mery-maat, often rendered as “Strong bull, beloved of Maat.”

Based on the size of the head, El-Leithy estimated that the complete statue stood about 28 feet (8.5 meters) tall, including its base. The estimate rests on proportions, since only parts of that statue have been recovered.

Ramesses II, whose monuments include a Luxor obelisk now in Paris, reigned for 66 years. His rule, from about 1279 to 1213 B.C., fell within the 19th Dynasty of Egypt’s New Kingdom.

The second find is the main body of a royal statue, about 7.9 feet (2.4 meters) long. Its legs and base are missing.

This figure wears the nemes, the striped royal headcloth, and part of its top is lost. The face remains clear and well preserved.

Those features resemble the first statue’s, and archaeologists believe the second figure may also depict Ramesses II. That identification has not been confirmed.

The granite block lay roughly 3.3 feet (1 meter) north of this second statue.

Excavation and study will continue at the site, El-Leithy said. The goal is a clearer picture of the statues, the area’s history and its architectural development.

The Isis temple at Behbeit El-Hagar is much younger than Ramesses II. Nectanebo II, who ruled from 360 to 343 B.C., built most of it in dark granite, in a severe style typical of his dynasty’s Delta temples.

That work came roughly 850 years after Ramesses II’s reign ended. Nectanebo II decorated only one important chapel before the second Persian invasion of Egypt.

The temple probably replaced an earlier one begun in the 26th Dynasty. Ptolemy II later completed the sanctuary and chapels, and Ptolemy III added a hypostyle hall and the western facade.

The temple collapsed sometime after Ptolemy III and before the early third century A.D. Weak foundations may have played a part, and an earthquake, possibly in 24 B.C., may have brought it down.

Blocks from the temple have reached museums abroad. One granodiorite relief at the Met shows Nectanebo II offering incense and a collar to the god Osiris.

The ministry’s announcement does not connect the two statues to the Isis temple.

Ramesses II had statues of himself made across his empire. Among them are seated figures about 69 feet (21 meters) tall at Abu Simbel.

His mortuary temple, the Ramesseum, stood on the West Bank at Thebes, modern Luxor. One colossal seated figure there was carved from a single block of granite quarried near Aswan, according to the British Museum.

The surviving head and torso, now in London, measure just over 8.2 feet (2.5 meters). The complete seated figure would have reached roughly 23 feet (7 meters).

A red granite sphinx found at Memphis carries Ramesses II’s titles, but his sculptors reworked it from an earlier king’s monument. Flinders Petrie excavated it near the temple of Ptah in 1913.

The sphinx’s original owner was erased so thoroughly that he is unlikely ever to be identified. Ramesses II’s son and successor, Merenptah, later added his own cartouches to its shoulders.
