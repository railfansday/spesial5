Tropical cyclones may follow a hidden winter pattern months before they form

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

A February weather pattern may offer clues to East Asia’s summer typhoon activity, providing information beyond El Niño.

Predicting how many tropical cyclones will reach East Asia months ahead is difficult. But a weather pattern that develops in late winter may offer an early clue.

New research suggests that this pattern could help researchers anticipate summer tropical cyclone activity beyond what El Niño alone can explain.

The study was led by Zeming Wu from Zhejiang University and examined more than 40 years of observations.

This year, many blamed the strong El Niño for the storms.

El Niño is a periodic warming of surface waters in the central and eastern tropical Pacific that can influence where tropical cyclones form and how they travel.

But the researchers argue it may not be entirely El Niño’s fault this time.

The team focused on the Pacific–North American pattern, or PNA, a large-scale pattern of atmospheric pressure variations over the North Pacific and North America.

It operates in phases. Its “positive phase” brings warmer temperatures across the western United States and Canada and cooler temperatures across the central and southeastern United States. The negative phase is the reverse.

The researchers found that the February PNA pattern was linked to tropical cyclone activity over East Asia the following summer.

The relationship remained after accounting for the influence of the El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO), a recurring climate pattern that includes El Niño and La Niña.

“Our findings suggest that late-winter atmospheric variability may provide valuable information about East Asian summer tropical cyclone activity beyond the influence of ENSO,” Wu said.

“This highlights the potential importance of atmospheric circulation outside the tropics as an additional source of seasonal predictability.”

The positive phase of PNA in late winter is linked to fewer tropical cyclones forming over the western North Pacific and fewer storms reaching East Asia several months later.

But how does a February pattern stay connected to summer storms?

The original atmospheric anomalies may have weakened or vanished. Ocean temperatures kept the events linked.

“Through interactions between the atmosphere and ocean, sea surface temperature anomalies can preserve and transmit climate signals across seasons,” said co-author Chundi Hu from the same university.

The team found two complementary pathways, both mediated by the ocean and linking late-winter PNA variability to summer tropical cyclone activity.

“The ocean appears to act as a bridge between winter atmospheric variability and summer tropical cyclone activity,” Wu said.

“More importantly, our results suggest that this connection involves not only the familiar ENSO-related pathway but also another pathway associated with atmospheric variability beyond preceding-winter ENSO.”

The first pathway involves ENSO. A positive PNA pattern in late winter is associated with warming in the tropical North Atlantic and a relatively rapid weakening of El Niño in the tropical Pacific.

According to the researchers, these temperature changes match shifts in large-scale atmospheric circulation.

Those shifts favored the development and persistence of an anomalous anticyclone over the western North Pacific into summer.

The second pathway works beyond the linear influence of preceding-winter ENSO. Hu pointed to a mechanism called seasonal footprinting.

This pattern lets anomalies in atmospheric circulation leave a lasting and developing imprint on ocean temperatures.

In this case, the anomalies were wind changes over the subtropical North Pacific and were associated with cooling in the central tropical Pacific.

“Despite their different origins, both pathways converge on a similar atmospheric response: an anomalous anticyclone, or unusual system of high pressure, over the western North Pacific,” Wu said.

“This circulation pattern suppresses conditions favorable for tropical cyclone formation and modifies the winds that steer storms, reducing the number of tropical cyclones reaching East Asia during summer.”

Next, the researchers plan targeted climate model experiments to investigate these pathways further.

“Ultimately, our goal is to better understand the sources of tropical cyclone predictability and contribute to more reliable seasonal risk assessments for East Asian coastal communities,” Hu said.

Seasonal forecasts give coastal communities time to plan for storm risks. The findings suggest that February’s PNA pattern could provide useful information alongside ENSO.

Further testing will help establish whether this winter signal improves forecasts of summer tropical cyclone activity.

The PNA pattern is large scale. It distributes air and heat across the North Pacific and North America. Its late-winter phase may offer a clue months before the storms arrive.

For coastal communities in East Asia, better forecasts could mean better preparation and more reliable seasonal risk assessments. That could help people get ready before the storms hit.

The study is published in the journal Ocean-Land-Atmosphere Research.
