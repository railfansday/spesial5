A 12.6% drop from Asia fueled the seventh straight month of U.S. inbound travel declines, more evidence that the U.S. is losing ground as other countries compete for the same long-haul travelers.

Sharp declines from Asia and Western Europe fueled the seventh consecutive month of declining overseas tourism into the United States, according to preliminary figures from the National Travel and Tourism Office released on Tuesday.

About 2.8 million overseas visitors entered the United States in October, down 7.2% from last year.

Much of last month’s decline stems from a 12.6% dip in travelers from Asia, the largest source region outside of Western Europe. South Korean arrivals were down 37.9%, Japan was down 10.3%, China down 2.3%, and India down 1%.

Overseas arrivals to the United States fell for a seventh consecutive month in October, with about 2.8 million visitors entering the country, down 7.2% year over year, according to preliminary National Travel and Tourism Office figures. Asia drove much of the decline with a 12.6% drop, led by a 37.9% fall in South Korean arrivals, while Western Europe fell 6.5% and France alone was down 14.4%. Arrivals by sea dropped 77% in October, and the NTTO did not immediately respond to a request for comment on why. South America was close to flat as growth from Brazil, Colombia, and Ecuador was offset by a 13.7% decline from Argentina. Brand USA surveys show key markets turning to alternative destinations, with cost and exchange rates deterring Asian travelers and the political climate cited by travelers from Canada, Germany, and Australia. The U.S. Travel Association projects inflation-adjusted international visitor spending will fall nearly $11 billion this year to $163 billion, and does not expect arrivals to return to 2019 levels until 2030. The U.S. share of global long-haul arrivals has fallen from 10.3% in 2019 to 7.7% this year, and is projected to slip to 7.1% by 2030.
