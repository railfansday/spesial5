In a July filing with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, Dominion said the merger needs approval from FERC, the Nuclear Regulatory Commission, the SCC, the North Carolina Utilities Commission and the Public Service Commission of South Carolina.

The SCC has been holding daily evidentiary hearings since Nov. 17, while South Carolina and North Carolina regulators have scheduled theirs to begin in December and February, respectively. Neither FERC nor the NRC have issued a decision.

Dominion and NextEra announced in a Sept. 14 release that they would double a proposed monthly residential bill credit for Virginia customers from two to four years.

In that release, the companies also said they would accelerate solar, storage and other resources in Virginia to “affordably power the Commonwealth’s growth,” advance the Virginia Clean Economy Act and deliver “long-term affordability benefits by leveraging the combined company’s scale to buy, build, finance and operate more efficiently.”

Last week, SCC regulators and attorneys for the state pressed witnesses, including Baine, NextEra CEO John Ketchum and Dominion Energy CEO Bob Blue, on how customer benefits could be calculated and ensured.

Baine said that when Dominion merged with SCANA in 2019, it saw “benefits of scale” where input cost was lowered, adding, “We believe it will be on a much bigger scale with this merger, but just can't do the math for you today.”

John Farmer, a senior assistant attorney general for Virginia, pointed Ketchum to a written response from Dominion and NextEra in which they said they “have not prepared a year-by-year quantification of benefits to Dominion Energy Virginia's existing customers.”

Ketchum said he stands behind “the fact that we've offered up the bill credits” and broadly outlined the potential operational benefits of a combined company.

“Shame on us if we can't realize the scale benefits that I've spoken about,” Ketchum said in Wednesday’s hearing. “That’s what we do.”

SCC Chair Kelsey Bagot asked Blue how he would ensure that “potential issues do not erode the benefits” that are “possible” as a result of the merger.

“Have you started developing a plan?” Bagot said.

Blue said that if the merger isn’t managed well, “it is possible that the longer-term benefits that we're describing won't materialize as quickly.”

“As I mentioned in my answer to you before, I don't believe that would leave our customers worse off,” he continued. “There may be some missed opportunities.”

Some intervenors also criticized the bill credit proposal.

Bloom Advisory Partners partner Teresa Ringenbach testified on behalf of the Retail Energy Advancement League that the bill credit structure “effectively penalizes customers for choosing a competitive supplier,” which she said “is discriminatory and undermines the statutorily mandated retail choice framework.”

“A large general service customer using 72,000 megawatt hours per year would receive approximately $225,000 if it shops, compared to approximately $642,000 if it does not,” Ringenbach said. “That difference is a price signal, and it’s funded by the utility's new parent, and it rewards remaining on the utility's generation service.”

“Shareholder credits should not be viewed as the price of getting a merger approved,” she added. “They should deliver real value to every single wires customer.”

Bagot asked Richard McCann, an energy expert testifying on behalf of Gov. Abigail Spanberger’s office, “Do we have the ability to provide rate credits to a single rate class? For example … only to residential customers and not to any of the other rate classes.”

“I recognize that's not exactly what you're proposing,” Bagot added, “but it does shift potentially what that rate credit would be, maybe closer to $29 per month.”

Raising the credit to $29 a month was a condition McCann proposed in his written testimony, along with a six-year residential rate freeze.

In response to Bagot, McCann said, “I know that at $10, they won't notice if it phases out or not. $30 a month, they might notice that the credit disappears if it's a sudden shock.”

He said that implementing the higher credit for residential ratepayers alongside other methods he proposed — such as raising the debt share of Dominion's capital structure — could lower bills over time and provide a “smooth glide path” for the end of the credits.

SCC Commissioner Jehmal Hudson pushed back on the rate freeze proposal, asking McCann what authority allows the SCC to “freeze future base rate increases irrespective of the future costs” and noting that rate freezes in Virginia “have primarily stemmed from the General Assembly legislation.”

“You are in an extraordinary position right now with this merger,” McCann said. “There are certain things that you have discretion about going forward — you will have to decide if you have that discretion.”
