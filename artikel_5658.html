Sjogren's Disease Brings a Lot More Misery Beyond Dryness

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

— Brain fog, fatigue, infections, death explored in new studies

Oral and ocular dryness are the hallmark features of Sjögren's disease, but understanding the full symptom burden is still an active area of investigation, with recent studies reported at the American College of Rheumatology's annual meeting shedding light on some of the condition's lesser-known aspects.

For example, one new study found that oral disease serious enough to cause tooth loss helps drive serious infections such as sepsis in Sjögren's patients, a risk that is worsened further with immunomodulators typically used to control the disease.

Another found that nonphysical symptoms including "brain fog" and fatigue are nearly as common as dryness in Sjögren's disease, and a third confirmed that patients face a significant risk for early mortality.

All in all, this research puts Sjögren's disease alongside conditions more famous for their broadly burdensome symptom spectrum, such as rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, and systemic sclerosis.

An analysis of U.S. commercial and Medicaid insurance claims led by Thomas Grader-Beck, MD, of Johns Hopkins University in Baltimore, looked at associations between oral disease indicators and infection rates among some 60,000 people with Sjögren's disease.

Those suffering tooth loss, typically the result of periodontal disease, were significantly more likely to also experience systemic infections -- modestly when five or fewer teeth were lost (OR 1.18, 95% CI 1.13-1.24), or more strongly with more extensive extractions (OR 1.69, 95% CI 1.36-2.11). Absolute risks were substantial too: whereas systemic infections were recorded for 36% of those with no tooth loss (itself an indicator of risk associated with Sjögren's disease), the percentage reached 43% in patients with up to five lost teeth and 54% among those with six or more lost.

Dental visits, too, tracked with systemic infection risk in a "dose-response" relationship. Odds ratios for infections rose with each additional visit in the previous year: 1.11 with one visit, 1.32 with two, 1.41 with three, and 1.60 for four or more (all P<0.001 relative to no dental visits).

Moreover, the researchers reported, use of immunomodulator therapy -- a category that spans virtually every drug used for Sjögren's disease including corticosteroids, antimalarials, biologics, and conventional immunosuppressants -- by itself raised the risk for infections by 49%.

Oral disease and immunosuppression are "independent, additive risk factors for systemic infection," the group concluded.

Lane Destro, PhD, and colleagues at the Sjögren's Foundation in Reston, Virginia, had the Harris Poll organization survey people with the condition last year, with a total of 6,360 responses. It found that so-called brain fog -- a feeling of mental sluggishness and inability to focus -- and fatigue were almost as common as salivary and ocular dryness (prevalences of 77% and 90%, respectively, versus 93% and 95%), and also in the same range as joint pain (81%).

Other nonphysical symptoms were common too, if less so than brain fog and fatigue. Some 73% of respondents complained of insomnia, 64% reported eye fatigue specifically, 56% said they suffered headaches, and 56% indicated feeling dizzy or faint.

Patients also complained of additional burdens. Close to 60% said Sjögren's disease had affected relations with their spouses or partners and two-thirds reported impacts on other family relationships. A large majority agreed that Sjögren's "adds an emotional burden to my life," while half said the disease made them feel lonely. Not surprisingly, most also reported difficulties with work and employment stemming from the diagnosis.

Furthermore, the problems arising from fatigue and brain fog were disproportionately burdensome for these quality-of-life (QoL) issues. Having relatively severe dry eyes and mouth, for example, raised the likelihood of experiencing negative QoL effects by factors of roughly 1.5 to 2; a high fatigue burden, on the other hand, raised the risk by factors of 4 to 10, and the negative impact of brain fog was nearly as great.

French researchers confirmed that patients with Sjögren's disease face increased mortality risk, in the same way as patients with other autoimmune inflammatory conditions such as lupus.

Led by Elodie Portier, MD, of Hôpital Bicêtre in Paris, the group examined vital statistics for a 395-member cohort of Sjögren's disease patients in France and compared them with the general national population. Followed from 2007 to 2025, the cohort saw 77 deaths. The rate was 41% higher than among similar-age French people (95% CI 11-76). The gap was even greater for a Sjögren's subgroup marked by high symptom burden (54%, 95% CI 10-110).

The researchers also identified several risk factors for early mortality in the Sjögren's cohort. In multivariate analysis, each year of older age added 13% to the risk, while being male nearly tripled the likelihood of death (HR 2.66, 95% CI 1.04-6.77).

Having more than mild disease was also a mortality risk. Multivariate analysis comparing patients with EULAR Sjögren's Syndrome Activity Index (ESSDAI) scores of 6 or more to those with ESSDAI 5 or less yielded a hazard ratio of 2.16 (95% CI 1.29-3.60). Each ESSDAI point conferred a 15% extra risk for mortality.

Patient-reported overall symptom burden showed a similar pattern, the researchers noted, but it fell just short of statistical significance.
