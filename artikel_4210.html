For better mental health, how often you exercise may matter more than how hard

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

How often you exercise may matter more for your mental health than how hard you push yourself during a workout. A large study of Norwegian college students found that those who exercised less frequently were more likely to experience depression and anxiety one year later. The findings suggest that maintaining a consistent exercise routine could be particularly important for supporting mental well-being.

The research, published in the open access journal PLOS One, was conducted by Michael Grasdalsmoen of Western Norway University of Applied Sciences and his colleagues. Their results point to the potential benefits of exercising regularly rather than focusing primarily on longer or more demanding workouts.

Why Exercise Habits Matter for Mental Health

Depression, anxiety, and other common mental health conditions are among the leading causes of disability in young adults worldwide. The college years can be an especially challenging period as students adjust to new academic, social, and personal responsibilities. Identifying everyday behaviors that may help protect mental health is therefore an important research priority.

Although previous research has linked regular physical activity to better psychological well-being, many young adults remain physically inactive. Scientists have also had relatively limited evidence about how exercise habits relate to the development of mental health conditions over time, particularly when those conditions are evaluated using standardized diagnostic tools.

To investigate this relationship, Grasdalsmoen and his team followed 10,460 full-time students enrolled in higher education in Norway. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 35.

At the beginning of the study, students completed questionnaires describing their exercise habits, including how frequently they worked out, how strenuous their activities were, how long they exercised, and how many total hours they were active each week. One year later, they completed a self-administered diagnostic assessment designed to identify common mental health disorders.

Exercise Frequency May Matter More Than Intensity

The researchers found that students who reported lower levels of physical activity were more likely to meet diagnostic criteria for anxiety or depression one year later. However, not every aspect of exercise showed the same relationship with mental health.

Exercise frequency emerged as a particularly consistent factor. How often students exercised was more reliably associated with their later mental health than the intensity of their workouts or the amount of time they spent exercising.

The researchers also identified differences between men and women. Among women, lower exercise intensity was associated with a greater likelihood of anxiety and depression. Among men, spending fewer total hours exercising each week showed a stronger association with these conditions.

Overall, the likelihood of common mental health disorders decreased as weekly exercise increased, with this pattern continuing up to approximately 10 hours of physical activity per week.

The results also support the World Health Organization's recommendation that adults complete at least 150 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity each week.

Regular Exercise Could Help Support Student Mental Health

Taken together, the findings suggest that exercising consistently may be especially valuable for reducing the likelihood of anxiety and depression among college students. Rather than concentrating exclusively on workout intensity or duration, maintaining a regular routine could be an important part of supporting mental health.

However, the researchers emphasize that their observational study cannot establish a direct cause-and-effect relationship. Although students who exercised more regularly tended to have better mental health outcomes, the results do not prove that exercise itself prevented depression or anxiety.

Even with this limitation, promoting accessible opportunities for physical activity could be a useful part of broader efforts to improve student well-being. Such efforts may be especially relevant for students who are beginning to experience mental health difficulties or symptoms that have not yet reached the threshold for a clinical diagnosis.

The authors add: "Our findings show that students who engage in regular physical exercise are at lower risk of developing common mental disorders over time. These findings highlight the importance of making physical activity an accessible and natural part of student life."

"Mental health among students is a growing public health concern. Our findings suggest that supporting students to be physically active may contribute to better mental health and should be recognized as an important part of a healthy learning environment."
