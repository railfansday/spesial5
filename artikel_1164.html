Intratumoural heterogeneity, originating from genetic, epigenetic and phenotypic diversity, is pervasive in cancer and a fundamental barrier to curative treatment1,2. How these heterogeneous cell states emerge and co-evolve within tumours remains poorly understood. Here we develop a deep-learning framework that quantifies colorectal cancer tissue architecture and nuclear morphology at subcellular resolution to predict patient outcome. Integrating this with spatial transcriptomics, multiplex immunofluorescence and patient-derived organoids, we identify a co-evolutionary relationship between tumour architecture and cancer cell states. We identify architectural interfaces in colorectal cancer that drive cell state diversification by promoting distinct cancer stem cell programmes. Specifically, tissue fragmentation generates compressive forces that activate YAP, suppressing classical stem cell programmes and inducing fetal/regenerative stem cell states that initially manifest as hybrid states with features of partial epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT). Subsequent tumour–stroma communication through TGFβ diversifies these into distinct stem cell and EMT states. Reciprocally, β-catenin activity tunes tumour cell responsiveness to architectural cues. Collectively, this work identifies a feedforward loop between cell states and tissue architecture that drives cancer heterogeneity and cell state diversification. Machine-learning-based analyses harness this co-dependency, independently of mutational status or cancer stage, to predict patient outcome.

Most cancers comprise genetically, epigenetically and phenotypically diverse cell states within a variable stroma1,2. A substantial proportion of this heterogeneity arises independently of somatic mutations3 and associates with poor prognosis by diversifying cell states that evade therapy1,2. Whether tissue organization and cell state heterogeneity co-evolve, and how cell-intrinsic and cell-extrinsic mechanisms interact to drive tumour progression, remain unresolved.

The intestinal epithelium is sustained by a resident adult stem cell (SC) pool4, which also gives rise to and drives colorectal cancer (CRC). During CRC progression, adult SC signatures are depleted while fetal-like/regenerative programmes, which are otherwise present only during development or tissue damage, re-emerge and expand further after metastasis or treatment5,6,7. This plasticity generates extensive heterogeneity and limits therapy responsiveness. Metastatic behaviour requires loss of canonical colonic SC identity, activation of TGFβ and YAP, and acquisition of a fetal-like SC state5,6,7,8.

Genetic drivers of CRC, including BRAF and KRAS mutations and microsatellite instability (MSI), are well established9, but phenotypic plasticity can also arise through epigenetic and transcriptional reprogramming10,11. CRC progression also involves microenvironmental remodelling, including immune infiltration and signalling from cancer-associated fibroblasts12,13,14, together with altered tissue mechanics such as increased extracellular matrix (ECM) stiffness and elevated interstitial pressure15,16. These changes are known to alter cell states and promote aggression17,18,19,20. Finally, nuclear shape abnormalities serve as diagnostic indicators of cancer21,22, suggesting that phenotypic heterogeneity is coupled to nuclear organization23,24,25,26. However, how nuclear architecture, tissue organization and cellular heterogeneity are related remains poorly understood.

Here we develop a machine-learning-based image analysis framework to quantify CRC architecture and nuclear morphology, and find that both are highly prognostic independently of mutational status or disease stage. Integrating spatial transcriptomics, multiplex immunofluorescence and patient-derived organoids reveals that distinct tumour architectures associate with specific nuclear morphologies and cell state programmes, and that architecture and phenotypic plasticity co-evolve within individual patients. Mechanistically, tumour fragmentation and associated compressive stress promote transition of LGR5-positive SCs to an ANXA1-positive fetal-like state through YAP, whereas ECM composition and stromal TGFβ signalling promote EMT. These findings reveal a feedforward loop between tissue architecture, nuclear shape and cell state diversification that drives poor outcome in CRC.

To investigate the relationship between nuclear morphology, tissue architecture and disease outcome, we obtained pathologist-curated tumour microarrays (TMAs) from 1,342 patients with CRC with complete clinical information (Extended Data Fig. 1a). TMA punches were taken from primary tumour resection specimens at paired tumour core (CT; 1,834 cores) and invasive margin (IM; 1,502 cores) locations (Fig. 1a and Extended Data Fig. 1b). The slides were stained for the epithelial marker pan-cadherin (pan-EPI) to identify tumour cell boundaries and to separate tumour from stroma, and with DAPI to mark all nuclei (Fig. 1a and Extended Data Fig. 1c). Nuclei were segmented at single-cell resolution27 (over 25 million cells) to derive quantitative descriptors of nuclear shape, size and deformation (Fig. 1b). Patients with MSI and microsatellite-stable (MSS) patients were analysed separately.

To identify the most informative nuclear morphology parameters, we computed pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients across single cells (Fig. 1c). Clustering revealed two principal parameter modules. The first captured nuclear deformation and irregularity: elliptic Fourier coefficient (EFC) ratio28 and solidity described deviations from a perfect oval at multiple spatial scales, whereas circularity and aspect ratio captured larger-scale deformations (Fig. 1b,c). The second captured nuclear size, including area and axis ratio (Fig. 1c). Parameters were averaged per patient and tumour region. In MSS patients, univariate Cox models of these averages showed that nuclear shape, more consistently than nuclear size, was associated with disease-specific survival: patients with rounder, smoother nuclei (higher circularity, solidity and EFC ratio, and lower aspect ratio) had poorer survival (Fig. 1d and Extended Data Fig. 1d; CT n = 773, IM n = 484). Multivariate models incorporating age, stage, tumour localization and Immunoscore29 confirmed this association in stage II–III but not stage I disease (Extended Data Fig. 1e,f). Nuclear circularity also correlated with tumour stage (Extended Data Fig. 1d–g).

This association prompted us to examine whether nuclear morphology reflects local tissue architecture. Histopathological inspection of the pan-EPI signal identified six predominant, quantifiable architecture types: well-polarized, tightly packed glands (termed glandular); glandular structures with emergent branches and minor stromal infiltrations (glandular infiltrated); densely packed glands with loss of polarity and minimal stromal contact (solid); an intermediate glandular-solid phenotype (glandular dense); gland-like structures with detaching cell clusters (moderately fragmented); and poorly differentiated glands with high stromal infiltration and disrupted continuity (fragmented; Extended Data Fig. 1h).

We then established a quantitative workflow to classify the architectural distribution within each TMA sample into three groups: glandular (consolidating glandular, glandular dense and glandular infiltrated), solid and fragmented (consolidating moderately and highly fragmented). A U-Net convolutional neural network was trained on samples with homogeneous, archetypal architectures to learn architecture-specific tissue embeddings (Fig. 1e). TMA cores were subdivided into tiles, and a classifier trained on these embeddings predicted the architecture type/tile (Fig. 1e and Extended Data Fig. 2a–c). The proportion of tiles within each category defined an overall architecture score/patient, where the specific architecture exceeding 50% of tiles designated the dominant type. Mixed classification was assigned in cases in which no single architecture reached this threshold.

Architecture classifications were validated against quantitative, interpretable histological features including lumen area, tumour–stroma interface roughness, cell density and cell–cell alignment (Fig. 1f,g and Extended Data Fig. 2d). The 196 cores selected as architectural archetypes formed significantly tighter, better-separated clusters in morphological feature space compared with size-matched random draws from the cohort (Extended Data Fig. 2e), confirming their representativeness.

Notably, the tissue architecture at both the CT and IM was highly predictive of patient outcome (Fig. 1h and Extended Data Fig. 3a). Patients with dominant fragmented architectures showed the least favourable prognosis (Fig. 1h). Fragmented cores were modestly more prevalent in patients with stage III/IV CRC (Extended Data Fig. 3b), but architecture retained independent prognostic value within the stage II/III subgroups (Extended Data Fig. 3c). Architecture was not prognostic in the MSI subset (Extended Data Fig. 3d), consistent with the distinct pathophysiology of this CRC subtype. The prognostic value of architecture in MSS patients was independently validated in a second cohort of 270 patients (Extended Data Fig. 3e,f).

Analysis of spatial concordance revealed that nearly all patients with fragmented tumour cores also displayed fragmented IMs, whereas the reverse did not hold: a substantial proportion of solid or glandular cores presented with fragmented IMs (Extended Data Fig. 3g,h). This asymmetry suggests that tumour fragmentation originates at the IM before propagating to the tumour core.

To contextualize these architecture classifications within established histopathological criteria, we compared fragmentation with tumour budding, an established clinical marker of aggressiveness30. High budding grades (BD2/3) were more frequent in patients with fragmented architectures, indicating partial correspondence between structural disintegration and budding morphology (Extended Data Fig. 4a). However, fragmented architecture retained prognostic value even among patients with low budding scores (BD1; Extended Data Fig. 4b). As budding is classically quantified at the IM, but has also been reported to occur within the tumour core31, an expert pathologist separately scored intratumoural budding from the tumour cores of fragmented TMAs. Most fragmented tumour cores did not score positive for budding, and budding score correlated only weakly with degree of fragmentation (Extended Data Fig. 4c), demonstrating that fragmentation and budding capture distinct features. The signet ring cell histological subtype was predominantly classified as fragmented, consistent with its structural characteristics (Extended Data Fig. 4d). Fragmented architecture was more prevalent in high-grade tumours but also detected in low-grade disease, and remained prognostic in both groups (Extended Data Fig. 4e,f). Notably, 45% of samples classified as both high-grade and high-budding lacked fragmented architecture (Extended Data Fig. 4g), and fragmentation remained an independent predictor of outcome in patients with stage II/III CRC after controlling for both budding grade and histological grade (Extended Data Fig. 4h). Finally, fragmented architectures occurred in both colon and rectal tumours and did not correlate with Immunoscore (Extended Data Fig. 4i,j). Thus, fragmentation captures tumour features that are not encompassed by budding, grading or Immunoscore.

Consistent with the initial hypothesis that single-nucleus morphology reflects local tissue architecture, tumour regions with glandular or solid architectures were enriched for elongated nuclei. Regions with fragmented architecture were dominated by rounder, more-regular nuclei (Fig. 1i,j and Extended Data Fig. 4k). Thus, tumour architecture is a strong independent prognostic indicator in CRC, and round, regular nuclei probably reflect the fragmented tissue context.

To mechanistically dissect the relationship between nuclear shape, tissue architecture and patient outcome, we performed spatial transcriptomics (GeoMx DSP) on eight whole-tumour resection samples from the same patient cohort (Fig. 2a). For each patient, 10–50 regions of interest spanning the full spectrum of architectural diversity were selected and the transcriptomes of tumour epithelium and surrounding stroma profiled separately (Fig. 2a and Extended Data Fig. 5a). Leiden clustering defined transcriptional clusters with distinct molecular profiles (Fig. 2b,c). Although transcriptomes were highly patient specific, transcriptional clusters within each patient were spatially organized into discrete domains corresponding to distinct tumour architectures (Fig. 2b–d and Extended Data Fig. 5b–f), suggesting that architecture associates with specific cell states.

To test this, we scored each architecture type for enrichment of known cell state signatures. Using published gene sets for classical LGR5-positive crypt base columnar SCs (CBCs) and LGR5-negative regenerative/fetal-like SC populations8,14,32,33,34, we found that moderately and highly fragmented architectures were enriched for regenerative/fetal-like programmes, whereas classical CBC signatures were enriched in glandular architectures (Fig. 2e,f and Extended Data Fig. 5g,h). EMT is a further hallmark of malignant progression, co-opted from developmental and wound-healing programmes to facilitate tumour cell dissemination35. Moderately and highly fragmented architectures were strongly enriched for published EMT signatures (Fig. 2g,h and Extended Data Fig. 5i). Thus, glandular and solid architectures associate with the classical CBC state, while fragmented architectures associate with fetal/regenerative SC programmes and EMT.

To determine whether the transcriptional signatures correspond to distinct cellular phenotypes, and to validate the relationship between cell states and architecture at scale, we applied multiplex immunofluorescence to the initial TMA cohort. MSS patient samples were stained with a panel of antibodies targeting CBC, fetal/regenerative SC, EMT and proliferative states (Methods). Single cells were segmented and clustered based on combined marker expression36 and closely related phenotypic clusters were consolidated into seven major phenotypes (Extended Data Fig. 6a). These comprised differentiated tumour cell states with varying expression levels of CDX2 and PROX1, a transcriptional repressor induced in the fetal/regenerative state that suppresses non-intestinal lineage genes to enable phenotypic plasticity8. We also detected a highly proliferative population that retained CDX2 expression. Moreover, annexin-A1 (ANXA1)-positive cells and vimentin (VIM)-positive tumour cells formed two distinct populations. ANXA1-positive cells showed increased levels of tenascin-C (TNC), a hallmark ECM protein defining the EMT programme (Extended Data Figs. 6a–c and 7a). This indicated that EMT-related features might manifest as partially overlapping programmes across phenotypic subsets rather than within a single discrete state.

We next examined the distribution of these phenotypes across tumour architectures and confirmed that fragmented regions were enriched in regenerative and VIM-positive EMT-like populations, whereas other architectures were preferentially associated with differentiated tumour cell states (Fig. 2i). Notably, fragmented architecture was not exclusively composed of regenerative or EMT-like populations, but also included areas dominated by well-differentiated or proliferative tumour cell states, indicative of state heterogeneity. Conversely, glandular architecture was not exclusively composed of differentiated cells, but also contained clusters of regenerative tumour cells (Fig. 2i and Extended Data Fig. 7a).

This partial overlap prompted us to examine whether specific architecture–cell-state combinations carried distinct prognostic information. Stratifying patients by tumour cell phenotype composition revealed that the combination of fragmented architecture and regenerative phenotype was associated with the worst survival, most prominently in the tumour core (Fig. 2j and Extended Data Fig. 7b,c), whereas cell phenotype composition alone was not predictive of outcome in CT (Extended Data Fig. 7d). Patients with fragmented regenerative tumour cores predominantly also displayed this signature at the IM, whereas the reverse was less frequent (Extended Data Fig. 7e), consistent with the state originating at the IM and propagating towards the tumour core. Collectively, combining architectural and phenotypic information provides a prognostic signature superior to either modality alone.

The tight association between cell states and tissue architecture raised the possibility that tumour architectures, like cell states, undergo dynamic transitions during cancer progression. Given that moderately and highly fragmented architectures were enriched for both EMT and regenerative SC states, we first examined whether these programmes co-exist within the same cells or define distinct populations. To assess the potential existence of hybrid EMT/regenerative states and their subsequent diversification, we mined two CRC single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets12,37. Subclustering of MSS patient tumour epithelial cells and GSEA analysis for CBC, EMT and regenerative SC signatures revealed that EMT and regenerative signatures were enriched within the same Leiden clusters in both datasets, while CBC signatures occupied separate clusters (Extended Data Fig. 8a–d). Examination of individual cells both within specific clusters and across the full dataset identified a substantial population with high co-enrichment of EMT and regenerative signatures, indicative of a hybrid state (Extended Data Fig. 8e,f). Co-expression of key marker genes including VIM, SNAI1 (encoding SNAIL1), ANXA1 and EMP1 in single cells further supported the existence of this hybrid state (Extended Data Fig. 8g). CBC signatures were not correlated with regenerative SC signatures, suggesting that cells must exit the CBC state before acquiring hybrid identity (Extended Data Fig. 8e,f).

Diffusion pseudotime analysis of a subclustered dataset encompassing CBC, regenerative and EMT states (clusters 0, 3, 4 and 6), initiated from the most stem-like cluster identified by CytoTRACE scoring38, revealed a trajectory from CBC towards a hybrid EMT/regenerative population. This trajectory was characterized by progressive downregulation of CBC and stemness-associated transcription factors ASCL2 and HMGA1 and the identity marker LGR5, followed by upregulation of regenerative SC markers and plasticity-associated KLF family transcription factors and, ultimately, upregulation of EMT transcription factors including SMAD3 and other established EMT markers39,40,41 (Extended Data Fig. 8h–j).

To spatially resolve the distribution of regenerative and EMT states within tumour tissue, we integrated spatial transcriptomics with single-cell resolution multiplex immunofluorescence across whole-tumour sections, using ANXA1 as a regenerative state marker and VIM, SNAIL1 and E-cadherin/pan-cadherin ratio as EMT markers. ANXA1-positive cells were highly enriched in fragmented regions (Fig. 3a–c and Extended Data Fig. 9a), consistent with the transcriptomic data. At the tumour–stroma boundary of moderately fragmented regions, ANXA1-positive cells also exhibited EMT features, confirming co-existence of regenerative and EMT programmes at this interface (Fig. 3a and Extended Data Fig. 9a–c). However, within fragmented regions, cells with high VIM expression were spatially segregated from ANXA1high cells, forming largely non-overlapping, spatially proximal populations (Fig. 3c,d and Extended Data Fig. 9d). SNAIL1 showed comparable expression across both populations (Fig. 3b and Extended Data Fig. 9a,c), consistent with a transcriptional continuum rather than binary state switching. We next used trajectory analyses of the spatial transcriptomes to infer the most probable routes of architecture transitions during CRC progression. Glandular architectures showed highest probability of transitioning to moderately fragmented, while solid architectures preferentially transitioned to highly fragmented (Fig. 3e). To control for potential confounding by unequal architecture representation across patients, we performed pseudotime analysis within individual patients with heterogeneous architecture distributions, using a central glandular region as the reference point and grouping regions by nearest neighbour to account for spatial proximity effects. Architecture and spatial cluster distributions within each pseudotime window were consistent near both start and end points across all three patients analysed (Fig. 3f–h and Extended Data Fig. 9e), and key CBC, regenerative and EMT marker genes showed monotonic changes along the pseudotime trajectory (Fig. 3i), indicating that architecture transitions are accompanied by corresponding changes in cell state. These transitions were recapitulated in the U-Net tile embedding space combined with multiplex immunofluorescence-based cell state classification, in which the strongest connections were found between glandular and solid architectures, and between solid and fragmented architectures, whereby transition of glandular to solid architectures corresponded to a transition from differentiated and PROX1high states to regenerative and EMT states (Extended Data Fig. 9f).

Together, these analyses demonstrate that cell states and tissue architectures co-evolve within individual patients, and that EMT and regenerative programmes form a continuum, distinct from the CBC state, which can diversify towards regenerative SC or EMT-dominant identities.

To investigate the mechanism driving architecture-linked cell state diversification, we first examined whether the distinct nuclear morphologies observed across architectures in whole-tumour resections reflected the same co-evolutionary relationship seen in the TMAs. Single-cell segmentation and quantitative nuclear morphology analyses confirmed that glandular architectures associated with nuclear elongation while fragmented architectures associated with increased circularity (Extended Data Fig. 10a), consistent with the TMA findings. We next examined microenvironmental signals potentially driving this transition. Spatial transcriptomic scoring of the tumour compartment revealed elevated TGFβ pathway activity and higher YAP transcriptional programme scores in fragmented architectures (Fig. 4a,b), and immunofluorescence confirmed increased nuclear YAP levels in these regions (Fig. 4c). Analysis of paired stromal compartments showed that fragmented regions were additionally enriched for T cell, natural killer cell and myeloid cell transcriptional signatures (Extended Data Fig. 10b). Collagen architecture in fragmented regions was characterized by increased structural complexity and reduced fibre coherence, and TNC showed similar, albeit less pronounced, architectural variation (Fig. 4d). Thus, fragmented architectures show coordinated stromal and ECM remodelling and elevated tumour cell TGFβ and YAP activity, consistent with an altered mechanical microenvironment.

To examine causative relationships between changes in the ECM mechanical microenvironment, YAP and cell states, we turned to disease modelling using patient-derived organoids that were whole-genome sequenced and validated to contain APC and TP53 mutations. A shallow scRNA-seq analysis showed that, like the in vivo tumours, the organoids also displayed hybrid EMT/regenerative SC states and mutually exclusive classic CBC and regenerative SC states (Extended Data Fig. 11a,b). We then examined whether changes in architecture could promote transition from a classic CBC to hybrid or diversified cell states. To this end we selected two independent patient-derived CRC organoid lines that, based on the scRNA-seq data, had high levels of classic LGR5/OLFM4 CBCs and only low levels of ANXA1/PLAUR regenerative SCs (lines 1 and 2; Extended Data Fig. 11a,c). We plated these two lines onto engineered colonic sheets42, where cells could grow unrestricted on flat surfaces or be confined in finger-like protruding channels (Fig. 4e–g). Analysis of RNA levels using in situ hybridization showed heterogeneous cell phenotypes with regions enriched for ANXA1high cells that were distinct from LGR5high cells (Fig. 4e). Notably, ANXA1high cells were enriched at bottoms of the confined finger-like channels whereas LGR5high cells showed no preferential localization (Fig. 4e). EMT states as marked by VIM expression were homogeneously low and distributed within the channels with partial overlap with ANXA1high cells (Fig. 4e).

Notably, while both patient lines responded to the architectures, the response of line 2 was stronger than line 1 (Fig. 4e,f). Examination of differences between these lines in the scRNA-seq data revealed that, while both lines when grown in Matrigel contained high levels of classic LGR5/OLFM4-positive SCs and low levels of ANXA1/PLAUR-positive regenerative SCs, line 2 showed a more prominent signature of β-catenin activity (Extended Data Fig. 11d), which is central to the regulation of stemness and EMT in the colon43,44. This was associated with high expression of the β-catenin target gene and plasticity transcription factor SOX48,40 (Extended Data Fig. 11c,d). Consistent with its elevated β-catenin transcriptional signature, high nuclear β-catenin was seen only in line 2 cells (Fig. 4f,g). Moreover, while YAP1 expression was not substantially different between the lines, line 2 showed higher baseline activity, as indicated by an enriched YAP transcriptional signature (Extended Data Fig. 11c,d). However, within this line, nuclear β-catenin was seen only in the unrestricted, flat areas of the engineered sheets, while it was undetectable in the channels enriched in ANXA1high cells (Fig. 4f,g). This implied that, while intrinsically high β-catenin activity correlated with the responsiveness to tissue architecture, potentially through promoting SOX4 expression, confinement of cells into channels suppressed WNT activity and promoted the emergence of an ANXA1-positive cell state. The role of β-catenin activity in tuning the responsiveness to mechanosignalling was confirmed by culturing the responsive line 2 in the presence or absence of Chir-99021 to activate canonical WNT. Upregulation of AXIN2 and the key CBC marker ASCL2 expression, as well as elevated SOX4 and β-catenin levels in the nucleus in response to β-catenin stabilization by Chir-99021 confirmed that these cells indeed were responsive to WNT manipulation despite their APC mutation (Extended Data Fig. 11e–h), consistent with previous studies45,46. Importantly, pretreatment with Chir-99021 enhanced the impact of YAP activation on ANXA1 expression (Extended Data Fig. 11i), indicating that high β-catenin/SOX4 state enhances mechanoresponsiveness.

To test whether mechanical compression was sufficient to promote SC fate transitions through YAP activation, we cultured the patient organoids in Matrigel, in which ANXA1 levels were initially low, and then subjected the organoids to mechanical compression47 (Fig. 4h,i and Extended Data Fig. 11c). Indeed, confining the organoids was sufficient to trigger emergence of ANXA1/PLAUR-positive cells with corresponding suppression of LGR5/OLFM4-positive cells (Fig. 4h). Importantly, confinement also activated YAP (Fig. 4i). However, confinement was not sufficient to promote a diversified EMT state (Extended Data Fig. 11j,k). Collectively, these data suggest that architecture-derived mechanical signals deplete the CBC state and induce a regenerative SC state but are insufficient to promote EMT, and that high nuclear β-catenin and SOX4 expression enhances the efficiency of CBC-to-regenerative SC transition.

Having earlier established that SMAD3 expression is correlated with progression towards EMT, while YAP activation is associated with mechanically driven regenerative SC states, we investigated whether the TGFβ1 and YAP pathways cooperate to drive cell-state diversification. Patient organoids (line 2) were treated with a LATS kinase inhibitor (TDI-011536)48 to activate YAP, recombinant TGFβ1 or both for 4 days, and then analysed using scRNA-seq. YAP activation triggered robust transcriptional state changes, whereas TGFβ1 alone had more-limited effects (Fig. 5a,b and Extended Data Fig. 12a). Benchmarking cell states against a previously published dataset37, which is enriched in regenerative state cells, showed that YAP activation depleted the CBC state (Fig. 5c,d), consistent with observations from the mechanical compression experiments and with CBC depletion in regenerative state cells in vivo49. YAP activation also shifted the hybrid state towards the regenerative pole of the regenerative SC-to-EMT continuum (Fig. 5b–d and Extended Data Fig. 12b). TGFβ1 alone modestly expanded the hybrid state and moderately induced EMT transcription factor expression, while combined treatment drove more robust diversification of the hybrid state into two distinct populations (Fig. 5a–d and Extended Data Fig. 12a,b).

Immunofluorescence analysis of ANXA1 and SNAIL1 confirmed the state distinctions: TGFβ1 alone induced SNAIL1 expression, whereas YAP activation increased ANXA1, but these states remained overlapping under single-treatment conditions. Only combined treatment produced spatially separated ANXA1high and SNAIL1high populations as quantified by signal anticorrelation (Fig. 5e and Extended Data Fig. 12c), recapitulating the in vivo patient distribution (Fig. 3c,d). YAP activation also triggered nuclear shape changes and loss of heterochromatin (Extended Data Fig. 12d–f). Consistently, reanalysis of the patient tumour spatial transcriptomic data confirmed that fragmented architectures showed reduced enrichment of heterochromatin regulators (Extended Data Fig. 12g,h). This was validated at the protein level by H3K9me2,3 immunostaining of patient samples (Extended Data Fig. 12i,j). Loss of heterochromatin was associated with increased nuclear circularity (Extended Data Fig. 12k), providing a mechanistic link between YAP signalling, nuclear shape and tissue architecture.

To determine whether these cell-state transitions were coupled to behavioural consequences, we analysed organoid dynamics. Untreated organoids displayed spheroid morphology with expansive low-level growth. TGFβ1 induced cohesive invasion without substantially enhancing overall net invasion, while YAP activation triggered dispersive invasion. Combined treatment further increased the prevalence of dispersive invasion (Fig. 5f and Supplementary Video 1).

To assess the in vivo relevance of these behavioural changes, patient-derived organoids (line 2) treated with LATS inhibitor, TGFβ1 or both were orthotopically transplanted into the mouse colon by endoscopy50 (Fig. 5g and Extended Data Fig. 12l). Untreated organoids did not generate visible tumours, whereas all three treatment conditions did so with variable efficiency. Mice receiving combined YAP activation and TGFβ1 treatment generated tumours (Fig. 5h,i) and, as expected, YAP activation enhanced nuclear YAP in the transplanted tumours while TGFβ1 treatment enhanced SMAD2/3 activation (Extended Data Fig. 12m). Quantitative analysis revealed that the combination treatment produced the largest tumours and the only tumours that invaded the stroma (Fig. 5i). Consistently, ANXA1 expression was elevated in YAP-activated transplants but was highest in combination-treated tumours (Extended Data Fig. 12n), collectively demonstrating that cell state diversification drives tumour aggressiveness in vivo.

Together, these experiments demonstrate that tissue architecture and stromal signals collaborate to drive cell-state diversification and invasive behaviour. Architectural confinement suppresses β-catenin signalling to promote regenerative SC identity, while soluble stromal signals enhance EMT features. The combination of mechanical, compositional and signalling inputs from the microenvironment drives diversification into distinct SC and EMT states and potentiates tumour invasion.

Identifying the mechanisms that drive intratumoural heterogeneity is central to improving diagnostic precision and treatment outcomes in CRC. We demonstrate that tumour architecture and associated nuclear morphology are strong independent prognostic indicators, and that these features co-evolve with cell states within individual tumours. Fragmented architectures specifically associate with transition from LGR5-marked adult SCs to a fetal-like/regenerative state linked to aggressive behaviour and poor prognosis8.

The prognostic power of architecture derives from its co-evolution with plastic, aggressive cell states. Chemotherapy, wounding, inflammation and mechanical damage are known to reactivate fetal and regenerative SC programmes in CRC34,51,52,53,54,55. TGFβ1 drives WNT independence and fetal-like lineage conversion in CRC organoids56, and fibrotic stromal stiffening promotes aggression across solid tumours57. Our data implicate compressive forces from stiffened ECM in distorting tissue geometry, inducing fragmentation and promoting adult-to-regenerative SC transition. The regenerative state emerges as a hybrid with EMT features that TGFβ1 and stromal signals further diversify into spatially proximal but transcriptionally distinct SC and EMT states, suggesting a continuum in which the balance of signals determines which state predominates. Coupling of this diversification to invasion explains the association between fragmented architecture and poor prognosis, supporting a model in which disrupted tissue organization and inflammatory signalling initiate a feedforward loop driving disease aggression.

Our image analysis framework, combining nuclear and epithelial membrane immunostaining with machine-learning-based classification, offers an efficient stratification strategy independent of mutational status, and suggests that quantitative architectural analysis could serve as a tractable proxy for tumour heterogeneity in clinical decision-making.

Preprocessing and segmentation were performed essentially as described previously36. In brief, TMA cores were de-arrayed using built-in tools from QuPath (v.0.5.1)62. Cores exhibiting folding artifacts, blurriness or limited tumour content were excluded from analysis. The tumour and stromal compartments were identified using a multi-label U-Net trained on 30 representative cores. Pixel-level annotations were generated manually in ilastik (v.1.4.0)64 based on pan-EPI staining.

Tumour architectural subtypes were classified through a transfer-learning pipeline comprising four stages:

Stage 1, tiling: each TMA core image was subdivided into non-overlapping 448 × 448 px (208 × 208 µm) tiles covering the full tissue area.

Stage 2, feature embedding: a multi-label U-Net was trained to segment architectural subtypes. Ground-truth masks were derived from the tumour–stroma segmentations described above, with tumour regions manually re-annotated according to three architectural subtypes: glandular (organized, lumen-forming structures), fragmented (disrupted tumour clusters) and solid (dense). Two additional classes (stroma and edge/background) were included to handle non-tumour regions. The training set comprised 196 cores selected as unambiguous archetypes of each architectural subtype. The validity of this selection was assessed by computing silhouette scores on a panel of interpretable morphological features (Fig. 1f) and comparing the archetype score to a null distribution generated from 1,000 size-matched random draws of cohort cores assigned matching per-class labels (Extended Data Fig. 2e). Each tile was then passed through the trained U-Net, and the activations at the deepest convolutional layer (bottleneck) were aggregated through global average pooling to yield a compact feature vector per tile, encoding the dominant morphological pattern without requiring a separate hand-crafted feature design.

Stage 3, tile-level classification: a multinomial logistic regression classifier (L-BFGS solver, scikit-learn) was trained on the bottleneck embeddings to assign each tile to one of five classes: glandular, fragmented, solid, stroma or edge/background. Features were standardized before classification. The training set comprised 1,154 manually annotated tiles (365 glandular, 251 fragmented, 148 solid, 199 stroma and 191 edge/background), randomly sampled from 760 cores across the cohort. Performance was evaluated using stratified fivefold cross-validation. At inference, the classifier output five class probabilities per tile; tiles dominated by edge or stroma (probability ≥ 0.5) were excluded, and the remaining glandular, fragmented and solid probabilities were renormalized to sum to one before assigning each tile to its most probable architectural subtype (argmax).

Stage 4, patient-level classification: tile-level predictions were aggregated per sample. The proportion of tumour-classified tiles belonging to each architectural subtype was computed separately for the CT (centre of the tumour) and IM. If a single subtype accounted for more than 50% of tiles, the sample was classified as that dominant architecture; otherwise, it was labelled mixed. The trained U-Net can be reused to extract morphological embeddings from other colorectal cancer datasets, provided that a membrane staining is available and that tissue tiles are of comparable physical size to those used in this study (448 × 448 pixels, corresponding to approximately 208 × 208 µm at the imaging resolution used here); tiles acquired at a different pixel resolution can be resampled (downsampled or upsampled) to match the model’s input size. However, the tile-level logistic regression classifier generally requires retraining to account for dataset-specific differences such as staining variability, imaging conditions or cohort composition. This is the strategy that we followed when applying the pipeline to the validation and multiplex datasets in this study: the U-Net embeddings were extracted directly, while the classifier was retrained on a new set of annotated tiles for each dataset. For the validation dataset, the classifier was retrained by supplementing the original training set with 205 additional annotated tiles (44 glandular, 49 fragmented, 28 solid, 50 stroma and 34 edge/background). For the multiplex dataset, 1,886 tiles were independently annotated (740 glandular, 446 fragmented, 123 solid, 491 stroma and 86 edge/background) and used to train a separate classifier. The pretrained U-Net model is available in ONNX format at Zenodo (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20343193).

Whole-core fluorescence images were segmented into 12 tissue compartments with a multi-scale tumour/stroma classifier, and tumour islands were defined as connected components of the tumour class on the cleaned compartment map after upsampling to image resolution. The stromal compartment was restricted to a strict definition that excluded muscle, vessel, adipose, dense-stroma and debris classes. For each tumour island, a peri-tumoural region of interest was constructed as the 50 µm band immediately outside the island border, intersected with the strict stroma mask. Within this region, fibre body masks were obtained for the collagen I and TNC channels (analysed independently) using a fine-tuned U-Net applied at a probability threshold of 0.5.

From each body mask, we derived three independent descriptors of the underlying fibre network65: (1) the box-counting fractal dimension of the binarized mask, capturing network roughness and branching complexity; (2) the mean structure-tensor coherence within the mask, ranging from 0 for isotropic networks to 1 for perfectly aligned fibres; and (3) the median local fibre thickness, computed as twice the Euclidean distance transform sampled along the fibre skeleton and converted to micrometres using a pixel size of 0.325 µm. Features were emitted per (image × island × channel × distance band × stroma definition × architecture) so that all downstream summaries remained traceable to the underlying islands and fluorescence pixels. To avoid within-image pseudoreplication, every subsequent comparison was performed on per-(image × architecture) means, obtained by averaging each feature across the islands sharing the same architecture within an image.

Whole-slide image immunostainings were registered using the warpy workflow in ImageJ and QuPath. Whole-slide images were subsequently divided into non-overlapping tiles of 1,000 × 1,000 pixels and selected for downstream analysis in a manner analogous to TMAs, including tumour/stroma classification and nuclear segmentation. For each nucleus, the average nuclear intensity of markers (ANXA1, SNAIL1) was quantified. For cytoplasmic markers (E-cadherin, ANXA1, VIM), the nuclear contour was dilated by 5 pixels to approximate the cytoplasmic region, from which the average cytoplasmic intensity was computed. Single-cell profiles were then clustered using the Leiden algorithm at a resolution of 0.1.

For each whole-slide-image tile, we computed a spatial-coherence score that quantifies whether cells with the same label group together or segregate. First, for every cell we counted its 12 immediate closest neighbours that shared the same label and averaged this count across all cells in the tile. Next, we generated a null range by randomly shuffling the position of all cells 100 times, repeating the same neighbour counting for each shuffle; the average value from these shuffled sets was taken as the expected mean. The score was then calculated as (observed – expected_mean)/(expected_max – expected_mean). A score above zero means cells with the same label cluster together more than would be expected by chance (attraction), a value of zero corresponds to a random arrangement and a negative value denotes spatial repulsion, where same-label cells are less likely to be adjacent than expected by chance.

Reads were mapped using STAR (v.2.7.10a)79 (--soloType CB_UMI_Complex --soloCellFilter None --outSAMtype BAM SortedByCoordinate --soloFeatures GeneFull_Ex50pAS --soloAdapterSequence NNNNNNNNNGTGANNNNNNNNNGACA --soloCBmatchWLtype 1MM --soloCBposition 2_0_2_8 2_13_2_21 3_1_3_9 --soloUMIposition 3_10_3_17 --soloCBwhitelist BD_CLS1_v2_draft.txt BD_CLS2_v2_draft.txt BD_CLS3_v2_draft.txt --runRNGseed 1 --soloMultiMappers EM --readFilesCommand zcat --outSAMattributes NH HI AS nM NM MD jM jI MC ch CB UB GX GN). Raw counts were imported as AnnData (v.0.10.7)80 objects in Python (v.3.9.11). We removed low-complexity barcodes using the knee plot method (1,500–3,000 counts per cell) and further filtered out cells with a high mitochondrial mRNA percentage (>50%). Doublets were predicted with scrublet (v.0.2.3)81. Finally, each sample’s gene expression matrix was normalized using scran (v.1.26.2)82, with Leiden clustering83 input at resolution 0.5.

At this stage, the samples were merged with scanpy (v.1.10.1)75. For 2D embedding, expression values were cell cycle regressed using sc.tl.score_genes_cell_cycle with G2M and S-phase gene lists from ref. 84 and sc.pp.regress_out. The expression matrix was subset to the 3,000 most highly variable genes (sc.pp.highly_variable_genes, flavor=“seurat”). We calculated and selected the top 100 PCs (sc.tl.pca), which served as basis for k-nearest neighbour calculation (sc.pp.neighbors, n_neighbors = 30). Nearest neighbours were then used as an input for UMAP layout (sc.tl.umap, min_dist = 0.3). Leiden clusters were calculated using sc.tl.leiden at resolution 0.7. Pseudotime and gene set enrichment analyses were performed as described for the spatial transcriptomics datasets. For reanalysis of the published tumour in vivo data, we chose a cell in the most stem-like cluster according to CytoTRACE analysis.

For generation of consensus gene sets, EMT gene sets (mesenchymal67, matrix remodelling68, TGFB activation (TGFB KEGG, TGFB CORE GENES/TGFB1/TGFB2)68,69, EMT activation (EMT Core Genes67, EMT Hallmark70, pEMT71, EMT-II (MP13)72) and SC gene sets (crypt base32, LGR5 intestinal SCs33, fetal8, regenerative34 and revival colonic SCs52) were merged and overlapping genes within and across gene sets were removed to generate the Core-EMT, Core-CBC and Core-RegSC gene sets.

All cells in the dataset were scored for the Core-EMT, Core-CBC and Core-RegSC gene sets using the Scanpy sc.tl.score_genes function. The scores were then converted to a per-dataset rank rescaled to [0, 1]. Cells were then ordered across the CBC to RegSC–EMT axis as a composite_trajectory = (RegSC_rank01 + EMT_rank01 − CBC_rank01 + 1)/3. Cells with composite_trajectory in the top 30% of the per-dataset distribution were classified hybrid.

For distribution across the EMT–RegSC axis, two curated marker panels were scored per hybrid cell with sc.tl.score_genes (random_state = 0): a RegSC-pole panel (GPRC5A, TSPAN1, PDZK1IP1, ANXA1, ANXA3, S100A6, S100A11, LCN2, SAA1, SAA2, KRT16, KRT17, SPP1, CD44, EMP1 and TACSTD2) and an EMT pole panel (SNAI1, SNAI2, ZEB1, ZEB2, TWIST1, TWIST2, VIM, FN1, S100A4, ACTA2, MMP14, PLAU, PLAUR, SERPINE1, TIMP1). Each panel score was z-scored within its dataset, and the pole axis was defined as axis_pole = z(score_EMT_pole) − z(score_RegSC_pole). The pole-axis shift among hybrid cells was computed as Cliff’s δ = 2 × U/(n_c × n_Unt) − 1, where U is the Mann–Whitney U statistic of axis pole over the two hybrid groups. δ ∈ [−1, +1]; δ < 0 represents that hybrid cells of c are shifted toward the regenerative pole relative to untreated-hybrid cells, δ > 0 represents that hybrid cells of c are shifted toward the EMT pole. By default, untreated sits at δ = 0.

Hydrogel scaffolds were engineered as described previously42,85. A PDMS stamp (see below) with a crypt-mimicking array of pillar topography was placed in the central top reservoir of the chip. To generate the ECM for the chip, bovine TeloCol-6 type I collagen solution (5225-50mL, Advanced Biomatrix) was neutralized according to the manufacturer’s instructions and then mixed with Matrigel (356231, Corning) at a 75/25 (v/v) ratio. After gel injection through gel-loading port, hydrogel was polymerized at 37 °C, 5% CO2 (hereafter referred to as incubator) for 15 min. After ECM polymerization, all chip reservoirs were topped up with PBS or cell culture medium.

Stamps featuring crypt-mimicking microtopography were fabricated from PDMS by replica moulding of 2PP-printed models. First, arrays of colon crypt-mimicking micropillars were designed in Autodesk Fusion. Several topographies were tested and optimized, the final microtopography design consists of the slightly conical pillars, ranging from 50 to 120 µm in diameter (top to bottom) and 700 µm in height. The 3D model was printed using a NanoOne (UpNano) 2PP printer on glass coverslips with UpPhoto resin and a ×10/0.4 NA objective. To generate a master mould with inverted topography, the printed structures were plasma-activated and silanized overnight using vapour-phase trichloro(1H,1H,2H,2H-perfluorooctyl) silane (Sigma-Aldrich). PDMS (Sylgard 184, Dow Corning; 10:1 base-to-curing agent ratio) was poured over the master, degassed under vacuum, and cured at 80 °C for 12 h. The resulting PDMS replica was cut, plasma-activated and silanized overnight as above. This PDMS negative was then used repeatedly as a mould for casting final PDMS stamps using the same procedure (Sylgard 184, 10:1 ratio, cured at 80 °C for 12 h).

Organoids were dissociated using TrypLE Express (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and resuspended at 40 million cells per ml in Advanced DMEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10 µM Y27632 after which 5 µl of cell suspension was plated onto each chip. Cells were allowed to settle for 1 h in a cell culture incubator, after which the chips were filled with Y27632-supplemented medium. The next day, Y27632 was removed and cells were cultured for 5 days. Chips were fixed in 4% PFA for 16 h at 4 °C, placed into 3D-printed custom histomoulds, filled with HistoGel (Thermo Fisher Scientific), embedded in paraffin and sectioned as described previously86. The samples were then processed for RNA in situ hybridization or immunofluorescence as described below.

In situ hybridization was performed using RNAscope technology (Advanced Cell Diagnostics) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. In brief, organoids were fixed in 4% PFA for 30 min at 37 °C, rinsed with PBS, dehydrated in a graded ethanol series and air dried. The slides were then rehydrated and 2 drops of hydrogen peroxide were added to each slide for 10 min. Primary antibodies were added and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The slides were washed and fixed with 4% PFA for 30 min at room temperature. After PBS washes, three drops of 1:15 diluted Protease Plus were added to slides and incubated for 10 min, followed by PBS washes.

RNA probes were prewarmed for 10 min at 40 °C, cooled down and added onto slides. The slides were incubated with probes for 2 h at 40 °C, washed twice with wash buffer, subjected to stepwise hybridization with amplifier solutions for 15 min each at 40 °C, followed by application of blocker solutions. Finally, samples were incubated with Alexa-conjugated secondary antibodies diluted in co-detection antibody diluent for 30 min at room temperature. After washes and brief incubation with DAPI, the slides were mounted in Elvanol and imaged. The following probes were used: ANXA1-c3 465411, LGR5-c2 311021, OLFM4-c1 311041, VIM-c1 310441. Images were obtained using the Zeiss LSM980 system using ×20 air and ×40 water objectives. Channels were acquired sequentially at room temperature using Zeiss Zen Blue software (v.3.13).

Organoids were fixed in 4% PFA for 1 h at room temperature, permeabilized in 0.6% Triton X-100 for 30 min, blocked with 5% BSA and 5% goat serum in PBS for 2 h, after which primary antibodies were added in blocking solution and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After multiple washes in PBS, secondary Alexa Fluor-conjugated antibodies were added in 1% BSA, 1% goat serum for 2 h at room temperature. Organoids were then mounted in ProLong Gold (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The following primary antibodies were used: ANXA1 (Abcam, ab214486, 1:1,000, overnight, 4 °C), YAP1 (Santa Cruz, sc-101199, 1:500, overnight, 4 °C), active YAP (Abcam, ab205270, 1:100, overnight, 4 °C), β-catenin (Cell Marque, 224M-14, 1:100, overnight, 4 °C), SNAIL1 (Santa Cruz, sc-271977, 1:50, overnight, 4 °C), HP1α (Cell Signaling, 2616, 1:200, overnight, 4 °C), lamin A/C (Cell Signaling, 2032, 1:200, overnight, 4 °C), SOX4 (Abcam, ab236557, 1:400, overnight, 4 °C), pSMAD2/3 (Cell Signaling, 3108, 1:100, overnight, 4 °C). Images were obtained using either the Zeiss LSM980 or Nikon Yokogawa CSU-W1 system with ×20 air and ×40 water objectives. Channels were acquired sequentially at room temperature using Zeiss Zen Blue software (v.3.13) or NIS Elements (AR 5.41.01).

To mechanically compress the organoids, organoids grown in Matrigel for 5 days were replated on top of Matrigel-coated 12–20 mm round glass coverslips and allowed to attach for 24 h. After attachment, the organoids were sandwiched with another 12–20 mm coverslip with a weight applying 200 Pa of vertical stress (generating a 45% reduction in organoid height) for 20 h.

For 3D organoid imaging, single cells were first embedded in 8 mg ml−1 Matrigel and, after 4 days of culture, were transferred either to fresh Matrigel or pH-adjusted 1 mg ml−1 collagen I matrices. The organoids were treated with 5 µM TDI-011536 (MedChemExpress, HY-150042), 10 ng ml−1 TGFβ1 (StemCell Technologies, 78067.1) or both with daily medium replenishment while being live-cell imaged every 30 min with a Sartorius Incucyte S3 incubator microscope for up to 4 days.

Bright-field images were segmented using Cellpose4-SAM87 and edge detection followed by morphological gap-closing and hole-filling to obtain binary organoid masks that were imported to ImageJ (Fiji). MorphoLibJ88 was used to quantify convexity, Geodesic diameter, Geodesic elongation and organoid area that were scaled between 0 and 1 to initially classify the organoids into expanded, dispersive or cohesive classes in an unbiased manner. As the resolution of the masks was not sufficient to accurately capture surface structures, the classifications were all manually inspected further and assigned to the final classes.

RNA was isolated using the NucleoSpin RNA plus Kit (Macherey-Nagel). RNA quality was assessed using the high-sensitivity RNA Screen Tape Analysis (TapeStation, Agilent) and subsequently 500 ng of RNA was reverse transcribed applying the SuperScript IV VILO Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. PCR was performed using the PowerUp SYBR Green Mastermix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) on the QuantStudio5 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Gene expression was quantified using the ΔΔCt method with normalization to B2M. The following primers were used. B2M: forward primer, GAGTATGCCTGCCGTGTGAA; reverse primer, GCTTACATGTCTCGATCCCACT; AXIN2: forward primer, GCGCTGACGGATGATTCCAT; reverse primer, TGGGTTCTCGGGAAATGAGGT; ASCL2: forward primer, CGCCTACTCGTCGGACGACAG; reverse primer, GCCGCTCGCTCGGCTTCCG.

Mouse colonoscopy was performed using the TELE PACK+VET system (Karl Storz, RP101S1). Illumination was provided by a xenon light source connected through the Adapt. KST fibre-optic light cable/Xenon 100 (495KS). The system includes a camera control, air insufflation units and an integrated endoscopic video unit with built-in documentation and memory storage. Colonoscopy imaging was performed using an integrated multipurpose Hopkins Telescope (67031BA) with an outer diameter of 11.5 Fr. Images were acquired using an IMAGE1 S HX FULL HD Camera Head (TH110). Mice were anaesthetized with isoflurane and organoid fragments corresponding to 5 × 105 single cells were transplanted into the colonic submucosa using a Hamilton syringe (Hamilton, Reno, 7656-01) equipped with a transfer needle for sample loading (7770-02) and an injection needle (7803-05). The needle was carefully inserted into the colonic submucosa, and 50 µl of organoid fragments suspended in 1 mg ml−1 rat collagen type 1 (Sigma-Aldrich, C3867) supplemented with 10% Corning Matrigel (Corning, 356231) was delivered and generated a visible submucosal bleb. The mice were treated for 5 days after transplantation with the LATS kinase inhibitor TDI-011536 (MedChemExpress, HY-150042). TDI-011536 was prepared at a concentration of 5 mg ml−1 by emulsification in 10% Kolliphor HS 15 (Merck, 42966) in PBS and administered by oral gavage twice daily at a dose of 2.5 mg per kg. Vehicle-treated animals received 10% Kolliphor in PBS alone.

TGFβ1 treatment was performed using human recombinant TGFβ1 (StemCell Technologies, 78067.1) within Affi-Gel Blue beads (Bio-Rad Laboratories, 153-7301). In brief, beads were washed four times with ice-cold bi-distilled water and incubated overnight with 500 µl of 50 µg ml−1 TGFβ1 in PBS supplemented with 10 mM HEPES. TGFβ1-loaded beads were mixed with organoid fragments in collagen solution at a final proportion of 10% (v/v) before implantation. For dual-treatment experiments, animals received both TDI-011536 administration and TGFβ1-loaded beads as described above. Each treatment group consisted of four mice (n = 4). Mice were monitored three times per week, and colonoscopy imaging was performed every 2 weeks for 4 weeks before euthanasia.

Tissue biopsies (intestinal rolls) were fixed in 4% PFA, embedded in paraffin and sectioned. Sections were de-paraffinized using a graded alcohol series and antigen retrieval was carried out using target retrieval solution (Dako) at pH 6 or pH 9 in a pressure cooker. Unspecific binding was blocked using 5% normal goat serum, 3% BSA in PBS, after which antibodies were diluted in DAKO antibody diluent and incubated overnight in a moist chamber at 4 °C. After PBS washes, Alexa-Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies were diluted in 1% BSA in PBS, incubated for 1 h at room temperature, after which samples were mounted in Elvanol. The following primary antibodies were used: ANXA1 (Abcam, ab214486), pSMAD2/3 (Cell Signaling, 3108) and active YAP (Abcam ab205270). Images were obtained using the Zeiss LSM980 system with ×20 air and ×40 water objectives. Channels were acquired sequentially at room temperature using Zeiss Zen Blue software (v.3.13). The tumour area was quantified using ANXA1 staining to generate a ROI mask in Fiji. The number of ROIs was used to extract the lesion number.
