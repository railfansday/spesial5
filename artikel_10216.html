Every SSL certificate comes with a limited lifespan. Following the latest update from the CA/Browser Forum, the maximum lifetime of a certificate has fallen from 398 days to 200, and it will drop to 100 days in March 2027 and 47 days in 2029. Renewal is no longer an annual chore. It’s an ongoing task that only stays manageable with automation.

So what does this mean if you already look after sites with SSL certificates, and will it bring drastic changes? For many setups, very little will change. For others, one missed renewal can mean an outage. This article explains what’s changing and what to do if you’re in the second group.

Today, an SSL certificate is valid for 200 days at most. The limit applies when a certificate is issued, so the ones you already hold keep their original expiration date.

Where you feel it depends on how your certificates renew. Sites on managed hosting platforms or behind a content delivery network (CDN) usually renew automatically already, often every 90 days, so the new limit changes little. The pressure lands on setups where someone buys a certificate once a year and installs it by hand. At 47 days, that becomes about 12 renewals a year.

A certificate is a promise from a certificate authority that a key really belongs to a domain, and that promise gets less reliable the longer it runs. If a private key leaks, or a certificate is issued by mistake, it stays usable until it expires. Cutting the lifetime shrinks that window, so a problem that once lingered for over a year is now fixed in weeks.

Shorter lifetimes also make revocation, the process for canceling a bad certificate, far less critical. Revocation has never worked perfectly, but a certificate that expires within weeks matters much less if a cancellation doesn’t reach every browser.

Another reason is about people. A task done once a year is easy to forget and hard to check from outside. Browser makers have pushed for years to move the industry onto automatic renewal, on the logic that a process that runs every few weeks gets tested constantly. If it breaks, you notice quickly instead of discovering it on the day the certificate expires.

For most people, the first effect is simply more work. A certificate that once needed attention every year will soon need it every few weeks. Plus, each renewal is a small chance for something to go wrong. Multiply that by dozens of sites, mail servers, and devices, and the chance of one slipping through grows quickly.

The safety nets are also thinner. If you relied on the renewal emails, nothing is watching your certificates now. Additionally, with a shorter life, there’s less time between this should have renewed and this has expired.

An expired certificate doesn’t make a site slow or dated. It makes it unreachable for most visitors. Chrome, Firefox, and Safari all show a full-page warning that the connection isn’t private, and most people leave instead of clicking through. If the site uses HSTS, a setting that tells browsers to connect only over secure links, there is often no way to click through at all, and visitors are blocked outright until the certificate is fixed.

The damage doesn’t stop at the home page. Checkouts, contact forms, and logins that depend on the secure connection fail along with it, often without an error message that points at the real cause. Other systems break, too, because apps and integrations that call your site over a secure link refuse to connect to an expired certificate.

Nothing about your site has to change for any of this to happen. A date passes, and a site that worked on Friday is down on Monday. In a 47-day lifetime, that date comes around about every six weeks.

Once a monitor is watching your certificates, the next question is when it should speak up.

Most expiration alerts are set to a fixed number of days: warn at 30 days left and escalate at seven. That works for a certificate that lasts a year and renews with a month to spare. It breaks when the whole certificate lasts for a limited number of days.

Here’s why: a healthy certificate renews with about a third of its life still left. If your alert appears earlier than that, it goes off on certificates that are doing nothing wrong, and soon everyone ignores it. As lifespans shrink, a fixed 30-day alert will start doing exactly that.

The fix is to set the alerts based on lateness, not on how close the expiry date is. Warn when a quarter of the lifetime is left, since a renewal is overdue by then, and escalate when a tenth is left. Because these are shares of the lifetime, they keep working, however short lifespans get.
