Hospital visits for heart failure and heart attacks rose faster when states legalized recreational cannabis

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

After recreational cannabis legalization, hospital stays for heart failure, heart attack and atrial fibrillation rose 2% to 4% a year faster.

Voting to legalize recreational cannabis feels like a decision about the people who choose to use it. The hospitals in a state get a vote too, in the form of who shows up at the door.

Legal sales began in the western United States in 2014. After that, hospital stays for heart failure, heart attack, and a common irregular heartbeat climbed faster there than in the rest of the country. The gap was 2% to 4% a year for each diagnosis. It did not appear for conditions cannabis has no known link to.

Samuel Slavin, a cardiologist at Boston University’s Chobanian & Avedisian School of Medicine, led the analysis of more than 50 million hospital records with colleagues at the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health and Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center. His team can’t say cannabis caused any one of those stays.

In an interview with Earth.com, Slavin was careful about what the numbers mean. “This is a modest relative increase. For any one person, the associated risk is likely small. But across millions of people, even small shifts can mean a large number of additional hospital visits,” he said.

The team tracked six diagnoses, and three of them climbed faster in the West after legal sales began. Hospital stays for atrial fibrillation, an irregular heartbeat, rose 2.3% a year faster than in other regions. Heart attacks rose 3.1% faster, and heart failure 3.7% faster.

The gaps are yearly, so they stack. After five years of legal sales, heart failure admissions in the West would sit roughly a fifth above what the rest of the country’s trend would predict.

Stroke and dangerous clots in the veins trended upward too, as did a fast rhythm from the heart’s lower chambers. The amounts were small enough that chance could explain them.

The West had started out healthier on every count. Before legalization, it recorded about 206 heart failure stays per 100,000 people a year, against 333 elsewhere.

The records came from the National Inpatient Sample, a federal database that collects one in five hospital discharges from states covering about 97% of Americans. Slavin’s team pulled 50,042,564 of them, from 2012 through 2018.

Colorado opened the first legal recreational shops in January 2014, and Washington followed that July. One more western state joined each year after that.

By the end of the study, nearly everyone in the Census Bureau’s Western region lived in a state with legal sales or next door to one.

Outside the West, no shop opened until Massachusetts in late November 2018, too late to matter. That timing made the comparison possible.

So the team compared the West’s trend before and after legalization with the trend everywhere else, a standard way to judge a policy. They adjusted for patients’ age, sex, race, and ZIP code income, and checked that the two regions had been moving in parallel before the first shop opened.

Two checks guard the result. Hospital stays coded as cannabis-related rose 13.6% a year faster in the West, so the method could see legalization at all. Stays for urinary stones and opioid problems, which cannabis is not thought to affect, did not budge.

Cannabis has a plausible route to the heart. THC, its main active compound, speeds the heart rate and raises blood pressure, and it may make blood more likely to clot.

Earlier studies had linked use to arrhythmias and heart attacks, though others found nothing. All of them, the authors wrote, struggled to separate cannabis from the pain, anxiety, or other drugs that often come with it.

Comparing regions rather than people sidesteps that problem, and legalization is also tied to more use among older adults, who already carry the most heart risk.

One earlier study of the same kind found cardiac deaths rose 1.3% to 2.3% after states passed medical cannabis laws. The authors read the two results as pointing the same way.

Slavin kept the result in proportion. “I think these findings matter more for policymakers than for someone deciding whether to visit a local dispensary,” he said.

States, Slavin added, should treat legalization with the same care as a new drug, and could build heart data collection into the policy from the start.

The hospital database only allows comparisons between census regions, so the whole West counted as legal from the start even though most of its states joined later. That would tend to shrink the gap, not inflate it.

The records also can’t say whether patients smoked cannabis or ate it, whether illegal use changed, or whether some other western trend began the same year by coincidence. And the records stop in 2018, before the legal market had matured.

Legalization changes many things at once, Slavin told Earth.com, including how many people use cannabis, how often, which products they choose, and how they interact with the health system. The study, he said, looks at whole populations and can’t show that cannabis caused any particular heart problem.

Whether the risk comes from smoke, from THC, or from something else is the question the team can’t answer yet. Slavin said those answers matter because they would tell people which products and ways of using carry more or less risk.

Two routes could get there. Sales records could show whether edibles and inhaled products differ in their effects, the authors wrote. And states still weighing legalization, Slavin said, could roll it out in staggered, randomized steps and collect heart data as they go.

Slavin also said cannabis’s Schedule I status, the most restrictive federal category, makes it harder to study. In his view, the findings strengthen the case for moving it to a less restrictive one.

For now, his advice is narrow. “People who use cannabis – especially those with existing heart conditions or risk factors – may want to talk with their doctor,” he said.

The full study was published in the journal BMJ Open.
