Putting down your phone may have less to do with willpower than you think

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Problematic internet use may affect over 10% of people worldwide, prompting experts to call for safer digital platforms.

You pick up your phone to answer one message. An hour later, you’re still scrolling through videos you never meant to watch.

That lost hour is usually put down to poor willpower. A report from an international panel of experts treats it instead as a global public health concern.

According to their estimate, problematic internet use may affect more than 10% of people worldwide.

Part of the blame, the authors argue, lies with the way apps and websites are designed by technology companies.

The report comes from the Lancet Psychiatry Commission on global action on problematic usage of the internet.

Psychiatrists, psychologists and public health researchers from several countries began the work in February 2023.

People who had struggled with their own internet use were involved from the start. They served as advisers throughout the project.

No new experiment was run. Instead, the commission reviewed existing studies and turned the findings into recommendations for governments.

Some online habits are already recognized as medical conditions. Gaming disorder and gambling disorder both appear in the World Health Organization’s ICD-11 classification.

Compulsive social media use and problem online shopping haven’t received that status yet.

Then there’s cyberchondria. That’s when someone keeps searching their symptoms online and grows more anxious with every search.

Not everyone who spends hours online has a disorder. The researchers describe a spectrum, running from useful, productive time online to habits that seriously disrupt daily life.

Zsolt Demetrovics is a professor of psychology at ELTE Eötvös Loránd University in Budapest and at Flinders University in Australia.

He sat on the commission’s four-member steering group, which was chaired by Naomi Fineberg of the University of Hertfordshire.

“Digital technologies are now woven into almost every aspect of modern life and bring enormous benefits,” Demetrovics said.

The commission isn’t trying to get people offline. Its focus is on stopping everyday use from turning harmful.

Internet use is now reported by an estimated 6 billion people, or roughly three-quarters of the world’s total population.

Social media platforms are used for an average of 3.5 hours per day by people aged 16 to 24.

Rates of problematic use above 10% have often been reported in meta-analyses that included pandemic-era studies.

A prevalence of around 7% had been estimated for the period before the COVID-19 pandemic.

Estimates vary depending on how researchers define and measure problematic internet use. One pandemic-era study put the figure at 25%, but stricter criteria brought it down to 7.9%.

Researchers still cannot say whether the problem is growing worldwide. Even the lowest estimates, however, represent millions of people.

Studies suggest that gaming disorder affects 2% to 12% of people. Around 5% struggle with social media use, while 16% struggle with compulsive online shopping.

Problems with gambling, gaming and pornography are more common among males.

Females more often struggle with social media, online shopping and cyberchondria.

Higher rates have also been reported in low-income and middle-income countries than in wealthier ones.

Young people seem especially vulnerable. Brain changes during the teenage years can make it harder to control impulses, which may be part of the reason.

Think about what happens when a video ends. Another one starts on its own, and there’s always something else waiting.

Features such as infinite scroll, autoplay and personalized recommendations can make it hard to stop.

The commission also flagged unpredictable rewards, gambling-like features, pop-ups and tools that invite people to compare themselves with others.

Andrea Czakó, a senior research fellow in psychology at Flinders University, was one of the report’s authors.

“Many digital products are intentionally designed to capture and hold our attention,” Czakó said.

The ever-increasing power of artificial intelligence to strengthen such designs and algorithms was flagged as a further concern.

The effects of AI chatbots on young people were described by the authors as barely investigated.

Most gambling and gaming and social media platforms are owned by a relatively small number of transnational corporations.

Ownership by a handful of gatekeeper firms was seen by the commission as both a challenge and an opportunity.

Powerful technology companies have tended to resist regulation. Natural checkpoints in the market could nonetheless be required to help enforce international standards.

A similar argument about design and young people was made in a recent Lancet series on digital media.

Problematic internet use frequently co-occurs with ADHD, anxiety, depression, sleep disturbances and eating disorders.

But which problem comes first? Most studies examined people at a single point in time, making it difficult to determine which problem came first.

In gambling disorder, however, other mental disorders were found to precede the gambling problem in about 75% of instances.

Less evidence exists for the direction of such relationships in other forms of problematic internet use.

Some longitudinal studies have linked online social activity in young people to subsequent ADHD symptoms, but not the reverse.

Poor sleep may help explain why problem social media use is linked to depression and anxiety.

Separate research has tied screen use in bed to a higher risk of insomnia among young adults.

In the commission’s view, these problems can feed each other. Low mood may push someone online, and heavy use may then make things worse.

How much does problematic internet use cost society? Few estimates of the economic costs or societal burdens were found to exist.

A worked example for the UK was therefore produced by the commission using a prevalence-based cost-of-illness approach.

A base case of about 5% prevalence and around 3.3 million affected individuals was assumed for this example.

The total annual economic cost of problematic use in the UK was conservatively put at approximately £10 to £12 billion.

That amount corresponds to about 0.4% of UK gross domestic product.

About half of the total was found to come from hard-to-see losses such as foregone productivity and quality-of-life decrements.

Most of the burden of problematic internet use was therefore judged to fall outside traditional health-care spending.

The precise figures were acknowledged to be open to debate by the authors of the model.

Gaming disorder had been estimated in one earlier study to cost South Korea approximately $3.5 billion per year.

The commission surveyed addiction societies in 38 countries across Europe, Asia, Oceania, the Americas and Africa.

In 31 of them, treatments such as cognitive behavioral therapy were available. This therapy helps people notice and change unhelpful patterns of thinking and behavior.

Yet 30 of the 38 societies said a lack of professional certification was a major gap.

Cognitive behavioral therapy has the strongest evidence so far, especially when it’s delivered in groups.

Still, few clinical trials have tested treatments for problematic internet use, and their quality is often low.

Regulatory frameworks have been used with success within similar areas of public health and safety such as alcohol and gambling.

A wide range of measures with potential for the regulation of problematic internet use was suggested on that basis.

Safety by design was described in the report as a key principle for regulating digital products and platforms.

Age restrictions and product disclosure of risks and harms and the enforcement of safety and quality standards were also recommended.

Australia was the first country to pass a law restricting social media access for children under 16. Platforms there must take reasonable steps to keep under-16s from holding accounts.

In the UK, the Age-Appropriate Design Code recommends limiting features such as autoplay and infinite scroll for children.

In the US, the Surgeon General has called for warning labels on social media platforms. A separate trial has tested how teenagers respond to such warnings.

Countries including Thailand, Vietnam, South Korea and China have introduced restrictions on online gaming time.

The outcomes of these policies have been controversial and their effectiveness has been questioned in recent research.

Loot boxes, which hand out random rewards in video games, have been classified as gambling in Belgium. Enforcement of the resulting ban has been difficult.

Restrictive school smartphone policies might reduce smartphone usage during the school day according to recent UK data.

No significant difference in overall phone usage or mental wellbeing among young people was made by such policies.

Some projects are trying prevention instead of restriction.

A European project called BootStRaP recruits young people through schools and offers them flexible support through an app.

“The challenge is finding ways to reduce harms while preserving the many benefits of being connected,” Demetrovics said.

Estimates of prevalence were acknowledged to vary substantially according to the definitions and assessment instruments and thresholds and regions used.

Proxy variables such as screen time have been used even in high-quality studies. Screen time is not equivalent to problematic use.

The effectiveness of many of the regulatory policies described in the report is yet to be fully evaluated.

The existing evidence base was nonetheless considered sufficient by the commission to justify immediate national and internationally coordinated action.

Large-scale longitudinal studies with standardised assessment were called for to clarify the course and outcomes of problematic use.

Future research could test whether restricting engagement features such as autoplay and infinite scroll reduces problematic internet use.

The full study was published in the journal The Lancet Psychiatry.
