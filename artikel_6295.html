Alaska Airlines Cuts Jobs As $700 Million Loss Looms, CEO Admits Overhead Costs Grew Too Fast

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Alaska Air Group is cutting about 200 jobs as CEO Ben Minicucci warns the company could lose as much as $700 million this year if current conditions persist. Higher fuel prices are the immediate problem, but Minicucci also acknowledged that overhead has grown too quickly. That is a sobering backdrop for an airline pursuing an ambitious international expansion.

According to Reuters, which reviewed Minicucci’s employee memo, the reductions affect non-contract positions, including 125 in Washington and 48 in Hawaii. No frontline, non-management positions are being eliminated. Affected employees will finish working November 13 but continue receiving pay through December 31, with severance and other support.

The cuts represent less than 1% of the group’s roughly 32,500 employees. That makes them relatively small across the company, but certainly not small to the people losing their jobs…

Minicucci warned employees that, if conditions persist, Alaska Air Group could record its first adjusted pretax annual loss outside the pandemic since 2003. That is a warning about a potential full-year result, not a $700 million loss already reported. It also concerns the parent group, which includes Alaska Airlines and Hawaiian Airlines.

In its October 20 earnings release, Alaska forecast a full-year adjusted loss of $5.00–$5.50 per share and said fuel would cost $1.8 billion more than its original annual plan. Its fourth-quarter forecast assumes fuel at $4.65–$4.85 per gallon. Third-quarter revenue rose 14% to $4.3 billion, but that commercial growth has not been enough to deliver the annual profit management expected.

Those numbers help explain why simply selling more tickets is not solving the problem.

There was another revealing part of Minicucci’s message:

“We must return to the financial discipline that has long been a key competitive advantage.”

He also acknowledged that overhead expenses had grown disproportionately to revenue in recent years, adding these costs had “grown at a disproportionate rate to revenue.”

Fuel prices are largely beyond an airline’s control, but the size and cost of its management structure are not. The question is why overhead was allowed to grow that way and whether these reductions address the problem or merely provide some near-term savings. Cutting 200 positions cannot will certainly not offset a fuel bill running $1.8 billion above plan.

It also puts management in the uncomfortable position of asking employees to absorb the consequences of spending decisions those employees may have had no role in making.

I have been following Alaska’s efforts to become a more substantial global competitor, including its latest European expansion to Athens and Paris and investment in premium cabins and lounges.

These job cuts do not automatically mean that strategy is wrong, though Alaska cannot cut its way to growth. It needs a product passengers will choose and a network that gives them a reason to return. Pulling back on customer investments when conditions deteriorate could leave Alaska with the costs of expansion but less ability to earn a premium.

Still, growth deserves scrutiny when the company is losing money. Management must be willing to adjust if demand or achievable fares do not justify the investment. Ambition is useful, but it cannot become an excuse to keep spending on something that is not working, though with every investment, it takes time for a sappling to grow into a tree and the “no pain, no gain” mantra certainly applies here too.

Minicucci intends to continue investing in the fleet, customer experience, and network while tightening spending elsewhere. That is a defensible approach…and it could be that these jobs are no longer needed with AI and simply a low-hanging fruit to trim unnecessary expense in order to focus on what is more important.

Alaska Air Group is cutting about 200 jobs while warning of a potential $700 million annual loss. Fuel costs explain much of the pressure, but management’s acknowledgment that overhead grew too quickly deserves attention too.

I want Alaska’s expansion to succeed. Heck, I want JetBlue and Alaska to merge. A stronger competitor is good for passengers. But the company needs to demonstrate that its growth can produce sustainable earnings, and employees losing their jobs should not be the only ones held accountable for restoring financial discipline.
