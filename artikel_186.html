I think it could happen in a couple of years, but it’s not just the money that you would need. I think you would need a couple of other things too. Number one, you just have to have the managers who can do it. So you remember Mark Zuckerberg; his whole thing was like, “Let’s get rid of all these middle managers.” But they have a function. And especially when you’re dealing with legacy systems, complex technical installations, and the management of just large networks of people, not just data, but people.

You kind of need those people who are in these glue functions to make sure that, hey, maybe let’s not do something on a Friday afternoon when even a small failure is going to bring down a busy air traffic controller. Maybe let’s have a technician on site who knows what they’re doing and who can oversee the work. Maybe let’s have a plan that says, hey, let’s check the factory settings when you plug in this thing.

It’s not just these giant top-line initiatives of a lot of money. It’s the baseline competence process and the ability to take things slowly when you need to. All of that is missing right now. If you think too hard about it, you can get really frustrated and despair about the future of air travel.

The thing that gives me confidence is that we’ve done it so far, even in these conditions where we’re not having massive numbers of crashes, and that we are willing to pause the system when it makes sense to do so. But you can only go on for so long like that.

I think this is really when we’re coming to a decision point, and at some point the equipment itself, regardless of what the politicians say, regardless of what the budget says, the equipment itself is going to tell us, “This is unsustainable. We can’t go on anymore.” I would rather we do the work to not get to that point if we can, because I don’t want to be the person on the airplane who hears the pilot say, “Oh, we’ve lost communication with the FAA and really have no idea what’s going on.” That’s a very scary and dangerous thing to hear.

We’re publishing this the Monday before Thanksgiving, which is historically one of the busiest air travel weeks of the entire year. Should people feel that the system is reliable right now?

Well, I was going to say that I’m staying put this Thanksgiving, so make what you will of that. I think the system, airlines, know what’s going on. Now people are on guard for that. And from what we’ve heard from the FAA, they’re not installing any new routers anytime soon. That particular risk is pretty much eliminated.

I would say your safety has never really been at risk, and people are still able to manage these crises when they happen. So I wouldn’t worry about safety. I would say give yourself a couple of extra hours either way.

For example, I flew to Boston over this past weekend. Saturday morning, even though the ground stop had been lifted, we were still delayed over an hour. It was not because of any communications problem, but because so many things had happened to put airplanes and people out of place that they were literally calling flight attendants on their day off to come in and work, and that’s what my aircraft was waiting for.

Recovery will happen. Disruptions can be managed to a certain extent. But I think if we get to another year from now and we’re still paralyzed in this world of, “Well, let’s just spend the time pointing fingers rather than doing anything meaningful,” then I think it really starts to call into question whether we do have the capacity to fix what’s wrong with the air traffic system.

FTI, the network at issue here, is due to be replaced in 2028. Just to wrap it up, what are the markers you’re looking for to see if that process is happening as intended and we are going to wind up with a better, more reliable network?

I think by the middle of next year, we’ll be able to see the pace at which some of these projects are being implemented. And so we’ll know if there’s a problem with implementation that’s going to push out timelines for the next two to three years. I mean, the FAA has gotten really good at overpromising and underdelivering over the years. There was an entirely new air traffic control system that was supposed to be put into place between 2000 and today that just died because of a lack of political will.

One of the good things that the FAA has done is actually created a website tracking a lot of these projects. And if you’re nerdy like me, you can go on there and see, okay, this week they put a router in there in Arkansas or wherever. And so that’s really going to tell us and give us the clarity that we’ve lacked over the last couple of years: the pace of some of these projects. And to give them credit, it is creating some accountability where there wasn’t really any.

But I think it will really be monitoring over the next six to nine months to see whether this has fundamentally changed the FAA’s appetite for taking risks that will get to a better system down the road, but that may cause disruption today. And that’s really what I’d be worried about: that it’s easy and it’s practically agency policy at this point to kick the can down the road. And so if this has created so much blowback that the FAA is now completely risk-averse, there may be a world where they don’t accomplish some of these things until the 2030s.

There is going to be a massively democratic Congress in power in just a few months here. All they can really do is oversight. I don’t think you want members of Congress doing the router upgrades.

They can do oversight. Do you think that oversight will lead to any meaningful change here? Or do we just have to wait until Sean Duffy and his band of merry managers are out?

The one thing that I would like to see from the incoming Congress is a lot of the restoration of the jobs that were cut by DOGE back in 2025. These are the people who should be doing this, who should be overseeing it. I don’t care what the Secretary of Transportation or Congress is or isn’t doing, but you do need these managers in place.

I think there’s a loss of those 400 people in the DOGE cuts, plus another thousand to 1500 retirements or just people quitting the FAA. You really want those people back because this is such a unique structure, and it’s such a unique kind of amalgam of partially obsolete, partially new technology. It’s the only thing like it in the world that I’m aware of. There’s only a certain number of people who even know enough to deal with it. You need those people in place before you can make those changes.

So I think if Congress is willing to make changes to bring those people back, to restore those jobs, and protect the funding for those jobs from the next random idea that someone has, then I would say that’s a good change. If it’s just more theatrics and pushing budget lines from one section to another, that would give me less confidence.
