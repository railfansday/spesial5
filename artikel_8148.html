The Top 20 Ski Resorts in the East (2027)

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

Ask 10 East Coast skiers what makes a great resort and you’ll get 10 different answers. Some want steep, challenging terrain; others want reliable grooming, family-friendly runs, or a good place to belly up to the bar after a long day of cruising. That’s why we publish SKI’s Resort Guide. Our annual Reader Resort Survey captures all of it, drawing on thousands of responses to offer an honest look at what it’s actually like to ski at the East’s top resorts.

The results aren’t simply a list of the biggest or most well-known mountains. They’re a reflection of what skiers value, where resorts deliver, and where they still have room to improve. Whether you’re planning a family vacation, looking for a new mountain to explore, or deciding where to spend your hard-earned ski days, consider the list below your inside track to the best skiing in the East—according to the people who know these resorts best: the skiers who visit them.

With one of the largest vertical drops in the state and plenty of White Mountain views, this is a resort that will satisfy every type of skier.

Wildcat is a bit of a hidden gem in the Vail Resorts portfolio. There’s no fancy base village, no slopeside lodging, and the base lodge is decidedly old-school. But readers who know this New Hampshire mountain keep coming back for what matters most: challenging terrain, easy access, convenient parking, and some of the best scenery in the East. With Mount Washington looming across the valley, the views from Wildcat are reason enough to make the trip. “The view is beautiful,” said a survey respondent. “It’s never crowded and when the resort gets natural snowfall, it’s perfect.”

At just 225 skiable acres, Wildcat punches above its weight class. Its 48 trails pack in a surprising range of terrain, from long, cruisy greens to the leg-burning steeps. Five lifts, including a fast quad, make it easy enough to get around, while the mountain’s 2,112-foot vertical drop delivers a genuine top-to-bottom experience. And when natural snow piles up, Wildcat’s higher-elevation terrain can be especially rewarding.

The trade-off is a decidedly bare-bones resort experience. Dining options are limited, the facilities could use some updating, and there’s no lodging at the base. Grooming and snow conditions can also be less consistent than at larger, more heavily developed resorts. But then, part of Wildcat’s appeal is that it hasn’t tried to become something it’s not. Set in the White Mountain National Forest, it feels more like a classic local ski hill than a polished destination resort. “Very old-school flavor,” commented one reader. .North Conway is about 20 minutes away, with plenty of places to stay, eat, and explore, as well as other nearby mountains to ski. For those willing to trade resort amenities for mountain character, challenging skiing, and a spectacular view, Wildcat is worth rediscovering. “

“This is my favorite local resort when I want to get miles in. The summit is extremely accessible with multiple ways down, including a nice long green run (Polecat), which is a stand-out feature of Wildcat for intermediate skiers like myself.”

An old-school New England mountain with no frills, just good skiing.

After a long absence from the Resort Guide rankings, Attitash is back—and it’s a reminder that good skiing doesn’t need a lot of bells and whistles. Spread across Attitash and Bear peaks, this old-school New England mountain offers a little bit of everything, from beginner-friendly cruisers to steep, challenging runs, with enough variety to keep families and mixed-ability groups happy. It’s not the biggest or snowiest mountain in the White Mountains, but it has a loyal following among skiers who appreciate its straightforward approach.

One of Attitash’s biggest draws is its location. Just a couple of hours from Boston, it’s an easy getaway for city skiers, while the nearby towns of Bartlett and North Conway offer plenty of lodging, restaurants, and nightlife. The surrounding White Mountains also put other ski areas, including Wildcat and Bretton Woods, within reach, making Attitash a convenient home base for a longer multi-resort trip.

Readers gave the resort high marks for access, lodging, and nightlife, but its lower scores for snow quality and value acknowledge some of the trade-offs. With 310 skiable acres and a spot on the Epic Local and Northeast Value passes, Attitash may not be the flashiest mountain in the region, but for skiers looking for a no-frills day on varied New England terrain, that’s part of the appeal. “Great all-around mountain with something for everyone.”

“Attitash has put in two new lifts in the last two or three years. Snowmaking has been strong. Vail has done a great job investing in Attitash since they purchased it.”

Southern Vermont’s biggest resort delivers reliable groomers, family-friendly amenities, and a seamless experience that skiers appreciate.

Stratton has the makings of an easy, enjoyable family ski vacation. Southern Vermont’s largest and tallest resort, it offers more than 670 skiable acres, 99 named trails, and enough variety to keep everyone occupied, from wide-open groomers to 160 acres of glades. Its approachable terrain, consistent grooming, and family-focused programming help explain why readers give it some of the highest marks for family friendliness. And with a charming base village—complete with the occasional covered-bridge moment—there’s plenty of appeal beyond the skiing.

Stratton is also no slouch when it comes to the comforts of a destination resort. Readers rank its dining and lodging among the best, and the après scene at Fiddlehead Biergarten is a favorite. The village has most of what you need for a weekend away, while Manchester, about 30 minutes down the road, offers more options.

Its location is both a blessing and a challenge. Easy access from much of New England and the New York metro area makes Stratton a popular choice on winter weekends, and crowds can build during peak periods. The resort’s fast lift network helps move skiers around, but readers would still like to see improvements to snowmaking and a little more personality in the base village.

The new Tamarack quad chairlift is the biggest improvement this season, replacing an aging triple and making it easier for beginner and intermediate skiers to access more of the mountain. Stratton may have slipped one spot in this year’s rankings, but its family-friendly skiing, strong dining and lodging, and convenient location continues to make it a dependable Southern Vermont favorite.

An $8.5 million detachable quad chairlift at Tamarack replaces the aging triple and marks the resort’s first major lift upgrade since 2018. Its top terminal will sit slightly farther uphill, with a 575-foot vertical rise across 10 towers, improving access for beginner and intermediate skiers and eliminating the need to skate or walk uphill to reach the Ursa Express.

“Easy to traverse the mountains. Love the ski run under the covered bridge and through the neighborhood. Fast lifts provide quick access to some nice fairly steep runs.”

This New Hampshire resort is the home mountain for plenty of Olympic skiers.

Back in the Resort Guide rankings after a hiatus, Cranmore makes a strong case for why smaller New England mountains hold such a big place in skiers’ hearts. Just outside North Conway, in the heart of the Mount Washington Valley, this family-friendly mountain pairs approachable skiing with a lively, distinctly local atmosphere. Its 170-plus acres and 60 trails may not offer the sprawling terrain of some bigger resorts, and advanced skiers looking for a challenge may want more, but Cranmore has a knack for making a ski day feel easy and fun.

This is also a mountain with serious skiing credentials: Plenty of Olympians have called it their home hill. “Far and away the best mountain for learning how to ski,” reasoned one Cranmore aficionado. “Their learning area is amazing, their instructors are fantastic, and their race teams and coaches are some of the best in the state.”

What Cranmore lacks in snowfall—it averages around 80 inches a year—it makes up for with 100 percent snowmaking coverage and a strong grooming operation. Readers gave it high marks for well-maintained trails, access, and parking, as well as the off-slope experience. Bonus: North Conway is right down the road, with plenty of lodging, dining, and après options.

Cranmore isn’t trying to be the biggest or burliest mountain in New England, but it delivers the kind of welcoming, well-run ski experience that keeps families coming back. “Great mountain for kids, families, and learners of all ages.”

“This is the best family resort in New England. Mt Washington Valley is full of fun for everyone. Cranmore does the best job with snowmaking and grooming to give you the best on-mountain experience.”

A Boston-area destination offering accessible skiing without the high price tag.

Just about an hour from Boston, Wachusett has been a go-to spot for day skiers for the last 60-plus years. The family-owned ski area offers a convenient place to get your  turns in without committing to a full weekend away. On a clear day, you can even spot the Boston skyline from the slopes. With 27 trails, eight lifts, and 110 skiable acres, Wachusett isn’t going to overwhelm anyone with its size, but readers say it delivers where it counts.

Grooming is the mountain’s calling card. Wachusett ranks among the best in the country for keeping its slopes in shape, with crews grooming twice daily and snowmaking coverage across 100 percent of the terrain. The result is a reliable supply of smooth, well-maintained trails, even when natural snowfall comes up short.

In addition to easy access, readers praise the reasonable prices, good food, and attentive guest service. “The Crowleys family that owns the mountain also skis the mountain,” said one reader. “It’s a family-owned mountain which makes it more skier-experience-oriented.” The place knows its limitations, though: With a 1,000-foot vertical drop and relatively modest terrain, it won’t satisfy everyone looking for steep pitches or a big-mountain challenge.

Weekend crowds and occasional frustrations with out-of-control skiers and riders are part of the deal at a popular local hill. But Wachusett’s loyal community clearly values what it offers: convenient access, dependable conditions, and a family-run operation that still feels personally invested in every guest’s experience. It may be small, but it has figured out how to make the most of what it has. “Feels like ‘Cheers,’” said a Wachusett local. “If you are looking for a place where everybody knows your name and  they’re always glad you came, this family-owned resort is it.”

“Love Wachusett! They do an amazing job making snow and grooming. The staff is always incredibly friendly and helpful, and I always leave with a smile on my face.”

Two distinct mountains and classic New England steeps geared toward skiers who put great terrain first.

Sugarbush has a little something for just about every skier, but it’s the challenging terrain that really sings. Spread across two distinct mountains—Lincoln Peak and Mt. Ellen—the resort covers 2,000 acres, with 111 marked trails, 28 wooded areas, and a healthy dose of classic Vermont steeps. From the tight trees and technical pitches of Exterminator Glade to the twisting turns of Rumble, there’s plenty to keep experts busy. But readers also appreciate the mountain’s variety, with approachable groomers and lower-angle terrain that make it easy for mixed-ability groups to ski together. “The variety of terrain was probably the best part,” shared one survey respondent. “Went to Mt. Ellen one day and Lincoln the next, and they have totally different vibes but both were super fun.”

Tucked into the Mad River Valley, Sugarbush has that authentic, old-school character that one associates with a Vermont ski vacation. Readers give it high marks for dining and lodging, with two convenient slopeside options at Lincoln Peak: the Clay Brook Hotel and the Lodge. This isn’t a big nightlife destination, though, so consider yourself warned if you’re looking to party late into the evening.

Readers continue to call out slow lifts and inconsistent grooming, particularly when weekend crowds descend. Still, Sugarbush’s terrain is the main attraction, and its roughly 250 inches of annual snowfall help keep the mountain’s glades, steeps, and sidecountry in good shape. For skiers who value variety and challenge over a polished resort experience, the appeal is obvious.

“Sugarbush doesn’t have the scale of Killington but there is everything you need, especially peak-to- lot skiing on both mountains. The challenge here is nothing to shake a stick at.”

Sugarbush focused much of its off-season work on keeping its lift network running reliably, resulting in decidedly unsexy but smart upgrades including replacing the haul rope on the Super Bravo Express Quad, overhauling the Green Mountain Express drive and gearbox, and addressing electrical and operational issues on the North Ridge lift ahead of a planned full replacement next season. The resort also installed 27 new snowmaking tower guns on Jester to help open upper-mountain terrain earlier, and added two new PistenBully 600 groomers to its fleet.

A Vermont favorite for families with dependable snowmaking, impeccably groomed cruisers, and a lift system that keeps things moving.

Okemo is having a strong year in the rankings, climbing five spots to No. 14—its best showing in several years. It’s a testament to the things this Vermont resort has long done well: making skiing easy, comfortable, and fun for families. Across 667 skiable acres and 123 trails, the terrain leans heavily toward intermediates, with plenty of wide-open cruisers for building confidence. And thanks to extensive snowmaking and rooming, those trails stay in good shape even when Mother Nature doesn’t cooperate.

Readers give Okemo some of their highest marks for grooming and lifts, two categories that can make or break a family ski day. The efficient lift network includes heated bubble chairs on the upper mountain, a welcome perk during a New England winter. Families also appreciate the resort’s convenient ski-in/ski-out lodging, particularly at the Jackson Gore base, along with off-slope diversions like the heated outdoor pool, ice rink, and fitness classes. The nearby town of Ludlow offers additional dining and lodging, with a classic Vermont village feel.

Okemo isn’t trying to be an expert’s playground. It ranks near the bottom for challenge and terrain variety, and advanced skiers may find themselves lapping the same handful of steeper runs. “Great for intermediates and perhaps even lower advanced skiers,” commented one reader. “ But there are also some great glades and bump runs if you know where to look.” Weekend crowds can also create bottlenecks, though readers appreciate that the mountain makes an effort to groom even its black-diamond trails.

This season marks 70 years of skiing at Okemo, and the resort is celebrating with a substantial investment in snowmaking infrastructure. With coverage across 98 percent of the mountain, the upgrades should help Okemo open terrain earlier and maintain more consistent conditions throughout the season. That’s a win-win.

“Okemo is great for everyone—so much terrain and so many lift options. Even for a busy resort, it’s well managed.”

Okemo has invested heavily in snowmaking and is upgrading over 700 snow guns while replacing fuel-burning air compressors with more energy-efficient options. The new equipment is designed to operate in temperatures about 10 degrees warmer than the previous system, helping the resort make snow earlier and recover more quickly after rain or warm spells.

This state-owned ski area might not have as many bells and whistles as some of its neighbors, but the terrain and challenge keep skiers returning year after year.

When you have some of the most demanding and varied terrain in New England, you don’t need a fancy base village to keep skiers intrigued. With 98 trails, nine lifts, and a 2,180-foot vertical drop—the largest in New Hampshire—there’s plenty to explore across the mountain. Experts gravitate toward the steep, narrow pitches and natural bumps of the upper mountain, while lower-elevation cruisers and a dedicated learning area offer options for less experienced skiers and families. “I like Cannon because it has a variety of slopes and a beautiful view of the surrounding mountains,” shared one survey respondent. “I am a disabled skier and I’m treated very well.”

The setting only adds to the appeal. Cannon’s 4,080-foot summit is the highest in the state, and its north- and northeast-facing slopes help preserve snow through the season. The mountain averages around 160 inches of snowfall annually, and when conditions line up, its rugged, often ungroomed terrain delivers the kind of skiing that keeps hard-charging locals coming back. Readers also love that Cannon remains a good value, especially compared with the increasingly expensive destination resorts in the region.

Of course, Cannon’s no-frills character isn’t for everyone. As a state-owned ski area, Cannon doesn’t have the same resources as its private competitors, and that can show. Base facilities are basic, nightlife and après options are practically nonexistent, and some readers would like to see more investment in infrastructure. But many readers see the relative lack of development as part of its charm. As one put it, “A skier’s mountain … no condos, cafés, or frou-frou bullshit. Not for p*ssies.”

“Cannon can’t be beat for the diverse terrain, the views, the access, and the vibe.  Great variety, the snowmaking is on par with the competition, and the lift system, although dated, is pretty efficient in moving skiers up the mountain.”

Cannon retired its historic red-and-yellow Aerial Tramway last year, and the state has secured $27 million to design and build a replacement. The new tram won’t be ready for the 2026–’27 season, but all ski terrain remains accessible via the chairlifts.

Cannon is also expanding free night skiing on Huckerbrook, with lights on Fridays and Saturdays from 4 to 6 p.m. through mid-February.

Big-mountain terrain plus a storybook Vermont village make Stowe one of New England’s most complete ski vacations.

Stowe has the kind of all-around appeal that’s hard to replicate. With 485 skiable acres, 116 trails, and 314 inches of average annual snowfall, it delivers a legit big-mountain experience for New England. Mount Mansfield is the main draw, with challenging steeps, classic New England tree skiing, and enough variety to keep strong skiers engaged. But there’s plenty for intermediates and families, too, making Stowe a destination that can accommodate a group with a wide range of abilities. “I like the traditional New England trails,” commented a Stowe fan. “They haven’t bulldozed everything into wide boulevards.”

The town of Stowe is one of the country’s most beloved small-town destinations, with clapboard buildings, independent shops, excellent restaurants, and a lively après and nightlife scene. Readers give the resort high marks for dining and lodging, and there are plenty of places to stay both slopeside and in town. It’s a combination that makes Stowe feel like a full-fledged vacation, not just a place to ski for a couple of days.

But all that appeal comes at a price. Stowe’s value score remains a sore spot, with lift tickets, lodging, dining, and weekend parking adding up quickly. Readers also continue to raise concerns about traffic, crowded base areas, and the parking situation, including the cost of driving on peak days. Some feel the resort could do more to manage skier numbers and ease congestion, while others worry that its increasingly upscale image is pricing out longtime Vermont skiers.

As the resort celebrates 90 years of skiing this season, its combination of serious terrain, a charming town, and abundant amenities remains tough to beat. Just be prepared to pay for the privilege—and plan your parking before you arrive.

“They have some really fun terrain, challenging steeps, and nice glades. I appreciate that they don’t overgroom and that they’ve kept a lot of the old-time New England feel on the Spruce Peak side— narrow and winding.”

Stowe’s 90th anniversary shines a light on the resort’s storied Ski & Ride School, its Ski Patrol tradition, and iconic Front Four trails on Mount Mansfield. Watch for special events throughout the season.

A Vermont favorite where family-friendly hospitality meets some of the state’s most challenging terrain.

Don’t make the mistake of writing off Smugglers’ Notch as simply a family resort. Yes, it has some of the best kid-friendly programming and guest service in the East, plus convenient lodging and even an arcade and decked-out kid’s center. But Smuggs is also a serious skier’s mountain. Spread across roughly 1,000 acres and three distinct peaks, its 78 trails offer everything from wide-open groomers on Sterling Mountain to the steep, demanding pitches of Madonna Mountain.

That combination helps explain why Smuggs scores so well in terrain variety and challenge, while also earning high marks for value and guest service. It’s a place where parents can introduce their kids to skiing, then head off to find more demanding terrain on their own—or take the kids with. Readers appreciate the mountain’s independent character and the sense that the resort is invested in creating a good experience for everyone, not just maximizing vertical or moving skiers through as fast as possible.

The biggest sticking point remains the lift system. (“More time to enjoy a conversation with a friend or loved one,” reasoned one glass-half-full skier.) Smuggs’ eight lifts include a number of aging, slow double chairs, and readers have repeatedly called for upgrades to improve comfort, capacity, and efficiency. Long waits can be frustrating, particularly around the base areas on busy days. Dining also leaves some skiers wanting more. But there’s a certain charm to the mountain’s unhurried pace, and plenty of loyalists argue that the old-school lifts are part of what keeps the place feeling like Smuggs. “This place has a real throwback vibe and has not changed much over the past 30 years.”

“Smuggs seems to have the perfect combination of terrain for everyone, a good local vibe, and a non-corporate feel.”

Smugglers’ Notch joins the Indy Pass for 2026–’27, giving passholders two days of skiing or riding, plus a third day at 25 percent off, with no blackout dates. Advance reservations are required to help manage visitation and preserve the resort’s uncrowded feel.

The season also marks Smuggs’ 70th anniversary and the first full winter under Bear Den Partners, which acquired the resort in February 2026. On the mountain, 100 new snow guns—including 80 energy-efficient fixed tower guns and 20 mobile guns—will expand snowmaking coverage and help improve early-season conditions.

New Hampshire’s most accessible resort lures the masses, which is both a blessing and curse.

Loon made one of the biggest moves in this year’s Resort Guide, climbing eight spots to break into the Top 10. Easy access is certainly part of the appeal: Set in the White Mountain National Forest and just a couple of hours from Boston, Loon is one of the most convenient major ski areas in New England, with enough terrain to keep a wide range of skiers happy. Its 403 acres span three peaks, with 73 trails and 13 lifts—including a heated bubble chair that’s a welcome luxury on a cold New England day. Readers gave Loon high marks for snow, grooming, and lifts, a combination that helps make the most of its roughly 160 inches of annual snowfall and keeps skiers moving around the mountain. “While not the most challenging ski area, it is always consistent with snowmaking and grooming,” confirmed one Loon skier.

Of course, being so close to Boston isn’t always a plus. Loon can get seriously crowded, particularly on weekends and holidays, and parking remains a sore spot for some readers. Warned one survey respondent: “Loon is a great place midweek … weekends are a whole different story.” But the mountain has become more than a turnkey day trip: The town of Lincoln offers a lively après and nightlife scene—if you make it that far. The base village’s own Paul Bunyan Room earned particular praise from readers for its beer selection and post-ski atmosphere.

It’s that mix of easy access, solid on-mountain operations, and places to unwind after the lifts stop spinning helps explain the jump in this year’s rankings. And there’s more terrain on the horizon: Construction on Black Ridge, a 272-acre, expert-only gladed expansion, is scheduled to begin in early 2027. The area won’t open for skiing until the 2027–’28 season, but when it does, it will bring Loon’s total skiable terrain to a respectable 675 acres.

“Loon Mountain is a fantastic ski resort. The town of Lincoln is amazing, so as you enter the town on your way to Loon you can already feel the excitement. There’s plenty of ski shops, restaurants, and lodging on the main drag which is great! The skiing at the resort was a blast!”

A little off the beaten path, this resort is well loved for fast lifts and a superb variety of terrain that keep skiers exploring for days.

Sunday River’s one-spot climb in this year’s rankings reflects a resort that consistently delivers on the things skiers care about most. Readers gave it especially high marks for grooming and lifts, two ski-centric categories that can make or break your day on the slopes, particularly at a sprawling mountain where efficient circulation and well-maintained trails matter. Terrain variety was another standout. As one reader put it, “you can ski nonstop all day without repeating the same trail or lift.” That range also helps make Sunday River a hit with families and groups of mixed abilities, while its high marks in lodging and nightlife suggest that this is a destination resort with plenty to do when the lifts stop turning.

The biggest knocks? Access and value. Sunday River is a haul for anyone coming from outside Maine, sitting about 90 minutes from Portland, and readers clearly feel the cost of a visit. But its remote setting is also part of the appeal: Visitors can stay slopeside or make Bethel, with its classic New England character, their home base.

The resort has continued to invest in the lift network, and this season it’s testing new snowmaking technology to make its already formidable system more efficient. Those upgrades reinforce what readers seem to appreciate most about Sunday River: It may take some effort—and money—to get there, but once you’re on the mountain, the skiing experience is thoughtfully put together. “Sunday River is a gem,” summed up one Sunday River visitor. “Lift upgrades have been excellent and grooming is very well executed.”

“The variety of lifts is a big plus, with a mix of high-speed lifts and a gondola that makes getting around easy and efficient. I especially love the Igloo—there’s truly nothing else like it in New England, and it adds a unique touch to an already incredible mountain experience.”

Jay Peak collected more snow than any other resort during the 2025-’26 season and Jay loyalists are hoping no one was paying attention.

Skiers happily drive past a dozen other resorts to reach Jay Peak. Ask them what makes this place worth the effort, and you’ll get some version of what one Jay devotee shared in the comments: “Jay consistently gets the best and most deep snow in New England.”  Indeed, Jay averages about 347 inches of snow annually and topped 400 inches last winter, helping cement its status as one of the East’s most reliable powder bets.

The resort climbed one spot to No. 8 in our rankings this year, and its top category results make one thing clear: Readers consider Jay a skier’s mountain, first and foremost. It ranked in the top 6 for snow, terrain variety, challenge, and value—a rare mix that speaks to its reputation for deep powder, serious glades, and demanding New England steeps without the premium price tag of some bigger-name destinations.

It’s not all sunshine and rainbows, though. Grooming isn’t a major selling point here, and the lift network can use an upgrade. Getting to the remote Northeast Kingdom takes some effort, too. But once you arrive, Jay offers plenty beyond the slopes, including comfortable slopeside lodging and the Pump House indoor waterpark, a hit with families and a welcome option on a rest day. The resort’s combination of authentic mountain character, affordability, and genuinely challenging skiing is what keeps its loyal fans singing its praises. “The best test for expert skiers in Vermont.”

“As far as East Coast snow goes, this resort is the pinnacle of natural snow. I have skied here every year over the last five years and more than once have enjoyed a foot or more of fresh powder on my visit.”

Jay is adding two-hour afternoon coaching sessions for advanced skiers and riders looking to sharpen their skills in trees, bumps, steeps, and terrain parks. Small group sizes are designed to keep the instruction focused. The resort is also introducing transferable Mission Affordable 4-Packs, which bundle activities including lift tickets, lessons, and waterpark access. Lift-ticket packs start at $329.

A local favorite, this resort offers something for the whole family in an unpretentious setting.

Waterville Valley made one of the biggest gains in this year’s rankings, jumping eight spots—and reader feedback makes a pretty convincing case for why. The mountain was voted No. 1 for family friendliness, but its appeal goes well beyond keeping the kids happy. Readers also gave it high marks for snow, grooming, value, and access, suggesting that this small, independent resort delivers where it counts: reliable conditions, well-maintained trails, and a ski day that doesn’t leave families feeling utterly frustrated.

That consistency is a big part of what makes Waterville such a natural fit for families, particularly those introducing kids to skiing. The mountain has enough beginner and intermediate terrain to build confidence and keep progressing, while its 100 percent snowmaking coverage steps up when conditions are less than favorable. “It’s small enough for us to feel at home and big enough to keep our family of four happy,” shared one survey respondent.

Just two hours from Boston, Waterville is an easy getaway, with a charming, walkable base village that skiers love. Plenty of readers shared their own happy memories of learning to ski at Waterville and now bringing their children back to do the same. “I began skiing Waterville when I was 3 years old in 1975,” shared one skier. “I have begun coming back to N.H. for the holidays and introduced my own son to skiing at Waterville. This will always be my home mountain!”

The trade-off to all of those family vibes is that advanced skiers may run out of new challenges, and the village gets quiet after the lifts stop spinning. But for families looking for an affordable, welcoming mountain with good snow, good grooming, and a genuine sense of community, Waterville checks a lot of boxes.

“A perfect family resort. As a parent, I feel confident that my kids are safe and having a great ski experience. The skiing is varied enough for us parents to have just as much fun!”

This Maine resort offers skiers a little bit of everything without compromising quality or its signature charm.

Sugarloaf takes a six-spot slide to No. 6 in this year’s rankings, but its category results tell another story: This is a mountain that knows exactly what its core skiers come looking for. Readers ranked it highly for snow, challenge, après, and local flavor, a combination that speaks to the ‘Loaf’s particular appeal. “Love the great snow and sustained vertical,” commented one reader. “Always feels like vacation when we go to Sugarloaf.”

Indeed, the skiing is serious, the atmosphere is distinctly its own, and the après scene gives people a reason to stick around after the bullwheel stops spinning. “The vibe during the day and well into après is nuts,” confessed a satisfied ’Loafer. “They party often.” For advanced skiers who want big-mountain terrain, Sugarloaf remains a compelling choice. Its remote location is part of the commitment, but also part of the charm.

Interestingly, the things that make Sugarloaf special aren’t necessarily the things that make a ski vacation effortless. Access and lodging continue to be challenges, particularly for visitors who want more places to stay close to the mountain. And while its terrain and snow keep devoted skiers coming back, the overall experience is decidedly more about the mountain and its community than luxury amenities. That’s not a knock so much as a useful distinction: Sugarloaf is for skiers who value character, challenging terrain, and a lively local scene over convenience and luxury. Its loyal following is proof that the trade-offs are worth it.

“I love the Loaf. Snow is great, trails are excellent, and beer is great. The bars are amazing, the resort has an old-style feel, there’s challenge, and there’s ease.”

Sugarloaf is putting some attention toward beginner infrastructure and on-mountain comfort this season. The Landing learning area is getting two covered conveyors, a 60-foot and a 120-foot, and the existing 400-foot conveyor is being repurposed for a dedicated terrain park that will be open on weekends, holidays, and during night operations. Finally, the Perfect Turn building is also being relocated to the base of the Bucksaw Express to serve as a new warming hut.

This southern Vermont ski area prioritizes natural, wild terrain and a community spirit that makes everyone feel seen.

Magic Mountain is back in the rankings, and not just barely: Readers put this under-the-radar Vermont hill at No. 5, rewarding it with top marks for terrain variety, challenge, guest service, value, and local flavor. That’s quite a mix, and it gets at what makes Magic special. This isn’t a resort built around flashy amenities or a sprawling base village. It’s a mountain for people who care about skiing, with challenging terrain, some of Vermont’s best glades, and a refreshingly unpretentious atmosphere. “More than other areas, they tend to leave a fair number of trails ungroomed,” said one Magic skier. “Some VERY steep trails that you might not expect in an area of this size.” It’s also a place where skiers feel welcomed and looked after, without feeling like they’re paying a premium for the privilege.

Magic’s appeal is its ability to deliver a little something for different kinds of skiers without losing its independent, community-minded character. Families can find an approachable place to introduce kids to the sport, while stronger skiers can head off in search of steeper pitches and tree skiing that will keep them entertained all day. The value is another big draw, especially for families looking to stretch their dollars.

Don’t come expecting a bustling slopeside village or a long list of resort amenities; lodging is limited nearby, and dining and après are relatively low-key, though the pub and nearby Londonderry offer options. Magic’s readers clearly appreciate it for what it is: a mountain with personality, great skiing, and a loyal following that doesn’t need bells and whistles to have a good time. ““Magic is the kind of place I wish I could go to every single weekend,” said one survey respondent. “The views are beautiful and the vibes are so laidback.”

“Old-school vibes. No judging going on. Magic doesn’t care what career you have or how much money you make. Everyone is there to share their love for skiing. Magic truly has soul.”

A fiercely independent mountain where challenging terrain, natural snow, and old-school character still rule.

In a ski world increasingly defined by mega-passes, high-speed lifts, and ever-expanding resort villages, Mad River Glen remains delightfully, stubbornly itself. And SKI readers are here for it. After slipping to No. 8 last year, this fiercely independent Vermont ski area is back in the Top 5, earning top marks for terrain variety, challenge, value, and local flavor. Those results speak to the heart of the Mad River experience: great snow, natural terrain, and a little adventure over polished perfect corduroy and resort amenities. Its bumps, glades, and notoriously challenging pitches have earned it a cult following, while its co-op ownership and famous single chair help preserve its distinctly old-school ski experience. As one reader put it, “Hard chargers, prepare to be humbled.”

Of course, MRG can be an acquired taste. The 115-acre mountain has a relatively small footprint, limited grooming, and few of the off-slope amenities that have come to define the modern vacation. Lodging and dining options are sparse, and parking remains a recurring complaint. But those shortcomings don’t diminish the affection skiers have for the place. In fact, the mountain’s lack of bells and whistles is central to its identity: Mad River is a skier-owned co-op, unaffiliated with any major pass network, and unapologetically committed to doing things its own way. For those who want a polished resort experience, look elsewhere. But if you’re looking for a little adventure, a lot of character, and terrain that rewards strong legs, Mad River Glen remains one of a kind. “Hasn’t changed for decades … and that’s a great thing.”

“Until you ski a bump run or tree line that has not been touched by a snowboard, you have no idea what you’ve been missing. Add that to the natural snow, and it is an experience that is increasingly hard to replicate.”

The Beast of the East offers the most terrain on the East Coast and doesn’t skimp on the nightlife.

Killington is back on the podium, climbing three spots from last year and reminding everyone that the East’s biggest ski area has staying power. Readers gave the resort high marks for snow, terrain variety, challenge, and lifts, which means that whether you’re chasing steeps, exploring different corners of the mountain, or just looking for one more lap, you’ll come away satisfied. “If it’s busy, you can find a section of mountain that is not as crowded,” said one skier. “It’s one of my favorite things about Killington.”

Its size is a major part of the appeal, but so is the consistency. Readers praised both natural and manmade snow, as well as grooming that keeps the mountain skiing well across a range of conditions. “Even on the worst days of the season, mountain ops does a great job making trails rideable.” Add in the dining, après, and nightlife, and Killington offers something few Eastern resorts can match: off-slope amenities that are equal to the on-mountain variety.

Readers continue to flag value and family friendliness as weaker points, with high prices and busy weekends making a visit feel less appealing for those on a budget or skiing with younger kids. But for groups of friends, strong skiers, and anyone who wants a vacation with as much happening after the lifts close as during the day, Killington delivers. The nightlife along the Access Road has a reputation of its own, and the resort’s return to hosting the Stifel Killington Cup this season adds another dose of big-mountain energy. As one reader summed it up, the terrain variety is immense, the snow and grooming are consistently strong, and the nightlife is legendary.

“Called the “Beast of the East” for a reason! Extensive and fun, varied terrain. Also, plenty of fast lifts and excellent grooming. But definitely a resort that excels during the week or early/late in the season because of crowds.”

The Snowdon Triple has been replaced with a new quad, increasing uphill capacity by 33 percent and adding a backup generator. The resort has also completed a $2 million project to cover all four beginner conveyor lifts across Killington and Pico.

On the snowmaking front, new pipelines and 23 low-energy guns have restored snowmaking to the Conclusion trail for the first time in nearly a decade, while new fan guns on the Mouse Trap and Lower Skyeburst runs will further bolster coverage.

In the heart of the White Mountains, Bretton Woods specializes in a seamless ski experience where everything from the corduroy to the après cocktails are top-notch.

There’s a certain pleasure in a ski day when everything just works: the snow is smooth, the lifts keep moving, the trails are freshly groomed, and nobody in the family is having a meltdown. That’s Bretton Woods. Set beneath the dramatic Presidential Range, New Hampshire’s largest ski area has built its reputation on a consistently pleasant, polished experience—and SKI readers continue to reward it for that. “Even if the weather isn’t playing ball, the conditions are always the best around,” shared one. The resort scores highly for snow, grooming, and lifts, but its appeal goes well beyond the mountain. Readers also gave it top marks for dining, lodging, and family friendliness, making it one of the East’s most reliable choices for a comfortable, low-stress ski vacation.

The resort’s connection to the Omni Mount Washington Hotel gives a ski trip a distinctly old-school New England feel, with comfortable lodging, good dining, and a memorable place to unwind after the lifts close. Ski valets, complimentary mountain tours, and attentive service add to the sense that you’re on vacation, not just skiing. Families appreciate the mellow, well-maintained trails and easygoing vibe. “My kids learned to ski here. The glades are super approachable,” commented one Bretton Woods fan.

On the flip side, Bretton Woods isn’t the place to go looking for massive steeps or a deep roster of challenging terrain. Readers gave it lower marks for challenge and nightlife, and while there’s enough variety to keep a range of abilities happy, more aggressive skiers may want a mountain with a little more bite. But for families, newer skiers, and anyone who values great grooming and a relaxed day on the slopes, Bretton Woods makes a compelling case. “I have never had a bad day skiing at Bretton Woods!”

“Lifts are all high-speed, parking is a breeze, staff is out of this world friendly and the mountaintop lodge is a slice of luxury in the mountains.”

The terrain park moves to Fabyan’s Express, where the setup now emphasizes progression with a wider range of features for intermediate and advanced riders. On the snowmaking front, additional infrastructure on Upper Express should help improve early-season consistency and coverage.

An independent mountain with big-terrain appeal and a down-to-earth personality—that’s a winning combination.

Two years at No. 1 is no fluke. Saddleback has earned the loyalty of SKI readers by putting the skiing first—and making sure the people who come to enjoy it feel like they belong. This is a mountain with plenty of personality, from its challenging glades and long, satisfying runs to the independent spirit that runs through the operation. Readers gave it high marks for snow, terrain variety, challenge, and value, but they also singled out guest service and local flavor. As one put it, “This is the friendliest resort I have ever been to.” Another praised the resort’s comeback—reopening in 2020 after a longtime closure—its local vibe, and the nearby town of Rangeley. All of those have helped turn Saddleback into not only a great place to ski, but an authentic destination with a community behind it.

The skiing is the main event, namely the tree skiing. With more than 600 acres to explore, including the steep trees of Casablanca and classic New England cruisers, Saddleback offers enough variety to keep strong skiers busy without losing its approachable, mid-sized feel. “The woods are unreal for New England,” said one survey respondent. “Truly a unique experience.” Its high elevation and reliable snowfall help, too, as does a recent $6.5 million investment in snowmaking.

Saddleback is a commitment for anyone coming from outside northern Maine, and readers gave it lower marks for access and off-slope amenities, including lodging, dining, and après. There are places to stay and eat at the resort and in nearby Rangeley, but this isn’t a mountain built around a sprawling village or a lavish après scene. For many, that’s part of the appeal. Saddleback doesn’t need to be all things to all skiers; it just needs to deliver the kind of day on snow that makes the drive worthwhile. For the second year in a row, SKI readers say it does exactly that. “It’s a secret gem that isn’t overpopulated yet.”

“The mountain is great—lots of variety in the types of trails, allowing all abilities to go to the top and enjoy the ride down. There is definitely a good amount of difficult trails giving folks something to shoot for. Love the size and variety there!”
