Paramount+ Just Made Its Biggest Mistake With Its Surging 99-Minute Fantasy Movie

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Amanda M. Castro is a Network TV writer at Collider and a New York–based journalist whose work has appeared in Newsweek, where she is an Associate Editor, and The U.S. Sun, where she previously served as a Senior Consumer Reporter.

She specializes in network television coverage, delivering sharp, thoughtful analysis of long-running procedural hits and ambitious new dramas across broadcast TV. At Collider, Amanda explores character arcs, storytelling trends, and the cultural impact of network series that keep audiences tuning in week after week.

Born and raised in Puerto Rico, Amanda is bilingual and holds a degree in Communication, Film, and Media Studies from the University of New Haven.

Avatar Aang: The Last Airbender has a lot of great things about it. Avatar fans have waited so long to see Aang, Katara, Sokka, Toph, and Zuko as grown-ups, and the movie's visual effects leave you in awe. At times, you realize the possibilities this franchise has beyond the original TV series. At the same time, Avatar Aang reminds you that the story could have played out differently if we'd watched the TV show instead of the film.

Set almost a decade after the Hundred Year War, the story follows Team Avatar as they take on the responsibilities of adulthood. Aang still faces the challenge of surviving as an Air Nomad when he gets into another mess after stealing an ancient relic and meeting the ancient airbender. It introduces new Avatar lore, a villain with complicated motivations, spiritual mythology, and a chance to explore how the world has changed since the gang defeated Ozai. Somehow, all of that has to fit into one movie, and it shows. Avatar: Seven Havens reminds us that this franchise is at its best when it has room to develop its characters and let its stories unfold. Avatar Aang could have used the same courtesy.

One of the biggest draws of this movie is seeing what happened to the Gaang after the war. They’re no longer kids traveling the world together. Sokka helps rebuild the Southern Water Tribe, Toph teaches metalbending, Katara has responsibilities in Republic City, and Zuko runs the Fire Nation. They have their own lives now, which is precisely what makes reuniting them so interesting, except the movie doesn’t do nearly enough with it.

Katara, Sokka, Toph, and Zuko are so caught up in Aang’s story that we rarely see how adulthood has changed them or what has become of their relationships. While the movie offers some clues about the dynamics fans may want to see, it doesn't give them enough time to unfold before yet another conflict, revelation, or plot twist. It is really disappointing that the original series created such a wonderful image of the characters as good friends in each other’s lives. They quarreled, made each other's lives difficult, and occasionally had absolutely nothing to do with saving the world. Not every word in a film should advance the plot; sometimes it's just about spending time together.

In this film, Team Avatar is back, yet it seems determined not to let us spend time with them. Fans didn't need another world-ending evil to excuse their reunion; we just wanted to see the characters get to know each other again, joke about their journey, or figure out the outcomes of their lives.

Allison King’s Avatar Aang: The Last Airbender: A Novel adds new scenes, perspectives, and storylines that weren’t included in the movie. It includes villain POV chapters, additional material involving Suki, conversations between Team Avatar, and more attention to Aang’s memories of the Air Nomads. It’s the sort of expansion that makes you wonder what the movie might have looked like with another few hours to work with.

To be clear, the novelization isn’t some miraculous improvement on the film. Expanding a story doesn’t automatically improve its characterization, and some of the book’s character dynamics and relationships don’t particularly work for me, but it does highlight how much more there was to explore. For example, Aang’s grief isn't new —the original series already showed how much losing his people hurt him, and the film explores that struggle again as he grapples with saving Air Nomad culture. However, understanding grief and seeing a character deal with it are two different matters. The novel gives room to revisit his memories, explore his fears, and reflect on moments the film rushes.

The same goes for everyone else. Toph’s personal life, Katara’s responsibilities, Sokka’s work rebuilding his home, and Zuko’s transition into leadership could have been meaningful subplots instead of background information. The novel doesn’t necessarily get all those characters right (especially some points about Toph), but the extra material makes it painfully obvious that this story wasn't running out of things to say.

The art book offers a different angle on the film's potential. Avatar: The Last Airbender: The Art of the Movie shows how character designs, locations, hybrid animals, and animation methods contributed to the filmmaking process. One of the film's best parts is the combination of 2D characters with a CG environment, and the book makes it easier for viewers to appreciate the work done behind the camera.

Even the returning characters’ designs have a story to share. Zuko’s design went through several variations before the animators settled on a look that matched the Fire Lord's image. The artists faced the challenge of showing how the characters evolved and representing that evolution visually while staying true to the original characters.

That’s the kind of detail the movie should have had time to appreciate itself. The world has changed, the characters have changed; there are new locations, new creatures, new political tensions, and a history that stretches far beyond the original series. Yet the movie is so busy getting through its story that some of its most interesting ideas never get the attention they deserve.

The success some movies achieve with different audiences is undeniable. The original series used its three seasons to build a unique world and develop the characters, leading to the exciting journey that culminated in the ultimate victory over Ozai. Importantly, the movie could afford long pauses, while the series’ best episodes focused on events with little relation to the main plot; that is what made them successful.

Avatar: Seven Havens, which has already secured a second season, continues that tradition. A serialized story has the luxury of developing its mythology over time, introducing conflicts without immediately resolving them, and giving its characters room to surprise us. That’s particularly valuable in a franchise where the world itself is just as interesting as the Avatar’s central mission.

It would have been nice to see Avatar Aang take this route. We need an episode where Aang goes back to the Air Temples while Zuko deals with all the matters that come with being the Fire Lord. Toph can live a life outside metalbending sessions and drooling over Tagah. Katara and Sokka can be siblings instead of just members of a group assembled to tackle yet another danger.

The film features great animation and a delightful reunion between the heroes, but that doesn’t substitute for good storytelling. Avatar Aang is full of enough ideas for an entire season, and it is sad to see them crammed into a single motion picture. Avatar has all the time in the world; it can take its time, and the world will wait.
