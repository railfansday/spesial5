Happy for You, Sad for Me

If you’re struggling with infertility, you’ve likely experienced the pit-in-your-stomach sensation when someone close to you announces a pregnancy. You may simultaneously feel authentic joy for her and overwhelming grief for yourself. That sadness may turn to feelings of jealousy, anger, or even spite. While you don’t want anything horrible to happen to her, you may have fleeting thoughts of their pregnancy not working out. And then comes the guilt and self-doubt, asking yourself questions such as Am I a terrible person for having these thoughts?, What kind of friend am I?, and Why can't I just be happy for her?

Research shows that women struggling with infertility consistently experience envy of pregnant women and women with children, painful reactions to the pregnancies of friends and family members, negative self-comparisons, and withdrawal from situations that serve as reminders of their own struggles to conceive (Aiello et al., 2025).

Understanding what triggers these reactions can help you manage them with less guilt and without compromising your relationships or emotional well-being.

Give yourself permission to have two contradictory feelings at the same time. Being happy for someone else and feeling sad for yourself are not mutually exclusive. You can love your friend, want the very best for her, and still feel devastated by her pregnancy announcement. Often, it’s not really about her pregnancy at all, but rather a painful reminder of what you so desperately want and have invested so much of your time, energy, money, and hope into achieving.

The inability to be unconditionally supportive doesn't mean you're selfish or that you're not a good friend. It simply means you’re going through a devastating period in your life and that you want to experience the same joy as she gets to have.

It's natural to compare yourself with other people, particularly when they're reaching milestones you had hoped to achieve. During infertility, those comparisons can become difficult to escape when you are surrounded by pregnancy announcements, baby showers, first birthdays, and family photos. Staying connected to friends can be difficult when all they seem to talk about is their children, complain about sleepless nights, or announce they're expecting another baby. In fact, research shows that painful reminders, envy, negative self-comparison, and feelings of social exclusion and isolation are recurring themes in women's experiences of infertility (Aiello et al., 2025).

The hardest part may be the feeling that you are being left behind while everyone else's lives are moving forward. You may find that you start to measure your own losses against their milestones, remembering that a friend first got pregnant when you started your first IVF cycle or that another friend announced being pregnant with her second child as you were experiencing a pregnancy loss from a third transfer.

These comparisons can be particularly painful because infertility involves so much uncertainty. There's often no clear timeline, no guarantee of success, and very little control over the outcome.

Jealousy and envy are emotions most would rather not admit to experiencing because we want to think of ourselves as loving, generous, and supportive. Feeling jealous often leads to shame and self-loathing, which only compounds an already difficult life crisis.

But jealousy during infertility often has much more to do with your own sadness and disappointment than with resentment toward someone else. Infertility involves losses that aren't always visible or acknowledged by others. These intangible losses include an imagined timeline, the expectation of conceiving easily, or the sense of control you once had over your reproductive future.

You don't have to put yourself in a painful situation to show support or prove you're happy for someone else. You can skip a baby shower, avoid a family gathering, or mute someone's pregnancy updates on social media. And you don't have to explain or justify every one of these decisions.

There are ways to be supportive while also taking care of yourself: You can send a thoughtful card or gift without attending the baby shower. You can congratulate a friend and let her know that while you're happy for her, you may need a little space to navigate your own struggles. You can take a break from social media or leave a gathering early if it becomes overwhelming.

Research suggests that social withdrawal and feelings of being misunderstood are common experiences during infertility (Aiello et al., 2025). While avoiding certain situations may provide temporary relief, losing connections with people who care about you and have good intentions can make the infertility experience even lonelier.

Try to maintain relationships in which you feel understood and supported. That might mean spending time with friends who are sensitive to what you're going through, connecting with others experiencing infertility, joining a support group, or working with a mental health professional who understands reproductive mental health.

Imagine that a close friend who's just been through her fourth unsuccessful IVF cycle tells you that her sister, who wasn’t even trying to conceive, announced she's pregnant—and now she wants nothing to do with her. You probably wouldn't tell her that she's a horrible person for feeling this way. Instead, you would recognize how complicated the situation is, how much she's been through, and how painful this news might be for her.

Self-compassion means extending some of that same kindness and understanding to yourself. And research suggests that self-compassion matters. In a study of women undergoing infertility treatment, self-compassion was one of the pathways that contributed to psychological well-being (Chu et al., 2021).

So next time you want to berate yourself for having thoughts and feelings that feel negative, remind yourself that it's understandable to have a difficult reaction to something you've wanted so badly and recognize that you can care deeply about someone and still feel sad about your own circumstances. Sometimes it simply means allowing yourself to feel disappointed without judging yourself for it.

One of the hardest things about infertility is learning to live with emotions that don't always seem to fit together. You can feel hopeful and afraid, grateful and jealous, optimistic and discouraged. You can love the people around you and still find certain moments with them painful.

The next time someone announces a pregnancy and you find yourself struggling with a mixture of happiness, jealousy, sadness, and guilt, know that it’s a completely normal reaction to a difficult situation and try not to judge yourself. You can be happy for other people and acknowledge your own sadness at the same time. Sometimes, allowing yourself to feel what you feel, without adding guilt or shame, can make a difficult experience a little easier.
