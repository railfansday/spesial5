Fiber VFL Test: Find the Break With a Visual Fault Locator

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

The fastest fault find on the floor is not a meter. It is a red light.

A visual fault locator — a VFL, usually a pen-sized tool with a 650 nm laser — puts a bright red light down the fiber, and wherever the fiber is broken, badly bent, or badly connected, the light leaks out of the core and you can see it glowing with your own eyes. No reading to interpret, no trace to decode: the fault glows. I reach for the VFL before the OTDR whenever the run is short enough to see, because it answers the question "where is it?" in under a minute. The OTDR answers "how bad is it?" — but first you need to know where to look, and that is the VFL's job.

Never look directly into the end of a fiber with a VFL connected, and never look into the VFL itself. The 650 nm laser is visible, which makes it tempting to stare at, but it is still a laser — most pen VFLs are Class 2 or Class 3R, and a direct view can damage your eyes. Look at the glow leaking from the jacket or the connector from the side, never down the bore. The same rule applies to any fiber that might carry live traffic: never look into a fiber end unless you have confirmed it is dark.

A VFL shows three things, and all three glow red. First, a break: the fiber is cut or snapped, and the light pours out at the break point — a bright spot on a patch cord or at the point where a cable was crushed in a tray. Second, a bad connector: light leaking at the ferrule or the adapter, which usually means a cracked ferrule, a connector that is not fully seated, or a face so dirty the light is scattering instead of passing through. Scope the face before you condemn the connector. Third, a macrobend: a bend tighter than the fiber's minimum radius leaks light along the curve, and the bend glows. If you see a glow at a bend, straighten the routing and watch the glow disappear.

A typical pen VFL reaches 3 to 5 kilometres on single-mode and about the same on multimode, but range is not the real limit — visibility is. The VFL is made for patch cords, panels, and short intra-building runs: the places where the glowing fault is in front of you. It is the wrong tool when the fault is in a buried duct, a sealed tray, a run behind a wall, or a long outside-plant span — the light is too weak to reach the far end, and even if it did, you could not see it through the duct. On a long run, use an OTDR.

The VFL also cannot measure anything: it will not tell you the loss in decibels, and a link can pass the VFL — no visible glow — and still fail its loss budget. A clean VFL result means "no visible break," not "good link." The measurement that proves the link is the light source and power meter test.

The check takes about a minute and has four steps. First, confirm the fiber is dark or safe to test: the run under test must not be carrying live traffic you would interrupt, and the far end must not be feeding a receiver you would blind — agree the window with the NOC first, the same as any intrusive test. Second, connect the VFL to the near end of the suspect run — usually at the panel port or the patch cord — with the right adapter for the connector type. Third, switch the VFL on — steady beam for a break hunt, pulsing beam if the room is bright or you are tracing a run among many — and walk the visible length of the run, watching the jacket, the connectors, and every bend. Dim the lights if you can; a faint glow at a bend is easy to miss in a bright aisle. Fourth, note exactly where the glow is: which panel, which port, how far from the panel, and whether it is at a connector, a bend, or mid-cable. That location is the finding, and it goes in the ticket.

The rule after any fix is the same: prove it twice. First the VFL — the glow is gone. Then the LSPM — the measured loss is inside the budget. A VFL that shows nothing after a fix is not proof the link is good; it is proof the visible break is gone. And if the link is still down with a clean VFL, do not keep re-testing the same patch cord — the fault is somewhere the VFL cannot see, and the next step is the OTDR, not a second VFL pass.

The VFL result belongs in the ticket as a location, not a feeling. The minimum useful line:

The shape to copy: run and fiber type, where the glow was, what you did, the VFL result after the fix, and the LSPM loss that proves the link. Five fields, and the next tech knows the fault was at the connector, not the cable, and that the fix was measured, not guessed. If the VFL showed nothing, record that too — "VFL: no glow along visible run, fault not in patching — escalated to OTDR" tells the next person exactly where not to look, which is worth as much as a found fault.
