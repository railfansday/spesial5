Switzerland is bringing Bitcoin self-custody into a global crypto tax reporting network

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

The country has approved 57 potential information-sharing partners, including every EU member state and the UK.

On Nov. 25, Switzerland’s Federal Council approved 57 potential partners for automatic exchanges of crypto tax information with Switzerland, so a Bitcoin withdrawal from a covered Swiss exchange to a hardware wallet could show up in an annual tax report

The list includes every EU member state, the UK, and several G20 jurisdictions. Switzerland plans to start exchanging data in 2028 with partners that want to participate and meet the OECD’s confidentiality, data security, and other requirements.

The rules are part of the OECD’s Crypto-Asset Reporting Framework (CARF). Under the OECD standard, providers must retain the addresses tied to certain outbound transfers for at least five years so tax authorities can ask for them later.

Suppose a customer whose activity is subject to reporting withdraws 1 BTC from a covered Swiss exchange to a hardware wallet.

Under the outbound transfer rule, if the exchange doesn’t know the destination to be associated with a crypto service provider or a financial institution, the transfer can add 1 BTC and its fair market value in regular currency at the time of the transfer, net of transaction fees, to the customer’s annual outbound totals.

The reporting framework accounts for each relevant asset separately, so Bitcoin and other assets have their own totals.

A transfer to an address the exchange knows is associated with a crypto service provider or financial institution falls outside that field, though other transfer rules can still apply. A wallet the customer already controls can fall within the external-wallet rule.

Say the customer bought Bitcoin on the same exchange before withdrawing it. A qualifying purchase can appear in the report under the purchase category, and a qualifying withdrawal can appear in the transfer totals.

The transaction fields cover qualifying purchases, sales, swaps and transfers.

The automatic report covers transactions the provider handles, so later movements between two private wallets without the provider’s involvement fall outside that provider’s report. The required fields omit private keys, seed phrases and full self-custody wallet balances.

Swiss law sets a longer retention period for covered compliance records. Article 17a of the Federal Act on the International Automatic Exchange of Information in Tax Matters requires covered providers to keep records and supporting documents created or obtained to meet their CARF and Swiss statutory obligations.

It refers to Article 958f of the Swiss Code of Obligations, which sets a ten-year retention period.

The Federal Council’s explanation of the legislation confirms the ten-year Swiss period when discussing CARF recordkeeping. Article 17a defines covered records by their role in meeting the provider’s obligations. It does not spell out how the ten-year period applies to individual wallet addresses.

Switzerland’s legal basis for crypto information exchange took effect Jan. 1, 2026. Its State Secretariat for International Finance says the crypto provisions do not apply in 2026 and that Jan. 1, 2027 is the earliest implementation date.

Under the Swiss filing rule, reporting providers have six months after the relevant calendar year ends to submit their reports to the Federal Tax Administration. The Swiss authority then forwards the information to eligible foreign counterparts under the applicable agreement.

If implementation begins in 2027, providers would collect that year’s data and submit it in 2028, when the first cross-border exchanges are planned.

For a reportable customer, a qualifying transfer in the first collection year could be in the record before anything is sent abroad. Keeping the withdrawal date, amount, and destination alongside the exchange record can help reconcile that transfer with other activity involving the same Bitcoin.
