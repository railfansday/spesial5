Ask anyone selling an old chain or a broken ring what they want to know, and it's one thing: what is this worth? A small calculator can answer that in a second, but most of the ones you find online get the details wrong. They ignore troy ounces, treat 14K as 58.5% one day and 58.3% the next, or show a "value" that no buyer will ever pay.

In this tutorial, we'll build a gold value calculator that gets those details right. It runs on plain HTML and JavaScript, updates as the user types, works with a screen reader, keeps its state in a shareable URL and pulls a live spot price through a small Node.js endpoint. The calculation lives in one pure function, so we can test it in a few lines.

Every gold calculator comes down to three inputs: how much the item weighs, how much of that weight is actually gold, and what pure gold is trading for today.

Purity is measured in karats, out of 24. A 14K ring is 14 parts gold and 10 parts other metals, so it's 14 ÷ 24 = 58.3% gold. You'll also see 14K stamped as "585", which is the hallmark many manufacturers use for the same alloy. For a calculator, the karat fraction is the safer default:

The second detail most tutorials miss is the unit. Gold spot prices are quoted in USD per troy ounce, which is 31.1035 grams, not the 28.35-gram ounce on a kitchen scale. Jewelers also use pennyweight (dwt), which is 1.5552 grams. If your calculator multiplies grams by a per-ounce price without converting, every result is wrong by a factor of 31.

For example, with gold at $2,400 per troy ounce, 10 grams of 14K gold contains 5.83 grams of pure gold, which is worth $449.85 at melt.

That $449.85 is the value of the gold content alone. Refiners and buyers pay a percentage of it, because refining, assaying and handling cost money. Depending on the buyer, the item and the market, the payout can sit well below melt value.

That gap is why good production tools separate the two numbers. A dedicated scrap jewelry gold calculator, like the one The Alloy Market offers, lets sellers estimate what their gold is worth from its weight and karat before they send anything in. Our calculator does the same job with a payout field: leave it at 100% to see melt value, or enter a buyer's rate to see a realistic offer.

A gold calculator looks like a ten-line script, but the details are what make it useful: converting troy ounces properly, separating melt value from payout, keeping the math testable and making the result accessible and shareable. The same pattern of a pure calculation module, a thin DOM layer and a cached server endpoint works for any calculator where the price depends on several inputs, from shipping estimates to currency conversion.
