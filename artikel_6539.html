Australia, Through the Eyes of Its Traditional Custodians

Tanggal: 2026-11-11

In the Kimberley, in Australia’s far northwest, land and human history are inextricably linked and almost incomprehensibly ancient. Its sandstone ranges, built from rock up to 1.8 billion years old, are almost half the age of the Earth itself. Close to the shores of Warrabii (Swift Bay), inside a cave carved from that ancient stone, are paintings of stenciled hands dating back an estimated 20,000 years. Lying on your back to look up at them feels like a living link to the person who made them—blowing a mix of ochre, water, and animal fat over a hand pressed to the rock—a portal through time to Aboriginal Australia, the world’s oldest continuous living culture, stretching back at least 65,000 years.

Bardi Jawi artist Bruce Wiggan at work at Cygnet Bay Pearl Farm, where he carves riji: pearl shell engraved with geometric or figurative designs, the grooves then rubbed with ochre or charcoal mixed with resin or fat.

A riji in progress on Wiggan’s workbench. Engraved pearl shell has long been a highly prized trade item, passed along routes that stretched from the Kimberley to central and southern Australia.

Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, who comprise hundreds of language groups, each with its own distinct culture and law, hold an unparalleled understanding of Country. Capitalized in Aboriginal English, the word encompasses far more than a physical space. As the Gay’wu Group of Women write in Songspirals: Sharing Women’s Wisdom of Country Through Songlines, “To talk of Country means not just land, but also the waters, the people, the winds, animals, plants, stories, songs, and feelings, everything that becomes together to make up place. Country is alive for us—it cares for us, communicates with us, and we are part of it.”

Navigating the waterways of the Kimberley between sandstone walls laid down some 1.8 billion years ago, on a coast where the tides can swing more than 11 meters.

Yet the fabric of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander societies was torn and could have been lost forever. After the British invaded the shores of what is now Australia in 1788, they set about colonizing its people and Country. The continent’s First Peoples were enslaved, massacred, and displaced. Between 1910 and 1970, as many as one in three Indigenous children, tens of thousands in total, were forcibly removed from their families and placed in government- and church-run institutions, forbidden from speaking their languages or practicing their cultures. They became known as the Stolen Generations. More recently, in 2023, Australians voted against enshrining an Indigenous Voice to Parliament, an advisory body that would have given First Nations people a formal say on the decisions that affect them.

Indigenous resilience and determination have ensured this is also a story of endurance and restoration. Through native title and land rights, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples’ connection to their traditional lands and waters is now legally recognized across more than half of Australia, though rarely as full ownership. Truth-telling processes, which build an official public record of historical and ongoing injustice, are underway in several states; in late 2025, Victoria signed the nation’s first treaty with its First Peoples.

Indigenous-led tourism is growing fast too: more than 1.25 million international visitors took part in an Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander experience last year, contributing an estimated $450 million to the economy across more than 2,500 Indigenous-owned businesses. Every one of those bookings is an act: to experience Country as a guest, through the eyes of its Traditional Custodians, and to put money directly into community-led hands rather than extractive, colonial models of travel.

A hand-painted sign welcomes visitors to Murdudjurl, the Murumburr family community on the edge of the Jim Jim Creek, home to Mandy Muir and her mother, Elder Jessie Alderson.

What follows are the voices of seven Custodians, each speaking for their own Country.

Mandy Muir is a senior Murumburr custodian and Kakadu National Park’s longest-serving guide, leading tours on Yellow Water Billabong and at the Burrungkuy rock art site in the Northern Territory. Her family runs Kakadu Billabong Safari Camp on the Jim Jim Creek system—carrying forward more than 65,000 years of continuous Bininj culture.

Mandy Muir, a senior custodian of the Murumburr clan, holds a freshly picked andem, or waterlily, on the banks of Jim Jim Creek, before preparing the plant's tubers to eat.

“Language is what ties us to this Country—it’s how my mob and I know exactly who we are and where we belong. I grew up here in the park, one of 36 kids at the little school when it first opened, learning the European curriculum in the classroom, then going out with elders each week to learn about Country.

Muir’s grandson fishes on Jim Jim Creek near her home, where the fresh tracks of a basking crocodile are pressed into the sand at his feet.

Our calendar follows the stars, and the cycles of animals, plants, and water. When andem (pink waterlilies) flower on the billabong during Yekke and Wurrkeng, our cooler dry months, it’s an important bush food: the tubers and seeds, mabala, eaten raw or cooked, or ground into flour for bush bread, along with the young flower stems, anbardmo. The yellow Andjedji flowers that bloom around the same time tell us Kumoken (freshwater crocodiles) are laying eggs on the sandy creek banks.

My family still weaves jewelry from kunngobarn (pandanus), harvested fresh from the bush with a manmarli (hooked stick), then dried and dyed with roots, berries, and flowers. It’s long been Women’s Business: a time to sit together, share stories, sip tea, and pass knowledge down through daughters, aunties, and grandmothers.

Muir’s cousin makes handwoven pandanus jewelry from materials gathered in the surrounding bush.

At home, while my nephew and I prepare a bush barbecue, my son and daughter paint rarrk—the cross-hatching we use in ceremony, once painted onto bodies to encode our stories and our connection to land, kin, and the Dreaming.

The sun sets over a Kakadu floodplain. In the Top End’s dry season, sunsets like this are almost a daily event, as dust and fine particles carried on dry southeasterly winds scatter the low evening light into deep reds and oranges.

This escarpment Country is home to animals found nowhere else on Earth, like the Oenpelli python and the black wallaroo. This river system, these wetlands, the crocodiles we live alongside (the park is home to around 10,000 of them) are part of our story and our responsibility. When we burn Country, it’s about rejuvenating the land and bringing food back for animals, the way our ancestors did.

Guests at Kakadu Billabong Safari Camp, owned by Mandy Muir, wake to still morning reflections on the billabong, in the heart of crocodile country.

But the climate is changing fast, and Burrungkuy’s rock art—20,000 years old, depicting everything from creation stories to environmental and social change—is deteriorating. If people don’t start listening to the land and the knowledge holders, we risk losing not only our home but our cultural heritage.”

On the beaches of Broome, Patricia Torres—a Djugun, Yawuru, Ngumbarl, Jabirr-Jabirr, Nyul-Nyul, and Bard woman, elder, writer, and bush tucker guide—reads the coastline as a living archive. Founder of Jarndu Ngaank Tours and Mayi Harvests, a native-food enterprise, and now a philosophy doctorate student, she speaks about what colonization took from her people, and what it will take to give it back.

Djugun Elder Patricia Torres holds a hand-painted baler shell during a Welcome to Country, a ceremony in which Traditional Owners welcome visitors to their land.

“When I stand here on this coast, every element carries a story: the plants we use to heal jellyfish stings and reef cuts, the tides that race out for miles, exposing 130-million-year-old dinosaur footprints at Gantheaume Point. Our people read land and water so closely that even the call of a small yellow-breasted bird was a warning—time to go—knowledge that kept us alive for generations.

This coastline once held thick mangroves—our defense against erosion, nurseries for fish and crabs. During the pearling boom, many were cleared for more boats and profit. From the early slave raids through that boom, my own ancestors were among those kidnapped off this coast, forced into pearling labor, and criminalized when they resisted.

Colonization didn’t stop at land and lives: it took our stories, designs, plants, even our identities. Today our motifs and native foods are used as branding by people who aren’t us, while we’re locked out of the wealth it creates—what we call black cladding. Our children and men are over-policed and over-imprisoned, often asked to leave their ways of knowing at school.

Yet our culture is profoundly sophisticated. We lit small controlled fires to hunt, clear pathways, and stop wildfires spreading—practices that kept Country healthy. Our flood stories remember the sea rising and swallowing land thousands of years before Western science could prove it; geologists have since matched them to the exact sea-level changes after the last ice age.

North of Broome, the red pindan country of the Dampier Peninsula gives way to salt flats and mangrove-lined tidal creeks fed by the Indian Ocean.

My work is about truth-telling and decolonizing the mind. By the 1970s, anthropologists had written the Djugun and Jabirr Jabirr off as ‘virtually extinct.’ After eight generations of dispossession, we are still here—that, for me, is decolonization: restoring truth, renewing our relationship to Country, and making sure our children feel powerful, connected, and fully seen.”

Out of Cygnet Bay Pearl Farm at Borrgoron, on the tip of the Dampier Peninsula, Terry Hunter runs Borrgoron Coast to Creek Tours, sharing 40,000 years of Bardi connection to this coastline. Descended from both a notorious enslaver, Harry Hunter, and those he enslaved, Hunter reconciles this family history to educate visitors about a darker chapter of Australia’s past.

Bardi man Terry Hunter on the beach at Cygnet Bay Pearl Farm, where he is the fourth generation of his family to live and work.

“For Bardi (land) and Jawi (island) people like myself, goowarn, or pearl shell, is a sacred link between land and sea. It’s one of Australia’s earliest forms of currency. I’m a fourth-generation pearler and Aboriginal Australian; my great-great-grandfather, Harry Hunter, was a notorious blackbirder who seized Indigenous people and forced them into slave labor. In the early pearling industry, through the 19th and into the early 20th century, blackbirders prized pregnant Aboriginal women as divers because there was a colonial myth that they had greater lung capacity so they could dive deeper and longer.

Today, I educate tourists about the pearling industry and my own family’s history, because I believe it’s time to acknowledge that slavery did happen in Australia.

The freshwater spring is one of our most important water sources. It has been naturally filtered through the beach sand, so it’s pure and full of natural electrolytes—our own ‘natural Gatorade.’

Our culture and food all come from watching this place closely. Each of our six seasons brings different winds, water, and animals. The wattle (Acacia) and the bauhinia (joomoo) trees serve as indicators, telling us exactly when to go fishing—when a species like mullet or trevally is at its best.

Timing matters with oysters too. In late June, they’ve just finished spawning, so they’re nice and plump. A traditional way of cooking them is to lay spinifex grass over the oysters and set it alight, so they pop open and smoke right there on the rocks.

Hunter cooks rock oysters the traditional way, heaping spinifex over them where they grow on the rocks and setting it alight. Once the flames die down, he prises the shells open with a pocket knife to reveal oysters that are warm and lightly smoked.

When you visit these shores, you’re moving through a living system of stories, tides, trees, seasons, and animals—each one telling us when to travel, what to hunt, what to leave alone, and how to respect this Country.”

A Jawi woman and founder of Oolin Sunday Island Cultural Tours, Rosanna Angus runs boat expeditions from Cygnet Bay across her people’s ancestral saltwater Country—work that saw her named Australian Tour Guide of the Year in 2023. Her itineraries retrace the story of the Sunday Island Mission, where her own family lived and led.

Jawi guide Rosanna Angus at Round Rock, near Ardyaloon (One Arm Point), with Jooloom Island behind her. As well as her regular boat tours, she leads women-only cultural journeys through Bardi and Jawi Country.

“We cruise past our community at One Arm Point, through billion-year-old Country where our Dreaming stories meet deep geological history, and into the Bardi Jawi Gaarra Marine Park, gazetted in 2025 after years of work between our people and government.

On Sunday Island, I tell the story of the old mission and my ancestors. My ancestor Igup was the first Chief of Sunday Island, and Kaori, from Boolnginy Island, was the last Chief of Sunday Island, handed the responsibility to look after the Jawi tribe. Our family name was originally Ungij, later changed to Angus by the missionaries. That history lives in old photos and stories I share on Country, where the mission stood. Our tribe is seven clan groups, five on the mainland and two on the islands, and I speak for Jawi Country through my ancestry and responsibility. We don’t have cave paintings here, so our history lives in our voices, our dances, our Ilma ceremonial objects, and the designs painted on our bodies—storytelling, for us, is governance, law, and identity.

Wild horses splash through rainwater on a red pindan track on the Dampier Peninsula. Beautiful as they are, these descendants of settlers’ horses are a declared pest in Western Australia, damaging Country and adding to the pressures on the peninsula’s endangered monsoon vine thickets.

Sharks hold an important place in our Sea Country and cultural stories; our totem shark is called Loolooloo. These connections, passed down through Dreaming stories, link our people directly to the ocean, our law, and our responsibilities to Sea Country. Sharks act as spiritual protectors for hunters and travelers navigating dangerous tides on traditional Biel Biel rafts, which is why we treat them with such deep respect.

Tourism keeps that culture strong and creates opportunities for our young people. I’ve been guiding since I was 16, inspired by my family, who’d always throw us in the deep end: ‘Come, you talk now.’ I’d rather collaborate than compete—sharing visitors with my brother, other guides, and local artists, so more of our community can care for this Country.”

Just a 10-minute drive from the skyscrapers of central Boorloo (Perth), Western Australia’s capital city, lies Kaarta Gar-up, or Kings Park, home to more than 3,000 species of Western Australian plants across its bushland and botanic garden. Steven Jacobs, a Whadjuk man and guide with In Culture Tours, explains why this land was so important to First Nations peoples, and how his community has fought to protect it.

Whadjuk Noongar guide Steven Jacobs at Kaarta Gar-up (Kings Park), with Boorloo (Perth) and the Derbarl Yerrigan (Swan River) behind him. At around 400 hectares, the park is larger than New York’s Central Park, and two-thirds of it is native bushland.

“Visitors from outside Australia are often surprised to find Indigenous culture here in the middle of the city. We’ve survived dispossession, the Stolen Generations, the massacres, and we’re still here, practicing and sharing our traditions with the world. In the Dreamtime, the Waugyl (Rainbow Serpent) moved across the earth, carving out rivers and mountains—not a god, just how our culture explains creation, without religion as it’s commonly understood.

Kaarta Gar-up isn’t just a park; it’s a cultural heart. Women came here to give birth, with everything they needed: reeds for baskets and mats, shelter, and fresh water from the Swan River. In our culture, women, the sun, and the river are the three main givers of life—we even use the same word, bilya, for river and umbilical cord. Women stayed here until the baby was ready to travel, then returned to the wider community.

The Pioneer Women’s Memorial at Kaarta Gar-up (Kings Park), a bronze of a mother and child unveiled in 1968 to honor Western Australia’s settler women. Long before it was built, this was a place where Whadjuk Noongar women came to give birth.

Our people lived off this river and land for thousands of years: fish, kangaroo, emu, plants, and bush medicines like a vapor rub made from peppermint tree, eucalyptus, beeswax, and balga resin. We were strong and healthy before European settlers arrived.

On Heirisson Island, just east of the park, stands a statue of Yagan, our greatest warrior, who fought British colonizers with a spear when they denied us access to the riverside. Eventually, they shot Yagan and beheaded him to claim a bounty; his head was later taken to England, exhibited in a museum, then buried in an unmarked grave.

Yagan’s head returned to Australia in the 1990s, and in 2010—177 years after his death—we finally laid it to rest, bringing deep spiritual healing to our people. It was about more than one man; it was about dignity, refusing to let our warriors remain curiosities in someone’s trophy cabinet overseas. They belong here, on their own land, with their own people.”

A Wardandi Bibbulmun woman known as the “Bush Tucker Queen,” Dale Tilbrook revives native food knowledge through Dale Tilbrook Experiences and Maalinup Aboriginal Gallery in the Swan Valley. With noncommunicable disease disproportionately affecting Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Australians, a legacy of systemic disadvantage, her work is about far more than flavor.

Wardandi Bibbulmun Elder Dale Tilbrook with her granddaughter Willow at Mandoon Estate, on Whadjuk Noongar Country in the Swan Valley. Here she and her brother Lyall run Maalinup (“place of the black swan”) Aboriginal Gallery, where visitors can browse works by Western Australian Aboriginal artists and taste bush foods such as quandong and finger limes.

“When people first smell cinnamon myrtle, they expect straight cinnamon, but what they’re really getting is the whole Christmas-spice family—the same compounds found in nutmeg, cloves, and mace. Lemon myrtle has the highest citral content of any plant in the world, which is why lemon myrtle tea is so powerfully antiseptic, antibacterial, and antioxidant, while anise myrtle helps with stomach cramps and chest congestion. Saltbush, meanwhile, thrives on marginal, salty land, and its leaves and seeds are rich in protein—everyday medicine built into what we ate and drank.

For tens of thousands of years there was no such thing as ‘bad food’ in our systems; everything we ate was nutrient-dense. Then came the settler-era ration system of white flour, sugar, salt, tea, jam, and tinned meat, and now we see very high rates of vitamin D deficiency, especially in remote Aboriginal communities. A lot of our work now is sitting with elders to pull old knowledge out of memory, and testing traditional foods for vitamin D and other micronutrients. We’re even finding that putting mushrooms in the sun for fifteen minutes before cooking can significantly boost their vitamin D content.

Finger limes are a native citrus that look almost like chilli peppers outside, but cut them open, and they release tiny, glistening pearls of juice—citrus caviar, packed with vitamin C and phytonutrients. Quandongs, sandalwood nuts, and desert limes are rich in everything from folate to B6. For us, these were simply the foods our Country gave us—but people call them ‘superfoods’ now, and they’re a blueprint for a more sustainable, nourishing future.”

For over 20 years, Juan Walker—a Kuku Yalanji man and founder of Walkabout Cultural Adventures—has guided visitors throughout his traditional Country, the Port Douglas Daintree region of Tropical North Queensland. He’s also a co-chair of the region’s First Nations Tourism Action Plan (FNTAP), working to put Indigenous tourism operators back in control of how their culture is shared.

Kuku Yalanji guide Juan Walker at one of his favorite swimming spots in Mossman Gorge, where the Mossman River tumbles over giant granite boulders at the southern end of the Daintree rainforest.

“FNTAP came about from a group of us—First Nations tourism operators from across the region—working with Tourism Tropical North Queensland to build a framework that properly recognizes that all tourism is on Aboriginal people’s Country. Coordinating a steering committee across a region this size, and making sure as many communities as possible were heard, was the hardest part of getting it off the ground.

For a long time, tourism products have been heavily restricted in terms of land access, even where Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people hold native title rights. Through this plan, we’re working with government agencies, hoping to ease those restrictions and create pathways for new Indigenous-owned businesses to develop and thrive. Historically, Indigenous tourism meant mass tourism—big numbers watching a quick dance or showcase, shaped by what non-Indigenous operators thought visitors wanted. Now we’re building fully Indigenous-owned operations with the freedom to teach culture and share our stories the way we want to.

Walker leads visitors across Kuyu Kuyu (Cooya Beach) at low tide in search of mud crab, just north of Port Douglas.

The places I take people are the same ones I grew up in, including swimming spots along Mossman Gorge where I went with elders, so everything feels natural and easy to take in. Hunting mud crab is part of that: we search for them through the mangroves and along the beaches, then cook them if time permits. The recipe is a family secret, but the key ingredients are coconut oil, garlic, chili, and brown vinegar.

This stretch of coast is the only place on Earth where two World Heritage areas meet: the Daintree Rainforest, around 180 million years old and one of the oldest tropical rainforests still standing, and the Great Barrier Reef just offshore. My people, the Eastern Kuku Yalanji, have lived along these coastal environments for thousands of years, and everything here is connected—land, sea, sky, and how we treat each other, governed by Lore, the law of the land. Through generations of observation, we’ve learned to live within it, not against it.

Dusk falls over Mossman Gorge. The valley sits beneath Manjal Dimbi, “the mountain holding back”: in Kuku Yalanji stories, its great rock face is Kubirri, who restrains the evil spirit Wurrumbu to protect the people below.

When people come out on Country with us, I want them to leave thinking differently—about this place, and their own home. Land and sea aren’t resources to use up; if we destroy where we live, we destroy our relationships too. Some of my strongest memories are being out here with elders, learning what the reef, rainforest, and waterfalls mean. That’s what drives me now: showing people the same love and respect for Country that was shown to me.”
