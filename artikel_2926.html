The Best ETF to Buy if You're Nervous About a Stock Market Crash

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

Rather than feeling nervous, here's a strategy you can adopt -- just in case.

By Dana George – Nov 23, 2026 at 11:00AM EST

Bad news sells, especially in 2026. If you get your news primarily from organizations that produce an endless stream of "the sky is falling" stories, you may worry about all sorts of issues. Whatever keeps you awake at night, from the potential of a nationwide blackout to an imminent stock market crash, it feels real -- even if it's not.

That's not to say the stock market can never crash. However, protections are in place to make major institutions more resilient. For example, the market can slow disorderly trading and limit spillover if a large company fails. While there are no guarantees, markets are less likely to crash today than they were in 1929.

Protecting your portfolio against a market crash is only part of your job. A more realistic scenario may involve a sudden downturn or bear market.

No matter what you're protecting against, the strategy is the same: diversify. And one way to diversify a stock-heavy portfolio is to take a different approach with an investment like the iShares 0-3 Month Treasury Bond ETF (SGOV +0.01%).

SGOV invests in very short-term U.S. Treasury securities, making its value less sensitive to interest rate changes than longer-term bond funds. While it doesn't guarantee against all losses and can't deliver the growth potential of stocks, it can provide a haven for cash you may need soon or to calm portfolio volatility.

The idea behind adding a short-term bond ETF to a portfolio that relies mainly on stocks is simple. Companies issue stocks to raise money, a deal that can work out well for all parties as long as the company performs well.

However, stock prices can fall sharply if investors fear weaker profits or a slowing economy. In fact, it doesn't take much more than a weak rumor or a poorly researched news story to scare some investors off.

In contrast, since SGOV holds U.S. government debt, performance is driven more by short-term interest rates and Treasury income than by corporate earnings or how everyday investors feel about the stock market.
