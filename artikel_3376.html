The Grammy Nominations Blanked Harry Styles. What Happened?

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

The Grammy nominations today are full of surprises — but the big shocker is the name that isn’t there. Harry Styles got zero nods for his acclaimed album Kiss All the Time. Disco, Occasionally. The last time he dropped an album, Harry’s House, it won Album of the Year, while his single “As It Was” got nominated for Song and Record of the Year. In January, he released KissCo, a deeply personal and experimental statement — but also a wildly successful hit, debuting at Number One and spawning the chart-topping “Aperture.” Yet it got no Grammy nominations at all. How did this happen?

You can practically hear fans around the world asking the Grammys: What kind of pills are you on? (Not the good ones, that’s for sure.)

It can’t be overstated how weird this is. Michael J. Fox got a Grammy nomination. Shaggy got one. So did Fred Armisen, Michelle Obama, Frank Zappa, Tim Curry. There’s Queen Latifah and the Pixies’ Black Francis — in the country category. But not Harry Styles. Hell, even Kanye West got nominated for Best Rap Album — who thinks West had a more successful year making music than Styles? The Recording Academy, that’s who.

The album was submitted in the major pop categories, including Album of the Year and Pop Vocal Album. “Aperture” was submitted for Record, Song, Pop Solo Performance, Music Video — all categories where he’s been nominated before. “Coming Up Roses” was submitted for Best Arrangement; the Netflix special One Night in Manchester for Best Music Film. (KissCo was also submitted for Album Cover, but this year’s nominees are tough to explain — Charli XCX and Slayyyter got shut out too.) Where Styles didn’t submit was the Dance Pop categories, or even Rock, which might have been easier going.

But this is just a drastic break with Grammy history. In the past 50 years, when any artist wins Album of the Year, they get nominated for their next album, in some category or another. It’s almost unheard of for them to get shut out next time. It’s happened only three times: Mumford & Sons won AOTY in 2013 (for Babel), Steely Dan in 2001 (for Two Against Nature), Toto in 1983 (for Toto IV). They all soon released follow-ups that got zero Grammy noms. But these follow-ups weren’t really comparable to KissCo — none of them hit Number One or included a Number One hit. So there’s no real precedent for this. Every other Album of the Year winner got thrown a bone for their next studio album, with a nomination or two. That means Christopher Cross, Phil Collins, Natalie Cole, Tony Bennett, Norah Jones, George Michael, Celine Dion, Bob Dylan, Santana, OutKast, Herbie Hancock, Arcade Fire, Kacey Musgraves, Jon Batiste. But not Styles. (Obviously, there are a few Best Album winners where there wasn’t a follow-up, when the artist dies or quits or is Lauryn Hill.)

The Grammys tend to be legacy-minded, by definition — once the Academy decides you’re a Grammy type of artist, you remain a Grammy type of artist. There’s a certain “incumbent advantage.” Grammy voters are old, old, old, and they’d always rather vote for someone they’ve already voted for. His one-time opening act Kacey Musgraves won AOTY for Golden Hour, then put out a follow-up that nobody liked much, Star-Crossed — but still snagged a couple of country nominations for the song “Camera Roll.” That’s how it usually works. Not this time.

It’s even stranger since Harry has been a key part of the Grammy ceremony in recent years — at this year’s show, when Bad Bunny won Album of the Year, Harry was the one who handed it to him. It was the big dramatic climax of the night — who can forget Bad Bunny breaking down in tears at his table after hearing Harry read the name?

But it was hardly the first show-stealing turn for Styles, as in his “Watermelon Sugar” that kicked off the 2022 ceremony. He had to fight an uphill battle getting Grammy recognition, since he started out as the Academy’s absolute least favorite kind of artist — a singer in a boy band, a British one no less, who formed on TV for a reality show, tsk-tsk. But the Academy eventually caught on to what the audience already knew, recognizing him with his first Grammy for “Watermelon Sugar.” He became one of the first winners in history to give his acceptance speech wearing a feather boa — a very Harry touch. Kiss All the Time has been acclaimed as one of the year’s boldest and best albums, moving way out of the Harry’s House comfort zone where he easily could have stayed. It’s not a universal pop crowd-pleaser like Harry’s House, without any undeniable radio blockbuster like “As It Was” or “Adore You” or “Watermelon Sugar.” It got plenty of bewildered first-week reviews, largely from people confused by the moody electroclash club sounds, misinterpreting the title to think this was the straight-up disco record it plainly wasn’t. So for many people, the music took time to appreciate. But it holds up to nine months of heavy listening, with bangers like “Dance No More” and “Ready, Steady, Go,” as well as intimate ballads like “Coming Up Roses” and “The Waiting Game.”

Styles chose not make the safe follow-up he could have cranked out in his sleep. He made sure nobody could mistake KissCo for Harry’s House Part 2: Better Homes and Gardens. That’s never been how he operates as an artist. He’s the guy who blew up in One Direction, yet made his solo move with a totally uncommercial and eccentric singer-songwriter statement, inspired by the Laurel Canyon 1970s. His debut solo single was “Sign of the Times,” a glam-rock piano epic about death, building for almost six minutes, conceding nothing to trends or the radio. A bold and brazen statement — but it proved him as an artist who was determined to go his own way, rather than chase anybody else’s idea of pop success.

That mindset has worked out for Harry, to say the least, after the astounding year he’s had in 2026. He’s been packing stadiums around the planet for his Together, Together Tour, with the innovation of playing multi-night residencies in a few cities. (He hasn’t made it to L.A. yet — is that why the Recording Academy didn’t notice?)

His 30-night run at Madison Square Garden — by this tour’s standards, the tiniest and most intimate of venues — was a career highlight, even when he wasn’t busting out the slutty shorts. But he’s kept the show evolving and changing as it travels, with so many ambitious peaks — in London, his orchestral performance with “Bridge Over Troubled Water,” or in New York, his indie-sleaze electro-punk detonation of “Kiwi.” He also had an unforgettable moment in one of the year’s biggest and best movies, Project Hail Mary, where the “Sign of the Times” karaoke scene is one of the year’s most poignant music moments.

If there’s anything we know about Styles, deeply private though he is, it’s that he’s just not interested in celebrity clichés. He’s not the kind of pop star who chases red carpets and award shows. Remember, last year on Oscar Night, he not only failed to show up, but he spent the day running the Tokyo Marathon without telling anyone he was doing it. (He did even better at the Berlin Marathon.) When he was in New York for his 10-week run at Madison Square Garden, he wouldn’t have been caught dead at a fancy club or party or gala. Instead, he simply walked to work every day, prowling the city streets with no handlers or entourage, just his trusty “Techno Is My Boyfriend” baseball cap. On the final night, the Garden gave him a banner to commemorate his record-breaking run of sold-out shows, presented by the Knicks’ Jalen Brunson. For this historic occasion, Harry was dressed as a pizza rat. The KissCo snub already looks like one of the great comic gaffes in Grammy history. But if winning Grammys were the kind of thing that interested Harry Styles, he’d be a very different kind of artist. But nearly 10 years into his solo career, he’s kept proving it over and over again — he’s a headstrong boy who does exactly what he wants, not what he thinks an audience will expect. He works with people he knows and trusts, not the buzziest pros, and he only releases music when he’s passionate about it, like his hero Thom Yorke. As he told Yorke this summer, in their Rolling Stone Musicians on Musicians cover story, “You have to just be, ‘What do I want to listen to at this time in my life? What is it that I feel like I want to do, say, make?’ And the fact that people are going to hear it — that realization happens later.”

That’s why he does everything from the heart — and that’s exactly why the audience has responded. It’s truly baffling that the Grammys missed out on a chance to be part of the KissCo story.
