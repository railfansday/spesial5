Why the Jefferson Curl Can Be Valuable for Some Lifters

Repeated or prolonged spinal flexion can increase stress on the tissues around the spine. The Jefferson curl targets spinal flexion by improving spinal control and strengthening positions you might find yourself in and out of the gym.

It’s considered an obscure exercise. If you’ve never heard of or seen it, you might think it’s the latest move for giving your biceps a pump. But the Jefferson curl is far from an arm-day exercise.

The Jefferson curl ia actually lower-back move—one that contradicts almost everything known about lower-back health. To perform it, you hold a weight in front of you and slowly lower it toward the floor while flexing your spine. In other words, you round your back under load and keep bending.

End-range spinal flexion is a key reason many trainers hesitate to add the Jefferson curl to their clients’ workouts. But for the right lifter, it can mean better control, greater flexion tolerance, and improved mobility. However, if it isn’t a good fit, it can increase your injury risk.

So, should you include it in your program?

According to two strength experts, the answer is, it depends.

The Jefferson curl is a controlled exercise where you deliberately flex the spine one segment at a time while holding a light weight. From the neck down, slowly round your back through the upper, middle, and lower back as you reach toward the floor.

The goal is not to see how far you can flex, but to control spinal flexion through a range you can own while placing the entire posterior-chain tissues under tension.

Sets and reps: According to Physical Therapist Bo Babenko, start with 3 to 5 repetitions at a time, using only 15 to 20 pounds as a stretch. Emphasize taking 2 to 3 deep breaths at the bottom, with an elongated exhale to stretch the tissues involved.

Placement: After your main strength work or during a mobility session.

The Jefferson curl challenges (arguably) one of strength training’s biggest blind spots: the idea that the spine should always stay rigid. Outside of a neutral spine while lifting, your spine is designed to move. You flex to tie your shoes, pick things up, sit, reach, climb, and perform plenty of athletic movements. Avoiding spinal flexion doesn’t prepare you for those demands.

That is where the Jefferson Curl can help. When performed well, it lets you train spinal flexion rather than encountering it when fatigued or in a variable environment. The point is not to teach you to round your back during a max deadlift. The point is to recognize that spinal stiffness and spinal movement are both movement skills.

But spinal flexion is dose-dependent. Too much of anything, even water, will hurt you.

That is why this lift deserves neither a blanket ban nor a free pass.

There are some rewards with the risk, and here they are.

One of its biggest benefits is that it asks you to move the spine one segment at a time, rather than treating your entire spine as a single rigid block. That controlled flexion and extension can improve body awareness and give you more options for how you move.

The Jefferson curl also places the hamstrings, spinal erectors, and other posterior tissues under tension through a longer ROM than many strength exercises. “I believe the Jefferson curl certainly has a place,” explains Babenko. “To decompress the spine and stretch out the posterior chain.”

Most flexibility work is passive, but the Jefferson curl takes a different approach by loading the range you are trying to improve. That means the hamstrings and trunk are not just becoming more flexible; they are learning to produce and control force in that range.

If your hamstrings are tight, your spinal control disappears, or you start chasing depth without control, you’ll find out quickly. That makes the exercise useful for both mobility assessment and training.

Repeated or prolonged spinal flexion can increase creep in passive tissues such as ligaments and discs, raising the risk of lower back injury. Critics view it as an unnecessary risk. Their argument is simple: if you can improve mobility without loading end-range spinal flexion, why take the chance?

Any lifter worth their salt knows to keep their back neutral during deadlifts, squats, and rows. “Most lifters need to generate, transfer, and resist substantial forces while maintaining core stiffness and controlling spinal motion,” explains strength coach Matt Wenning. “These exercises can develop these qualities without requiring loaded end-range spinal flexion.”

Loading flexion can blur the line between a controlled mobility drill and poor technique during heavy lifting.

It’s not meant as a strength test, but some people will always try to see how much weight they can do. “I had a patient show up with back pain,” says Babenko. “She was trying to build up well over 150 pounds on the Jefferson curl, and, well, she hurt her lower back.”

Too much load increases the risk. Instead of slow, controlled segmental flexion, you get a loaded collapse with little benefit.

Hamstring stretching, hip-hinge drills, and RDLs can all enhance hamstring mobility without taking the spine through deliberate loaded flexion. That makes the Jefferson Curl optional.

There is no direct research on the Jefferson curl itself—a point backed up by Wenning.

“Direct evidence that Jefferson curls improve athletic performance, prevent low-back injuries, or outperform established strength and mobility interventions is insufficient.”

But most research focuses on spinal flexion, segmental spinal movement, flexibility training, and how spinal tissues behave under repeated or prolonged flexion.

A 2023 study comparing spinal flexion and extension exercises with more trunk movement found improvements in spinal flexibility, including finger-to-floor distance. This supports one of the Jefferson curl’s main purposes: learning to move through spinal flexion gradually rather than treating the spine as a single rigid unit.

One study found that 20 minutes of full lumbar flexion altered passive tissue contributions and increased demand on the spinal extensors afterward, meaning the extensors had to work harder after sustained flexion.

That study focused on sustained or repeated flexion exposures, not a few slow Jefferson curls. A set of five deliberate reps is not the same dose as sitting or hanging in deep flexion for 20 minutes.

No current research says spinal flexion is dangerous. “The spine can certainly flex, and spinal flexion itself is not inherently dangerous,” says Wenning

You know the pros and cons, but that doesn’t determine whether the Jefferson curl is a good fit. But this does.

The Jefferson curl sits in the moderate- high risk, moderate-reward category. The risk comes from loaded spinal flexion. That does not mean flexion is dangerous, but it does mean the dose matters. Too much load, range, fatigue, or volume can turn this mobility drill into unnecessary stress on the spine’s passive tissues.

“Jefferson curls offer limited advantages for most lifters,” says Wenning. “Particularly when more established exercises can address the same training goals with greater specificity and more controllable loading.”

The reward is that the Jefferson curl can train something most strength programs avoid: controlled spinal flexion. It can improve segmental spinal control, reveal mobility restrictions, and load the posterior chain through a long range of motion. For healthy lifters who tolerate flexion well, it can be useful.

The biggest factor in the risk-to-reward equation is intent. If you treat the Jefferson curl like a max-strength lift, the risk climbs fast. If you treat it like a light, slow, carefully progressed mobility-strength drill, the equation changes.

The reward isn’t massive, and the risk isn’t trivial, which is why this exercise belongs in the “use with a reason” category rather than the “everyone should do it” one.

The Jefferson curl isn’t one-size-fits-all, and people have opinions on both sides of the spectrum. Critics see a rounded spine and assume danger. Supporters sometimes swing too far the other way and act like controlled flexion makes the spine bulletproof.

Many exercises have a larger margin for error, like biceps curls, while others have a smaller margin, like the Jefferson curl. That means you should approach this exercise with caution.

But for the right lifter, used with light loads and a slow progression, it can be one way to train a position most strength programs avoid altogether. Spinal flexion sometimes happens, so it helps to be prepared.

The problem isn’t always spinal flexion; it’s treating flexion without respect.
