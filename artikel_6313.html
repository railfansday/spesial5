Whale sharks actually eat and digest plants, making them the largest omnivores on the planet

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Researchers found that giant whale sharks digest the floating seaweed they swallow. By eating both plants and meat, this fish is now officially the world's largest known omnivore.

The whale shark is the biggest fish in the sea. Like all sharks, it’s generally thought of as a meat-eater.

But a whale shark feeds by swimming with its mouth wide open. Along with small animals like fish and phytoplankton, those enormous mouths also grab bits of floating seaweed and seagrass, which are plants.

A study of 17 whale sharks in Western Australia suggests the sharks don’t only swallow that seaweed. They appear to digest some of it and use it as food.

If so, the whale shark is the largest known omnivore, an animal that eats both plants and animals.

Mark Meekan, a fish biologist at the Australian Institute of Marine Science, and five colleagues reported the finding in 2022. The team ran chemical tests on small samples of shark skin and didn’t test digestion directly.

Whale sharks can grow up to 59 feet (18 meters) long and weigh about 75,000 pounds (34,000 kilograms).

A fish that big needs a lot of food in one place. Whale sharks feed on dense swarms of krill, which are small shrimp-like animals.

Near the coast, though, the same ocean conditions that bring krill together also collect floating seaweed.

At Ningaloo Reef, a brown seaweed called Sargassum forms large floating mats, and whale sharks have been seen feeding among them.

Swallowed seaweed takes up room in a shark’s gut that food could fill. A shark can throw up what’s in its stomach, but then it loses its meal along with the seaweed.

One simple solution, the team wrote, might be to digest as much of the seaweed as possible.

The skin samples came from sharks that gathered at Ningaloo in May of 2015 and 2017. A snorkeler took each sample with a hand spear.

The team also collected krill and Sargassum, along with droppings from five sharks.

The first of two chemical tests measured nitrogen in each sample to work out where a shark feeds in the food chain.

Seaweed and other living things that make their own food are at level one. An animal ranks about one level above whatever it eats.

The 17 whale sharks had an average rank of 2.0. Krill from the same reef had an average rank of 2.4.

If whale sharks ate mostly krill, the sharks should rank about one level higher than the krill. Instead, the sharks and the krill ranked about the same, and krill eat both plants and animals.

The team wrote that this low rank implies whale sharks regularly eat plant material.

The second test compared fatty acids, the building blocks of fats. One fatty acid that is rare in most fish made up 16% of the fatty acids in whale shark skin.

The same fatty acid made up 10% of the fatty acids in Sargassum, but only 3% to 5% in krill. That made Sargassum the more likely source of the fatty acid in the sharks’ skin, the researchers wrote.

The sharks’ droppings, though, contained fats similar to those in krill.

“The poo did show that they were eating krill,” said Patti Virtue, a biological oceanographer at the University of Tasmania’s Institute for Marine and Antarctic Studies. “But they’re not metabolizing much of it.”

Skin samples can’t show what happens inside a shark’s gut. The results suggest that whale sharks may be able to digest at least some parts of floating Sargassum.

Some of that nourishment might come from small animals and algae living on the seaweed.

The fatty acid could also come from tiny organisms in seafloor mud, passed along by krill that spend the day on the bottom.

The ranks also depend on a conversion number that’s hard to pin down without feeding experiments. Other values were unlikely to change the ranks much, the team wrote.

All 17 sharks came from one reef, but sharks tagged at Ningaloo travel across the Timor Sea and the eastern Indian Ocean.

On land, the biggest animals have always been plant-eaters, Meekan said. “Turns out that maybe the system of evolution on land and in the water isn’t that different after all.”

The team wrote that manta rays, basking sharks, and possibly some filter-feeding whales could be swallowing seaweed too.

The full study was published in the journal Ecology.
