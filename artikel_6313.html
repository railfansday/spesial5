The earliest corporate psychopath on record may have been been this traitor from the 1600s

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

A management researcher scored a 1629 mutiny leader against a modern corporate psychopath checklist, and he matched all ten traits.

For about 30 years, researchers have studied corporate psychopaths. They are the charming, ruthless bosses who rule by fear and blame everyone else. Most of the examples that come to mind are usually living or recent executives.

Now the earliest one on record may be a merchant who ran a survivors’ camp after a 1629 wreck. Scored against a ten-item checklist built for today’s boardrooms, Jeronimus Cornelisz of the Dutch East India Company matches every item.

Clive Boddy, an associate professor of management at Anglia Ruskin University in England, wrote the paper alone. Boddy has spent years studying corporate psychopathy.

This paper is a historical case study rather than an experiment. It leans mainly on the historian Mike Dash’s 2003 book about the wreck.

Other sources are the expedition commander’s journal, a pastor’s letters, trial records, and skeletons dug up on the island.

“What makes this case remarkable is that when we analyzed Cornelisz using a modern management psychopathy framework, he displayed every major characteristic associated with corporate psychopaths,” Boddy said.

The Batavia was the company’s new flagship, bound for the port the Dutch had renamed Batavia, now Jakarta.

On the morning of June 4, 1629, it struck a reef in the Houtman Abrolhos islands. The reef lies 80 miles (130 kilometers) off Western Australia, and most of the people aboard managed to reach a small coral island.

Somewhere between 180 and 250 people made it ashore, and 30 of them were women and children. The island had no fresh water of its own.

The commander, Francisco Pelsaert, the ship’s captain, and 46 others then left in an overloaded longboat to fetch help from Jakarta, 900 miles (1,450 kilometers) away.

That left Cornelisz, a junior merchant and the most senior company man still on the island, in charge of everyone else.

Cornelisz had been plotting a mutiny before the ship went down. Once in command, Boddy wrote, he replaced the lawful council with his fellow plotters and had them swear personal oaths of loyalty.

He moved the money chests, water, and food into his own tent, and the plan was to seize whatever rescue ship arrived and turn pirate.

He also sent groups of survivors to two other islands, supposedly to look for water, and left them there. Then he told his followers that the island’s population had to shrink if supplies were to last.

Between 96 and 115 men, women, and children were murdered over the following weeks, and some of the women were subjected to sexual violence.

Cornelisz ordered the killings and, with one exception, left others to carry them out. A pastor who survived wrote that the remaining people lived in constant fear of being next.

In Boddy’s reading, that climate of fear is the same tool corporate psychopaths use to keep a workforce in line.

The soldiers Cornelisz had dumped on the farthest island did not die. Their island had two freshwater wells, seabird colonies, fish, wallabies, and seals.

Under a soldier named Wiebbe Hayes, the group of 46 men and a boy fed itself and took in people who escaped the killings.

When word of the murders reached them, Hayes had his men forge pikes from wreckage, pile up coral to throw, and build a small stone fort on a hill.

They turned back two raids, captured Cornelisz during a third attempt, and were holding off a fourth, this time against muskets, when the rescue ship appeared. Hayes’s men reached it first and warned the crew.

Cornelisz and his ringleaders were tried on the spot, confessed, and were hanged. Hayes was promoted.

Boddy calls the two islands a natural experiment in leadership: same wreck, same weather, and one group built cooperation and found food while the other descended into murder.

The checklist is a ten-item measure of what researchers call primary psychopathy, written for management research rather than for prisons.

Boddy’s rule for judging someone from history is that they must clearly show at least eight of the ten. The same test has been applied to the financier Bernard Madoff, the media owner Robert Maxwell, and the Nazi leader Hermann Goering.

Cornelisz scores on all ten in the paper’s table of characteristics shown by corporate psychopaths. The items include superficial charm, habitual lying, total self-regard, no remorse, and shallow emotion.

They end with a calm, rational manner while ordering murders, and no insight into his own behavior. Dash had already called Cornelisz a psychopath on an informal reading of the criminal criteria.

Boddy’s paper is the first to run him through a management measure item by item. The record it draws on includes the wreck itself, found by divers in 1963 and partly reconstructed by the Western Australian Museum.

Boddy also visited the Museum of Geraldton, which holds finds from the wreck, as background.

Everything in the assessment comes from documents and from one historian’s account of them. There is no way to interview, test, or observe the man himself, and the paper reports no new data and has no limitations section of its own.

Prevalence is the other open number. About 1 percent of adults are usually counted as categorically psychopathic, and a newer pooled analysis that used non-criminal measures found 4.5 percent. Either way, the share is small in the general population.

Among senior corporate leaders, one estimate comes to about 4 percent, with some industries drawing more of the money-hungry personality than others. How many of them run companies today isn’t something a shipwreck from 1629 can say.

Boddy’s next candidate is another company man, Governor General Jan Coen, who ordered massacres while building the Dutch spice monopoly.

The paper closes by asking personnel departments to weigh character and values when they pick senior leaders. The pattern it describes, Boddy wrote, has apparently been in place since the first shareholder-owned company existed.

The full study was published in the journal Asian Journal of Business Ethics.
