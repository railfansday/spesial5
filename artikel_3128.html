Pfizer's 6% Yield vs. AbbVie's 53-Year Dividend Streak: Which Stock Is the Better Buy?

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Pfizer is facing down big patent expirations, but that's pretty normal in the drug sector.

By Reuben Gregg Brewer – Nov 27, 2026 at 3:05PM EST

AbbVie's (ABBV -0.88%) 2.3% dividend yield pales in comparison to Pfizer's (PFE +0.21%) 6%. But it is important to recognize that the average drug company's yield is only 1.3% and the broader market's yield is just 1%, so AbbVie's yield isn't exactly small. Here's why conservative dividend investors wouldn't be making a mistake by buying AbbVie, but if you can handle a little uncertainty, Pfizer's much higher yield may end up being the better option.

One of the big positives of AbbVie as an investment is that it is a Dividend King. That means that the company has increased its dividend annually for 50+ consecutive years (53 to be specific), which is not something that happens by accident. It requires a strong business model that is executed well in both good and bad markets. The only problem here is that AbbVie hasn't actually existed for 50 years. It was created in 2013 when it was spun off from Abbott Laboratories (ABT +0.07%). It retained Abbott's dividend record and then, to AbbVie's credit, continued to raise its dividend each year.

Still, it probably isn't appropriate to give AbbVie full credit for its Dividend King status. You should pay extra attention to its actual business, which is well-run and fairly well-positioned. It has strong positions in immunology and oncology. Meanwhile, the drug maker's aesthetics business doesn't face the same patent risks as other drug niches because brand names are important differentiators. As a business, it is highly desirable.

There's just one small problem: the stock isn't cheap. Its price-to-sales ratio is 7.9x compared to a five-year average of 5.4x. Its price-to-earnings ratio is 50x compared to a five-year average of 41x. You are paying full fair, if not more, for the stock's above-average yield.

That's where Pfizer starts to look relatively attractive. Not only is its dividend yield likely to be highly attractive to income seekers, but the stock is also much cheaper than AbbVie. Pfizer's 2.5x P/S ratio is in line with its five-year average and far lower than AbbVie's 7.9x. Pfizer's P/E ratio is 40x, which is biased higher by one-time charges and still lower than AbbVie's 50x. If you use the $3.00 per share midpoint of Pfizer's 2026 adjusted earnings guidance, the P/E is around 10x. That's below the stock's nearly 14x five-year average.

A big yield from a cheap stock will likely have more appeal than a smaller yield from an expensive stock for most investors. But there are issues to consider, most notably Pfizer's upcoming patent expirations in 2028 (Eliquis) and 2031 (Vyndamax). Those are big deals and will likely result in a substantial decline in revenues and earnings. The thing is, patent expirations are entirely normal in the pharmaceutical sector. Pfizer is working hard to develop new drugs to offset the hit, but sometimes the timing doesn't perfectly align.

Still, given the company's strong history, which dates back to the mid-1800s, it seems highly likely that it will manage this patent expiration cycle, just as it has many times before. As for the dividend, the company is publicly supportive of it and continues to tell investors that its long-term goal is to grow the payment. And even if there were a 50% dividend cut, which seems like a reasonable worst-case scenario, the yield would still be higher than AbbVie's 2.3%.

If you are a risk-averse investor who simply can't tolerate any kind of uncertainty, AbbVie is a perfectly fine, though perhaps expensive, stock to buy. However, Pfizer's higher yield and more attractive valuation will probably make it a better option, assuming you believe it can muddle through its patent-expiration issues, as it has many times before. Indeed, the risk-versus-reward balance is likely tilted toward reward for long-term dividend investors.
