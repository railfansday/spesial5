The Tour du Mont Blanc Is Getting New Rules in 2027. Having a Guide Is More Important Than Ever.

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

A view of the of the Alps on Tour du Mont Blanc. (Photo: Getty)

The trail ahead of us narrows to a ledge, with rock on our left and a sheer drop on our right, camouflaged by trees. After hearing the now-familiar heavy breaths of runners behind me, I move to the mountainside to let them pass. It’s mid-August, and the Ultra-Trail du Mont-Blanc (UTMB), the ultramarathon circling Mont Blanc, is kicking off later this month in Chamonix. We started our own Tour du Mont Blanc in Chamonix, the mountain town known as the birthplace of alpinism, which was buzzing with excitement and packs of runners strapped into hydration packs. Ahead of me, a young man is stopped on the narrow dirt trail, calling out to a friend on the forest slope below. Before I can even turn around, our guide, Jason Day, springs into action, grabs a rope from his pack, and helps pull the hiker, whose pack had tumbled down the slope, back to safety.

Moments like that are becoming more common with more trekkers on the Tour du Mont Blanc (TMB), the 170-kilometer (106-mile) loop around Western Europe’s highest peak through France, Italy, and Switzerland. Roughly 80,000 people hike it each year, and visits are up about 50 percent since 2020. “People can only see the backsides of those in front of them,” Jean-Marc Peillex, mayor of Saint-Gervais-les-Bains, a mountain town at the base of Mont Blanc in France, told Swiss broadcaster SRF. That wasn’t my exact experience for most of the trek, but in a few places, especially near Chamonix, the trail was congested.

Chamonix and the six other French towns along the TMB route, from Vallorcine to Bourg-Saint-Maurice and Beaufort, have drafted a charter to manage crowds, with local officials aiming to put it in place by spring 2027, ahead of next year’s trekking season. The new rules, aiming to curb overtourism and help slow environmental damage, are awaiting final signature. The key proposals include reservations for every night on the trail; a ban on wild camping, with paid, bookable bivouac areas replacing the informal spots; and new limits on luggage transfers. Guided groups would be capped at twelve and led by guides trained in rescue and licensed in all three countries.

The charter is voluntary, covers only France for now, and still depends on local ordinances for enforcement. But the message is clear: the era of showing up and figuring it out on the TMB is ending. Chamonix’s mayor, François-Xavier Laffin, who is similarly introducing quotas at nearby Lac Blanc, an alpine lake also suffering from its popularity, puts it this way: “To reserve is to preserve.”

Adding to the growing traffic on the trail are the effects of climate change on the Alps. Rising summer temperatures are accelerating permafrost thaw and glacier retreat, contributing to record rockfall activity in the Mont Blanc massif. While this mostly impacts the summit climbing routes, hot summers also change trail and water conditions for hikers on the Tour du Mont Blanc.

“As for the environmental impacts, trail erosion is of course the most obvious one, but we must also take into account wild bivouacking, which has multiplied, with impacts on the flora as well as the disturbance of wildlife,” says Daniel Rodrigues, general director of the Compagnie des Guides de Chamonix, the oldest mountain guide organization in the world.

“The reservation system is fair. Everyone can book their places, and tour operators can do the same. So the same rules apply to everyone, including amateur mountain users, and that matters to us: the mountains are a space of freedom,” says Rodrigues. “That said, this space must be protected, in terms of biodiversity and environmental impacts, but also economically.”

The charter puts licensed mountain professionals at the center of safety on the trail. According to Rodrigues, that means operators will no longer be able to sell self-guided packages (just bookings and transfers) and will have to include a guide certified in all three countries.

“This is a major step forward, because the guide ensures that everyone stays safe and that everything goes smoothly. It also creates work for local people and delivers a genuine positive impact on the inhabitants of this mountain range,” adds Rodrigues.

I hiked the Tour du Mont Blanc in August with Live Breathe Hike, and I came home convinced that, especially under the potential new rules, having a local guide is no longer a luxury. It’s the smartest way to hike this trail.

The TMB isn’t one trail; rather, there are a lot of different paths and detours.”There’s a classic route, but there’s a lot of different variants,” says Allison Fleece, cofounder of WHOA Travel, a women’s adventure-travel company that runs guided trips, including on the Tour du Mont Blanc. Some variants are higher and more exposed; others go along lower valleys. On our third day, instead of following the crowds, Day led us off the Col du Bonhomme on a variant toward Lac de Roselend, where we barely saw another hiker for miles. “You feel like you have the place to yourself,” says Live Breathe Hike founder Tanya Perret, who started the Chamonix-based trekking company in 2016 and says she has guided the TMB more than 75 times.

Under the proposed rules, every night would have to be booked in advance, across three countries with different systems. Guides already do that work, and finding gems to stay at like Le Chalet de Roselend takes deep knowledge of the area. With only ten rooms, the chalet, whose balconies overlook the Lac de Roselend, was a godsend after one of our longest days of trekking. Years ago, Perret drove there to book it in person, because the elderly owner at the time only took reservations face to face. The young couple who now runs it cooks traditional dishes with local produce.

The charter asks operators to buy local and partner with local producers. Good guides already do. “Many of the places that we stay are generational families, and I’ve known those families for 30 years,” says Perret. On our trip, that meant a stop at a family cheese farm as well as the small lakeside chalet. “That’s the micro-economy,” adds Perret.

Nearly every day on my trek, Day chose a variant. Perret says the choice comes down to two factors: where accommodation is available and which trail will give the group the best experience that day.

The variants usually turned out to be my favorite stretches. Self-guided trekkers can research the variants, but a guide knows which one fits the day. “By giving your guides a little bit of autonomy, it means they can just tweak things here and there, which makes for a unique journey,” says Perret.

“The weather patterns change a lot in the mountains and they change really fast,” adds Fleece, who partners with Compagnie des Guides de Chamonix on their guided TMB trips. “Guides have years and years, most of the time decades, of experience.” A guide, she says, can help you avoid dicey situations, “but also, if you get yourself into a dicey situation, having a guide is like a safety measure to get you quickly and swiftly out of it.”

Day has been trekking Tour Du Mont Blanc since 1992 and knows the ever-changing mountain conditions demand quick judgment. On a recent trip up Mont Blanc’s Italian route, he and his climbing partner turned back in poor snow. “We binned the whole trip,” he says. Two friends kept going, and on a ridge with snow cornices on both sides, one side broke away. “Either side could have gone,” he says.

Multi-day treks around the world are my favorite way to travel. With WHOA, I’ve summited Kilimanjaro and Mount Elbrus, Europe’s tallest peak (not Mont Blanc, as many assume). I’ve taken guided trips on the Trans Bhutan Trail and Japan’s Shikoku pilgrimage, which were not nearly as challenging but would have been far less rich experiences without learning the culture from our local guides. I’ve trekked the full O Trek in Patagonia without a guide, but with friends and the help of the company Chile Nativo for booking refuges. Half of the O circuit, the W Trek, I hiked alone. So I know the benefits of taking guided multi-day treks and hiking alone at your own pace, stopping when you want.

I can understand why travelers would want to do the TMB without a guide; many hikers do so without any issue. You can go at your own speed and take a long break for an afternoon coffee at a refuge with an open table facing the sun. A guided trip costs more. Middle options have been growing, including self-guided packages that handle the bookings for you, but even those require your due diligence and research, and could be affected by these new rules for 2027.

One day on the trek, we stopped for lunch at a refuge and ran into a woman with a bandaged hand. She and her friend were on a self-guided trip whose itinerary, she told us, had started them on their first day hiking one of the route’s most exposed stretches—the ladders below Tête aux Vents—where she got injured. On the standard counterclockwise route, that section usually comes at the very end. Day told us that those ladders can easily be bypassed in busy seasons or wet weather.

If you want to escape the crowds on the Tour du Mont Blanc, guides know the variants that lead to the quietest parts of the trail. Except for a few days, on certain passes, we managed to find stretches where it was just us, as far as I  could see. On our fourth day, we crossed the Col de la Seigne into Italy, past a block of granite barely knee-high: the real border marker, not the big monument nearby. I’d have walked right past it. My guide didn’t let me.
