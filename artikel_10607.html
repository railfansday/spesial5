Zero-Based Learning: Why Spending Billions In AI Training Isn't Building Capability

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Brendan Hooft, CEO & Managing Partner of ESPER.

​Picture a civil servant somewhere in the Gulf finishing her third AI certificate of the year. She’s proud of it, and she should be. Now ask her manager what she can do today that she couldn’t do a year ago. The room goes quiet. That silence is the most expensive sound in government.

The investment is historic. Saudi Arabia’s Human Resources Development Fund alone spent more than 8.29 billion Saudi riyals on training and related support in 2025. SAMAI trained more than 1 million citizens in AI, and SAMAI 2 carries that ambition into the government workforce itself. Adoption followed: A June 2026 BCG report found that 93% of front-line employees in the GCC were using AI several times a week.

The Gulf has won the adoption race, but the capability race has barely begun.

Earlier in this series, I set out the Human-AI Symphony playbook and defined what AI-native means. The harder question is why learning investment isn’t becoming capability.

BCG’s 70-20-10 rule holds that organizations scaling AI put 70% of their effort into people and processes. However, only 4% of companies consistently generate significant value from AI, and Gartner Inc. warned that leaders are “mistaking basic access or adoption metrics for transformation.”

If 70% of AI’s value rests on people, learning is the largest dependency in any AI strategy. Across the region, we’ve handed it to someone else.

Most AI learning arrives through a cloud provider’s academy, a model provider’s curriculum or a platform certificate. It’s generous and often free, and that’s the point. Free training is a sales channel. You buy the platform, the provider trains your people on the platform, and the certificate proves they can use the platform. You’ve built familiarity with a product, not a skill. The capability belongs to the vendor, and you lease it back with every license renewal.

Capability a nation owns must be defined and measured independently of any vendor. Otherwise, every AI program deepens the dependency it was meant to end.

Free carries no invoice, but a few hours across a million people is a large bill.

Our own learning platforms offer tens of thousands of courses and no map. People browse, start and drift away, and we blame engagement for low completion. What was missing was direction. Dashboards then report reach, from enrollments to certificates. They measure effort, not capability.

Building AI capability is every leader’s duty, and it can’t be outsourced because the training is free.

When capability is measured directly, the picture changes. We recently assessed multiple entities in one GCC government—30 roles and 141 skills at five proficiency levels against a national-grade standard, informed by 26 structured interviews.

The entities scored 195 out of 500 on our priority index, signaling a significant gap across every capability assessed. Of 105 capability items, 103 needed immediate action.

The technology was there. The capability to deploy, operate and scale it wasn’t.

Closing that gap calls for a discipline every finance ministry knows: zero-based budgeting, where nothing renews by default. Applied to learning, no AI program continues simply because it ran last year.

Adopt one governed standard defining what organizations must be able to do, what people must know and how they must work with AI. Measure every role, program and provider against it.

Break each critical role into tasks and decide what happens to each one: automate it, delegate it to agents, augment it or keep it human-led. Your real AI opportunities surface here, not in a vendor’s catalog.

Assess each person against the AI-native profile for their role, and the gap becomes their pathway. Give real weight to curiosity, judgment, systems thinking, agency and adaptability, where the hardest gaps sit. AI amplifies character.

Build what’s core and sovereign, buy what’s urgent and scarce, and borrow what’s specialist and temporary. Where a partner must deliver now but the capability must end in national hands, use build-operate-transfer. That’s where vendor academies belong, with clear handover obligations.

Zero-based learning needs a capability system of record: one place that holds the standard, assesses every person against their role, maps which tasks can be automated or handed to agents and connects to the learning platforms, academies and HR systems you already own. The platforms deliver; the record directs and proves. It reports time to proficiency, coverage of critical roles and verified progression and feeds hiring, mobility and succession.

The difference is measurable. When we ran a 12-month program this way for 20 senior leaders, their proficiency rose by 35% measured against the same standard at the start and end.

Similar results start with three questions that should be asked before any AI learning program is approved:

1. Which task will it change? If no one can name one, you’re training people for work nobody has redesigned.

2. Which role does it serve? A program built for everyone serves no one, and the hours scatter.

3. What proficiency will it prove? If success means completions or certificates, you’re buying activity, not capability.

A program that passes all three earns its budget. One that fails any of them shouldn’t renew. Ministers and CHROs can start this quarter—adopt one standard, rewrite 10 critical roles and assess the people in them.

The civil servant with three certificates deserves more than a fourth. She deserves to know what her role now demands, where she stands against it and what will close the gap. The Gulf moved faster than almost anyone on adoption. It can move just as fast on capability once it stops counting learners and starts counting what they can do.
