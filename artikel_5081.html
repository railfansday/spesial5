Claude-assisted proof of Riemann hypothesis validated by independent checkers

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

A clean rebuild reproduced the Lean proof export byte for byte, and separate tools checked its conclusion and reasoning.

Independent checkers validated Levent Alpöge’s Claude-assisted proof of the Riemann hypothesis, the Lean and Mathlib communities said on Nov. 21. Teams rebuilt his roughly 17-million-line library, matched its conclusion to the original conjecture and checked each step of the exported proof. They established what the code proved while a complete mathematical write-up was still to come.

The conjecture dates to 1859. It says that when the Riemann zeta function equals zero, the solutions other than a known set of exceptions all lie on the same vertical line, where the real part is one-half. Where those zeros lie helps explain how the distribution of prime numbers deviates from its average pattern.

Alpöge released the proof on Nov. 20 as code for Lean, a system for writing and checking mathematical proofs. In his release note, he said he chose a setting where two mathematical ideas could be combined and credited Anthropic’s AI assistant Claude with suggesting advances late in the argument.

In his MIT-hosted verification logs, Andrew Sutherland reported that a rebuild on a separate machine, with networking disabled and the source files set to read-only, produced a byte-for-byte match to the published proof export. The export contains the formal proof steps in a form that other checkers can read.

In their joint statement, the Lean and Mathlib communities said they put the target theorem in a separate challenge file that imported only Mathlib, Lean’s library of formalized mathematics. Two comparison tools confirmed that the conclusion in Alpöge’s proof matched Mathlib’s existing statement of the conjecture.

Two independently written checkers, nanoda and con-leche, accepted the full export, Sutherland reported. They checked whether the steps justified the conclusion using the exported data, which kept executable code in the original source files out of that part of the process.

Sutherland’s team also replayed all 175,187 compiled modules through Lean’s own checker. The team’s additional Lean runs on the raw export were still underway in his Nov. 22 update. The checks also covered a generalized version of the conjecture for Dirichlet L-functions.

Alpöge said he planned to write up the proof for mathematicians to read. Michael Rubinstein at the University of Waterloo posted a working guide dated Nov. 21 that explains parts of the argument for the original conjecture. The guide still relies on the Lean development for the proof of a central inequality.

Clay Mathematics Institute’s problem page still listed the conjecture as unsolved when checked on Nov. 22. Under Clay’s prize rules, the institute considers a proposed solution only after publication in a qualifying outlet, at least two years since that publication and general acceptance in the global mathematics community.
