‘70% chance’ we’re already in an AI bubble: The economist who said ‘not yet’ in January now sees all four horsemen of the bubble apocalypse

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Owen Lamont told Fortune in January that the stock market wasn’t in an AI bubble. Now he’s mostly convinced.

“I think there is a 70% chance that as of November 2026 we are already in an AI bubble in the U.S. stock market,” Lamont, a portfolio manager at Acadian Asset Management and a former University of Chicago finance professor, told Fortune in an email. “I think a lot has changed this year.”

What changed is the one signal he used to say was missing. Lamont tracks what he calls the “four horsemen of the bubble apocalypse”: overvaluation, bubble beliefs, issuance and inflows of new money. At the time, he said three were present. The holdout was issuance, meaning companies selling new stock to the public. Back then, U.S. firms had bought back roughly $1 trillion of their own shares over the past year, and Lamont treats corporate executives as the “smart money.” They weren’t selling.

They are now. U.S. companies issued a net $2.4 trillion of stock over the 12 months to Nov. 12, by Lamont’s count. In the 12 months to November 2025, they bought back a net $69 billion, and a year before that, $397 billion. In his latest Owenomics blog post, he walks back his January headline: “Today, I would no longer write those words.”

“I think we basically have all four horsemen now,” he told Fortune.

The big number has a big asterisk. Of the $2.4 trillion issuance, $2.2 trillion comes from SpaceX’s June IPO, which Lamont’s method counts at its full market value. Strip it out and net issuance is $192 billion.

That’s still a reversal from net buybacks a year earlier, and the turn includes the biggest names: the Magnificent Seven went from net buybacks of $144 billion in the year to November 2024 to net issuance of $276 billion now, according to Lamont. He writes that Alphabet and Meta switched from buying back shares to issuing them, Tesla sharply increased issuance, and Meta announced plans to raise nearly $98 billion in equity to fund its AI spending.

Lamont admits the SpaceX question is “tough.” “I think one company is not enough to constitute a ‘wave of issuance,’” he said. “On the other hand, we live in a world of concentrated stock markets where a handful of companies have huge market cap, so maybe we should expect waves of issuance that are also concentrated.”

Professional investors are moving the other way: fund managers just cut their net overweight in tech to 12% from 37%, the biggest one-month drop since June 2006, according to Bank of America’s November survey.

Why companies are selling is the open question. In his note, Lamont laid out two explanations: “the smart money is selling because the market is overpriced,” or firms “need to fund the AI buildout.” Asked which he leans toward, he said: “I’m not sure. Could be both.”

Either way, the shares are going to someone. TD Securities’ rates strategists flagged the same shift from the bond side this week, noting that Meta and Oracle have issued equity and that more AI capex funded with stock could ease pressure on long-term Treasury yields.

He also thinks the case for overvaluation got stronger this year because real long-term interest rates rose about 100 basis points. But he isn’t calling a top. “It is not a slam dunk case: the market might be overvalued, but it is not absurdly overvalued,” he said. “Net issuance has turned positive, but it’s not strongly positive.”

By his measure, net issuance is running at 3.1% of total market value, still below the peaks of the 1999–2000 tech bubble and the 2021 COVID boom.

Lamont gave Fortune three conditions that would take him from 70% to a flat call.

“First, the Anthropic IPO occurs and is a success. Second, we see a wave of smaller IPOs, similar to what we saw in 2021: hundreds of IPOs, say five IPOs a week, many of them doubling on the first day. Third, CAPE ratio or other valuation ratios blow through prior records.”

The second one is far off. The U.S. had 90 IPOs in 2025, against 311 in 2021 and 677 in 1996, according to Jay Ritter’s data cited in Lamont’s March note. In January, he told Fortune the shortage of IPOs was “baffling,” and that a full bubble could look like the Nasdaq 100 doubling in a year with the Shiller CAPE ratio near 80. It was around 40 then.

The first condition is about a day away. Anthropic is set to price its IPO Wednesday and begin trading on Nasdaq on Thursday under the ticker ANT. Lamont writes that the deal, if it goes ahead, would push issuance above its 2021 peak. Asked if that’s the moment to watch, he said yes, and pointed back to his three conditions.
