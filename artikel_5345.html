Why businesses are embracing ‘good enough’ open-weight AI—and how geopolitics could get in the way

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

As the performance gap between open-weight and closed-weight models narrows, a growing number of businesses—from local start-ups to large multinationals—are turning to open-weight models for greater control, customization and cost efficiency.

“Frontier models at scale are incredibly expensive,” Damian Kim, the Asia-Pacific vice president of social media platform Pinterest, tells Fortune. “The cost to serve can be reduced dramatically when using an open-weight model versus a frontier model, where you’re at the whims of the API pricing.”

Anyone can download and customize the weights behind an open-weight model, which means firms can cut token costs while keeping tighter control of their data. They’re also taking a growing share of AI traffic. In August, open-weight models ran the majority of traffic on Vercel’s AI gateway; 56% of tokens routed through the cloud platform went to open-weight models, up from 7% last year.

“Models are converging on similar performance for industry use: U.S. government evaluators put the best Chinese model about eight months behind the American frontier,” says Olaf Groth, a professor of practice at UC Berkeley’s Haas School of Business. “For most enterprise workloads, that is good enough.”

Rather than paying for the most powerful model for every task, companies are turning to cheaper, customizable alternatives for routine work. But with Chinese developers behind many of the leading open-weight models, geopolitics may matter as much as cost or performance.

Before GPT-3.5 propelled AI to mainstream fame, most high-performing models were either small academic releases or commercial systems with restricted outside access. The first open-weight models originated in academic research, where sharing model weights to ensure reproducibility was standard practice.

In 2019, OpenAI went against tradition by initially withholding the full 1.5-billion-parameter version of GPT-2, citing risks of misuse such as disinformation, impersonation and phishing. Most U.S. frontier labs, including Anthropic and OpenAI, keep the underlying parameters private, giving users access through apps or APIs rather than letting them download and run the models themselves.

In the early years of generative AI, most users preferred closed-weight models because they delivered performance that open-weight alternatives could not match. Yet in recent years, the improved capability and cost-efficiency of powerful Chinese models like Moonshot AI’s Kimi K3.1 Pro and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash has swung the pendulum back in favor of open-weight models.

“For the longest time, the idea has been that you can get the highest level of performance using closed-weight models, so many firms start off by using them,” says Hariharasudhan Saminathan, cofounder of Friday, a sovereign AI startup backed by global venture capital firm Antler. But now, “through distillation and other processes, open-weight models’ performance almost matches that of closed-weight models,” he says.

“Many of our pipeline customers are now adopting open-weight models right off the bat,” he adds. “They’re not even touching the closed-weight ones.”

Pinterest, for example, uses Alibaba’s open-weight Qwen models to power its online platform. “We’ve driven down our inference costs using post-trained open-weight models,” Kim explains. “They offer over 90% cost reduction.” (Many leading Chinese open-weight models activate only a subset of parameters per token, cutting compute and energy needed per request.)

Kim also cites the flexibility of these models as a draw. Last October, the firm released Pinterest Assistant: an AI-powered tool that pairs a customized Qwen multimodal LLM with the firm’s proprietary Taste Graph, to generate shopping recommendations for users based on their individual aesthetic preferences.

“[With open-weight technology], we can create a specialized model that actually does what we need it to do very effectively, which is understanding visual information,” says Kim.

Rather than choosing one or the other, some firms are using a mix of open-weight and closed-weight AI models.

Eu Leong Oh, medical director at The Longevity Suite Asia, a Singapore-based wellness clinic, says he’s using Claude and Google’s Gemini to build the team’s in-house healthcare portal, but plans to pivot to open-weight models for daily use. “For things like prototyping, exploration and research, it’s still very useful to have a large-parameter, trained, closed-weight model,” Oh says.

He compares a top-tier frontier model to a Michelin-starred chef. “You’ll want a chef like Gordon Ramsay to decide the processes in your restaurant, but you don’t need him to do the cooking all the time,” he says. “Similarly, within companies, a lot of the heavy lifting and token budget will eventually be taken over by more cost effective open-weight models.”

Token costs are also skyrocketing with the rise of agentic AI. An AI agent might consume five times more tokens than a chatbot interface, because it runs multi-step loops rather than a single task. Those increasing costs may push companies to adopt cheaper open-weight models on top of existing closed-weight ones.

“Companies are becoming more cost-conscious about their AI deployment,” says Srividya Jandhyala, a professor of management at ESSEC Business School. “Not every task needs the power of a frontier model, and companies are recognizing that ‘good enough’ models work for a large variety of operations.”

Yet a company’s decision whether or not to use an open-weight model could come down to politics, rather than cost or technical ability. The leading open-weight models come from Chinese companies like DeepSeek, Alibaba, and z.AI; their growing usage in both U.S. and global business is worrying some in Washington about China’s growing influence over the technology.

“The American AI stack remains far stronger than China’s and we must continue to lead,” Representative John Moolenaar (R-Mich.), chair of the U.S. House’s Select Committee on China, wrote in a Sept. 23 statement.

On Nov. 18, Reuters reported that the Trump administration was considering requiring U.S. firms to publicly disclose their use of Chinese AI models, in hopes of discouraging use of the technology without imposing new restrictions that might anger Beijing.

In a Nov. 20 report, Moolenaar warned that American businesses were adopting inexpensive Chinese AI models. “Federal investigations, however, have found that major PRC developers used proxy networks and fraudulent accounts to harvest outputs from American frontier models and replicate their capabilities. While America is investing billions and racing to build ‘Super Intelligence,’ Beijing is using stolen intelligence to close the gap,” he wrote.

Cursor—whose Composer coding agent was built on Moonshot’s Kimi model—told Congress that it’s considering phasing out Chinese AI models due to “geopolitical risk,” CNBC reported.

“For global companies, especially in Western markets, there is a trade-off between cheaper Chinese alternatives versus concerns about continued access,” explains Jandhyala of ESSEC. “If they think that future policies may force them to reduce their reliance on Chinese AI models, companies may pre-emptively start using U.S. open- or closed-weight models.”

A handful of U.S. companies—including Thinking Machines, Reflection AI and Nvidia—are releasing open-weight models. Nvidia and its recently returned CEO, Jensen Huang, have also championed the technology. According to Reuters, Huang’s support for open-weight AI helped persuade Trump and Vice President JD Vance to push for his return.

The concern that Chinese models may come with hidden backdoors may be overstated, says Erik Cambria, a professor of AI at Singapore’s Nanyang Technological University (NTU).

“Concerns about Chinese models should be distinguished between genuine technical security risks and geopolitical perceptions,” he says. “An open-weight model deployed locally, without transmitting sensitive data externally, presents a very different risk profile from a remotely hosted API, regardless of the model’s country of origin.”

Still, geopolitical concerns may drive companies to diversify where they get their models from. “Europeans optimize for sovereignty and compliance,” says Groth of UC Berkeley. “In Asia and the Gulf, pragmatists hedge across both stacks and refuse to pick a side, unless they need models and applications which implicate national security.”

For businesses, the goal may not be picking a winner in the AI race, but keeping their options open. “Businesses will combine models from different providers and select the most appropriate one for each workload,” says Cambria.
