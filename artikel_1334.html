Towards observing interstitial hydrogen in solution in metal lattices

Hydrogen-bearing materials are central to low-carbon energy technologies1, but hydrogen itself is difficult to detect and localize at the nanoscale2,3,4,5,6. This hinders the development of advanced materials with better efficiency. Atom probe tomography (APT) can map hydrogen in three dimensions and at high resolution, yet characterization of dilute, mobile, interstitial hydrogen in metals has remained challenging because of low solubility, the ambiguity owing to hydrogen trapping by structural defects, as well as the artefacts introduced during hydrogen introduction4,5,6. Here we address whether interstitial hydrogen in solution in materials can be distinguished from defect-trapped hydrogen in APT. Using deuterium as a proxy for hydrogen, together with face-centred cubic (FCC) multi-principal-element alloys (MPEAs) containing coherent FCC precipitates, we show that deuterium atoms reproducibly concentrate in hydrogen-affinitive phases in a manner consistent with theoretical predictions. We further resolve differences in deuterium content between phases with distinct local chemistries and crystal structures, consistent with their different calculated hydrogen solution energies. These findings demonstrate that APT can move beyond identifying hydrides and solute-decorated defects to examine hydrogen in solid solution, providing new insights into hydrogen partitioning in structural alloys and informing materials design for hydrogen-related applications.

Hydrogen (H) and hydrogen-containing compounds such as ammonia offer promising routes for low-carbon energy storage and utilization, supporting global decarbonization efforts1. Developing hydrogen energy technologies requires a fundamental understanding of how hydrogen interacts with materials. Hydrogen embrittles high-strength alloys in transport and storage systems2,7,8; its behaviour in metals determines the capacity and reversibility of solid-state storage9; hydride formation in zirconium-alloy cladding threatens fission reactor integrity10; and isotope retention is critical to fusion viability11. Across all of these applications, knowing where hydrogen resides in materials is essential.

Hydrogen in crystalline materials exists in three principal states: dissolved as an interstitial solute in solid solution8, bonded to hydrogen-affinitive elements as thermodynamically stable hydrides (for example, titanium hydride)12,13 or trapped at crystal defects, including vacancies14,15, dislocations3,16 and grain boundaries3,17. Experimentally, these states can be analysed by APT2,4, which uses time-of-flight mass spectrometry to identify hydrogen with nanoscale spatial resolution. In practice, deuterium (2H or D) is often introduced instead of protium to distinguish genuine hydrogen signals from background hydrogen in the instrument, which is predominantly 1H (refs. 4,5).

Among the three states, hydrides and defect-trapped hydrogen have been investigated more extensively by APT. Hydrides are identifiable through their characteristic stoichiometries12,13 and defect-trapped hydrogen can often be co-localized with solute-decorated defects (for example, Cottrell atmospheres such as that of carbon)3,16. When defects are not decorated with identifiable elements, associating hydrogen with defect traps becomes challenging because such defects are ‘invisible’ in APT, unless correlative techniques such as transmission electron microscopy (TEM) are used16,18.

By contrast, characterizing hydrogen in solid solutions is particularly challenging for several reasons. First, the solubility of hydrogen is generally low in many alloys; for example, body-centred cubic (BCC) iron typically contains only a few tens of atomic parts per million (at. ppm) of hydrogen2,7,8, which approaches the APT detection limit6,19. Second, unlike hydrogen in stable hydrides, interstitial hydrogen is highly mobile and can diffuse to the surface and desorb before analysis20,21; cryogenic sample transfer (cryo-transfer) for APT mitigates, but does not eliminate, such signal loss4,20,22.

A further complication arises from the methods used to introduce hydrogen into the needle-shaped APT specimens. Electrochemical charging is commonly used2,4, but it can impose extremely high hydrogen fugacity on the specimen and generate large numbers of vacancies—so-called ‘superabundant vacancies’14,23. Further artefacts can be introduced during specimen preparation: electron-beam or ion-beam irradiation in scanning electron microscopy (SEM) or focused ion beam (FIB) can also create vacancies that subsequently trap hydrogen15. Careful interpretation of APT hydrogen maps is therefore required whenever SEM-FIB processing has been used.

In this study, we use a series of FCC alloys as model systems to address this challenge. Compared with BCC alloys, FCC lattices offer higher hydrogen solubility and lower diffusivity7,24, improving signal-to-noise ratio and reducing diffusion-related losses. Our results provide experimental evidence for interstitial hydrogen in FCC metallic solid solutions and establish a methodological basis for robust, artefact-aware APT characterization of hydrogen in materials.

Throughout this study, APT tip specimens were electrolytically charged with deuterium, which saturates specimens more efficiently than gaseous charging, while reducing corrosion concerns3,4. Voltage pulsing rather than laser pulsing was used for all APT analyses to minimize ambiguity in deuterium signal identification against instrumental background hydrogen4,6. Full experimental details are provided in Methods.

As shown in Fig. 1, we first present results from an equiatomic CoCrNiFeMn MPEA, first developed by Cantor et al.25. This alloy is known to have a locally distorted lattice, which can more effectively hinder hydrogen diffusion and thus reduce signal loss26. Immediately after D charging, we spent approximately 3 s to remove the D-charging electrolyte and then quenched the charged tips in liquid nitrogen (LN2) to ‘freeze’ the deuterium in the MPEA. Once LN2 evaporation stopped—indicating thermal equilibration between the tips and LN2—the specimens were transferred into the APT system using the cryo-transfer procedure described previously3. As shown in Fig. 1a, the APT mass spectrum of the D-charged CoCrNiFeMn specimen exhibits a clear D peak in red at mass-to-charge ratio of 2 Dalton (Da), distinct from the peak of ambient hydrogen (predominantly protium, 1H) at 1 Da, together with the peaks from the principal alloying elements. The overall D concentration reaches 1.3 atomic percent (at.%), equivalent to 473 parts per million by weight (wt. ppm). Figure 1b presents the three-dimensional reconstruction of the APT dataset and 30-nm-thick two-dimensional slices showing the spatial distributions of the major elements, together with H in light blue and D in red. The H map shows a hydrogen-rich cap at the specimen apex, which arises from the field-induced migration and evaporation of ambient hydrogen adsorbed on the tip surface during APT analysis4. By contrast, the D map shows a relatively homogeneous and random distribution, consistent with what is expected for interstitial hydrogen in solid solution. To confirm reproducibility, we repeated this experiment on a second tip and obtained a comparable result, as shown in Extended Data Fig. 1.

Because deuterium can segregate to defects that are not visible in APT (ref. 16), we carefully evaluated whether the observed deuterium distribution is truly random. We performed nearest-neighbour analyses of deuterium atoms, as shown in Fig. 1c,d, for the first and fifth nearest deuterium neighbours, respectively. This approach detects clustering by comparing interatomic distances between deuterium atoms in the experimental dataset with those in a randomized dataset of equivalent size19,27. A marked shift towards shorter distances indicates clustering, whereas agreement with the random distribution is consistent with deuterium occupying a large population of equivalent interstitial sites27.

Figure 1c shows the distribution of deuterium–deuterium pair distances for the first nearest neighbour. The most probable pair distance in the experimental data is close to that in the randomized dataset (both approximately 0.8 nm), indicating that the deuterium atoms are randomly distributed. We repeated the analysis for the fifth nearest deuterium neighbour (Fig. 1d) and obtained a consistent result, with a larger most probable distance of about 1.2 nm, as expected when a more distant neighbour is considered. These results are consistent with interstitial deuterium (as a proxy of hydrogen) in solid solution. Notably, hydrogen solution in the CoCrNiFeMn matrix is not highly thermodynamically favourable according to our first-principles calculations, for neither octahedral nor tetrahedral interstitial sites (Extended Data Fig. 2a). Figure 1 therefore suggests the effectiveness of cryo-APT for preserving the charged D atoms and enabling observation of their distribution in solid solution. Random D distributions were also consistently observed in solution-treated CoNiV and (CoCrNi)94Al3Ti3 alloys (Extended Data Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 1), supporting the generality of this observation among FCC MPEAs.

As a similar randomness of D distribution could also arise if a large number of superabundant vacancies was introduced during electrolytic charging and act as traps14,28, we used a dual-phase FCC (CoCrNi)94Al3Ti3 MPEA containing L12 Ni3(Al, Ti) intermetallic precipitates with highly coherent interfaces with the matrix, which provides a heterogeneous microstructure less favoured to form superabundant vacancies. The specimens were first solution-treated at 1,000 °C for 1 h to obtain a precipitate-free state and then aged at 800 °C for 5 h to induce precipitation. Structural and compositional characterization of the aged (CoCrNi)94Al3Ti3 FCC MPEA is shown in Fig. 2. The APT mass spectrum of a deuterium-free specimen in Fig. 2i shows peaks from the major elements and a H peak at 1 Da, but no D peak at 2 Da. Figure 2j shows the three-dimensional APT reconstruction and 10-nm slices of the main elements, clearly revealing precipitates enriched in Ni, Al and Ti. Figure 2k presents a proximity histogram (proxigram) across an isoconcentration surface (isosurface) defined at 60 at.% Ni, showing the agreement between the theoretical stoichiometry and the composition of the L12 precipitates measured by APT. APT reconstructions from a precipitate-free (CoCrNi)94Al3Ti3 specimen are provided in Supplementary Fig. 3, which show homogeneous elemental distributions and a mass spectrum similar to that in Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 3, respectively, confirming the precipitation owing to heat treatment in good agreement with literature29.

Such high-strength, ordered FCC L12 Ni3(Al, Ti) precipitates embedded in a high-entropy CoCrNi matrix can be more resistant to damage arising from high-fugacity electrolytic charging30,31. Also, this dual-phase material provides an opportunity to study interstitial hydrogen behaviour in two different FCC lattices. Because L12 Ni3(Al, Ti) has a strong affinity for hydrogen12,13 (Extended Data Fig. 2b), we anticipated that such precipitates should bind hydrogen more strongly and therefore retain a higher deuterium concentration than the surrounding CoCrNi matrix.

To compliment APT analyses, we used thermal desorption spectroscopy (TDS) to compare hydrogen retention in the precipitate-free and precipitated (CoCrNi)94Al3Ti3 specimens on the macroscopic scale. As indicated by the arrow in Supplementary Fig. 4, the precipitated specimen shows an increased amount of hydrogen desorbed at higher temperatures, indicating the presence of microstructural features that hold hydrogen more strongly than the FCC matrix; these features are most likely the L12 Ni3(Al, Ti) precipitates. Although the experimental approaches are different, these TDS results are consistent with the trends predicted by our first-principles calculations using density functional theory (DFT) for evaluating the solution energies of hydrogen in all relevant interstitial sites (that is, both octahedral and tetrahedral), as shown in Extended Data Fig. 2b, considering in the CoCrNi matrix, at the L12–matrix interface and within the L12 Ni3(Al, Ti) lattice. Owing to the chemical disorder, the solution energy values for hydrogen in the MPEAs are broadly distributed compared with those in pure metals. We find that hydrogen exhibits the lowest solution energy (approximately −0.28 eV) at the specific octahedral sites of the L12 Ni3(Al, Ti) phase.

For the (CoCrNi)94Al3Ti3 specimen containing Ni3(Al, Ti) precipitates, we repeated most of the procedures used for the CoCrNiFeMn specimen. The only modification was an extended holding time for 10 s before LN2 quenching and cryo-transfer, with an intention to promote preferential deuterium desorption from the matrix, thereby enhancing the deuterium concentration contrast between phases with low (matrix) and high (precipitate) hydrogen-binding energies in the APT dataset. This controlled holding after deuterium saturation allows the charged deuterium atoms to remain mobile briefly before quenching, leading to more pronounced deuterium loss from the low-binding-energy phase than from the high-binding-energy phase. The measured deuterium distribution can reflect both thermodynamic and kinetic effects during this brief redistribution, as demonstrated in our previous work16.

The APT results are shown in Fig. 3. The mass spectrum in Fig. 3a shows a clear D peak in red at 2 Da, together with the peaks of the major elements. Because of the controlled holding at room temperature, the D peak is less intense than that observed for the CoCrNiFeMn specimen in Fig. 1a. Figure 3b shows the three-dimensional reconstruction with the principal elements and D atoms (red), with green outlines added to enhance the visibility of the L12 precipitate. A distinctly higher D concentration is observed inside the L12 Ni3(Al, Ti) precipitate. In Fig. 3c, we plot the one-dimensional concentration profiles of D, H and the major alloying elements along a cylindrical region of interest indicated in the Al map in Fig. 3b. The D profile (red) is clearly different from the H background (blue), indicating that the D signal is not associated with the artefact related to background hydrogen. Figure 3d shows another view of the reconstruction, together with the D atoms, the Ti isosurface and the positions of the 10-nm two-dimensional slices used to generate Fig. 3e–g. We repeated the experiment on another specimen and the corresponding results (Extended Data Fig. 4) show the same D enrichment in the L12 precipitates, confirming reproducibility. Taken together, these observations show that the Ni3(Al, Ti) L12 precipitates contain a higher hydrogen (represented by deuterium) concentration than the surrounding CoCrNi FCC matrix. We interpret deuterium enrichment in the L12 FCC precipitates as being predominantly associated with interstitial hydrogen in the FCC lattice, for the reasons discussed below.

First, our previous work has shown that APT can resolve D concentrated within a second phase while showing little or no D at an adjacent interface with a low elastic strain field18. It demonstrated that APT detected D in cementite rather than at the pearlite interface, consistent with DFT predictions for the relative hydrogen affinities of cementite, ferrite and their interface, ruling out artefacts related to ion-trajectory effects in APT (ref. 19). The present study has a related microstructural configuration—a hydrogen-affinitive FCC L12 second phase, an FCC matrix and a coherent interface with weak local elastic strain—so a similar enrichment of D in the L12 phase, rather than at the interface, is expected.

Second, to validate the low populations of vacancy traps in our specimens, we performed positron annihilation measurements on both the precipitate-free and precipitated (CoCrNi)94Al3Ti3 specimens, their 2%-strained counterparts (which should contain more vacancies) and pure nickel reference specimens. The results, summarized in Supplementary Table 1, show that, although the nickel references give measurable positron signals (rows 1 and 2), all (CoCrNi)94Al3Ti3 specimens (rows 3–6) are below the detection limit of the instrument. This indicates that the vacancy concentrations in these alloys are indeed low. Our DFT calculations also show that the vacancy formation energies in CoCrNi and L12 Ni3(Al, Ti) are greater than 1.5 eV (Supplementary Fig. 5), indicating that the formation of a substantial equilibrium vacancy population is unlikely.

We further examined whether the electrochemical D-charging process could introduce crystalline defects that might act as trapping sites for deuterium, as suggested in previous studies31,32,33,34. Quasi in situ TEM observations were performed on the same L12 precipitates before and after D charging, and no stacking faults, dislocations or other newly introduced crystalline defects were detected (Extended Data Fig. 5). Also, as shown in Extended Data Figs. 6 and 7, positron annihilation spectroscopy, which is commonly used to characterize vacancy defects and hydrogen–vacancy complexes35,36, showed no notable increase in vacancy concentration after D charging. This contrasts with the irradiated specimen, which contains more defects (primarily vacancies) and thus exhibited higher positron annihilation signals.

The combination of DFT calculations, quasi in situ TEM observations during D charging and positron annihilation measurements suggests that the vacancy concentrations are low, so detected D is predominantly associated with interstitial lattice sites in the L12 phase, although a minor contribution from non-detectable vacancies or vacancy–Hn/Dn complexes, or even elastic fields or the intrinsic APT detection limitations, cannot be completely excluded14,28,37,38.

With the establishment that APT can resolve hydrogen in different phases, we extended our study to a more complex alloy system containing several types of precipitate, which is an Inconel 718 nickel-based superalloy (54Ni–19Fe–21Cr–2.6Nb–1.6Mo–0.97Ti–0.58Al–0.24C at.%), by 3D printing and tailored it to contain two types of coherent precipitate—Ni3Nb body-centred tetragonal (BCT) D022 and Ni3(Al, Ti) L12 FCC—embedded in an FCC matrix. As shown in Extended Data Fig. 8, we conducted TEM analyses to confirm the presence of a high number density of precipitates in the Ni matrix, Ni–Al–Ti enrichment in the L12 phase, Ni–Nb enrichment in the D022 phase and the high coherency of L12–D022 and matrix–D022 interfaces. This co-precipitation strategy enabled both phases of interest to appear within the small APT field of view, allowing a comparison under identical experimental conditions.

We performed identical treatment for the (CoCrNi)94Al3Ti3 specimen. The results are presented in Fig. 4. As a result, D atoms were found predominantly within the L12 precipitates, as confirmed by the one-dimensional concentration profile in Fig. 4e, which was obtained from the cylindrical region of interest indicated in Fig. 4c. To quantify this further, we acquired proxigrams using either the turquoise Al isosurfaces (representing L12) or the green Nb isosurfaces (representing D022), as shown in Fig. 4j,k, respectively. These profiles clearly show an increased D concentration in the L12 precipitates (Fig. 4j) and only a low D concentration in the D022 precipitates (Fig. 4k). We repeated the experiment on another Inconel APT tip and the data in Extended Data Fig. 9 and Supplementary Fig. 7 show the same trend, confirming reproducibility. We also conducted DFT analysis to determine the hydrogen solution energies in a pure Ni reference (Extended Data Fig. 2c), the Ni2FeCr Inconel FCC matrix (Extended Data Fig. 2d) and the Ni3Nb D022 BCT precipitate (Extended Data Fig. 2e), finding that none of the sites in D022 can hold hydrogen better than those in the L12 precipitates, which is consistent with the APT results in Figs. 3 and 4.

In summary, we investigated interstitial hydrogen/deuterium in solid solutions of FCC MPEAs, in both bulk matrix and precipitate forms, by APT, with results consistent with DFT predictions of hydrogen solution energies. The deuterium partitioning reflects interstitial occupancy, although minor contributions from vacancies, vacancy–hydrogen/deuterium complexes or elastic fields cannot be fully excluded. Chemical affinity and crystal structure are shown to govern local hydrogen distribution. These findings establish an experimental protocol for connecting atomistic predictions with direct observation, enabling new opportunities for understanding hydrogen thermodynamics and kinetics in materials and providing guidance for designing alloys with improved performance for hydrogen-related applications.

The ingot of CoCrNiFeMn high-entropy alloy was prepared by vacuum arc melting a mixture of pure elements with purity greater than 99.95 wt.% under an argon atmosphere after a vacuum of 3.3 Pa has been reached. The ingot was melted at least four times to ensure compositional homogeneity, followed by homogenization at 1,200 °C for 24 h, cold rolling to a 90% thickness reduction (from 10 mm to 1 mm) and annealing at 950 °C for 4 h.

The sample with a nominal chemical composition of (CoCrNi)94Al3Ti3 (at.%) was synthesized through vacuum arc melting high-purity metals (purity ≥ 99.9 wt.%) under an argon atmosphere. To ensure compositional homogeneity and prevent element segregation, the sample was melted five times before being poured into a round ingot with a diameter of 50 mm. The as-cast sample was homogenized at 1,200 °C for 3 h and then cold-rolled along with its thickness to reduce the thickness by 50%. Two different microstructures were achieved by applying distinct heat treatments to the as-rolled samples: heating at 1,000 °C for 1 h, yielding the precipitate-free state (solution-treated), followed by 800 °C for 5 h, yielding the precipitated sample. All heat-treated samples were water-quenched.

The ingot of CoNiV alloy was synthesized by vacuum arc melting under an argon atmosphere (99.99% purity) after a pressure of 3.3 Pa. To ensure compositional homogeneity, the ingots were remelted five times. The ingots were then hot-forged at 1,200 °C into rectangular slabs with a thickness of 5 mm and a width of 20 mm, followed by homogenization at 1,200 °C for 3 h. Subsequently, the homogenized alloys were cold-rolled to a total thickness reduction of 50%. Finally, the cold-rolled sheets were subjected to solution annealing at 1,000 °C for 1 h (solution-treated).

The Inconel 718 nickel-based superalloy had a chemical composition of 54.72Ni–18.60Fe–18.70Cr–4.24Nb–2.62Mo–0.80Ti–0.27Al–0.05C wt.% (53.93Ni–19.26Fe–20.80Cr–2.64Nb–1.58Mo–0.97Ti–0.58Al–0.24C at.%). This alloy (diameter 15 mm, length 70 mm) was fabricated using a commercial selective laser melting system (EOS M 400) with an average powder size of 30 ± 11 µm, a scanning strategy of 67° rotation, a laser power of 340 W, scanning speed of 1,100 mm s−1 and layer thickness of approximately 50 µm. The printed samples were initially homogenized at 1,100 °C for 1 h and then water-quenched to room temperature. Subsequently, the samples were subjected to a two-step ageing treatment: first at 720 °C for 1 h, followed by furnace cooling to 620 °C, at which they were held for 8 h before final air cooling to room temperature. The sequential D022 (γ″-type) and L12 (γ′-type) precipitates are introduced during the 720 °C and 620 °C ageing treatments, respectively.

Samples for the EBSD analysis were initially polished using 5000-grit SiC sandpaper, subsequently polished with diamond paste for 3 min and finally electrolytically polished in a 1:9 solution of HClO4 and C2H5OH at 243 K for 20 s under a DC voltage of 25 V. The pre-thinned TEM foils, with a thickness of 50–60 μm, were polished using double-jet electropolishing at 243 K in 10 volume percent of HClO4 in methanol to prepare the TEM sample.

EBSD mapping used a Symmetry S2 detector (Oxford Instruments) and TEM imaging was conducted on a JEM-2100F (JEOL). High-resolution STEM imaging was performed using the FEI Themis Z microscope equipped with an EDS detector. Atomic-resolution chemical characterization with compositional mapping was performed using four in-column Super-X silicon drift detectors.

The sample preparation began with matchstick bars with dimensions 1 × 1 × 13 mm3. The samples were initially polished using 800-grit sandpaper. Coarse electropolishing was then performed using a 25% perchloric acid solution in glacial acetic acid at voltages ranging from 15 to 30 V to polish the samples into needle-like specimens. Fine electropolishing was subsequently performed using a 2% perchloric acid solution in ethylene glycol butyl ether at voltages ranging from 10 to 25 V. This fine electropolishing procedure used a platinum wire ring cathode and was monitored under a 40× optical microscope. A key step involved forming a neck until the top portion detached, creating a sharp tip.

Before deuterium charging, FIB tip sharpening was performed on the electropolished tips to ensure that the samples satisfied APT requirements in tip sharpness. APT was then performed using a LEAP 4000X instrument (CAMECA), operating in voltage-pulsing mode with a 0.2% detection rate, 200-kHz pulse frequency, 20% pulse fraction and sample temperature of 50 K. Data processing was performed using AP Suite software (version 6.1, CAMECA). The image compression factors and k factors used for reconstruction were 1.3 and 3.3 for the CoCrNiFeMn alloy and the Inconel 718 nickel-based superalloy and 1.5 and 3.3 for the (CoCrNi)94Al3Ti3 alloy, respectively. These reconstruction parameters were selected following standard APT reconstruction procedures and, for the precipitate-containing datasets, refined by comparison with precipitate morphologies independently observed by TEM. Peak ranging was performed by considering the full isotopic distributions of the major alloying elements and possible molecular ions; the bulk composition analysis is shown in Supplementary Tables 2–4. A peak-free region around 2.5 Da was defined as noise and used to estimate the local low-mass background adjacent to the 2-Da signal. Three representative mass spectra are shown in Supplementary Fig. 8 and the results for all eight successful cryo-APT datasets out of 68 trials are summarized in Supplementary Table 5. Because D+ and H2+ overlap at 2 Da and cannot be resolved directly, this overlap was treated separately as a systematic uncertainty and assessed using uncharged reference specimens analysed under identical voltage-pulsing conditions. APT reconstruction sensitivity was further evaluated by reconstructing the dataset shown in Fig. 3 using an alternative image compression factor value of 1.3, which did not alter the observed preferential deuterium enrichment within the L12 precipitate (Supplementary Fig. 9).

A charging solution of 0.1 M NaOH in D2O (Sigma-Aldrich) was used to create a deuterium-rich environment around the samples at the cathode in an electrolytic circuit, with a gold counter-electrode. This was carried out in a custom-designed cryo-transfer glovebox (M. BRAUN Inertgas-Systeme). Four sharp specimens were pre-mounted in a four-needle atom probe sample puck with polyether ether ketone insulation. The four tips protruded from the puck to the same height and were immersed into the charging solution at a depth that was barely visible and could only be identified by means of the capillary of the liquid solution. Deuterium charging was conducted at 1.6 V for 90 s powered by Keysight 2901A, followed by immediately immersing the whole puck in a LN2 bath.

After deuterium charging and LN2 quenching, the specimens for Figs. 3 and 4 were transferred from the glovebox to a Hydra plasma focused ion beam (PFIB; Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a cryo-stage using a cryogenic sample carrier (Ferrovac). The sharpening process was completed on a cryo-stage at approximately −190 °C. Two ion-beam conditions were used—30 kV–0.1 nA and 30 kV–30 pA—to remove ice and further refine and smooth the tips during the annular milling process. A final polishing step at 5 kV–50 pA was applied for 10 s to remove the possible ion-damaged layer. The APT specimens were then transferred using the cryogenic transfer suitcase from the PFIB system to the APT system for APT measurements.

For the TDS tests, samples measuring 1 × 8 × 10 mm3 were polished using 5000-grit SiC sandpaper. The samples were then charged for 72 h at a constant current density of 20 mA cm−2 in a solution containing 0.5 M H2SO4 and 2 g l−1 CH4N2S. TDS measurements were subsequently performed using a mass spectrometer (JTF-20A), with heating rates of 100 °C h−1, 200 °C h−1 and 300 °C h−1.

All DFT calculations were performed using the projector augmented wave39 method, as implemented in the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP)40. The exchange-correlation effects were selected as the generalized gradient approximation determined by the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof functional41. To simulate chemical disorder, the special quasirandom structure (SQS) approach42 was applied using the Alloy Theoretic Automated Toolkit (ATAT)43,44. A plane-wave cut-off of 450 eV was used and the Brillouin zones were sampled with a gamma-centred Monkhorst–Pack grid45. Next, the Methfessel–Paxton method46 was used with a smearing width of 0.1 eV. The supercell size and atomic positions were allowed to relax until the Hellmann–Feynman forces were less than 0.01 eV Å−1 and convergence criteria for energies and forces were set to 1.0 × 10−5 eV per atom and 0.02 eV Å−1, respectively.

CoCrNiFeMn MPEA, CoCrNi MPEA and L12 Ni3(Al, Ti) precipitates, pure Ni, Ni2FeCr (matrix of Inconel 718 superalloy) and D022 Ni3Nb precipitates were studied in this work. The CoCrNiFeMn, CoCrNi, L12 Ni3(Al, Ti), Ni and Ni2FeCr are all FCC structures and D022 Ni3Nb has a tetragonal structure (I4/mmm, space group 139), the Ni atoms occupy the 4d (0, 1/2, 1/4) and 2b (0, 0, 1/2) positions and the Nb atoms occupy the 2a (0, 0, 0) positions. The SQS method was used to model the disordered phases including CoCrNiFeMn, CoCrNi, L12 Ni3(Al, Ti) and Ni2FeCr. The CoCrNi, Ni2FeCr, L12 Ni3(Al, Ti) and Ni phases were modelled using 108 atoms with 3 × 3 × 3 supercells according to their compositions, with all of the Ni atoms occupying the face-centred sites in L12 Ni3(Al, Ti). CoCrNiFeMn and D022 Ni3Nb were constructed by a 5 × 3 × 2 supercell containing 120 atoms and a 2 × 2 × 1 supercell containing 36 atoms, respectively. The CoCrNi (\(1\bar{1}\bar{1}\))/L12 Ni3(Al, Ti) (\(1\bar{1}\bar{1}\)) interface was constructed on the basis of experimental data and more than three SQS configurations for each bulk and interface structure were evaluated for comparison.

Electropolished TEM foils of the aged (CoCrNi)94Al3Ti3 alloy were used for quasi in situ D-charging experiments. Before charging, specific regions containing L12 precipitates were identified and characterized using bright-field TEM, high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM), selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) and HRTEM. The specimen was then electrochemically charged in 0.1 M NaOH prepared in D2O using a gold anode. A charging time of 1.5 min and the charging conditions used are the same as cryogenic APT experiments.

After charging, the specimen was removed from the electrolyte, dried and re-examined by TEM. The same regions and individual precipitates were relocated and compared with their former states. SAED and HRTEM analyses were performed after charging to assess whether D-charging introduced crystalline defects, including stacking faults, dislocations or other lattice defects.

Ion irradiation was performed using a NEC 2 × 1.7 MV tandem accelerator at Peking University. The (CoCrNi)94Al3Ti3 specimen (10 × 10 × 0.9 mm3) was irradiated with 3 MeV Ni2+ ions to a fluence of 1.5 × 1016 ions cm−2 at 500 °C, corresponding to a peak damage level of approximately 16 dpa, as calculated by SRIM-2013 using the quick Kinchin–Pease model. The displacement threshold energies used in the SRIM calculations were 40 eV for Co, Cr, Ni and Ti and 25 eV for Al. To minimize oxidation and other possible surface contamination during high-temperature irradiation, the target chamber was evacuated to a pressure below 1 × 10−4 Pa before heating and irradiation were initiated. After irradiation, the specimen was kept under vacuum during cooling and removed from the chamber only after the temperature had decreased to less than 50 °C.

Positron annihilation lifetime spectroscopy was performed using a 22Na positron source to complement the Doppler-broadening measurements and assess vacancy-type defects in the investigated alloys, corresponding to Supplementary Table 1. Vacancy-type defects introduced by hydrogen charging, performed under the same conditions as those used for the cryogenic APT experiments, and by ion irradiation were characterized by Doppler-broadening positron annihilation spectroscopy using a slow positron beam51,52 at the Institute of High Energy Physics, Chinese Academy of Sciences. A 22Na radioactive source was used to produce the positron beam, with a beam diameter of approximately 0.55 cm. To minimize specimen-to-specimen variability, Doppler-broadening positron annihilation spectroscopy measurements were performed sequentially on the same specimen in the pristine state, after electrochemical hydrogen charging and after subsequent Ni-ion irradiation. The incident positron energy varied from 0.5 to 25.0 keV. An increase in S accompanied by a decrease in W indicates enhanced positron trapping at vacancy-type open-volume defects.
