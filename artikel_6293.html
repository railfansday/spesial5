A metal detectorist found a Viking sword and rare oval brooches on the Isle of Man, and his father had helped find the island’s only other pair

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

Daniel Crowe found a Viking sword, oval brooches and trading weights over 1,100 years old on the Isle of Man. His father helped find the island's only other pair.

A metal detectorist on the Isle of Man has found a Viking sword, a set of bronze brooches and trading weights and scales, all more than 1,100 years old. The objects are believed to come from a double Viking burial.

The finder, Daniel Crowe, is not the first in his family to make a discovery like this. In December 2018, his father, John Crowe, and fellow detectorist Craig Evans found the only other pair of Viking oval brooches ever discovered on the island.

The new collection was declared Treasure by the Isle of Man Coroner for Inquests, and Manx National Heritage announced it on November 9. Part of it went on display at the Manx Museum in Douglas two days later.

The finds date to around AD 900. Besides the sword, the haul includes three bronze brooches, decorated lead weights, fragments of bronze balance scales, a bronze ladle and an axehead.

Fellow detectorist Rhett Britton helped with the search. He found the axehead, part of the scales and other iron objects.

The sword is the thirteenth Viking sword found on the island. About two-thirds of it survives. X-rays revealed fine silver inlay and twisted silver wire on parts of its hilt, and only two other Manx Viking swords carry comparable decoration.

It is broken, probably on purpose as part of the burial ritual. Some more damage happened later, while it lay in the ground.

Two of the brooches are oval and decorated with interlocking animals, a distinctive feature of Viking art. Brooches like these were usually worn by women.

The pair found by John Crowe and Craig Evans in 2018 dates to around AD 900 to 950. The new pair could be about 50 years older, which would make it potential evidence of the earliest known female Viking Age burial on the Isle of Man.

Oval brooches were particularly common in the Viking homelands and are seen almost as national dress.

In 2020, Manx National Heritage said their absence from the island until the 2018 find had led to theories that only men from Scandinavia settled there in the early Viking Age.

The third brooch was originally gilded. It has spiral decoration and a glass center, and it is the first of its kind found on the island.

Jewelry typically associated with women, found alongside a sword traditionally associated with men, raises a possibility. The objects may come from a double burial, or from separate graves of the same period.

The site was disturbed at some point over the last thousand years, so the exact circumstances are still uncertain.

At the site of the 2018 brooches, archaeologists from York Archaeological Trust carried out a small excavation but found no grave.

The decorated lead weights and bronze scales would have been used to weigh precious metals and coins during trade. The weights are the first of their type found on the Isle of Man.

Silver and coins also turn up in hoards across the Viking world, like a buried 10th-century hoard that revealed vast Viking trade networks.

One weight carries a bronze fitting, possibly taken from a small chest or a religious object. Its art style is typical of Britain and Ireland at the time, which suggests the settlers reused objects from other cultures.

The ladle, perhaps used at feasts or ceremonies, is another type of object never recorded before on the island.

“This is an extraordinary discovery and one of the most significant groups of Viking Age objects found on the Isle of Man,” said Allison Fox, Curator for Archaeology at Manx National Heritage.

Crowe chose not to clean the objects and handed them over in the state they came out of the ground. That kept traces of evidence that could otherwise have been lost.

His father and Evans did the same in 2018. In 2020, Fox said this mattered because the brooches were fragile and small fragments of textiles from the clothes the woman was buried in might still survive.

This time, before conservation began, the radiology team at Manx Care scanned the objects. The scans looked beneath layers of compacted soil and guided the conservator in uncovering and preserving the objects.

Detectorists elsewhere have made similar finds, such as the hiker with a metal detector who found a Viking treasure hoard and the detectorist who found Finland’s largest Viking Age silver hoard.

Manx National Heritage has not said exactly where the objects were found, only that the landowner cooperated. It also has not given a date for the discovery.

Conservation and research are still underway, and they may reveal more about who owned these objects. Several of them are the first of their kind on the island, so they offer a rare look at early Viking settlement, burial and daily life there.

Visitors can see a selection of the finds in the Viking Gallery of the Manx Museum.

The information was obtained from Manx National Heritage and its 2020 announcement on the earlier brooches.
