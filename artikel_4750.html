Ice Dives Are the Simple Therapy Trick that Rewired My Anxious Brain

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

A few weeks ago, I was more anxious than I had been in a long time—or maybe ever. My thoughts were racing, and even for someone who deals with her share of anxiety, I was way beyond my normal self. So, I did what any person would do in that moment: I called my sister.

While calling your sister is always a good idea, mine also happens to be a clinical fellow at McLean Hospital, a.k.a. one of the leading psychiatric institutions in the whole country, so let’s just say she knows how to handle an anxious person. Her suggestion was to go to the kitchen, grab a big bowl, fill it with ice water, and then dunk my face in it. I wasn’t exactly thrilled by the idea. It seemed like a whole bunch of work for something that couldn’t possibly help me in such a state.

“I’m not hanging up until you do it,” she said. She could tell I thought the idea was silly, and that I was probably going to skip it. And, I did think it was silly, but she seemed weirdly passionate that I submerge my face into a bowl of freezing water, so I complied. I grabbed a bowl, filled it with water and ice cubes, and reluctantly got my face wet, just barely making sure my nose and eyes were underwater.

I felt better basically instantly. The racing thoughts subsided and I could finally see straight. Even more impressive is the fact that once I reentered the situation that was making me anxious, all that nervous energy simply didn’t return. I was reset and relaxed. All it took was a bucket of water and about 30 seconds.

The trick—or, skill, I should say—that my sister taught me comes from dialectical behavioral therapy (DBT), a modality that was created by Marsha Linehan, PhD, who recently passed away at 83 years old. It was a breakthrough treatment for people facing extreme mental health challenges like chronic suicidality and borderline personality disorder, which Linehan revealed she struggled with in 2011, decades after DBT became the gold standard for treating those conditions.

Whether or not you are someone who deals with a formal mental health diagnosis or the occasional tough moment, DBT skills can make a big difference. The cold water trick my sister taught me is called an “ice dive,” and it’s one of four simple DBT tools that can help snap you out of a range of intense emotions quickly. Now, any time I feel myself getting heightened, my immediate next thought is: I should go grab a bucket of ice water. Call me converted.

Here’s how you can practice this skill too, plus, the three others that work just as well—and don’t involve water.

Meet the experts: Shireen Rizvi, PhD, is a clinical psychologist and director of psychology training and DBT services and research at Montefiore Einstein in New York City. Vibh Forsythe Cox, PhD, is a clinical associate professor at the University of Washington and the director of the Marsha M. Linehan DBT clinic.

At this point, you’ve pretty much gotten the entire gist of an ice dive, but let’s recap: All it takes is a bowl big enough to get your full face in and some chilly water. (The colder the better, but it doesn’t need to reach ice bath territory.) Once you’ve got that, place it on a low-set table or even on the ground—you want to be bending over when you dunk your head in to mimic something closer to a dive—take a deep breath, and submerge your face, especially around your nasal passages and eyes, for as long as possible. 30 seconds is ideal, but that may feel like a really long time at first. Just go for as long as you can, and feel free to repeat multiple times for a cumulative total of 30 seconds.

As for what is going on when you do this: Dunking your face in cold water triggers what is called the mammalian dive reflex, says Shireen Rizvi, PhD, a clinical psychologist and director of psychology training and DBT services and research at Montefiore Einstein in New York City. “It’s automatic,” she says. “You’re not doing anything consciously to create the reaction, which is there to help mammals survive cold water.”

An ice dive triggers a survival mode where our bodies reduce our heart rate, slow down our breathing, and redirect blood to our vital organs, she explains. Under water, it helps you stay alive, but on land, it helps calm just about any activating emotion you might be experiencing. And, your face being what’s submerged is critical to get this specific reaction; getting in an ice bath where your face isn’t underwater actually spikes your heart rate due to cold shock, for example.

Basically, an ice dive forces your body to focus on the bare necessities, like staying calm. It’s best used anytime your emotions are reaching a 90-plus on a scale of 1 to 100, Rizvi says, whether the emotion is anxiety, anger, stress, jealousy, or anything else. If you’re feeling it intensely and your heart rate is up, an ice dive can help.

There’s one other handy use case too: If you’re ruminating. “When your body is in a survival mode, it's pulling resources back, and it actually also pulls your attention too, so if you have a lot of thoughts floating around in your head making you anxious, this can also help clear your mind temporarily,” says Vibh Forsythe Cox, PhD, a clinical associate professor at the University of Washington and the director of the Marsha M. Linehan DBT clinic. Think of it this way: “You can't be thinking about a grocery list if you have just fallen into ice water,” she says.

And, while this trick has completely changed the game for me, I have some good news: There are three others that all have the same effect sans H2O.

An ice dive makes up the very first letter—T for temperature—in the TIPP acronym, which is a part of DBT’s distress tolerance pillar. The rest (intense exercise, paced breathing, and paired muscle relaxation) can all quickly calm and reset your nervous system, no liquid necessary.

All four skills have a similar basic mechanism. They take you from a heightened state (or a distressed one, to use DBT-speak) where your sympathetic nervous system is activated and bring you back into a relaxed one, where your parasympathetic nervous system is in the driver’s seat again. They’re not about talking or reflecting; they’re about helping you feel better in a moment when emotions are extreme.

“These are skills that act to regulate your physiology,” Cox says. “Oftentimes when you are having an intense emotion, you think you have to handle it with your mind. These are skills that help you handle it with your body.” Of course, once you’re out of that intense moment, you may want to call on other mental tools and process what happened—but that’s not where you’re at when you’re calling on a TIPP skill. They’re for when you’re in crisis mode.

You can use any of them at any time with one important caveat: All TIPP skills are meant to rapidly slow down your heart rate, so if you’re someone with a heart condition or who takes meds like beta blockers, talk to your doctor first before trying any of them, Rizvi says.

Without further ado, here’s what you need to know about the other three skills.

The I in TIPP stands for intense exercise. This may sound similarly prohibitive to needing a bowl of water, but it’s actually quite simple. All you really need to do is get moving for about 30 seconds. Do some all-out jumping jacks, pump your arms up and down as fast as you can while sitting in your chair, or get into a plank. Just get your heart rate up and your muscles feel fatigued. “It feels paradoxical, but when you end that short burst of exercise, everything slows down,” Rizvi says.

There’s also an added benefit to going with exercise. When you’re feeling a heightened emotion, one of the ways your sympathetic nervous system reacts is by sending out glucose into your bloodstream, Cox says. Again, this is an automatic, physical reaction, and it’s what gives you the energy for either a fight or flight response. But since you're probably not feeling anxious or angry because you need to escape a pack of hungry wolves, you probably won’t end up literally running away or fighting them off and using that energy. Exercising eats up some of that extra glucose, helping you get back to baseline.

If you’ve ever done box breathing, you’ve done the first P in TIPP. All it involves is breathing in and out in a controlled manner for a set number of seconds each time. “The main core of this technique is that your out breath needs to be longer than your in breath,” says Cox. “That is what activates your parasympathetic nervous system and slows everything down.”

In addition to keeping your out breath longer than your in breath (say four seconds in and eight out), you also want to make sure you’re breathing from your diaphragm, Rizvi says. You can monitor this by placing your hand on your belly. If it’s rising and falling with each breath, you’re breathing from your diaphragm.

If you’re looking for an entry-level DBT skill, or one that you can do in pretty much any context, this is a good one to master. “I find paced breathing in particular is something that you can usually do no matter where you are or what you're doing,” Rizvi says. It takes no setup, no resources, and you can even do it in plain sight without people noticing.

Finally, the last TIPP skill is paired muscle relaxation. “When we are having an intense emotion, our body tenses up,” Cox says. “This helps release some of that tension.”

To do this skill, start at one end of your body and systematically work your way up or down, tensing and releasing your muscles throughout and saying the word “relax” each time. Pairing the muscle tensing with the word relax also helps train them—and therefore you—to relax on command, Cox says.

There are plenty of guided paired muscle relaxation routines you can follow online, but you can also just do it on your own, tensing as many muscle groups as you’d like (or have time for).

No matter which one you go for, remember this: “Emotions fire in the order of milliseconds,” Cox says. That means that no matter how intense the thing you’re feeling is, it will pass, and probably much faster than you think. Sometimes, all our brains need is a reset initiated by our bodies.
