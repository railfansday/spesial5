The 6 Best Electric Snowblowers Are Plenty Powerful—and Quieter Alternatives to Gas Models

Two more reasons to go green: Battery-powered outdoor equipment is also easier to operate and store.

If you’re looking for the quickest, least labor-intensive way to dig out after a big snowstorm this winter, a snowblower is it. While it’s only fall, it’s not too early to think about getting a snow blower if you found yourself wanting one during last year’s winter storms but struggled to find a retailer that wasn’t already out of stock. Getting ahead of the game is a good idea.

Electric models are more capable than ever, and in some cases meet or exceed their gas-powered brethren in some respects. Cordless, battery-electric blowers can clear a path up to roughly 2 feet wide through as much as 20 inches of snow. They start with the push of a button, require less maintenance than gas machines, and run relatively quietly. They’re also easier to store than their gas-powered counterparts.

Ahead, we review both two-stage snow blowers and single-stage snow throwers and recommend models from Toro, Ego, Milwaukee, and more.

Our selections for this list are based on our years of testing snow blowers of all varieties. Most of these examples we’ve tested in the last few years, and others are updated versions of models we’ve tested. We are also planning for a substantial update to this guide in the coming weeks with new models.

Tony Carrick, the original author of this article, is a regular PopMech contributor and writes about everything from landscaping to home improvement. He grew up in rural Pennsylvania, where he became well-acquainted with blizzards and the backbreaking labor of digging out after them. His recommendations are based on his personal experience testing snow blowers at home, along with plenty of research to inform the picks. He compared specs for a variety of corded and cordless models, while also plowing through recommendations and feedback from dozens of customer reviews.

Electric snow blowers may not be able to match the power of their gas-powered counterparts, but this self-propelled two-stage model from Ryobi comes close. It sports four rechargeable 40-volt 6-Ah batteries, which Ryobi claims can power the snow blower for up to 45 minutes, and move the same amount of snow as a 252-cc gas-powered two-stage snow blower. It has the power to scoop up a path that’s 24 inches wide and 21 inches deep, then chuck it up to 55 feet away.

We’re also impressed with the cockpit, which allows you to control the speed of the drive wheels and the auger, change the direction of the chute, and toggle the LED headlights without missing a beat. The handlebar even heats up so your hands don’t get numb as you're working.

Our only concern is with the charger. While we like the fact that this Ryobi supports rapid charging, the charger only has two slots, which means you’ll have to go through two rounds of recharging before you’re back to full power.

We like Ego’s battery-powered outdoor equipment because it’s among the best when it comes to power and run time—and this two-stage snow blower is no exception. With its two 56-volt, 7.5-Ah batteries and 24-inch-wide intake, the Power+ SNT2405 can clear an 8-inch layer of snow from an 18-car driveway on a single charge.

We also like the cockpit on this self-propelled machine. You can change speeds while in forward or reverse, adjust the auger speed, and change the chute direction all without having to leave the controls. It also comes equipped with four bright LED headlights, useful in the event your snow clearing efforts take you past sundown.

We also like the fact that Ego uses mostly steel construction with this machine, so you can expect it to hold up for many a winter.

Ego claims its 21-inch Power+ snow blower can throw snow up to 40 feet with a 180-degree, adjustable chute, which is pretty far for a cordless machine. In the wet sawdust test, we were able to throw 24 feet with the heavier, denser material—longer distances are certainly achievable with lighter, drier snow. It took the Power+ 4 minutes 16 seconds to clear the sawdust down to pavement in our area test.

Like most single-stage blowers, the Power+ is not self-propelled, so we needed to push it into the snow. In our dense, heavy sawdust, that took some effort to make forward progress. It’s not a bad thing, though, as it makes it easier to prevent the machine from digging in too deep and bogging down. In very wet or ice-packed snow, we expect you’ll have to put in a similar effort.

The Power+ has a variable-speed auger, so we were able to adjust speed for the conditions and conserve battery when less power was needed. There isn’t too much to worry about in the battery department, since it comes with two 5-Ah batteries, enough to clear 8 inches of snow on a 12-car driveway—that’s six cars long by two cars wide.

The Power+ comes with a dual-port charger, so the two batteries can be charged simultaneously to get you ready for the next round faster. Ego rounds things out with front-mounted LEDs for low-light and dark conditions, plus an easy-folding handle for stowing the unit when not in use.

If you don’t relish the idea of pushing heavy machinery through heavy snow, Snow Joe makes a lightweight single-stage snow blower that’s fairly easy to push around. At less than 38 pounds with batteries installed, the Snow Joe 24V-X2-SB18 is easy to roll out of storage, push down the driveway, and maneuver through turns.

The scraper bar clears snow right down to the pavement, and it can launch snow up to 20 feet away. On top of that, it’s very cheap—one of the most affordable cordless snow blowers we’ve found.

Unsurprisingly, that light weight and low price come with some compromises. The 18-inch-wide chute cuts a narrow path, so you’ll need to take a few extra passes to get the job done right compared to a wider machine. Meanwhile, its two 24-volt batteries offer a modest 40-minute run time.

Combine those two things, and the 24V-X2-SB18 is best reserved for smaller jobs like clearing decks, walkways, and compact driveways. If that’s what you need, though, it makes snow removal cheap and easy.

The 60-volt 26-inch Power Max e26 is one of the models we tested in November 2025, and it’s among the burliest two-stage cordless snow blowers we’ve used. It is built virtually the same as a gas model, except with an electric motor and two batteries. That includes the frame, drive system, auger, and auger housing.

Since it’s built the same, we can confirm it performs the same—so it could easily replace a similar gas model. No caveats here; the Power Max e26 isn’t a compromise in any significant way that we could determine. It comes with two batteries and a (claimed) runtime up to 70 minutes—enough to clear a driveway 30 car spaces long.

Operating the Power Max e26 was a pleasant experience, if you can call snow blowing pleasant. It has effective heated hand grips, and an axle with a differential that allows you to turn quickly, on a dime—no having to muscle it around.

The e26 easily handled our testing with wet sawdust, throwing it far enough to cross an average driveway—even when making a full 26-inch-wide pass. If you’ve questioned whether a cordless snow blower could ever be as good as a gas machine, the e26 answers that affirmatively.

Milwaukee’s M18 single-stage snow blower is the second machine we tested in November 2025, and it also impressed us. It's noticeably heavier than many other similar cordless models, which seems to be due to its metal frame. At 90 pounds, it isn’t heavy, at least in comparison to two-stage machines, but the weight is an advantage, helping prevent the blower from bouncing around or riding up when encountering denser, crusty snow.

We tested this snow blower with wet sawdust, which always presents a little bit of a challenge with single-stage snow blowers, as they have to lift the snow and eject it in one operation. Milwaukee’s M18 blower did this well enough in testing—and if it can throw wet sawdust, it will really throw the snow.

To facilitate ejecting snow, the M18’s chute is wider than that of most other models. This eliminates potential bottlenecks and ultimately clogs, where the snow passes from the blower body into the chute. Milwaukee's single auger is edged with a rubber paddle that both sweeps the pavement clean and pulls the machine forward. It does this well enough that, in some instances, we had to hold it back a little in testing.

The chute has a remote adjustment—you can crank a knob just below the handlebar to change the direction of the chute, allowing you to put snow where it needs to go. However, ejection elevation is manually adjusted at the top of the chute—but this isn't something you typically need to adjust as often.

Milwaukee claims the M18 blower can clear up to 11 parking places, or 500 feet of sidewalk on the two included 12.0-Ah batteries. But with two extra battery ports, you can double the runtime by adding another pair of batteries. We’ll be testing this more as we have snow available.

There are two types of snow blowers for homeowners, based on the number of “stages” in the mechanical process they use to collect and distribute snow.

Single-stage snow blowers have an auger that pulls the snow into a large intake at the front of the snow blower, and propels it out of a chute mounted to the top of the machine. Single-stage blowers, also frequently referred to as “snow throwers,” are small, lightweight and easy to maneuver, making them ideal for clearing light snowfall and smaller driveways and walks.

Dual-stage snow blowers, as the name suggests, have a two-stage process for collecting and expelling snow. Like single-stage models, they have an auger that pulls snow into the intake. The dual-stage snow blower follows up by pushing the snow into an impeller that ejects it out through the chute.

The one-two punch of auger and impeller allows a two-stage snow blower to remove more snow quicker, and throw it a longer distance. That makes them perfect for large driveways. That said, they are larger and heavier than single-stage machines, so they may not be able to maneuver around narrow walking paths as easily.

The width and depth of a snow blower’s intake determine how much snow it can move with each pass, and thus, how long it takes you to get the job done. Most single-stage electric snow blowers have a clearing width of around 20 inches and a clearing depth of around 8 inches. That’s good for carving out walks and medium-sized areas. Some heavy-duty two-stage electric snow blowers can remove a 24-inch-wide and 20-inch-deep swath of snow, which will make quick work of a large driveway.

Throwing distance refers to how far a snow blower can launch snow out of the chute. This gives you a sense of how to plan when and how you’ll dispose of the snow in your path. The average single-stage snow blower typically has a short throwing distance—around 25 feet. Unsurprisingly, a two-stage snow blower can send its snow as far as 50 feet.

Snow blowers typically have a throttle that allows you to change their mechanical speed, which will adjust how far it throws your snow. There are also controls that allow you to rotate the chute to change the direction of the flow of snow. If you can map out your snow-blowing route, check and make sure your snow blower throws the snow far enough to keep it out of your way until it melts.

Most electric snow blowers receive power from multiple large rechargeable batteries, which allow them to produce nearly as much power as a gas snow blower. No battery supplies uninterrupted power for as long as a tank of gas, though: On average, you should expect a battery-powered snow blower to run for about 30 to 45 minutes on a single charge. You can extend that time if you have multiple batteries, but the low run time still caps how much ground they can cover.

Though we largely consider them to be obsolete, some manufacturers also still produce corded electric snow blowers, which plug into a standard electrical outlet. They’re often cheaper than battery-powered models and run as long as you need them to. However, having a power cord substantially reduces their range: You will likely need to purchase an extension cord to extend its reach slightly beyond the length of the area you need to clear. No matter how much slack you have, though, the cable makes the whole process a little more awkward.

“Cordless snow blowers are easier, better, and safer to use,” says Popular Mechanics Test Editor Brad Ford. “Corded is old, out-of-date tech in most cases.”

They’re also limited to smaller, 15-amp motors that run on the power an outlet can provide. That usually translates to narrower clearing widths, shallower clearing depths, and shorter throw distances. Unless you have a very tight budget, we highly recommend going with a battery-powered electric snow blower rather than a corded one.

The most important thing you can do to keep your electric snow blower running season after season is to simply make sure that you store your charger and batteries indoors when you aren’t charging or using them. Lithium cells aren't able to hold the same amount of charge when the temperature drops below freezing. By storing and charging those batteries indoors, you’ll get the most run time out of them.

If you use a corded snow blower, you shouldn’t plug it into any old extension cord. These machines draw a lot of power, and not every cord can handle it. Using the wrong one can create a fire hazard or damage your snow blower. Make sure to check your snow blower’s specs before buying an extension cord to pair with it, so you can be sure it can handle its amperage.

Most corded electric snow blowers have a 12-amp rating, in which case you’ll want a 12-gauge extension cord that can provide at least 12 amps of power. You’ll also want to make sure it’s long enough for you to reach the far end of whatever walkway or road you plan to clear, preferably with some slack to spare for maneuverability. We recommend starting with 100 feet and scaling up based on your property.

Regardless, it would be wise to buy an extension cord to match the specs you need for your snow blower, rather than using one that you happen to have lying around.

With advancements in battery technology, there are now powerful two-stage cordless snow blowers capable of clearing deeper, heavier snow.

That said, the most powerful electric snow blowers still don’t match the snow-moving capabilities of the largest gas-powered snow blowers on the market. The most powerful electric snow blowers work best at maximum depths of about a foot, while some gas-powered models can clear snow up to 2 feet deep.
