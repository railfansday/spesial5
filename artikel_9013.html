From design work to watching movies, these are the best laptops for every kind of pasttime.

If you need to buy a laptop for creative work, it's so important to get it right. Prices have climbed sharply this year, so you don't want to be saddled with the wrong thing..

Our top pick for graphic design is the MacBook Pro 14-inch (M6). It’s a brilliant all-rounder for almost any creative job. However, depending on what you need it for, other laptops are worth investigating too.

So in this article, I've highlighted the best laptops for a range of different tasks, based on hands-on testing by our reviewers using real design software. For further options, see our guides to the best laptops for graphic design and the best laptops for CAD.

The MacBook Pro has been the default laptop for designers for years, and nothing has yet changed that. The M5 model came first by a clear margin in our Photoshop benchmarks. Its screen is sharp and colour-accurate, and the battery comfortably lasts a working day. Now Apple has replaced it with the M6 version, which went on sale on 6 November.

We haven’t tested it yet, but it’s a spec bump rather than a redesign. Apple claims up to 30% faster graphics, and it adds Wi-Fi 7 and Bluetooth 6. Crucially, it costs the same as the M5 model, which rose to $1,999 / £1,999 in Apple’s June price rises.

So my advice is: either buy the M6 at full price, or pick up the M5 if you find it heavily discounted over Black Friday. Alternatively if money’s no object, Apple’s new MacBook Pro with a PROLED XDR display starts at $2,899 / £2,899 and adds a touchscreen. Our writer Daniel John called its OLED panel “the best laptop screen I’ve ever seen”.

The ProArt P16 is the best Windows laptop we’ve tested for creative work, and it's our top pick in both our laptop for CAD and laptop for animation guides.

If you're using CAD, the fact that this laptop runs Windows really matters. Revit and SolidWorks don’t run natively on macOS, while the P16’s Nvidia graphics and 64GB of memory give you plenty of headroom for large models. The built-in dial is handy for zooming and rotating, too.

If you're an animator, meanwhile, the star is the screen. It’s a colour-accurate OLED touchscreen, and as our reviewer Erlingur Einarsson says: “The 120Hz refresh rate is a welcome upgrade too, especially for animators and video editors.” The RTX 5070 also speeds up rendering in GPU-heavy tools.

The ASUS ProArt P16, in short, is pretty much everything a creative professional could hope for However, if you need even more power, ASUS also sells a version with an RTX 5090 and a 4K screen for $3,999.99.

The 16-inch MacBook Pro is the top pick in our 3D modelling laptop guide. You get a big, sharp screen, plenty of memory and the kind of battery life that 3D-capable Windows laptops can only dream of.

When our reviewer Rob Redman tested the previous M4 Pro model in Blender, Cinema 4D and Maya, he was impressed by its rendering speed and smooth viewport. “Simple things like dragging and dropping materials, which can be a little laggy on some systems, are perfectly smooth,” he says. The M5 Pro builds on that with a faster chip and Apple’s latest GPU cores.

Two things to bear in mind, though. First, if your pipeline relies on Nvidia’s CUDA or Windows-only plugins, you’re better off with a Windows machine. In which case, the Acer Nitro 16S AI is our best-value pick, while the RTX 5090 inside the Alienware 16 Area-51 is ideal if you need maximum rendering power.

Second, the MacBook Pro 16-inch (M5 Pro) is a lot of money, and the price climbs quickly once you add memory. If you have a lot of money, though, there's a very nice PROLED XDR version with a touchscreen costing $3,399.

Looking for the best laptop for Cricut? Then here's some good news. Cricut Design Space isn’t a demanding app, so you don’t need to spend a fortune on a laptop to use it.

Cricut recommends a minimum of an Intel Core i3 and 4GB of RAM, but 8GB is a more comfortable starting point, and 16GB will help with complex, multi-layer projects. The IdeaPad 5 2-in-1 gives you 16GB as standard, which is why it tops our Cricut laptop guide.

The 360-degree hinge is the clincher. You can fold the screen back into tablet mode and tap, drag and resize your designs directly on the screen. It also has a good range of ports, including USB-A, which is handy if you’re plugging in your cutting machine or a USB stick full of SVG files.

It’s a bit heavier than a regular laptop, and the screen isn’t the brightest or most colourful, but neither matters much for crafting.

The PZ13 is a Windows tablet with a detachable keyboard, and it’s the top pick in our drawing laptop guide. The big selling point is that ASUS includes the stylus, keyboard, stand and sleeve in the box, so the price you see is the price you pay.

The 13.3-inch OLED screen is bright, sharp and very colour-accurate, with a Delta-E of 0.3 in our testing. Battery life is excellent too, lasting over 20 hours in our video streaming test. “It’s unique and ideal for creatives,” says our reviewer.

It’s not built for processing power, though. Its Snapdragon chip is better suited to drawing and photo editing than 3D, and some Windows software, including InDesign, isn’t fully compatible with Snapdragon chips. So check your favourite apps run well on it before you buy. The glossy screen can also be reflective.

For most students, and certainly for creative ones, the MacBook Air is the laptop to get. It’s thin, light and silent, the battery easily lasts all day, and it has enough power for design, photo editing and light video work. “A thin and light laptop that performs as well as this will always be attractive to a creative audience,” says our reviewer Ian Evenden.

That power is the reason to choose it over the cheaper MacBook Neo. In our testing, the Air M5 was much closer to the MacBook Pro than the Neo: it scored 17,091 for multi-core performance in Geekbench 6, against 17,897 for the M5 MacBook Pro. In Photoshop, it more than doubled the Neo’s scores.

It’s not so cheap any more, though. Apple raised the price by $200 / £200 in June, although students can get quite a lot of money off through Apple’s education store. If you want to save cash and your course doesn’t involve creative software, the MacBook Neo at $699 / £699 is a great-value alternative, as long as you can live with 8GB of memory.

The MacBook Pro 14-inch (M6). It’s fast, has a superb screen, lasts all day on a charge, and it handles everything from design and illustration to photo and video editing. If you prefer Windows, the ASUS ProArt P16 is the best alternative.

It depends mainly on your software. Most design, photo and video apps run on both, and Macs win on battery life and build quality. But if you work in CAD, Windows is the safer bet, as Revit and SolidWorks don’t run natively on macOS. Windows laptops are also the only option for Nvidia graphics, which many 3D and rendering tools rely on.

For creative work, 16GB is the minimum we’d recommend, and 32GB is the sweet spot if you work with large files. You’ll only need 64GB or more for heavy 3D, video or AI work. Note that RAM on Macs and many thin Windows laptops can’t be upgraded later, so buy what you’ll need.

Not for graphic design, illustration or Cricut crafting, where integrated graphics are fine. For 3D, CAD, animation and video editing, a powerful GPU will save you lots of time, which means either an Nvidia RTX graphics card or a MacBook Pro with an M5 Pro or Max chip.

For drawing and Cricut work, yes. Being able to tap, drag and sketch directly on the screen makes both much more natural. For most other creative work, it’s a nice extra rather than an essential.

Cricut says that Design Space isn’t supported on Chromebooks, so we’d recommend a Windows laptop or a Mac. Some users run the Android version of the app on a Chromebook, but you may lose some features.
