3 Signs Your Adult Child's Problems Are Draining Your Life

During a recent presentation for a local chapter of the National Alliance on Mental Illness, I spoke about the emotionally taxing challenges of overcoming helplessness when parenting struggling adult children. I emphasized a central theme I see play out in the parents I coach, those contending with the overwhelming realities of seeing their grown children struggle in heartbreaking ways. By this, I mean how easily it is for parents to ironically lose themselves when they attempt to help their adult children find their way.

For sure, challenges like serious mental illness, chronic avoidance, substance use, personality factors, and other challenges can limit what adult children can realistically handle on a day-to-day basis. From working with so many parents in these situations, I appreciate these challenges. But no matter what difficulties parents face, they deserve a quality of life that includes moments of calm, serenity, self-nurturing, and pursuing activities that give them some joy, too.

As I discuss in my book—ou Ruined My Life—Now You Owe Me!—parental guilt and excessive rescuing create a powerful one-two punch that results in a relentless, emotionally exhausting cycle. Here are three signs that you may be caught in this cycle.

The parents of struggling adult children I coach often tell me they can only relax when their child is having a good day. For many parents in these situations, their state of mind is hypervigilant, constantly waiting for the next outburst, demand, or emotional push-pull cycle from their adult child.

One of my parent clients said: "When my daughter is angry or disappointed, it's like a pit in my stomach." What does this mean for parents in these circumstances? The effects I hear: Checking their phones when out with friends or being otherwise internally preoccupied about when the next ball is about to drop. Related, one distinction I emphasized during my recent presentation was caring versus carrying. By this I mean reminding yourself that you can care deeply about your adult child's struggles without emotionally carrying all of them

Do you open your laptop and research job or volunteering possibilities, look for therapists, provide money, try to discuss finances, and offer what feels like seemingly endless suggestions? If this is you, you are not alone. Nor are you alone if you end up feeling depleted and even resentful. It is so seductive to fall into the rescue trap, but remember that rescuing today to relieve distress may come at the cost of your adult child's self-confidence. Alternatively, even small accomplishments can help your adult child feel less helpless.

When you feel that compelling urge to offer solutions to your adult child, instead of rushing in wearing your "first responder" uniform, try asking, "What's one small step you feel capable of taking?" And to help this land well, be curious rather than critical about what your adult child can realistically manage.

It can feel maddening when your adult child accuses you of being selfish or reminds you of mistakes made when raising them. Many parents confide in me that they back down in these situations because they fear losing the relationship. Well, I have some really big news for you. You can acknowledge your past parenting setbacks and mistakes without spending the rest of your days feeling like you have to keep paying emotionally or financially. Your adult child can validly feel disappointed in things you did as a parent, but that doesn't automatically mean you did everything wrong.

I hope you can come away from this post realizing that reclaiming your life does not mean abandoning your adult child. You have spent much of your life trying to support and help your struggling adult child. You are still entitled to build, live, and enjoy your own life as well.
