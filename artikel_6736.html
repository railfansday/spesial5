What to Read, Watch, and Listen To According to the Spring 2027 Collections

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

Our extensive analyses of the spring 2027 season now contine into the broader realm of culture. This, on the heels of decoding the trends—both what to where and how, clocking the art influences, and highlighting trenchant quotes from the reviews.

One way to fill in the six-month lag between the close of the season and the arrival of clothes in stores is to go deeper into designers’ inspirations. The list below collects the books, movies, and tracks that inspired the spring 2027 collections.

The French philosopher Michel Foucault, the Bloomsbury writer Virginia Woolf, and the photographer Bettina Rheims all make more than one appearance here, as does Baz Luhrmann’s Romeo + Juliet, a perennial fashion fave. You might want to take a listen to the 1972 Italian song written in gibberish that was the soundtrack to Fendi’s show or rewatch the romantic idyll that is Merchant Ivory’s 1985 film A Room With a View (based on E.M. Forster’s novel), which was Simone Rocha’s touchstone for the men’s collection she presented at Pitti Uomo. And how fitting that Valentino’s show, presented in a library, should include a sheer tulle pullover, the equivalent of a glossy book cover. Alessandro Michele had been ruminating on a concept put forth in the book Black Swan.

“The astounding Bibliothèque Sainte-Geneviève played host to Alessandro Michele’s latest Valentino show. For the occasion, Michele produced a sort of bibliography of imagined books, complete with descriptions, made-up authors, and phony ISBNs. Among the many creative titles were A Garden of Small Errors, Elsewhere Is a Precise Place, and The Social Life of Velvet. In the copious show notes, he talked up the value of an ‘anti-library’ of unread books (a concept lifted from the Lebanese American scholar Nassim Nicholas Taleb), comparing the enormous potential such a trove contains to a closetful of unworn clothes.

‘Even the garments that we will never wear, no differently than those books sitting unopened on our shelves, still speak to us of the lives we could have lived. Not as regrets, but as promises,’ Michele wrote.” —Nicole Phelps

“‘I read that book called Foucault in California,’ Tron said before the show. ‘It’s about him experimenting with LSD in Death Valley, and basically this idea of the desert as a space for expanding consciousness, experimenting, also disillusion.’ From there, he said, came ‘American desert psychedelicism, Jim Morrison, sexuality, Joan Didion, Walter De Maria’—a collision between the strictures of French intellectual thought and the psychic expansiveness of the American West.” —Luke Leitch

“Pay Per View was a collection about the slippery, sensual business of being watched—and what happens to the self when we know there is an audience. Zinko had been reading Michel Foucault on Jeremy Bentham’s panopticon: the circular prison structure in which inmates cannot know at any given moment whether the guard at its center is observing them, and therefore start to regulate their own behavior. ‘Modern society revolves around live-streaming and social media, and it forces us to behave as if we are constantly being perceived,’ she said.” —Anna Cafolla

“She had been reading Women Who Run With the Wolves, Clarissa Pinkola Estés’s 1992 bestseller about reconnecting with an instinctual female power. In Ahn’s home of Tokyo, the tension between the expectations of society and the pull of nature is palpable: ‘Japanese people are really in their heads, especially in the city, because there are so many unwritten rules we need to follow. Because of that, the city is like a well-oiled machine, and everything is functioning, but when you’re in it, I feel like it drives all that energy up to your head.’” —Ashley Ogawa Clarke

“For her spring 2027 collection, Lost to Virtue, Laura Gerte explored the deconstruction of femininity’s moral ideals. Her starting point was research into Dadaism and the work of artist and poet Mina Loy, whose feminist manifesto, written in 1914, framed female ‘virtue’—or rather virginity—as a patriarchal tool of control. Published only decades later, the text still felt radical enough to hint at just how provocative it must have been in its own time.

‘One of the manifesto’s central ideas is the destruction of virtue. For generations, the idea of female morality has been used to keep girls and women small, forcing us into predefined roles. And we don’t want that anymore,’ Gerte said after the show.” —Valerie Specht

“Dushane Noble is tapped into the ongoing discourse around taste as a social signifier. Can taste be acquired like wealth or commodities? (Tech bros sure think so.) Is it just something else to be flattened by the algorithm?

Status and Culture: How Our Desire for Social Rank Creates Taste, Identity, Art, Fashion, and Constant Change by W. David Marx informed Noble’s spring 2027 collection. ‘[Marx] talks about this idea of status-seeking being the quiet engine behind taste and culture—that what we see as cool or good taste is really status,’ Noble explained.” —Hannah Jackson

“The designer built her spring 2027 collection around Florian Illies’ book, 1913: The Year Before the Storm. ‘It was about how all the European artists and thinkers and scientists at that moment were living their life as if there was no tomorrow,’ the designer explained. ‘There was really this sense of creativity and how they were working together that was really inspiring to me; there was something that felt so parallel to the time that we live in today.’” —Laird Borrelli-Persson

“For their [10th-] anniversary collection, the duo drew inspiration from the recently published book Modestadt Berlin by historian Gesa Kessemeier; through years of research, she documented the flourishing fashion scene of prewar Berlin and highlighted long-forgotten names such as Joe Strassner, Johanna Marbach, and Uli Richter.” —Kerstin Weng

“‘If you know me, you know that I'm obsessed with a thing that’s just for a thing,’ said Daniella Kallmeyer. That thing this season was a book about traditional Japanese packaging titled How to Wrap Five Eggs. The cover art, which features eggs gently crocheted together, became a bit of an obsession for its structural discipline and textural intrigue. It captures when an obsession becomes a meditative practice, and in the Kallmeyer world, that inevitably becomes a collection. Sharp eyes hopefully caught the bright flash of eggs nestled in their crocheted carrier swinging against a trench-length blouson-bottomed coat.” —Alexandra Hildreth

“His latest he dubbed Remnants and Relics, and very good it was too: a time-traveling 400-year odyssey with an Orlando-type character who gathers up a wardrobe of foppish, high-buttoned ivory suiting with a Death in Venice vibe to it. ‘The collection is based on the Virginia Woolf character,’ Mario Keine said after his show, ‘but it’s also a development of last season’s, which was based on the Enlightenment’—the 18th-century movement where fearful superstition gave way to rational, empirical knowledge. ‘Today we’re also looking for knowledge, even though we’re meant to have it all because it’s on our phones.’” —Mark Holgate

“Home as a shelter was the genesis of Marco Rambaldi’s spring 2027 collection. From a reference to Virginia Woolf’s A Room of One’s Own, the Bolognese designer developed the concept of a space where anyone who enters can express themselves freely, without any judgment.” —Alberto Calabrese

“‘One can do without meat, but cannot live where there is no bamboo; lacking meat merely leads to weight loss, whereas lacking bamboo means losing peace of mind.’ This poem by the Song dynasty scholar Su Shi became a guiding reference for this knockout IM Men collection, which was all about bamboo.” —Ashley Ogawa Clarke

“This season he’s been thinking about The Little Mermaid, and related with the metaphor of looking up toward the surface. This wasn’t a Disneyfied ‘Part of Your World’ expression, but one that in typical Yusho fashion drew on the original story’s darker themes: the pain of walking on one’s feet in an unfamiliar world, the realization that the seawater running down the mermaid’s cheeks was really tears, and maybe the hint of regret at making a choice that can no longer be reversed.” —Ashley Ogawa Clarke

“Many models wore floppy white cloche hats, which Murakami said referenced Shiroi Boshi (The White Hat), a Japanese children’s story. The premise is too convoluted to summarize here, but the point Murakami was making was one of beauty in abstraction. ‘It’s about dissolving the boundaries between the real world and fantasy,’ he said. ‘That’s how I view memory, after all. We might casually look at things like the sky, or a coat hanger’—he gestured to one in the room—‘but the images we’ve built up in our minds are often slightly twisted or blurred at the edges. I feel there’s immense value in being able to sense and appreciate those kinds of nuances.’” —Ashley Ogawa Clarke

“The collection was entitled Skin and based around the idea of an outer surface continually shed, renewed, and rewritten, with the phoenix, specifically Osamu Tezuka’s Phoenix, providing the central image of rebirth.” —Luke Leitch

“She started, she said, from reading the play The Bitter Tears of Petra von Kant. It’s a reference, with its woman fashion designer as a central character that has inspired many for years; yet Dujancourt took it in her own way. Drawing on her experience of synesthesia, the visual suggestion of the title triggered her to create a ‘tears library’—strings of lace and crochet, representing ‘despair, grief, pain, and relief.’” —Sarah Mower

“Susan Fang has been in the midst of designing costumes for M. Butterfly, an opera by Huang Ruo showing at the La Monnaie - De Munt in Brussels next year. Naturally, the plot of M. Butterfly has been top of mind for Fang, though rather than lifting references directly from the story to inform her spring collection, she looked to holistic moods and feelings. Specifically, the ‘imagined worlds people create around love,’ as she explained at a preview.” —Alice Cary

“The invitation was a wedding announcement: that of Ippolita and Antiogu, in Alghero, ‘the midday after St. John’s night.’ …the collection’s title hid another layer: ‘A Midsummer Midday Wedding’ nodded to Shakespeare, whose midsummer comedy ends with the wedding of Theseus and Hippolyta, Ippolita in Italian, while Antiogu is the Sardinian form of Antioco.”—Giorgia Feroldi

“Casa Susanna, a photo book chronicling the lives and loves of a group of midcentury men who dressed as women at a country retreat in the Catskill Mountains, was Joseph Altuzarra’s starting point for spring. Something about Susanna Valenti and her friends’ expressiveness served as an unlock for Altuzarra. ‘I just love how joyful it was,’ he said. ‘I really wanted the collection to feel joyful, to be all about color and this idea of freedom.’” —Nicole Phelps

“Isabel Marant is the quintessential Parisian brand, but for spring creative director Kim Bekker found her inspiration a world away, in LA. There, she came across a ’60s book of photographs by Sam Haskins showing women bathed in hazy light, dressed or undressed. ‘The colors were almost translucent,’ Bekker said.” —Tina Isaac-Goizé

“‘I started from a very simple idea—to continue exploring an Italian way of life defined by authenticity, quality, and a natural relationship with objects,’ Matteo Tamburini said. Simplicity, however, is seldom simple. Tamburini’s point of departure was Viaggio in Italia, Luigi Ghirri’s seminal 1984 photographic project, which offered a new way of seeing the country: attentive rather than sentimental, curious and slightly raw rather than steeped in clichés.” —Tiziana Cardini

“Inspired by Modern Lovers (1990), photographer Bettina Rheims’s portrait series exploring the performance and plurality of gender identities, Yanagawa subtly disrupted his signature tailoring—blazers and suits cut long or cropped—with conventionally feminine gestures: lace peeking from sleeves, mesh tulle, crushed velvet, glossy patent finishes, and shorts designed to resemble skirts. Altercasting completed the picture with models that looked lifted straight from Rheims’s photographs, slicked-back hair included.” —Valerie Specht

Charlotte Knowles and Alexandre Arsenault “will be celebrating their brand’s 10th anniversary next year, and found themselves returning to a central source of inspiration for their graduate show at Central Saint Martins: the French photographer Bettina Rheims’s landmark book Chambre Close, which featured images of women undressing across a series of cheap, anonymous hotel rooms. ‘We’ve been feeling nostalgic about where we started, and we thought it would be an interesting exercise to think about how we see things now, versus how we saw them back then,’ Arsenault said at a preview. Added Knowles: ‘All the women in the book are in different stages of undress, so we translated that into the collection quite literally: a lot of deconstruction and layering. We also took inspiration from the colors and went a little more zingy with the palette. It’s really about our twist on femininity.’” —Liam Hess

“‘I always try to go with a little bit of romance and beauty, but I always try to give it this grunge element,’ Hillary Taymour said at a preview. The designer carried over some 18th-century loveliness (see the opening ruffled moiré cape) from the not-for-sale collection she presented at Copenhagen Fashion Week, and introduced medieval (see the plaid printed sequined fabric that is a stand-in for chain mail) after watching Heath Ledger and Shannyn Sossamon in A Knight’s Tale on the flight back. ‘My whole goal is to make everything big and voluminous and as light as possible,’ said Taymour.” —Laird Borrelli-Persson

“For spring 2027, his 11th season, he felt the urge to connect back to the brand’s foundations, in part as a response to his father’s recent passing. ‘There’s a more somber tone,’ he said. Miles Davis and Tupac Shakur served as touchstones, as did the movie Ichi the Killer and the Japanese actor Takeshi Kitano.” —Max Berlinger

“Wong Kar-wai’s In the Mood for Love was the starting point for Patrick McDowell’s spring ’27 collection, titled Love. It’s also the designer’s partner’s favorite film. In it actress Maggie Cheung wears 21 cheongsams of different prints. It was one of those prints McDowell translated into the green stripe silk material that was the protagonist of the show, taking inspiration from the film’s dominant hues of red and green.” —Electra Kostoni

“There was a whiff of the 1980s, she said, in the Hawaiian shirts (the print based on the one Leonardo DiCaprio wears in Baz Luhrmann’s Romeo + Juliet and a picture of Bruce Willis), and cropped, garment-dyed, coated-nylon cargo jackets in Miami Vice hues.” —Max Berlinger

“Dsquared2’s Dean and Dan Caten went deeper into Italian-ness for their spring 2027. In fact, the duo drew on the Mediterranean folklore of southern Italy, exploring religious symbolism through a spicy lens. Crosses and holy references, along with religious-inspired tattoos, were at the core of this season, with nods to Baz Luhrmann’s Romeo + Juliet.

This was especially evident in the gangster-inspired atmosphere, a choice that showcased Dsquared2’s propensity to mix up very different references, often rooted in pop culture.” —Alberto Calabrese

“The starting point was Green Snake, Tsui Hark’s 1993 film starring Maggie Cheung and Joey Wong, a modern retelling of the ancient Chinese legend of the white snake. For designers Samuel Yang and Erik Litzén, the duo behind the label, there was no shortage of material to shed and reassemble.” —Tiziana Cardini

“While gestating the collection, Rocha returned to the 1985 Merchant Ivory movie adaptation of Forster’s book, reacquainting herself with an Oscar-winning suite of Edwardian costume design by Jenny Beavan and John Bright almost as exquisitely realized as Florence itself. ‘This is my Irish man and he’s arrived in Florence and become the main character,’ she said of a designed ensemble that was inhabited by multiple character studies.” —Luke Leitch

“Opening with sharp tailoring and an elegant Dalmatian print on skirts and blouses, it read like something Cruella de Vil would have worn had she been a Wall Street big shot in the ’80s.

Instead, it was the much cooler 1982 cult classic Smithereens that Reid Baker and Inês Amorim were inspired by, which also informed the bold punky checks that were mixed in, all finished off with leather driving gloves and wraparound statement sunglasses that lent a sleek edge.” —Ashley Ogawa Clarke

“‘I always tell the Los Angeles story, the rock and roll, something sunny and optimistic,’ Amiri said backstage. ‘But one night I watched American Gigolo, and I saw Los Angeles in a very dark way, a seductive way.’ From there, he was sketching out a script involving a cast of sharp-dressed night crawlers, thinking about where they’d be glimpsed in the shadows of dimly lit bars or the back rooms of private members’ clubs. ‘There’s something dangerous about these characters and the places they go.’” —Sarah Mower

“As the designer acknowledged, it’s ‘kind of strange’ for Ann Demeulemeester to take inspiration from LA. But he said he’s fascinated by the darker side of the city. During his trip, Gallici watched Billy Wilder’s 1950 dark comedy Sunset Boulevard and started to see Los Angeles through a more sinister lens. ‘I was thinking about how LA is the ghost of a city that once was so florid. It’s almost a city worn to death,’ he said.” —Lucy Maguire

“As they were building out the theme, the pair watched any number of movies relating to Hollywood—Sunset Boulevard, Boogie Nights, Hollywood 90028, All That Jazz, They Shoot Horses, Don’t They?, Cabaret. The last is especially up August Barron’s alley; the designers are interested in the elusiveness or the decay of fame, the wearing away of its gold plating.” —Laird Borrelli-Persson

“Yuka Kimura called this Mukcyen collection Low Energy. The designer, who is on her sixth season of the brand, had been watching Michelangelo Antonioni’s 1964 film Red Desert, which explores the psychological toll of industrialization, and was thinking about how it relates to our present reality.

Nowadays the grind of factories and machinery has been replaced by a ceaseless hum of digital noise. ‘A messy, overwhelming variety of inputs,’ as Kimura put it. ‘People are becoming increasingly sensitive to this kind of noise, or simply exhausted by it,’ she said. ‘I created this collection to express that feeling.’” —Ashley Ogawa Clarke

“Romance, ERD-style, has a dark undertow: The show notes evoked ‘a mysterious unnamed figure who destabilizes a wealthy bourgeois family by seducing every member of the household.’ Pasolini fans will recognize the late, great Terence Stamp in Teorema. There’s always a hint of trouble brewing.” —Tina Isaac-Goizé

“Leandro Cano is frustrated by how often Andalusia is underestimated. That frustration became the starting point for Le Chien Andalou, his latest collection.

Religion and flamenco traditions became two of the collection’s foundations, filtered through the same surrealist lens as Luis Buñuel’s famous film, which gave the collection its name. ‘This time I’m working from three ideas: hyperbole, beauty, and surrealism, which I accept as something completely natural,’ said Cano.” —Nuria Lui

“Francesco Murano went to Metropolis for his starting point (a particularly timely choice, given that Fritz Lang’s 1927 vision is set in 2026), and to the film’s two Marias for his central dichotomy: the virtuous woman and her seductive robotic double.

His response, a collection called Éidola, which is ancient Greek for ‘image,’ took the two and, sensibly, refused to choose.” —Tiziana Cardini

“Biga landed on Theodore Dryer’s black-and-white 1928 film The Passion of Joan of Arc as his spring inspiration. This martyr might be fashion’s favorite saint, and pop culture likes her too: Hlynur Pálmason released his own Joan of Arc film last year, and Baz Luhrmann is developing one as well. Biga was drawn to the he/she aspects of this historical figure who refused to give up her faith—and the menswear she wore.” —Laird Borrelli-Persson

“The show melted two stories together, those of A Bug’s Life and the fairy tale of the princess and the frog—except ‘in this case it’s the prince and the frog,’ Browne said.” —Luke Leitch

“The designer explained that he had three points of departure: Studio Ghibli’s The Secret World of Arrietty; the Sylvanian Families he has been collecting with his young relatives (those inspired the tactile velvets, wide-wale corduroys, and lamés), and Alice’s mushroom-eating adventures in Wonderland. But he leavened the vibe with pointed commentary: ‘I figured since the world is so fucked up right now, we might as well turn everything upside down,’ Ortuño Perez quipped.” —Tina Isaac-Goizé

“For spring 2027, designer Louis-Gabriel Nouchi looked to America’s Pacific Northwest, specifically the David Lynch version as depicted in his cult-classic TV show, Twin Peaks. That not only gave the collection its desired outdoorsy feel (those Boy Scout shorts and ranger shirts!) but provided a cast of archetypes (the detective, the cop, the endangered ingenue) and a creeping sense of darkness, of strangeness. Add to that the navy/gray/brown/purple palette of Francis Bacon and a touch of Blade Runner’s dystopian futurism, and the broad contours of his collection started to coalesce accordingly.” —Max Berlinger

“‘[The Golden Girls] was not actually the inspiration for the collection, but in hindsight we realized that it has a lot to do with it and there are elements that feel very Golden Girls,’ added Lazo. ‘I would say 90% of that series is the women in their dressing gowns or bathrobes eating cheesecake, and that also became a talking point in the collection.’ ‘We put a beefcake in the cheesecake,’ Schmidl quipped.” —Laird Borrelli-Persson

“In 1972 the Milanese musician Adriano Celentano conducted an experiment: He released a song without meaning, its lyrics written entirely in gibberish meant to sound like English, to see if it could still move an audience. At first the song bombed. But two nationally televised performances featuring group choreography and Celentano’s own distinctive dancing—his nickname was Il Molleggiato, or The Bouncy—saw it rise high in the charts, first in Italy and then in multiple countries across Europe.

One of those two TV performances featured another Italian pop progressive, Raffaella Carrà, whose immortal Italo-disco banger ‘Rumore’ was mixed into the ‘Prisencolinensinainciusol’-dominated soundtrack at Maria Grazia Chiuri’s Fendi show. You could see Celentano’s experimental approach in 1972 as a mirror of Chiuri’s in 2026. Speaking at a preview, she said: ‘Fashion is part of our language.… Clothes give another sense to the body.’ She added: ‘Fashion is that. What do you like? What is your memory? Putting this together is like being a DJ. I think it is impossible to concentrate on only one aspect.’” —Luke Leitch

True Blue, presented by the Brodie Sessions, as the Skovgaardettes, from left: Lauren Glading, Sharleen Chidiac, and Maya Laner.

“Skovgaard, who grew up with the Spice Girls (and identified with Baby Spice) was turned onto the Supremes, The Ronettes, and the Shangri-Las while watching a Danish television series about the ’60s. He started researching them, ‘but instead of just reusing the vibe of those three girl groups, I wanted to create a fictional girl group instead because I couldn’t really see [the others] representing the collection I was dreaming of making,’ he explained. So ‘I decided to come up with this imaginary girl group called the Skovgaardettes.’” —Laird Borrelli-Persson

“The show had two phases, each delineated by music: Chaka Khan’s ‘Like Sugar’

and Michael Jackson’s ‘I Just Can’t Stop Loving You.’” —Luke Leitch

“It looks like goodbye at Saint Laurent. Ten years and precisely two days since his 2016 debut, Anthony Vaccarello took his last bow at the Kering-owned brand. At least, the signs pointed that way.

The music playing as guests filtered in was Miles Davis’s soundtrack to Louis Malle’s first film, the meaningfully named Elevator to the Gallows.” —Nicole Phelps

“The collection began with an assortment of images by photographer Trevor Hughes, whose bike-messenger series in 1990s Toronto captured an innate understanding of styling. Partly born from athletic and functional necessity, and partly from pure inherent fashion, such as a zip-up minidress styled over bike shorts, Hughes’s messengers captured what so many are trying to chase, becoming the basis for Attersee’s spring collection.” —Alexandra Hildreth

“‘I wanted to explore falling in all its forms,’ Kegels said. The runway included portions of stretch fabric, suspended just above the ground, to show the weight of each step. (It was inspired by a YouTube video of science educator Dan Burns, who used a similar setup to explain Einstein’s theory of general relativity.)” —Lucy Maguire
