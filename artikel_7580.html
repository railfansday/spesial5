New Mexico commission approves proposal to improve retirement benefits for judges

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

The New Mexico Judicial Compensation Commission, which recommends compensation for judges annually, approved a proposal to match judges' retirement plans with that of state employees.

ALBUQUERQUE, N.M. (CN) â New Mexico judges would get a higher maximum pension and receive benefits sooner under a proposal the state’s Judicial Compensation Commission approved Tuesday. The changes would match what employees in the state’s general employee benefits plan already receive.

The commissioners unanimously approved lowering the vesting period from eight years to five years and increasing the maximum pension a judge could receive from 85% to 100% of a judge’s final average salary.

The New Mexico Judicial Retirement Act and Magistrate Retirement Act establish retirement plans for judges and justices in the state, which are sub-funds administered by the Public Employees Retirement Association.

The approved proposal puts the Judicial Retirement Act back on the path toward solvency, according to Karl Reifsteck, director of the Administrative Office of the Courts and staff to the commission.

The judicial retirement fund has been underfunded, according to a fiscal impact report from the Legislative Finance Committee. In June 2024, the Public Employees Retirement Association reported the judicial retirement fund had $214.3 million in total liabilities and $120.2 million in total assets.

In a report submitted to the Public Employees Retirement Association, the commission requested the association evaluate it with an increased service credit of 4%, rather than 3.25%, for judges’ first or next ten years of service. The association found the credit would not offer a solution to the Judicial Retirement Act’s solvency, and the commission scratched it.

The removal of the increased credit requires judges and the state to pay a small amount more each year, according to Reifsteck, but it also allows the act to become solvent in 58 years. “It gets us back in the right direction,” Reifsteck said.

Similarly, the approved changes to the Magistrate Retirement Act push it to be solvent in 24 years, rather than 37, Reifsteck said.

Four commissioners were present at the meeting: Camille Carey, chair of the commission and dean of the University of New Mexico School of Law; Allison Block-Chavez, president of the State Bar of New Mexico; Patrick Ortiz, retired Santa Fe attorney and appointee of the Chief Justice of the state Supreme Court; and Kathryn Brack Morrow, Las Cruces attorney and appointee of the New Mexico Senate’s president pro tempore.
