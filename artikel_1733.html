Messi was a deity among Argentina’s unholy rabble

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Little sorcerer calls time on playing for his country six weeks after World Cup final defeat which was marred by brutality of his team-mates

Lionel Messi’s haunted expression told you more than a three-page retirement statement, artfully displayed alongside a golden pen, ever could. Sobbing and desolate under a cloudless New Jersey sky, he understood that his last game for Argentina had also been among his worst.

Where four years earlier he had adorned the greatest World Cup final with two goals, he found no artistry to salvage this abomination of an encore, drifting into anonymity while his team-mates triggered a bare-knuckle brawl. No wonder he stood apart while Leandro Paredes and Nahuel Molina did their worst, preferring to absorb one more chorus of adoration from the blue-and-white disciples before forsaking the stage for good.

You could make the case that Messi, as captain of an unholy rabble that had sought only to kick lumps out of Spain, should have taken a moral lead by hauling his team-mates into line, rather than reducing himself to their level by agitating to have Marc Cucurella sent off.

And yet for 20 years, the little sorcerer stayed aloof from his nation’s most lurid antics on the pitch, establishing himself as a strangely beatific presence on whom the highest ideals could be projected. Anybody who has heard Messi’s name being chanted by 50,000 fervent Argentines would have felt it. He was as close as 21st-century sport has come to a secular deity, the object of such veneration that Pope Francis felt compelled to remind Catholics that it was sacrilegious to keep calling him God.

Today, with one agonised announcement, that glorious delirium is over, and already Argentina seems not to know what to do with itself. Pablo Giralt, a sports anchor in Buenos Aires, was unable even to speak when he heard the news live on air, while shattered young fans described how they had called their parents in tears.

Everybody had known the moment was approaching, given the finality of his exit from the final and the subsequent death of his father, Jorge, but it still triggered an unbearable sense of loss.

Messi was the force who galvanised a nation, propelling his side from the unfathomable low of defeat to Saudi Arabia at the 2022 World Cup to holding the trophy aloft 26 days later. Who could ever hope to emulate him, to detonate such joy that the team’s homecoming bus parade in the capital had to be abandoned for a helicopter ride because of the millions on the streets?

“Thank you, God, for making me Argentine,” Messi wrote in his earnest sign-off, his strength of allegiance unfailing to the end. As a veteran of 207 international matches, with his last at La Bombonera distinguished by an ecstatic ovation, he understood both the passions he unleashed among his people and the extent to which his own story became that of an entire country.

You struggled to imagine what life was like in the eye of this madness, with his every move stalked. For the wonder of Messi was that the cult went beyond borders, that the joy he inspired across six World Cups made him beloved to billions. An abiding memory of the 2022 final is of the walk back to the metro station in Doha, where thousands of Nepalese and Bangladeshi supporters, all part of the vast migrant workforce that had built the stadiums, united in “Messi, Messi” refrains even at 1am.

Messi was majestic at that tournament, so imperious in filleting Croatia in the semi-finals that Argentina’s TV reporters abandoned even the pretence of objectivity. “Truly, you made your mark in everyone’s life, and that to me is beyond winning any World Cup,” gushed Sofia Martinez of Television Publica. And when he won it all, the mark only deepened. To occupy any press box at the World Cup where Argentina were involved this summer was to see South American colleagues in “Messi 10” jerseys, helplessly in thrall to his greatness.

As a 39-year-old, he confounded every expectation, scoring a hat-trick against Algeria and breaking Miroslav Klose’s World Cup goals record. Even when engineering those implausible fightbacks against Egypt and England, he held a volatile team together with a minimum of fuss.

To study Messi at his last World Cup was to see a man spared the obligation to do anything off the ball. For large periods of each game, the most minutely scrutinised athlete on the planet would do little besides wander around, only to ignite when it mattered most. You saw it in the fateful semi-final with England, where, at 1-0 down, he sloped off to the right touchline as if absent-mindedly gardening. Except he was plotting his opponents’ downfall, working out the precise spots from which to unleash two sumptuous crosses and shatter English hearts.

That alchemy would elude him in the final, where Argentina’s bid for immortality brought only instant notoriety, not to mention £236,000 in fines for misconduct. Was Messi’s image hurt by his connection to the chaos? Not in the long run. Nobody likes to cling to the fact that Pelé’s swansong for Brazil was a drawn friendly with Yugoslavia, or that Diego Maradona bowed out of World Cup football by failing a drug test for ephedrine. What endure are the indelible masterpieces those players bequeathed en route.

It will be the same for Messi, a genius defined less by his ending than by the electrifying adrenalin rush he produced along the way, spanning three finals, 21 goals, and countless flourishes to take the breath away. “Thank you” was all he had left to say. And yet the gratitude, ultimately, is all ours.
