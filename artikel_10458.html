23 ways to handle tasks that trigger dread

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Everyone has them: Tasks they dread. They can range from mundane or routine items that clog the calendar to difficult conversations and meetings that cause stress. Avoiding them doesn’t make them go away. It just makes the dread heavier. Figuring out how to get through them frees up the time and headspace they take up. 

We asked members of the Fast Company Impact Council how they handle tasks they dread. Here, 23 of them provide their best tips for getting them off your to-do list.

1. BODY-DOUBLE THE TASK

I don’t tackle them alone. We call it body doubling: pulling in a teammate for a focused session to knock out the task neither of us wants to face solo. It depends on the task at hand too! My default is our incredible chief of staff. She’s the left brain to my right brain. From there it’s our executive team if the task requires finance, operations, technology, etc. Consistently routing the task with the right team members keeps the business moving forward. — Cody Barbo, Trust & Will

2. JUST DO IT AND DO IT NOW

Dread is what you feel when you aren’t just doing it. Just do it. And when you do, more often than not you enter flow state, and you just keep doing stuff. — Eric Simons, Bolt.new

3. APPROACH IT LIKE AN ATHLETE

When I face a task I’d rather avoid, I try to tackle it right away, before I give myself a hundred reasons to postpone it. I approach these situations like an athlete: Discomfort is part of progress, not a reason to stop. Whether it’s a challenging negotiation or a difficult decision, I’ve learned to take action even when I don’t feel ready. Years of daily training and building at my company have taught me that motivation comes and goes, and discipline is the key to keeping you moving forward. You can’t always control how you feel about a task, but you can train yourself to show up and get it done anyway. — Victoria Repa, BetterMe

4. TAKE ONE SMALL STEP

In my mind, most dread comes from a task that has no clear definition or parameter. I’ve found it helpful to jump on the first physical step, small enough to finish in a few minutes (e.g., open the file, draft the subject line, pull last month’s numbers) and physically stop there to build next steps. This creates a sense of internal accountability. Once the task has a shape, the rest usually goes faster than the dread predicted. Also, if it still sits there after a week, that’s useful information: It may belong to someone else, or it may not need doing at all. — James Chester, WVN

5. CHIP AWAY EACH DAY

Momentum beats motivation. I start small and chip away a little each day. Procrastinating doesn’t buy you time, it just weighs you down, and eventually it crowds out the work you love. — Tami Rosen, executive board and advisor to technology and finance companies

6. TACKLE THEM FIRST, EARLY IN THE DAY

I do them first, and I make them smaller. Dread grows in the gap between knowing a task needs doing and starting it. So I close that gap early in the day, before anything else competes for my attention. If it’s big, I commit only to the first part. Momentum handles the rest. I’ve also learned to ask what the dread is telling me. Sometimes it’s a sign the task needs a conversation, not more effort, and the fastest way through is a call to the right person. — Megan Noel, Covista

7. NAME WHAT YOU DREAD AND WHY

Usually, people dread something because it is uncomfortable, ambiguous, or carries some sort of emotional weight. I try to name what I dread and why, instead of pretending the task itself is the problem. If it matters, do it and do it now, because avoidance compounds just like progress does. The things we put off usually get heavier, not lighter. — Ethan Braden, Texas A&M University

8. TAKE OUT THE TRASH DAY

A colleague once inspired me with her “take out the trash day,” dedicating a chunk of time each week to knock through dreaded tasks. With all the noise on Slack, texts, and email, I block an hour at the end of the week, mute my notifications, and cruise through. It’s how I take care of budgets, expenses, and the general paperwork, so I can get back to the creative work where I’m in my flow. Sometimes there’s a snack reward waiting at the end. — Courtney Brigham, Typeface

9. SET A REWARD FOR YOURSELF

I break it down into chunks, set a target date and time to complete each chunk, and then block out time and get it done. I also set a reward for myself after doing it. For example, I’ll get to have this great lunch after I finish it. It sounds silly, but it’s nice to have rewards after doing a project that is not enjoyable! — Felicia Curcuru, Binti

10. RELY ON PREPARATION

During dreaded high-stakes situations like meetings with multiple stakeholders or delivering sensitive performance-based feedback, I rely on preparation. Coming armed with a brief outline of mission-critical points ensures you stay on track and helps you recover if the conversation gets derailed. If I’m ruminating on a complex problem that I just can’t seem to crack, I put everything down and do something completely unrelated. Taking a walk, listening to a podcast, or even washing the dishes allows my brain to marinate, discover hidden connections, and eventually the solution becomes clear. — Celia Jones, FINN Partners

11. PRIORITIZE ACCORDINGLY

Tasks I dread are rarely the most important for our business, so I prioritize them accordingly. However, they tend to bubble up to the higher end of the list of less critical tasks. The longer they remain on my to-do list, the more days they get to impact my morale, so I’m motivated to get them done. — Brad Weber, InspiringApps

12. CONNECT IT TO OUTCOME

I try to stay positive by focusing on the value the task will create once it’s completed. I put it in the broader context of the goal it supports and the impact it will have. As a big-picture person, details can be challenging, but connecting the work to a meaningful outcome helps me stay motivated and push through with a positive mindset. — Chris K Bailey, Bailey Brand Consulting

13. TAKE THE MEDICINE ALL AT ONCE

I put them off. Then I bundle them up and take care of them all at once. Like medicine. It tastes bad but works. Sort of like Buckley’s cough and cold medicine. — Phillip Haid, Public Inc.

14. STOP AND MAP IT OUT

If I catch myself spending real time on something where I’m just going through the motions, I stop and map it out. What are the inputs, what am I doing with them, and what does the output need to look like? Once the process is clearly mapped, it’s usually pretty obvious that a lot of it doesn’t need me. Then I build an automation around it. I still get the result; I just skip the mundane part. — Mike Sewell, Gresham Smith

15. ASK WHETHER YOU’RE THE RIGHT PERSON TO DO IT

I try not to spend too much time negotiating with myself. If something needs to be done, I focus on the next step and start. Usually, the anticipation is worse than the task itself. As a CEO, I’ve also learned to ask whether I’m the right person to do it. There are many things I can do, but I don’t need to do all of them. Sometimes leadership means taking something on yourself. Other times it means trusting someone else to own it. What I try to avoid is letting something I don’t enjoy become something the organization has to wait for. — Yuping Huang, Quantum Computing Inc.

16. CARVE UP YOUR DAY

Technology marketing is constant context switching and rapid decision-making. If you’re not careful, a whole day passes without a minute of real thought or creativity. You give in to the “critical” transactions, and eventually the work that’s most critical to your job disappears entirely. So I carve up my day. Mornings are for content, strategy, and creative problem-solving. Midday is the transactional work. The last couple of hours go to administration and anything that wasn’t urgent. And if something that needs real creativity slips through until late in the day, it gets the first look the next morning, with fresh eyes. — Colby Proffitt, Seekr

17. DIVIDE IT INTO MANAGEABLE STEPS

When I face a task I dread, I focus on understanding what makes it difficult and what depends on completing it. This matters because postponing an uncomfortable responsibility does not remove it. It can affect decisions, relationships, and other people’s work. I divide the task into manageable steps, establish a clear deadline, and begin with the first action. For me, discipline is not about enjoying every responsibility. It is about addressing what needs to be done before hesitation becomes a larger problem. — Manuel Freire-Garabal, Gioya HEI

18. DO THE WORST THING FIRST

Do it first. I was told once that you should always do the worst thing on your to-do list first because the day then only gets better. Have stuck to it ever since. And never overlook the power of reward: For every annoying task give yourself a little treat in return. The promise of a future good thing coming your way works every time. — Helen Andrews, Johannes Leonardo

19. AUTOMATE THE MUNDANE

Recently, I’ve unlocked a new cheat code in chipping away at those dreaded tasks. When walking to work, I’ll start talking with my favorite large language model of the moment and we can hammer out 10 open items from unanswered Slack messages and emails to meeting recaps and other nagging tasks that can quickly pile up. When all the right connectors are in place, the efficiency unlock is tremendous and has been a game changer for knocking out those dreaded to-dos. — Matt Conlin, Fluent

20. KNOW WHEN THE TASK BELONGS TO SOMEONE ELSE

I like to understand every aspect of my business, even the things that don’t come naturally to me. I’m always open to learning something new, but I also recognize when something falls outside my skill set. I believe in knowing your strengths and surrounding yourself with people who bring expertise you don’t have. If it’s something I can tackle, I get it done. If someone else can do it better, I delegate. My time is best spent where I can make the greatest impact. — Kim Wileman, No Makeup Makeup

21. USE THE HEADWIND AS A TAILWIND

I once heard Jimmy Iovine, co-founder of Beats, talk about using a headwind as a tailwind, and it’s stuck with me ever since. If there’s something you hate doing, run towards it, not away from it. Delay is a false economy because it just extends the dread. Then I break it into tiny actions and get the first one done. Momentum beats motivation every time, and most tasks are never as bad as the story you’ve told yourself about them. — Emily Kortlang, Meaco

22. MEET IT AT YOUR COGNITIVE PEAK

I use power hours. I know my brain is sharpest late in the evening or early in the morning, so I save the tasks I would normally procrastinate on for that window. I don’t do it every day. The idea is simple: Dreaded work takes far less energy when you meet it at your cognitive peak, so an hour or two of focus can clear what would otherwise linger for weeks. — Muneer Panjwani, Engage for Good | The Halo Awards

23. ATTACK THE FIRST FIVE MINUTES

I try to attack the first five minutes rather than the whole task. Dread grows in the space between knowing you have to do something and starting it. Once I take action, the task usually gets a lot less intimidating. — Ran Mullins, Psympl
