'Used substances attached': Alastair Cook mentioned in his book how David Warner admitted to him to altering condition of ball in the past

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

Alastair Cook wrote that David Warner described tampering with the ball in a first-class match.

Warner made the remark after Australia's 2017-18 Ashes win, with Steve Smith standing beside him.

The conversation took place roughly ten weeks before the Cape Town Test.

Every account of what Australia was doing with the ball before Cape Town is being read again this month, and one of the earliest came from an opponent who was in the room. Alastair Cook set it down in his autobiography in 2019, and it has sat there largely undisturbed since.

The England opener was describing the end of the 2017-18 Ashes, which Australia had won 4-0, and the drinks that followed between the two sides in the dressing room.

"David Warner, a couple of beers into his celebration, mentioned that he used substances attached to the strapping on his hand to accelerate the deterioration of the ball during a first-class match," Cook wrote. What gives the passage its weight is who else Cook says was listening.

"I looked at Steve Smith, who shot a glance that said, 'Ooh, you shouldn't have said that,'" he added. The timing is the other thing worth holding on to. That series finished in Sydney on January 8, 2018, and the third Test at Newlands began on March 22, which puts barely ten weeks between the remark and the afternoon Cameron Bancroft was filmed.

Cook's point in the book was not that Australia was cheating in that Ashes series, but that they already had what they were later caught chasing. He put the argument in his teammate's words.

"Stuart Broad sums it up pretty well and says they got the ball to reverse swing in that Ashes. Why change what you've been doing? Why suddenly use sandpaper?" Cook wrote.

Australia's attack had reversed the ball heavily through that summer, with Mitchell Starc, Josh Hazlewood, and Pat Cummins sharing 61 wickets between them across the five Tests. The question Broad was asking is why a side with that method needed a new one two months later.

Cook's own conclusion was about the culture rather than the equipment. "People know what was going on. But it's been the best thing for Australian cricket because they realised it wasn't acceptable. The win-at-all-costs culture they created isn't what the Australian public needed or wanted. They'd gone too far," he wrote.

Bancroft said this week that the practice did not begin at Newlands but at Port Elizabeth, in the Test before it, and that the bowlers and the wicketkeeper gave him feedback on the altered ball. Cook's recollection pushes at the same idea from further back, with Warner describing something similar in a domestic match and Smith close enough to react to it.

The caveats are real and should be said plainly. This is a secondhand account of a conversation after drinks, no investigation has ever examined the first-class match Warner is said to have referred to, and Warner has not publicly answered the claim in the seven years since the book appeared.

It still sits awkwardly alongside the official version of 2018, which held that three men improvised on one afternoon. Cricket Australia has announced a forum for the players and staff from that tour to set out their recollections, and the question of how far back this goes is the one it will have to deal with first.
