Trump Blames Biden For Nationwide FAA Meltdown But Union Points To Sloppy Contractor Work On His Watch

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

The Trump administration wants Americans to blame Joe Biden for the nationwide FAA meltdown. But the union representing FAA safety specialists points to a far more immediate failure: contractor maintenance performed in the middle of the day without the risk assessment and federal oversight historically used for such work. Separate questions surrounding a large FAA technology contract and a political contribution to Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy’s son-in-law also deserve a careful look, though the evidence does not appear to connect that system to the outage.

President Donald Trump finally addressed Friday’s nationwide ground stop on Sunday morning. Rather than apologize to travelers or promise an independent investigation, he blamed the previous administration:

“The Biden Administration was an FAA disaster. Everything they touched turned bad. Big money spent unwisely, making an old and broken system worse than when they began. We have the knowledge and plan for the Flight Control Systems, we need Congress to give the money, FAST! Our FAA is doing a great job, and is ready to go.”

He followed that with another demand for congressional funding:

“If Congress approves the desperately needed money for Air Traffic Control, we can get it built quickly and efficiently. It will be the BEST SYSTEM anywhere in the World. Much of our current air control system is 50 years old – RIDICULOUS! We have already started the work, but it should be done all at once, not in drips and drabs. Congress must approve. The Biden Administration set it back years!”

There is plenty of blame to distribute across multiple administrations. The air traffic control system is dangerously old, and the Biden administration did not solve that problem during its four years in power. It also experienced its own nationwide ground stop in January 2023 after the NOTAM system failed.

But the telecommunications network did not suddenly become antiquated when Biden entered office. Congress and administrations from both parties have tolerated obsolete FAA infrastructure for decades, including Trump during his first term.

More importantly, Friday’s failure occurred in November 2026, nearly two years into Trump’s second term, while a federal contractor was performing work under the supervision of the current FAA, not the Biden FAA.

Blaming Biden does not explain what happened, full stop.

The Professional Aviation Safety Specialists (PASS), which represents approximately 11,000 FAA employees, has offered a much more specific criticism. According to the union, the problem was isolated to two contractor-owned and maintained routers being updated during the middle of the day.

PASS says that contractors have historically followed a process in which FAA employees conduct a risk assessment and coordinate the maintenance. Its technicians were not involved in the work that preceded Friday’s ground stop. The union explained:

“Historically contractors have followed a protocol where FAA employees conduct a risk assessment and coordinate the maintenance of the system or equipment.”

That raises an obvious question: why was that process not followed here?

While the union stops short of saying that a FAA risk assessment would definitely have prevented the failure, risk assessments exist precisely because even routine work can produce extraordinary consequences when performed on critical infrastructure. If federal technicians had historically provided another layer of review before this sort of work, excluding them deserves a much better explanation than the blame game we have received from current leaders.

Duffy disputes the implication that PASS technicians were improperly sidelined.

Appearing on CBS, he argued that union technicians work on FAA-owned radios, radar equipment, and other systems, while L3Harris owns and maintains the telecommunications network involved here. He said contractors have performed this sort of work thousands of times and maintained that the process was handled appropriately.

That’s true, but the union did not claim that its members ordinarily install L3Harris routers. It claimed FAA employees historically performed a risk assessment and coordinated the maintenance before contractors worked on the equipment.

Duffy’s explanation that the contractor owns the network does not answer whether FAA personnel should evaluate the operational risk of changing equipment that can destabilize communications and radar feeds across the entire National Airspace System. The FAA’s own questions and answers page acknowledges that the agency will reevaluate when this sort of maintenance is performed.

The FAA says data flooded the network at a rate of 1,000 connection attempts every four to five seconds. Investigators are examining a new router installed by L3Harris at a radio communications site in El Dorado, Arkansas, as well as equipment at a radar site in Amarillo, Texas (amazing, isn’t it, that a simple router update in Arkansas and Texas could cripple air traffic control across the USA?).

The agency stabilized the network by blocking those locations and disabling the relevant broadcasting function on approximately 60 similar routers.

So yes, the older network was vulnerable. But the immediate event was not simply “old equipment.” It occurred during maintenance involving a new router connected to that old network. That brings us back to planning, testing, oversight, and timing.

There is another issue circulating online involving Air Space Intelligence (ASI), the Boston technology company developing the FAA’s new Strategic Management of Airspace, Routes and Trajectories system, known as SMART.

For the avoidance of doubt: SMART did not cause Friday’s outage.

The FAA says SMART is a data aggregator that does not feed information into the air traffic control system. ASI has likewise denied that its technology or FAA integration played any role. There appears to be no connection between SMART and the failed L3Harris telecommunications equipment.

The fact that SMART expanded nationwide three days before the ground stop naturally attracted attention. Timing alone, however, does not establish causation. Calling ASI “the AI startup involved in the flight chaos” is therefore misleading based on the evidence presently available…there’s plenty of fodder without drawing links that do not exist.

But ASI’s federal contract and political spending still merit scrutiny on their own terms. According to federal spending records, the FAA awarded ASI a contract on June 23, 2026, with a potential value of $875.95 million through 2038 (that is the contract ceiling, not money already paid to the company. Approximately $23.34 million has been obligated so far).

Federal Election Commission records also show that the Air Space Intelligence political action committee contributed $2,500 on March 31, 2026, to the congressional campaign of Michael Alfonso, Duffy’s son-in-law. Alfonso subsequently won election to represent Wisconsin’s 7th Congressional District, the same seat Duffy once held.

There is no smoking gun that the contribution influenced the contract award, that Duffy intervened in the procurement, or that ASI received favorable treatment in exchange for political support. But the appearance is still unfortunate. A company seeking an FAA contract potentially worth hundreds of millions of dollars should recognize the problem with its PAC donating to the transportation secretary’s son-in-law shortly before the award.

Even when completely lawful, such contributions predictably create suspicion about whether access and family connections played a role and frankly warrant investigation by Congress (it should not be a Democrats vs Republicans issue). The government should be able to explain the procurement process, the competition involved, and the safeguards used to keep political relationships separate from contracting decisions.

Duffy now wants Congress to provide another $50 billion for air traffic control modernization, which was $20 billion higher than his $30 billion request to Congress the day before the meltdown.

He may be right that much more money is needed. But Friday’s failure also demonstrates why money alone cannot solve this problem. Would another $50 billion have prevented the meltdown on Friday? I don’t think so. And it also merits mentioning that the FAA has already been allocated $12.5 billion and only spent about $1 billion so far…

Modernization itself introduces risk when old and new technology must operate together. That makes careful testing and coordination more important, not less. The issue is not money, but proper testing and transitioning.

Congress should demand a precise accounting of what failed, why maintenance was performed at that time, whether the usual risk assessment was skipped, and what will prevent a recurrence before writing another very large check. Their job is not only the power of the purse, but to provide accountability and careful manage the taxpayer dollars they have been entrusted to steward.

Congress should also insist on transparency surrounding major technology contracts like the one awarded to ASI…

President Trump can blame Biden, and there is no question that the previous administration left behind an air traffic control system badly in need of modernization. But so did Trump 45, and Trump is president now. Sean Duffy runs the Department of Transportation now. The FAA and its contractors performed the work that immediately preceded this failure now. Trump’s criticism rings hollow and Americans are not buying it.

The ASI political contribution does not explain the outage, but does warrant more transparently. More importantly, the administration should explain why a contractor was updating critical telecommunications equipment during the day without the FAA risk assessment the union says was historically performed. It should disclose how major modernization contracts are awarded and insulated from political influence. And perhaps most importantly for this administration, it should stop treating every failure as another opportunity for self-congratulation and partisan blame.

Americans can accept that replacing ancient technology will produce occasional setbacks. What they should not accept is an administration that asks for another $50 billion before candidly explaining how the system it already controls was allowed to fail. And even more fundamentally, Americans showed on November 3rd that they were tried of finger pointing and blame games. May the current administration finally realize that so the next two years are not totally wasted…
