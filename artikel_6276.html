Some fish in Montana live past 130, and their longevity may conceal a problem

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

Blue suckers in Montana’s Upper Missouri River can live to 131, but few young fish appear to be joining the population.

According to new research, blue suckers in Montana’s Upper Missouri River can survive for more than 130 years. Their extraordinary longevity may be concealing trouble: too few young fish are joining the population.

The study was co-led by Alec Lackmann at the University of Minnesota Duluth. Fellow leaders were Luke Holmquist of Montana Fish, Wildlife & Parks and Allen Andrews at the University of Hawai’i at Mānoa.

The blue sucker, Cycleptus elongatus, is native to 23 states. It is listed under various conservation concern categories in 22.

In Montana, biologists measured 1,056 blue suckers between 1996 and 2024. Almost 96% exceeded 65 centimeters in length, and average body size gradually increased.

Small fish were scarce throughout those decades. The adults were still there, but few younger fish seemed to be replacing them.

“For decades, there has been conservation concern for the species across much of its range,” the researchers wrote.

“Nonetheless, no validated age-based life history information exists for the species, which is a major knowledge gap.”

The team examined 42 fish collected in 2023 and 2024, including recovered carcasses. Their estimated ages ranged from eight to 131 years.

Earlier studies had reported maximum ages of 42 and, subsequently, 61 years. The new maximum more than doubles that later estimate.

The answer lay in earstones, or otoliths, inside the fishes’ skulls. These structures accumulate yearly growth bands, which researchers count under a microscope after cutting thin sections.

“Blue suckers have extremely small otoliths, smaller than the buffalofishes, but we figured out how to work with them,” Lackmann said.

After counting those bands, “we were seeing the population had ages up to 130 years old,” he said.

“When age estimates are that much older than previously thought, an additional line of evidence is needed to rigorously test their validity.”

That second check came from an unlikely source: nuclear weapons tests. The radioactive carbon they released left a dated signal in material growing at the time.

“Thermonuclear bombs that were tested in the mid to late 1950s and early 1960s more than doubled the amount of radiocarbon in the atmosphere,” Lackmann said.

“This left a time-specific marker in organisms that lived through the bomb pulse. And if you’re able to quantify growth bands in an individual (for example, in trees, otoliths of bony fish, etc.) that is estimated to have lived through the bomb pulse, you can actually test to determine if the ages you are quantifying are accurate.”

“Bomb radiocarbon dating is one of the best ways to age-validate long-lived organisms. So that’s where we have multiple lines of evidence here, otoliths providing the age estimates, and bomb radiocarbon confirming and validating the otolith ages and extreme longevity of the blue sucker.“

The team checked samples within individual earstones and across different fish. The radiocarbon rise and peak matched the timing predicted from the growth bands.

Half the sampled fish were at least 99 years old. About 64% had hatched before Fort Peck Dam was completed in 1940.

Successful recruitment, meaning young fish surviving to join the population, became less likely after major dams were built.

Evidence of recruitment was stronger in years with higher spring flows. Even at comparable flows, recruitment was less likely after 1954.

Dams can change currents, block migration routes, and alter nursery habitats. The researchers have yet to establish which mechanisms are restricting young blue suckers’ survival.

Old age alone did not appear to prevent reproduction. Tissue examinations found males and females capable of spawning across ages ranging from 19 to 130 years.

That leaves an urgent question: what happens between spawning and the arrival of a new generation?

Such longevity is especially surprising in a species whose adults live year-round in fast-flowing rivers.

Those habitats demand energy, and researchers generally associate them with a faster pace of life.

Blue suckers now join bigmouth buffalo, smallmouth buffalo, and black buffalo as four sucker species known to reach 100. All four discoveries have come since 2019.

Their long lives can carry populations through years of poor breeding conditions. But surviving adults can also make prolonged recruitment problems harder to recognize.

The findings come from a single population near the northern edge of the species’ range. Lifespans in other populations will need their own investigation.

The authors call for research into reconnecting spawning and nursery habitats, and restoring river conditions that help young fish survive.

For Holmquist, the ages also make the transformation of the river unusually tangible.

“To put a timeline on some of those older fish: the oldest one hatched around two years after Montana became a state, around the dawn of the automobile, like 20 years before the Titanic sank – it’s just wild,” Holmquist said.

“We start putting it in the context of what the landscape looked like when these fish hatched, and all the changes that have happened since – and they just keep swimming along in the river.”

The study is published in the journal Communications Biology.
