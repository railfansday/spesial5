CT scans may hold more health clues than doctors were looking for

Tanggal: 2026-11-15

A new AI tool finds clues to existing disease and future health risks in routine CT scans, though it is not yet used in patient care.

A CT scan usually gets ordered to answer one question, such as whether a stubborn cough is pneumonia. The picture it takes doesn’t stop at the lungs, though.

Depending on the area scanned, the images may also capture the heart, liver, kidneys, or bones.

A new artificial intelligence program was trained to read that whole frame.

From scans ordered for all sorts of reasons, it could pick out which patients had an irregular heartbeat, failing kidneys, or a scarred liver.

Some of these conditions are not normally diagnosed from CT scans.

The program also gave a rough forecast of which patients would receive the most new diagnoses afterward.

The program is called Percival, and it was built at Penn Medicine.

Percival remains a research tool and is not yet used in patient care.

Study senior author Walter Witschey is an associate professor of radiology at the University of Pennsylvania.

“Our teams have developed something that learns broadly from imaging data and uncovers connections between scans, lab values, people’s physiology, and clinical outcomes to potentially give more accurate assessments of health, or even ring alarm bells early,” Witschey said.

Percival learned from the Penn Medicine BioBank, a collection of health records from more than 350,000 patients who agreed to let researchers study their data.

The team pulled roughly 400,000 CT scans from about 50,000 of those patients, each paired with the radiologist’s written report.

The program was not taught what each disease looks like. It learned to match each three-dimensional scan to its own report, and to tell that report apart from the reports of other scans.

Training on those scan-report pairs helped it learn features that radiologists describe.

The team then tested Percival on more than 20,000 other BioBank patients, checking its reading of each scan against the diagnoses recorded within 60 days.

The score runs from 0.5, coin-flip guessing, to 1.0, every patient sorted correctly.

Atrial fibrillation, an irregular heartbeat, is not normally diagnosed from a CT scan.

Percival scored 0.84 for it from chest scans alone. It scored 0.91 for a form of heart failure in which the heart still pumps out a normal share of its blood with each beat.

The results suggest that the scans contained clues associated with both conditions. Chronic kidney failure and iron deficiency anemia showed up too.

Percival also did better than a program that outlines each organ and measures its size and density.

On pancreatic cancer it scored 0.91 to that program’s 0.84, and on plaque in the coronary arteries 0.85 to 0.79. In all, it beat every comparison program on 279 conditions in chest scans and 230 in scans of the abdomen and pelvis.

Study co-author Charles Kahn, a professor of radiology at Penn, said that matters because of who gets scanned.

“Because many patients already have substantial heart or metabolic disease when they enter specialty care, routine imaging may provide an opportunity for earlier recognition and intervention,” Kahn said.

The team also asked whether a scan could stand in for a blood draw. For most of the 64 lab values they tried, the answer was no.

On average, Percival’s reading of a scan explained only about 4% of the difference in a value from one patient to the next.

A few values were different. For albumin, a protein made by the liver, the scan reading accounted for 40% of that variation.

For hemoglobin it was 30%, and for a standard measure of how well the kidneys filter blood, about a quarter.

The scan estimates don’t replace a real test, the authors wrote.

What they might do is flag a patient whose blood values are likely to be far outside normal, during a scan that was done for something else.

The patients’ records kept going after their scans, which let the team ask a harder question. Did the scan say anything about diseases a person had not been diagnosed with yet?

The scan did, modestly. Across more than a thousand conditions, Percival ranked patients by how soon they would receive each diagnosis.

Its average score was 0.69 for chest scans and 0.70 for abdominal scans, where 0.5 is chance.

The organ-measuring program scored 0.65 and 0.66, and age and sex alone about 0.61.

So the scan added something that a patient’s age and a tally of organ sizes did not. The forecast was sharper for some conditions, including cirrhosis of the liver and the heart failure type above.

One group identified by the program went on to receive an average of 12.5 new circulatory diagnoses after their scans.

Witschey said the team wants to put that to work.

“We’re hoping to elevate ‘opportunistic screening,’ where results are found beyond the original purpose of a scan, such as finding the early traces of cancer on a scan that was maybe assessing pneumonia.”

Every patient in the study was scanned for a medical reason, and the authors wrote that this leaves a question the data can’t answer.

Someone scanned because their chest hurts is already more likely than average to get a heart diagnosis, so part of the link may come from why the scan was ordered.

The authors were also careful about what Percival has learned. It has picked up image features that tend to go with certain diagnoses, they wrote, not an understanding of the disease.

They also can’t tell how much it learned from the pictures and how much from the reports.

The patients all came from one health system, and no outside data set with linked blood tests and years of follow-up exists yet to repeat the work.

The study did not test whether alerts from routine scans lead to earlier treatment or better health outcomes. To find out, researchers will need to test the program on new scans and follow patients over time.

The team hopes to begin testing it early next year, and has released the model and its code so other hospitals can try it.

The full study was published in the journal npj Digital Medicine.
