Why Israel Is Losing America

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Since its founding in 1922, Foreign Affairs has been the leading forum for serious discussion of American foreign policy and global affairs. The magazine has featured contributions from many leading international affairs experts.

ANDREW P. MILLER is a Senior Fellow in National Security and International Policy at the Center for American Progress. He was Deputy Assistant Secretary of State for Israeli-Palestinian Affairs in the Biden administration and Director for Egypt and Israel Military Issues on the National Security Council in the Obama administration.

Only a Radical Course Change Can Win Back Its Most Important Ally

American politicians have woken up to the fact that the public has soured on Israel. On November 3, as Americans headed to the polls, U.S. President Donald Trump made the surprising announcement that the United States would stop sending aid to Israel, valued at around $4 billion annually, by the end of the year. That night, Democrats, many of whom are increasingly outspoken in their criticism of Israeli actions, swept the House and Senate.

As I wrote in Foreign Affairs a year ago, the “exceptional relationship” between the United States and Israel, in which Israel is treated differently than any other U.S. ally or partner, is coming to an end. No other country receives the tremendous latitude and unconditional backing that the United States extends to Israel. In addition to directly hurting Palestinians, the United States’ support for Israel incentivizes reckless Israeli behavior and dubious strategic thinking. The consequences of the blank check are clear. Over the past three years, tens of thousands of Gazans have been killed, progress toward peace and reconstruction in the Gaza Strip has stalled, the annexation of the West Bank proceeds at an unprecedented rate, and the United States is stuck in an unpopular war in the Middle East with seemingly no end. Israel, for its part, is no safer than on October 6, 2023, and unquestionably more isolated.

Things are about to change. For better or worse, the United States will recalibrate its relationship with Israel over the coming years and a new paradigm for the bilateral relationship will emerge—one in which Israel is expected to comply with U.S. and international law, the two countries refrain from interfering in each other’s domestic politics, and the United States limits its support when Israeli goals conflict with its own.

This change is inevitable, and there is little that Israel and its supporters can do to stop it. But Israel can help keep the United States onside by making itself a reliable partner. As it begins what could be a drawn-out government formation process after last month’s elections, Israel can opt to change direction and plot a course that will not further alienate its closest ally.

Whoever leads the next Israeli government can begin rebuilding trust with the United States before a new U.S. president takes office, in 2029. It won’t be easy. Just as Americans have grown critical of Israel over the past three years, Israelis have shifted further to the right. Israel’s next leaders will need the courage to curb the country’s expansionist policies and resurrect the much-maligned two-state solution. But if they fail to overhaul their approach to Palestinians and the wider region, Israel risks losing its biggest champion and defender—perhaps for good.

Trump, who seems to delight in bullying U.S. allies and partners, has maintained the United States’ long-standing exceptional approach to Israel. He joined Israel in launching strikes on Iran in June 2025 and a larger war in February 2026, embarking for the first time on a truly joint military operation with the Jewish state. Like other presidents before him, Trump has provided Israel with bombs, missiles, and aircraft, largely with no strings attached. He and Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu have also interfered in each other’s political systems to an unprecedented degree by endorsing candidates.

Time and again, Trump has deferred to Israel even when such support has run against American interests. The 2026 war on Iran is the most blatant example. Netanyahu persuaded Trump to give up nuclear talks with Iran and join a war that has failed in its objectives and upended energy markets. Even where the Trump administration has staked out U.S. priorities, it has largely refrained from trying to compel Israel to adhere to them. Israel, for example, has obstructed Trump’s peace plan for Gaza, which is meant to restore governance to the Palestinians, disarm Hamas, and lead to a permanent Israeli withdrawal from the territory. Israel has undermined the plan by expanding its military footprint in Gaza beyond the agreed demarcation lines and denying entry to the National Committee for the Administration of Gaza (NCAG), an interim technocratic body that is intended to govern the territory under Trump’s plan and is currently marooned in Egypt. But the Trump administration has barely sounded a single note of disapproval, reinforcing Netanyahu’s long-standing view that he can defy the United States at little cost.

Moreover, it remains to be seen whether Trump will follow through on his pledge to cut military aid to Israel. The timing of his statement suggests it could have been an electoral stunt, and he said himself that the announcement would deprive the left of a talking point. One possibility is that the United States continues gifting Israel weapons to the tune of billions, just under a different guise. The National Defense Authorization Act for fiscal year 2027, which passed the House this summer and could pass the Senate before Democrats take control of both chambers of Congress this coming January, contains a provision that would expand the Pentagon’s financial and material assistance to Israel.

Trump has made no effort to bring U.S. military assistance or transfers to Israel in line with what it provides to other countries. For decades, the United States has been reluctant to subject arms sales to Israel to U.S. regulations or condition them on Israeli behavior. All recent presidents, for example, have effectively exempted Israel from so-called Leahy laws, which prohibit U.S. assistance to foreign military units credibly implicated in human-rights violations. Despite rampant settler terrorism in the West Bank, in many cases facilitated by the Israeli military, the Trump administration has not indicated that it would declare any Israeli units ineligible to receive U.S. weapons.

Trump has also intervened in Israeli politics more than any other U.S. leader. He has hectored Israeli President Isaac Herzog to pardon Netanyahu so that the prime minister can escape the consequences of an ongoing corruption trial. Ahead of October’s election in Israel, Trump offered Netanyahu a “complete and total endorsement” in his bid for another term. Such blatant interference in Israel’s domestic politics not only undermines the rule of law and integrity of Israeli elections but also serves to normalize Netanyahu’s repeated interventions in U.S. domestic politics. True to form, the Israeli prime minister has praised Trump as “by far the greatest friend Israel has ever had or will have in the White House” just two weeks before the U.S. midterm elections, effectively endorsing Republican candidates for Congress. As in other areas of the relationship, Trump has made what is already an unhealthy dynamic worse.

Unconditional support from the United States has hurt Israel in a crucial way: it indulges the worst instincts of Israeli leaders and does nothing to dissuade them from their embrace of a near-perpetual state of war. After Hamas’s attack three years ago, Israel has bombed Gaza, Iran, Lebanon, Syria, Yemen, and even Qatar. Although Israel has dealt blows to its adversaries, there is no evidence that these wars have made Israel any safer. Iran and its proxies have been weakened, but in the course of fighting, Tehran has discovered the extent of its leverage over the Strait of Hormuz and the global economy and appears even more interested in developing a nuclear weapon. In Lebanon, Hezbollah still poses a threat and is devising new ways of striking Israeli territory. Although confined to a smaller portion of Gaza, Hamas is reconstituting its capabilities and quashing any internal Palestinian challenge to its rule. And in the West Bank, runaway settler violence, the proliferation of illegal Israeli outposts, and the growing threat of terrorism or even a Palestinian uprising have pushed the territory to the breaking point.

Israel has also drifted ever closer to pariah status, isolated internationally thanks to its aggressive tactics. Even the country’s closest historical allies have imposed punitive measures that would have been unthinkable just five years ago. Canada, France, and the United Kingdom, for example, announced wide-ranging sanctions against Israeli settlements in the West Bank in September. Such policies are no longer only the purview of traditional critics of Israel, such as Indonesia and Ireland.

Israel’s reputation within the United States has also tanked. According to Pew polling, 60 percent of Americans have an unfavorable view of Israel, up from 42 percent in 2022. So far, Trump has stopped Congress from restricting support for Israel—both through threatening vetoes and applying pressure on congressional Republicans—but he cannot hold back the tide of American public opinion indefinitely. Once it breaks, Israelis will have to face more than criticism from its closest ally and security guarantor. With Democrats soon to be in control of both the House and the Senate, Congress will likely pass bills that sanction settlement building and condition military aid on adherence to U.S. and international law.

Without changes in U.S. and Israeli policy, the two countries could see their relationship rupture as continued support for Israel becomes politically untenable in the United States. To rehabilitate itself in the eyes of Americans—and therefore ensure long-term cooperation with the United States—Israel will need to change course. It must abandon Netanyahu’s bludgeoning approach to both the Palestinians and the wider region.

It is no exaggeration to say that the future of the U.S.-Israeli relationship rests on what type of government Israel forms in the next few months. Netanyahu’s exclusion from the next government represents the best hope for preserving bilateral relations with the United States. Netanyahu has proved unwilling to stay out of U.S. politics or modify draconian security policies that have run afoul of the American public. Unfortunately, most of Netanyahu’s rivals for prime minister share his hawkish position on the Palestinians and the Middle East. Were they in power, they seem likely to resist fundamental revisions to Israeli policy that could safeguard the bilateral relationship.

Israel’s next leaders need to journey outside their comfort zone. They will have to come to terms with new realities: that tensions between the United States and Israel are now deep-rooted and that they can’t rely on the adamantine support of the Republican party any longer. To win back Americans, the Israeli government will have to rigorously follow through on its commitments under Trump’s plan for Gaza, daring Hamas to follow suit. In addition to flooding Gaza with humanitarian aid, Israel should let the NCAG enter the territory to offer an alternative to Hamas’s rule and nihilistic agenda. Enabling the deployment of newly trained Palestinian security forces and the formation of the International Stabilization Force, a peacekeeping mission, would also help show that, whatever unfolds in Gaza, Israel is operating in good faith. Israeli forces should immediately return to the demarcation lines traced in Trump’s Gaza peace plan and pull back even further in concert with Hamas’s progress in fulfilling the terms of the plan until Israel has completely withdrawn from Gaza.

Israel must also rein in violent settlers in the West Bank and hold them accountable through the courts. The Israeli security services can identify the leaders and primary instigators behind the violence and should arrest them and bring them to justice. Given that settlers have killed and injured American citizens in these attacks, continued inaction by Israeli authorities is a recipe for more friction with the United States, especially if a Democratic government comes to power in Washington.

Israel must also halt its creeping annexation of the West Bank. Over the past three years, the Netanyahu government has increased its military presence in the West Bank, deprived the Palestinian Authority of funds, asserted Israeli government control over larger swaths of Palestinian land, approved a slew of new Israeli settlements, and retroactively legalized illicit outposts. In sum, these actions aim to render a Palestinian state impossible. Although reversing these measures would not arrest the expansion of existing settlements and Israeli infrastructure throughout the West Bank, they would be received positively in the United States.

Israeli leaders also need to break the taboo around Palestinian statehood. Only a couple of decades ago, the idea of a Palestinian state was conceivable to most Israelis. Today, Israeli politicians, with precious few exceptions, cannot bring themselves to endorse a two-state solution. It will take time and no small degree of political resolve, but Israeli leaders must convince the public to accept the necessity and the virtues of a Palestinian state existing side by side with Israel. An Israel that can view Palestinians as neighbors entitled to the same rights would provide a very different image to the world than a messianic government bent on expelling whole populations.

These steps will not satisfy all American critics of Israel. Anti-Israeli attitudes cannot be erased in a matter of months. Where anti-Israeli opinion intersects with anti-Semitism—evident in incidents such as a recent boycott of Jewish-owned restaurants in Philadelphia organized by the group Philly Bike on Your Left—the task is even more difficult and will necessitate a different set of policies that transcend Israel, including the formation of diverse domestic and international coalitions to identify and condemn anti-Semitic acts. Yet a far less belligerent Israel and one newly committed to the resolution of the unanswered question of Palestinian self-determination would start to shift U.S. public opinion back in the country’s favor.

If Israel fails to change course, it will find itself less secure and more isolated internationally. Without access to international markets and capital, its economy, which has withstood the turmoil of the last several years remarkably well, will suffer and living standards will fall. Under these circumstances, Jewish Israeli citizens with second passports may elect to leave, depriving the state of its raison d’être—providing a safe and prosperous homeland for the Jewish people. Put simply, an Israel without American support will struggle to survive.
