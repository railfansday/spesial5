Martin Hall elected to World Golf Teachers Hall of Fame

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

WEST PALM BEACH — Not once, while Martin Hall was growing up in England chasing the dream of playing – and then teaching – golf for a living did he ever consider his skills would someday have him inducted into a major Hall of Fame.

But when Hall recently was informed by Palm Beach Gardens' Kellie Stenzel he'd be inducted into the World Golf Teachers Hall of Fame, his reaction said it all.

"I burst into tears," the 71-year-old Hall said, "and I'm not a crier."

It wasn't just that he was inducted into one of the sport's most important Hall of Fames; it was he was entering the same shrine as men he looked up to and helped mentor him along the way. Without their assistance, he would not have spent the last 30 years as the Director of Instruction at The Club at Ibis in West Palm Beach; he would not have appeared on more than 430 shows of Golf Channel's School of Golf" – becoming the most-watched instructor of all time - and he would not be rubbing shoulders with superstars such as Rory McIlroy, Jack Nicklaus and Shane Lowry.

"I'm very honored to be on that list because of the other people who are on it, like John Jacobs, Jim Flick, Henry Cotton. Harvey Penick and Bob Toski," said Hall, who was inducted in early November during a ceremony at La Costa Resort in California.

"I would consider them to be legends of the golf teaching industry. I'm not sure I should be on that list, but some people think I must have been at the same level. It truly is a great honor to be on that list."

And The Club at Ibis officials are thrilled to have Hall teaching their members for three decades, offering lessons with his trademark set of props, teaching aids and dry, English wit.

"I have had the privilege of knowing Martin for more than 30 years and I value him not only as one of the finest golf professionals in the industry, but as a trusted friend," said Robin Boretti, The Club at Ibis' Director of Golf. "When it comes to the game of golf, Martin has unquestionably given back far more than he has ever taken.

"What has always impressed me most about Martin is that despite his accomplishments, recognition, and worldwide influence, he has never lost sight of what matters most: the people he teaches. His ability to connect with golfers, build confidence, and make the game enjoyable has changed countless lives. The game of golf is better because of the impact Martin Hall has made on it."

Hall's journey to becoming the sport's most-watched instructor in golf wasn't a straight line. He grew up in England within 40 miles of future World Golf Hall of Famers Sandy Lyle and Ian Woosnam, so Hall was exposed to top-level golf at an early age. He qualified for the European Tour and played two years.

His playing career came to an end when he missed a 4-foot putt at Q-school, but Hall now says it's the best thing that could have happened to him. It forced him to become an instructor.

In the early days, Hall said he'd wait until Golf Magazine or Golf Digest came in the mail. "Whatever their cover story was, that's what I taught for the next month," Hall said. "I had no idea what I was doing when I started, other than I was enthusiastic and patient."

An issue of Golf Digest brought an unexpected opportunity when Hall spotted a small ad that said Toski, Flick and Peter Kostis were doing a golf school in nearby Scotland. Hall finagled an invitation, which led to him being offered a job at St. Andrews Country Club – the one in Boca Raton – which brought him to America in 1982.

He worked for the Jack Nicklaus/Jim Flick schools for a year, then St. Andrews and Ibis. Hall's big break, fittingly, came at Golf Channel in 2010. The all-golf network held a talent contest to be the host instructor on "School of Golf." Hall was chosen among thousands of entries.

Hall made himself a promise: "I was going to be the same on TV that I am if you come to see me at Ibis," he said. "I was the person who talked 'funny,' but the English accent didn't hurt me."

Neither did working at a golf-centric place such as The Club at Ibis. The only golf club in the world with three Nicklaus family-designed courses allowed Hall's career to blossom.

"Ibis gave me the freedom to chase things I wanted to do," he said. "They allowed Martin to be Martin by understanding my many eccentricities and habits."

Major champion Morgan Pressel started working with Hall when she was in single digits – age, not handicap – and her family were members at St. Andrews. How close is their friendship? Pressel showed up at Ibis on a rainy Tuesday to watch Hall honored by the club

"I realized he was one of the very best, and I'm so grateful to have worked with him," said Pressel, who has raised more than $15 million for the foundation she started in her mother Kathy's honor (she died of breast cancer). "Martin worked really hard to try and make me a better player. Just grateful for our years of friendship.

"There's no question I would have never been where I was without Martin. His creativity and commitment meant a lot to my game. He really cares about the people he teaches."

Hall isn't ready to walk away from a job that never felt like one. He'll continue to teach at Ibis for four days a week as long as he can, with the support of his wife Lisa, who played in two Solheim Cups and remains the better player in the family.

"Why am I going to quit when I enjoy it too much?" Hall said. "It's a wonderful thing when your hobby is what you do for a living. I just try to get better every day."
