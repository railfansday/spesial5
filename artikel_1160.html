CRISPR-associated transposases (CASTs) achieve site-specific DNA integration by combining the RNA-guided targeting action of a nuclease-deficient CRISPR–Cas system with the assembly of a Tn7-like transpososome complex1,2. Understanding the detailed mechanisms of this elaborate process is paramount to engineering CAST systems into programmable genetic tools3,4,5,6. Type I-F Pseudoalteromonas CAST (PseCAST) shows the highest activity in mammalian cells so far7 and has been adapted extensively through directed evolution8. However, its continued rational engineering requires addressing critical gaps in our understanding of transpososome assembly and activation9. Here we perform structural analyses using cryogenic electron microscopy, validated by DNA transposition assays, to visualize the PseCAST system in a series of functional states that define the stepwise mechanism of RNA-guided DNA integration. The structures of the PseCAST targeting and transposase recruitment complexes revealed that conformational changes in the Cascade–TniQ module, induced by R-loop formation, are associated with target-DNA stabilization and TnsC heptamerization. This, in turn, results in the recruitment of the TnsAB transposase by conserved interactions with the carboxy-terminal tail of TnsB. Finally, the structure of the 1.2 MDa PseCAST transpososome holocomplex revealed allosteric coupling of TnsC–TnsB and TnsB–target-DNA interactions to the conformational remodelling of the TnsB catalytic site, activating donor-DNA integration. Together, these findings establish a unified mechanistic blueprint for RNA-guided DNA integration by type I CASTs and provide a structural framework to guide their engineering for genome-editing applications.

Transposons are pervasive DNA segments capable of mobilizing within and between genomes, thus serving as potent drivers of genome evolution and horizontal gene transfer10,11,12. CASTs have evolved repeatedly by exaptation of minimal, nuclease-deficient CRISPR–Cas systems to enable RNA-guided, site-specific DNA integration13,14,15. These systems operate through the concerted action of a CRISPR-based targeting module and a Tn7-like transposition machinery comprising the TnsC AAA+ ATPase, the TnsB transposase and, in some systems, the TnsA endonuclease. The CASTs characterized so far differ mainly in the organization of their DNA-targeting and transposition modules1,2,16,17,18. Type I variants typically use a multi-subunit Cascade–TniQ (QCascade) complex for target recognition guided by CRISPR RNA (crRNA) and TnsA–TnsB for cut-and-paste transposition19,20,21. By contrast, type V-K systems rely on the Cas12k–TniQ–S15 complex for crRNA-guided targeting5 and use only TnsB for copy-and-paste transposition, resulting in cointegrate products22. TnsC plays a pivotal part in all CASTs, acting as an adapter that bridges the targeting module and the transposase enzymes to define the insertion site23,24.

The inherent programmability of CASTs has driven their development as genome-editing tools that enable the site-specific insertion of multi-kilobase DNA cargoes without generating double-stranded breaks in the target DNA. CAST-based editing has been implemented in bacteria25,26,27,28 and mammalian cells7,8,29,30, offering considerable potential for research and therapeutic applications. Type V-K CASTs generally show low integration efficiencies in human cell lines29,30, limited specificity2,26,27,31 and heterogeneous insertion profiles22,30. By contrast, type I-F systems can perform precise, simple insertions with substantially higher efficiencies1,7,22,26. In particular, the type I-F system of the Pseudoalteromonas Tn7016 transposon (Fig. 1a) has emerged as a highly promising candidate for genome-engineering applications, because of its uniquely high integration activity in human cells7,9. Directed evolution and structure-guided engineering of PseCAST components in Escherichia coli have resulted in the evoCAST system. This system has markedly improved integration efficiencies, reaching up to 10–30% in human cells, yet maintains precise, cointegrate-free insertion outcomes8. Although structural studies of type V-K CAST systems have provided mechanistic insights into various stages of transpososome assembly5,6,32,33,34,35,36, structural information about type I CASTs is currently limited to sub-complexes9,19,20,21,37,38,39,40,41,42. This presents a knowledge gap in the mechanistic understanding of the RNA-guided DNA-integration pathway. Specifically, the mechanism by which the RNA-guided targeting module induces the assembly and catalytic activation of the transposase components remains unclear.

Here we report a series of structural snapshots of the PseCAST system obtained using cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM), including a previously unresolved transpososome holocomplex assembly comprising all molecular components. These snapshots reveal the molecular determinants and critical checkpoints at key stages of the RNA-guided DNA-integration pathway, including target DNA recognition, TnsC oligomerization, transposase recruitment and catalytic activation. Our findings define the molecular mechanisms that govern RNA-guided DNA integration by type I CASTs, thereby establishing a structural foundation for the further development of these systems as genome-engineering tools.

TnsC oligomers function as bridging modules for the assembly of the transposition machinery in both type V-K and type I-B CASTs, interacting with the RNA-guided CRISPR module through their amino-terminal face and with the transposase through their carboxy-terminal face6,20,36,41. To elucidate the structural basis of the guide-RNA-dependent assembly of TnsC in the type I-F PseCAST, our initial cryo-EM analysis yielded a reconstruction of a target-DNA-bound complex comprising PseCascade–TniQ and a heptameric TnsC ring (hereafter the targeting complex) at an overall resolution of 3.1 Å (Fig. 1b–d, Extended Data Table 1 and Extended Data Fig. 1a–c). Resembling the architecture of TnsC heptamers from the canonical Tn7 element of E. coli and other type I CAST systems20,40,42,43 (Extended Data Fig. 2), the TnsC heptamer has a near-perfect seven-fold symmetry without a helical rise or seam, fully enclosing the DNA duplex in a positively charged central channel, in which all seven TnsC ATPase sites are occupied by ATP molecules and Mg2+ ions. The enclosed DNA adopts B-form geometry and is contacted by the Glu105–Asp120 loops of TnsC.1 and TnsC.5 through nonspecific interactions.

Notably, the structure of the targeting complex includes a complete 32-base-pair R-loop (Fig. 1e), which was only partially formed in the previously reported intermediate PseQCascade complex structure9. In contrast to the open arrangement of the partial R-loop intermediate9, the Cas8 α-helical bundle (residues 272–421) undergoes an approximately 90° rotation into a closed or locked state (Extended Data Fig. 3a), establishing additional contacts with the protospacer-adjacent motif (PAM)-distal segment of the target DNA, both TniQ subunits and Cas7.5 (Extended Data Fig. 3b–d). Moreover, three nucleotides of the displaced non-target strand (NTGS) are bound in a pocket in the Cas8 bundle (Extended Data Fig. 3e), which likely facilitates reannealing of the NTGS with the target strand (TGS) in the PAM-distal DNA segment and its interactions with the TniQ dimer. This feature is absent from the partial R-loop and open helical-bundle states of type I-F CAST QCascade complexes published previously19,21. As the presence of a full R-loop and a closed Cas8 helical-bundle conformation have been suggested to be a prerequisite for stable target engagement21, our structural observations indicate that the formation of the locked state serves as a target fidelity checkpoint upstream of transposase recruitment.

Unexpectedly, the structure of the PseCAST targeting complex revealed that the TnsC heptamer interacts with both TniQ.1 and Cas8 through extensive interfaces spanning 947 Å2 and 818 Å2, respectively (Fig. 1d and Extended Data Fig. 3f), forming a ‘super-ring’ assembly that bridges the PAM-proximal and PAM-distal ends of the QCascade subcomplex. At the N-terminal face of the heptamer, TnsC protomers use the same surfaces for interactions with both TniQ and Cas8 (Extended Data Fig. 3f). At the PAM-distal end, the N-terminal region of TniQ.1 is wedged into the TnsC.1–TnsC.7 inter-subunit interface. An aromatic pocket in TniQ.1 comprising Phe3, Phe5, Phe29 and Phe30 interacts with a hydrophobic loop in TnsC.1 containing Leu101, Leu104, Leu106, Phe107 and Ile112 (Fig. 1f). TnsC.1 further engages TniQ.1 in a series of ionic interactions (Fig. 1g) and bridging interactions that also contact the TnsC.1–TnsC.7 interface (Fig. 1h). Individual mutations in the inserted TnsC loop (L106S or F107A) did not affect the transposition activity of PseCAST in E. coli. By contrast, a strong synergistic effect in the TnsC double mutant L106A, F107A led to a near-complete loss of transposition activity (Fig. 1i). Whereas alanine substitutions in pairs of residues in the TniQ hydrophobic pocket (either F3A and F5A, or F29A and F30A) had a mild effect on transposition efficiency (Fig. 1i), charge-reversal substitutions at these positions (either F3E and F5E, or F29E and F30E) had a substantially stronger effect (Extended Data Fig. 3g). Deletion of the seven N-terminal TniQ residues resulted in the loss of transposition activity (Fig. 1i). Moreover, cross-subunit mutant combinations involving residues in the TnsC loop and the TniQ hydrophobic pocket, either TnsC(L106S) and TniQ(F3A, F5A), or TnsC(F107A) and TniQ(F29A, F30A), resulted in a marked decrease in or the complete loss of transposition activity in vivo (Fig. 1i), confirming the importance of the TniQ–TnsC interactions for the assembly of the PseCAST transpososome.

At the PAM-proximal end, Cas8 and TnsC.5 form a shape-complementary interface, mediated by aromatic and electrostatic interactions (Fig. 1j–l). The combination of TnsC mutations R68A, Y75A, R77A and Pheresulted in a pronounced reduction in transposition activity in vivo (Fig. 1m), indicating that the Cas8–TnsC interaction contributes to transpososome assembly. However, mutations of interacting residues in Cas8, alone or in cross-subunit combinations with TnsC, had a mild or negligible effect (Fig. 1m), suggesting that the sensitivity of the TnsC–Cas8 interface to mutational perturbations is lower than that of the TnsC–TniQ interface. Similarly, whereas the individual charge-reversal TnsC mutant Y75E alone was sufficient to decrease transposition activity substantially, single charge-reversal mutations in Cas8 (T486R, W465E or H469E) had variable effects on transposition efficiency (Extended Data Fig. 3g). Together, these structural observations define the molecular coupling between the CRISPR targeting machinery and TnsC that results in the assembly of an ATP-loaded TnsC heptamer on the target DNA.

In the canonical Tn7 element in E. coli44, as well as in the type V-K CAST system of Scytonema hofmanni (ShCAST) and the type I-B CAST system of Peltigera membranacea cyanobiont 210A (PmcCAST), transposase recruitment is mediated by TnsC interactions with the C-terminal region of TnsB6,23,41. In the structure of the paired-end complex (PEC) of PseTnsAB, the C-terminal tail of PseTnsB (residues 574–613) is disordered45. To assess whether the unstructured tail plays a similar part in interacting with PseTnsC, we performed co-precipitation experiments using affinity-tagged PseTnsB constructs comprising either the full-length protein or the standalone C-terminal tail (TnsB(575–613)) (Fig. 2a). Both full-length TnsB and TnsB(575–613) co-precipitated target-DNA-bound QCascade and TnsC efficiently in the presence of ATP (Fig. 2b), indicating that TnsB interacts directly with the targeting complex and that its C-terminal tail is sufficient for this interaction. The amount of co-precipitated TnsC remained unchanged in the presence of the non-hydrolysable ATP analogue ATPγS (Extended Data Fig. 4a), indicating that, similar to the type I-B PmcCAST system41,42, TnsB does not stimulate ATPase-dependent depolymerization of TnsC in PseCAST. Furthermore, co-precipitation of TnsC by TnsB(575–613) decreased strongly in the absence of ATP, suggesting that the interaction requires ATP-dependent TnsC oligomerization (Extended Data Fig. 4b). This result was corroborated by the loss of TnsC co-precipitation in the absence of QCascade or after introducing mutations in the TniQ- and Cas8-interacting interfaces (Extended Data Fig. 4c). Together, these results indicate that TnsB recruitment requires QCascade-dependent assembly of TnsC on the target DNA.

To validate these findings structurally, we aimed to reconstitute and determine the structure of QCascade–TnsC in complex with TnsAB bound to the excised transposon ends. To this end, we performed affinity co-precipitation using a recombinant TnsAB fusion protein construct (Fig. 2a), pre-assembled with a segment of the right end of the transposon (RE), to capture the QCascade–TnsC complex assembled on the target substrate comprising a linear double-stranded DNA (Extended Data Fig. 4d). Single-particle cryo-EM analysis of the resulting sample yielded a 2.8-Å reconstruction of a PseCAST transposase recruitment complex comprising target-bound QCascade and heptamerized TnsC decorated with TnsB C-terminal segments (or TnsB hooks; residues Trp605–Glu613). The TnsB hooks are bound to all TnsC subunits at the junction between the N-terminal α/β core domain and the C-terminal α-helical bundle close to the ATP binding pocket (Fig. 2c,d, Extended Data Table 2 and Extended Data Fig. 5a–c), similar to the arrangement described for type V-K34,36 and I-B41,42 CASTs (Extended Data Fig. 6). They are anchored by hydrophobic interactions of TnsB residues Trp605, Leu609 and Phe612 and an additional hydrogen bond between the backbone carbonyl of residue Asp606 of TnsB and Arg212 of TnsC (Fig. 2e). Corroborating these observations, TnsB mutation W605A caused a substantial decrease in the transposition activity of PseCAST in E. coli. Moreover, individual TnsB mutations L609K or F612A, as well as the deletion of the entire TnsB hook (Δ604–613) resulted in a complete loss of transposition activity (Fig. 2f). These findings highlight the functional requirement of the interactions between the TnsB hook and TnsC for the transposition activity of PseCAST and demonstrate the critical role of the TnsB C-terminal tail in the recruitment of the TnsAB transposase and for the delivery of the transposon DNA to the target site before integration.

To determine the structure of the PseCAST transpososome holocomplex comprising Cascade, TniQ, TnsC and TnsAB, we used a modified ‘one pot’ affinity co-precipitation strategy to trap the transpososome in a post-integration strand-transfer complex (STC) state (Fig. 3a and Extended Data Fig. 7), which has been used previously to visualize transpososome structures because of its stability6,34,36,41,46,47. The assembly used a minimal symmetrized RE donor sequence, which supports transposition in related systems48,49 and is sufficient for the formation of a paired-end PseTnsAB complex45. Cryo-EM analysis of the resulting sample, supported by three-dimensional variability analysis combined with heterogeneous and locally focused refinement, yielded a reconstruction with a global resolution of 3.1 Å (Fig. 3b, Extended Data Table 3 and Extended Data Fig. 8a–d). The reconstruction revealed that the transposase adopts an intertwined, pseudo-twofold arrangement with a TnsA2–TnsB4 stoichiometry to assemble on the structured DNA in an STC configuration (Fig. 3c,d). All seven TnsC subunits are bound to the C-terminal hook motifs of TnsAB (Fig. 3b,c); it is not possible to ascertain which of these are connected to the bound TnsAB or contributed by unbound TnsAB molecules in trans, as the connecting segments (residues 824–843) of TnsAB are unstructured. The target DNA exits from the central channel of the TnsC heptamer and extends towards the TnsAB tetramer assembled on the pseudo-palindromic target site duplication (TSD) region and the adjacent transposon end sequences (Fig. 3a). As in the structure of the TnsAB PEC45, each transposon end is occupied by two TnsB protomers: a proximal catalytic subunit (TnsB.1 or TnsB.2)—the catalytic domain of which engages the other transposon end in trans—and a distal structural subunit (TnsB.3 or TnsB.4) (Fig. 3c). Only two TnsA subunits (TnsA.1 and TnsA.2), bound to the catalytic TnsB protomers, are resolved in the structure. The corresponding interaction interfaces in the structural TnsB subunits are occupied by the bound RE DNA duplexes, precluding TnsA interactions.

The structure of the PseCAST holocomplex reveals the molecular interactions responsible for the TnsC-dependent recruitment of TnsAB to the target DNA to effect site-specific donor integration. The TnsB.1 subunit is tethered to the C-terminal face of the TnsC heptamer by its β-barrel domain (residues 434–500), positioned at the interface of the TnsC.1 and TnsC.7 subunits (Fig. 3e). TnsC.1 inserts its C-terminal loop—spanning residues 314–322 and including Leu318—into a hydrophobic pocket formed by Val472, Gln467 and Leu441 in the TnsB.1 β-barrel domain (Fig. 3e). In turn, Asn316 of TnsC, situated in the same loop in TnsC.7, interacts through hydrogen bonds with Arg443 and Asn444 in TnsB.1 (Fig. 3e). Aspartate substitutions of Leu318 in TnsC (L318D) or Val472 in TnsB (V472D) resulted in a pronounced reduction in transposition activity in vivo (Fig. 3f). The single TnsC mutation N316A or replacement of TnsC loop residues 316–321 with a (Gly–Ser)3 linker resulted in modestly impaired activity. By contrast, the single TnsB mutations R443E or N444D strongly reduced transposition activity (Fig. 3f), confirming that the formation of this interface is essential for PseCAST-mediated DNA transposition.

In addition to interactions with TnsC, the β-barrel domain of the catalytic TnsB.1 subunit also interacts with the backbone of the target DNA segment downstream of the TnsC heptamer, forming a network of hydrogen bonds and electrostatic contacts using Arg439, Thr440, Lys447 and Gln449 of TnsB (Fig. 3g). Furthermore, Lys520 and Arg524 in the C-terminal helical domain of TnsB also contact the target DNA backbone. Notably, this domain adopts a helical bundle fold that bridges the catalytic and β-barrel domains in the catalytic TnsB.1 and TnsB.2 subunits. By contrast, the helical domain of the structural TnsB.3 and TnsB.4 subunits forms two stretched helices that are sandwiched between the catalytic domain and DNA-binding domain 2 of TnsAB.1 and TnsAB.2, contributing to intra-subunit contacts.

Together, these insights highlight the functional role of the TnsB β-barrel domain in transpososome assembly. The shape complementarity of the TnsC and TnsB β-barrel interaction surfaces, together with cooperative interactions with the target DNA, provide the structural basis for the molecular ruler mechanism that defines the integration site, enabling the transposon to be inserted at a fixed distance of 49 nucleotides from the crRNA-specified target site. Moreover, the synergistic contacts with two TnsC subunits and the target DNA underlie a checkpoint mechanism that senses target DNA-dependent TnsC oligomerization, thereby explaining the strictly sequential assembly of the transpososome observed in type I-F systems40.

The structure of the PseCAST transpososome holocomplex captures the TnsAB STC tetramer in a post-integration state (Fig. 4a,b). As in the PEC structure45, the active sites of the TnsA.1 and TnsA.2 subunits interact with the 5′ termini of the transposon ends, adopting a catalytically competent, post-cleavage geometry in which the catalytic triad residues Glu34, Asp71 and Lys87 of TnsA coordinate the 5′-terminal phosphate group and a single Mg2+ ion (Fig. 4c). In contrast to the PEC structure, the catalytic sites of the TnsB.1 and TnsB.2 subunits also assume an activated conformation in which a Mg2+ ion—coordinated by the catalytic triad residues Asp220, Asp304 and Glu340 of TnsB—interacts with the 3′-hydroxyl group of the terminal nucleotide (dG76 in the NTGS) of the target DNA and the phosphodiester group that links the TSD segment to the 3′-terminal nucleotide of the transposon end (Fig. 4d). Furthermore, the catalytic domain establishes contacts with RE and TSD nucleotides that are crucial for STC formation (Fig. 4d). Specifically, Asp225 of TnsB positions an additional Mg2+ ion such that it simultaneously coordinates the phosphate groups of dA(−82) and dG(−80), stabilizing a kink in the DNA backbone at the TSD–RE junction that enables STC formation. The backbone is additionally contacted by Thr222, Arg239 and Ser360 of TnsB (Fig. 4d). Consistent with these observations, alanine substitution of Asp225 (D225A) or a charge-reversal mutation of Arg239 of TnsB (R239E) resulted in a complete loss of PseCAST transposition activity in E. coli, whereas TnsB mutations T222A or S360A only reduced PseCAST activity (Fig. 4e).

Finally, the PseCAST transpososome holocomplex structure highlights a potential allosteric relay mechanism that underlies the target-DNA-dependent catalytic activation of TnsB. In the PEC structure, the β-barrel and C-terminal helical domains of the catalytic TnsB subunits are disordered45. By contrast, both domains are structured in the STC state and interface with the catalytic domain (Fig. 4b) through a network of hydrogen bonds and ionic interactions (Fig. 4f). This is coupled with local structural remodelling of the catalytic domain, resulting in the ordering of an adjacent loop (residues Glu348–Lys369 of TnsB) (Fig. 4g) and an approximately 11-Å concomitant shift of the helix bearing the catalytic Glu340 residue of TnsB (Fig. 4g). The conformational rearrangement marks the transition of the catalytic site from an inactive, post-excision state to an active, strand-transfer configuration that supports Mg2+ coordination and catalytic activity. Consistent with these observations, combined alanine substitutions of Lys567, Gln571 and Asn577 in the C-terminal helical domain of TnsB strongly reduced the transposition activity of PseCAST in E. coli (Fig. 4i). Additional ordering was observed for the N-terminal segment (residues Met1–Pro11) of the TnsA helical domain, which positions the Arg4 residue of TnsA such that it contacts the 5′-terminal phosphate of the cleaved transposon end (Fig. 4c,h), implicating TnsA in TnsB activation. Accordingly, truncation of the TnsA N-terminal segment (ΔMet1–Asn12) resulted in a complete loss of transposition activity (Fig. 4i). Together, the structural observations and their functional validation indicate that the molecular interfaces formed between the C-terminal, β-barrel and catalytic domains of TnsB after binding to the target DNA and TnsC are required to support the integration activity of PseCAST. Both target DNA and TnsC synergistically couple transposase recruitment with domain ordering and catalytic-site remodelling, resulting in the allosteric activation of TnsB for site-specific DNA integration.

Our structural and functional analyses of the PseCAST system comprehensively revealed the molecular mechanism that underlies RNA-guided transposon integration by type I-F CASTs, in which an orchestrated series of interactions and conformational rearrangements couples target recognition with site-specific DNA insertion. Together, these insights support a mechanistic model in which DNA transposition is controlled by a succession of molecular checkpoints (Fig. 5). The formation of the QCascade–TnsC targeting complex requires R-loop completion, enabled by a conformational rearrangement of the Cas8 subunit, including the approximately 90° rotation of the helical bundle domain into a locked conformation. In contrast to the open conformations reported previously for type I-F CAST QCascade complexes9,19,21,37, this locked state positions the PAM-distal target-DNA duplex for binding to TniQ, which facilitates ATP-dependent TnsC assembly on the target DNA by interacting with Cas8 and TniQ. As the locked Cas8 conformation is achieved only with fully matched targets, it acts as a fidelity checkpoint that discriminates against mismatched sites, underpinning the high target specificity of PseCAST. Instead of forming extended filaments as in type V CASTs32,33, PseTnsC assembles into a symmetric heptamer to fully encircle the DNA duplex exiting QCascade. The transposon insertion site is thus defined by the physical footprint and register of the TnsC ring, rather than by TnsB-dependent TnsC depolymerization6,32,33. The initial recruitment of the TnsAB transposase PEC is mediated by the TnsB C-terminal tail hook motifs. As these interactions are dependent on TnsC heptamerization, this step provides another fidelity checkpoint that makes transposition dependent on the correct recognition of the DNA target. Finally, productive target engagement by TnsAB requires conformational ordering of the TnsB β-barrel domain and C-terminal helices, driven by interactions of the TnsB β-barrel domain with the target DNA and the TnsC heptamer. This is coupled with structural remodelling of the TnsB active site into a catalytically competent configuration. Therefore, the TnsC- and target-DNA-dependent catalytic activation of TnsB acts as a final fidelity checkpoint, ensuring that transposon DNA insertion proceeds only once the transposase is correctly anchored to the target DNA. Together, our findings reveal the structural coupling of target DNA engagement and transposase activation, but the precise role of allosteric interactions in the observed conformational rearrangements requires further investigation. We speculate that similar structural coupling is involved in activating TnsAB for transposon excision after interacting with the flanking segments of the donor DNA.

The Cascade–TniQ complex is guided to the target DNA by base-pairing with its crRNA, leading to the initial formation of a partial R-loop. Coordination of the displaced NTGS by the Cas8 helical bundle is coupled with the transition of the bundle from open to closed, full R-loop formation and the stabilization of the double-stranded PAM-distal target-DNA duplex by the TniQ dimer. TnsC is recruited to the target site, where it assembles into a closed heptameric ring by binding to the PAM-distal target-DNA duplex in the presence of ATP, TniQ and Cas8. At the donor site, excision of the transposon DNA by TnsAB involves the formation of a PEC. The PEC is initially recruited by the TnsC heptamer by interacting with the C-terminal TnsB hooks. Interactions with the target DNA and TnsC result in structural remodelling of the β-barrel and C-terminal helical domains of the interfacing TnsB copies, which is allosterically coupled with a conformational rearrangement of the catalytic domains into an active conformation (the asterisks indicate active TnsB sites). The transposon DNA is integrated by two transesterification reactions catalysed by the active sites of the interfacing TnsBs, covalently linking both transposon 3′ ends to the target DNA with 5-base-pair spacing that results in a target-site duplication (TSD).

Directed evolution and structure-guided engineering are powerful complementary strategies for improving CAST activity8,9, recalling previous studies in which structural insights have enabled the rational design of hyperactive variants of the Sleeping Beauty transposase50,51. To provide a structural context for current engineering efforts, we mapped the activity-enhancing substitutions of evoCAST onto the PseCAST holocomplex structure (Extended Data Fig. 9). Two of these, F43S in TnsB and P88T in TnsA, have already been interpreted structurally in the PseTnsAB PEC as potentially facilitating transposon end recognition and stabilizing the cleaved NTGS overhang, respectively. Several of the remaining substitutions in TnsB, which were mostly unresolved in the PEC, can now be interpreted. Y349N, P352T and D396N are found close to the target-DNA substrate, potentially enhancing its binding. Q410K, V526E and Q549R are predominantly surface-facing and thus likely to enhance protein solubility or stability. Q594L, although unresolved in the holocomplex structure, may provide additional hydrophobic interactions to the interface formed between the C-terminal hook and TnsC, which is essential for transposase recruitment. Finally, H464R may promote the interaction between the TnsB β-barrel domain and TnsC through enhanced electrostatic contacts. The substitutions R197I and N314K in TnsC map to the TnsC oligomerization interface and the TnsC–TnsB interface, respectively. Lastly, in addition to the PAM-enhancing mutations N125D and A244N in Cas8, the A410R substitution in the helical bundle likely contacts the PAM-distal target-DNA duplex after R-loop completion (Fig. 5), potentially stabilizing the locked QCascade conformation and promoting TnsC assembly. Together, these insights suggest that evoCAST activity results from the synergistic optimization of DNA binding, intersubunit contacts, folding and solubility. Therefore, future engineering efforts should continue to use both structure-guided and directed-evolution approaches.

In conclusion, the structural findings presented here reveal the molecular mechanisms underpinning RNA-guided transposition by type I CASTs, providing a framework for their further rational engineering for technological applications.

An initial model of the TnsC heptamer bound to seven TnsB(575–613) copies was generated using AlphaFold3 and docked manually. The model placed the last nine TnsB C-terminal residues at the additional densities of each TnsC subunit. The recognizable density of the bulky TnsB residues Trp605 and Phe612 aided in fitting the TnsB hook in the density. Refinement statistics are included in Extended Data Table 2. Structure and map figures were prepared using UCSF ChimeraX (v.1.9) and Adobe Illustrator (v.25.4.1). The local model-to-map fit for all residues shown for the Cascade–TniQ–TnsC–TnsB hook complex in Figs. 1–5 is presented in Supplementary Fig. 1g–m.

The cryo-EM sample of the Cascade–TniQ–TnsC–TnsAB holocomplex was prepared using a pull-down approach. The strand-transfer substrate was prepared by annealing oligos oMS733, oMS734 and oSO146 (Supplementary Table 3) in a 1:1:1 ratio to a final concentration of 100 μM each. The annealing program consisted of incubation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by a 1 °C min−1 rampdown until 12 °C was reached. Next, 1,000 pmol of substrate, 22.5 μl of assembly buffer A (40 mM HEPES-KOH pH 7.5, 20 mM MgCl2, 200 mM KCl, 2 mM DTT) and water were mixed with 2,000 pmol of His6–MBP–TnsAB to a final volume of 45 μl, and the sample was kept on ice for 10 min. In the meantime, 35.8 μl (1,000 pmol) of Cascade–TniQ was mixed with 55 μl of assembly buffer B (40 mM HEPES-KOH pH 7.5, 20 mM MgCl2, 100 mM KCl, 2 mM DTT) and 1.6 μl of water to reduce its KCl concentration to 260 mM. This mixture was added to the initial pot and incubated at room temperature for 10 min. ATP (1 mM final concentration) was added, followed by 7,000 pmol of TnsC, reaching a final volume of 155 μl. The mixture was then incubated at room temperature for 30 min. After the addition of 45 μl of binding buffer (20 mM HEPES-KOH pH 7.5, 10 mM MgCl2, 300 mM KCl, 1 mM DTT, 0.025% Tween-20, 1 mM ATP), the sample was mixed with 50 μl of 50% amylose resin pre-equilibrated in binding buffer. The mixture was incubated at 4 °C on a rotating wheel for 1.5 h, and after centrifugation (500 g at 4 °C for 2 min), the supernatant was discarded and the protein-bound resin was washed three times with 500 μl of binding buffer. After adding 25 μl of binding buffer supplemented with 10 mM maltose, the sample was centrifuged (14,000 g at 4 °C for 10 min). The supernatant was isolated by careful pipetting and centrifuged two additional times. Next, 2.5 μl of sample was applied to a freshly glow-discharged (60 s) Quantifoil R 1.2/1.3 Au 300 mesh grid, blotted for 3 s (grid 1) or 2.5 s (grid 2) at 100% humidity and 4 °C, plunge-frozen in a cryogenic mixture of liquid ethane and propane using a Vitrobot Mark IV plunger (FEI) and stored in liquid nitrogen. Cryo-EM data were collected on an FEI Titan Krios G3i system operating at 300 kV and equipped with a Gatan K3 direct electron detector in super-resolution counting mode. In total, 43,220 movies were acquired as two separate datasets (dataset 1, 11,964 movies of grid 1; dataset 2, 31,256 movies of grid 2) at 130,000× magnification, yielding a super-resolution pixel size of 0.325 Å. Each movie consisted of 37 subframes with a cumulative exposure of 60.059 e− Å−2 (dataset 1) or 65.580 e− Å−2 (dataset 2). EPU automated data-acquisition software for single-particle analysis (v.3.9, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used for data acquisition, with three shots per hole and a defocus range of −1.0 to −2.4 μm in 0.2-μm increments.

For both datasets, the exposures were imported to cryoSPARC live (v.4.7) for real-time processing. Datasets 1 and 2 were pre-processed separately. After patch-motion and patch-CTF correction, particles were identified using blob picker (270–290-Å diameter, elliptical blob), extracted (extraction box size: 600 pixels; Fourier-cropped to box size: 200 pixels) and classified into 100 2D classes. Next, 93,462 particles of dataset 1 and 203,735 of dataset 2 were selected for a two-class ab initio reconstruction, which resulted in a Cascade–TniQ–TnsC volume and a TnsAB tetramer volume. The Cascade–TniQ–TnsC particles were re-extracted with a bigger box size (extraction box size 1,024 pixels, Fourier-cropped to box size 256 pixels) and used for non-uniform refinement. Using a bigger box revealed additional density at the edge of the particles, corresponding to a flexible TnsAB tetramer next to the TnsC density. This volume was low-pass filtered and used to create masks for particle subtraction. After reducing the box size (extraction box size 700 pixels, Fourier-cropped to box size 350 pixels), the signal for Cascade–TniQ was subtracted from the selected set of particles and the TnsABC moiety was locally refined after aligning the volume to the centre of mass of the TnsABC mask. A 3DVA (filter resolution: 5 Å; mode: simple) yielded one reconstruction with clear density for both TnsC and TnsAB and another with a well-resolved TnsC density but only a weak TnsAB density. The volumes were used as input for two rounds of heterogeneous refinement with the initial set of particles. Next, 49,752 were assigned to the TnsAB-bound TnsC volume, re-extracted (576 pixels) and locally refined, focusing on TnsAB. To adjust the box size for the creation of a composite map, the particles were re-extracted with a larger box-size (700 pixels) and re-aligned to the centre of mass of the Cascade–TniQ–TnsC mask, yielding a 49,606-particle set. An ab initio reconstruction, followed by non-uniform refinement, resulted in a Cascade–TniQ–TnsC–TnsAB consensus volume at 3.10 Å GSFSC resolution. Using the consensus map, new masks were generated for the Cascade–TniQ–TnsC, TnsC and TnsAB moieties as described above. The masks were used for particle subtraction of the TnsAB signal and local refinement of the Cascade–TniQ–TnsC complex. To improve the signal of the TnsB hooks bound to TnsC, a final particle subtraction of the Cascade–TniQ signal was followed by local refinement of TnsC. The TnsAB local refinement was repeated using the final set of 49,606 particles to match the rest of the volumes. This procedure yielded focused cryo-EM maps for the TnsAB tetramer, the hook-bound TnsC heptamer and the Cascade–TniQ–TnsC-hook complex at GSFSC resolutions of 3.36 Å, 3.30 Å and 3.09 Å, respectively. After re-alignment of the TnsAB volume, the global consensus map and the locally refined maps were combined into a composite map using the volume maximum function of ChimeraX (v.1.9). All maps and models have been deposited in the EMDB and PDB (see Data availability and Extended Data Table 3). A detailed processing workflow is shown in Extended Data Fig. 8. The QCascade–TnsC–TnsB hook model was rigid-body fitted in the cryo-EM density map using UCSF ChimeraX (v.1.9) and adjusted manually in Coot (v.0.9.4) using the above-mentioned restraints. The density corresponding to the tetrameric TnsAB was rigid-body fitted using our previously determined TnsAB PEC structure (PDB 9T7L)45. Missing regions in the protein and nucleic acid chains were built manually in Coot (v.0.9.4) with the aid of an AlphaFold3 model of TnsAB. It was not possible to determine whether the hooks bound to TnsC could be assigned to any of the TnsB copies observed in the structure, as the region between the hook and the last modelled TnsB residue (TnsB(586-604)) is disordered. It should also be noted that the 7:7 TnsC:hook observed stoichiometry probably reflects the excess of bait (His6–MBP–TnsAB) used in pull-down preparations, rather than the physiological interaction ratio, which was observed to be 7:4 in other transpososome structures6,36,41. Refinement statistics are included in Extended Data Table 3. Structure and map figures were prepared using UCSF ChimeraX (v.1.9) and Adobe Illustrator (v.25.4.1).
