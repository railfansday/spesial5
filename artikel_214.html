Porsche 718 Boxster prototype drive: This electric roadster is real

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Technical details are still limited, but we already know it’s fun to drive.

It wasn’t until I was stuck in traffic on the way back from a run up and down some canyon roads that I realized just how low Porsche’s upcoming all-electric 718 Boxster truly is. I was idly looking around at the other cars, doing the math on the EV adoption rate in this small sample of Los Angeles gridlock (roughly 50 percent, impressively) when I noticed that I was eye-level with a Tesla Model Y door handle.

That’s not particularly low for a sports car, but it’s practically unheard of for an EV. Nearly all modern electric cars are built atop a skateboard-style battery pack, a stack of cells that elevates the seating position by a good 3 inches. In these SUV-heady days, that’s usually not a problem, possibly even an advantage. But, in a car like this, the closer you can get your keister to the road, the greater the sensation of speed.

So, for the company’s first all-electric sports car, the 718 Boxster that will be fully revealed next year, Porsche engineers put the battery pack behind the driver. That powers a single motor spinning the rear wheels and creates a driving experience that both honors the simple joy of motoring in a mid-engined Boxster while pushing the 718 to new heights of power and performance.

That EV battery layout, stacked behind the two-seat cabin rather than sandwiched below it, isn’t novel. It’s the same setup that Tesla used on the original Roadster back in 2008. Back then it was done for cost reasons as much as anything, enabling Tesla to repurpose a Lotus chassis meant to hold an internal combustion engine in the middle.

For Porsche’s new Boxster (and the Audi Concept C, which is built on the same platform), that pack enables the low seating position and also delivers a rearward weight bias (40:60 front/rear) that’s in keeping with the last-generation 718 Boxster, the internal combustion model killed by EU cybersecurity regulations last year.

Porsche isn’t saying how big the new roadster’s battery is in terms of charge capacity, only that its size was chosen to balance range and weight. We also don’t have an idea of rated range, charging speed, nor battery composition, but I can say the battery pack is based on an 800 V architecture like the electric Macan, so hopefully we can expect charging performance that at least matches that SUV’s 270 kW.

The new Boxster also shares a motor with the Macan. Porsche is not quoting any specs yet, but it puts out about 500 hp (370 kW) to the rear wheels on the Macan Turbo. You can place your bets on whether the Boxster application will result in more or less power. I would take the over, but regardless, it’s enough to sprint this electric roadster to 62 mph (100 km/h) in under 3.0 seconds, making it the quickest Boxster ever.

But it’s also the largest and heaviest. I was told the new Boxster’s curb weight will be somewhere in the ballpark of a current-generation 911, so in the region of 3,300 to 3,700 pounds (roughly 1,500 to 1,700 kg), meaning it has gained at least a few hundred pounds. That’s light for an EV but heavy for a roadster. Its wheelbase has grown accordingly.

You can absolutely feel that on the road. The 718 just doesn’t have the same buzzy eagerness as the latest 718 Boxster or Cayman. But that doesn’t mean it’s less fun to drive. In fact, I think most people would say it’s much more fun. Nearly doubling the old car’s base power output will do that.

This car, though, is about a lot more than acceleration. It still turns and handles incredibly well, borrowing much of its suspension, steering, and even chassis from the current-generation 911. Few would call a 911 a boring car, and the 718 is a step more aggressive even than that coupe.

That’s in part thanks to the response afforded by the electric powertrain, which isn’t saddled by the lazy throttle application caused by turbochargers and modern emission regulations. It’s sharper even than the latest 911 GTS, which required a trick electrically assisted turbocharger plus a battery and electric motor to bring the EV feel to a car driven by internal combustion.

To bring some of the charm of internal combustion to an EV, Porsche followed in the footsteps of the Hyundai Ioniq 5N by adding synthesized shifting and engine noises to the mix. Many cringe at the thought of an EV wailing unnecessarily, but having driven many battery-powered machines that make gas-powered noises, I can say with assurance that they always dial up the fun factor.

What they often lack is authenticity. Porsche sampled the sound of what its engineers determined was the sweetest-sounding 718: the 981-generation Boxster GTS. The new 718 Boxster replicates both that car’s 3.4 L flat-six and its seven-speed dual-clutch transmission, controlled by the paddles behind the steering wheel.

Enable the system, which Porsche calls E-Shift, then put the Boxster into launch control, and you’re in for a bit of a show: The car not only sounds like it’s bouncing off of a rev limiter, but the car itself actually shakes. It does this by pulsing the electric motor, vibrating in much the same way that the original Harley-Davidson Livewire did.

Lift off the brake and the Boxster blasts forward, again doing the sprint to 60 in less than 3 seconds. All the while, the pretend engine is screaming and running through its mock gears, either automatically or at your command with the paddles. In manual mode, the car will hit that artificial rev limiter and abruptly stop accelerating, so you’d better be quick to grab that next gear.

This whole system is there to add a greater sense of emotion to the car, but it does serve a practical purpose. Slinging the 718 around the tight, twisty circuit at the Los Angeles Porsche Experience Center, I was better able to judge my speed through corners by listening to the pretend engine that sounded all the world like it was spinning happily behind my head.

Don’t like it? Press the blue button on the steering wheel and it all goes away, but I have a feeling most people will want it on, at least when they’re driving for fun. They will certainly want it on when cruising into their local Cars & Coffee, since you can hear the sound outside the car as well as on the inside.

Cruising is better in the new 718 than the older model as well. Its adaptive suspension, dialed down to Normal mode, handles concrete separation joints on the highway and bumpy roads without issue.

And it offers something extremely rare on the EV scene: true drop-top motoring. The top folds itself down in somewhere around 10 seconds, tucking itself neatly on top of that battery pack. It also offers reasonable practicality, with a frunk in the nose plus a decently wide if shallow trunk out back. Both are roughly big enough to handle a carry-on suitcase or a couple of backpacks. That’s about it, though. There’s effectively no room behind the seats.

So, from a lifestyle perspective, it’s a bit more flexible and a bit more comfortable than the previous-generation Boxster, and from a power and aggression standpoint, it’s on another planet. Still, there’s no hiding that increase in weight and wheelbase. This truly feels like a cross-breed between a Boxster and a Carrera, which will disappoint some purists, but if you had to pick a pair of parents for a new model, you could do a lot worse than those two.
