Franco-Moroccan Director Nabil Ayouch on Exploring ‘More Intimacy in My Characters’ With Next Film ‘Run, With No Tears’

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

For Franco-Moroccan filmmaker Nabil Ayouch, the freedom to tell the stories in which he believes has always mattered more than winning universal approval. Over nearly three decades, he has built a career exploring complex social realities and giving a voice to characters determined to challenge the limits imposed upon their lives. Along the way, he has learned to embrace the possibility that his films won’t always be understood.

“Making films means taking the risk of being misunderstood,” Ayouch tells Variety at the 47th Cairo Intl. Film Festival, where he serves as president of the international competition jury and was honored with the Golden Pyramid Lifetime Achievement Award at the festival’s opening ceremony. “Everyone wants to be loved. “It’s more important for me to express myself, to say what I have to say, to try to be understood, to touch souls, than to be loved,” he adds.

His return to Cairo carries particular significance. Nearly three decades ago, Ayouch presented his debut feature, “Mektoub,” at the festival, where it won two awards. Since then, he has returned several times, drawn to a city whose energy he describes as both overwhelming and exhilarating.

But the convictions that first drove Ayouch to make films remain as strong as ever. “The rage that has driven me since I started making cinema is intact, perhaps even stronger today,” he reflects, pointing to the injustices he sees around the world. “Every morning, I wake up full of this energy, this anger, but also full of love and passion for human beings. I want to continue seeing the beauty of this world and telling it through films.”

That tension between anger and hope has its roots in Ayouch’s childhood in Sarcelles, a working-class suburb north of Paris, where he spent his formative years. He credits the teachers and youth workers at a local youth and cultural center with helping him channel difficult experiences into something constructive.

“They taught me how to transform things that hurt me and that rage into a saving energy,” he recalls. “I don’t think I would have become a director” without them.

The experience has shaped not only his filmmaking but also his efforts to support emerging talent. Ayouch has opened seven cultural centers across Morocco, hoping to offer young people opportunities similar to those that changed his own life.

His films, from “Ali Zaoua” and “Casablanca Beats” to “Much Loved” and “Everybody Loves Touda,” have consistently focused on people seeking to take control of their own lives. But Ayouch resists defining his characters solely through their suffering.

“I need to meet someone whose soul attracts me, not simply because they’ve had a difficult life, but because of their desire to escape a cage, to break free from something that confines them,” he says. “I don’t like characters who are victims.”

Ayouch believes his refusal to portray women as powerless was a key reason “Much Loved,” his 2015 drama about sex workers in Marrakech, sparked such controversy in Morocco.

“Things would have been much simpler if the women in the film had been poor women who were beaten from beginning to end,” he says. “It’s because they’re seeking independence, because they’re demanding and want to escape their circumstances, that the film caused so much trouble.”

The controversy surrounding “Much Loved” prompted moments of doubt, but Ayouch insists that provoking outrage has never been his objective.

“I don’t make films to create controversy,” he maintains. “I make films because something outrages me, and I can’t imagine that those films won’t be divisive.”

Ayouch is a member of the Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences and France’s Académie des Césars. As president of Cairo’s international competition jury, he prefers to enter screenings knowing as little as possible about the filmmakers or their stories.

“I like to be surprised,” he says. “Cinema is an encounter, a shock, a moment of wonder.” He is particularly drawn to films that continue to reveal themselves long after the credits roll.

When it comes to jury deliberations, he favors discussion and consensus over voting, hoping that the jury’s diverse perspectives will help them reach a collective decision rather than one imposed by majority rule.

Despite receiving a lifetime achievement award, Ayouch is hardly ready to call it a day. He jokingly dubbed it a “mid-lifetime achievement award,” making clear that he hopes to have plenty more films ahead of him.

Looking ahead, he is interested in a different approach. “I’d like to explore a little more intimacy in my characters,” he reveals. “To make cinema that’s less political, and to find politics elsewhere, particularly in intimacy.”

That exploration will continue with his next feature, “Run, With No Tears,” which is scheduled to begin shooting in Morocco in early January. The film will star Vimala Pons, who replaces the previously announced Noomi Rapace, alongside Maryam Touzani and Nisrin Erradi, reuniting Ayouch with two actresses from his previous work. The film will be produced via his company Ali n’ Productions.

While he is eager to explore new directions, Ayouch has little interest in revisiting or correcting his earlier work. “If I had to remake the films I’ve made, I’d remake them all, scene by scene, film by film, and change absolutely nothing.”
