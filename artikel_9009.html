What AI Reveals About Architecture That Was Never Built

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

Some architectural projects only survive as drawings, competition entries, or models in archives. Others were built, but not as first imagined, and the completed building gradually replaced the original proposal in collective memory. Christopher Wren's St. Paul's Cathedral is familiar to many, but less well known is his Warrant Design, the spire-topped proposal approved in 1675 that he subsequently reworked as construction progressed. London was also once offered a very different monument: Thomas Willson's vast pyramid-shaped necropolis for Primrose Hill, designed to accommodate millions of burials and dominate the city's skyline.

These projects have outlived the circumstances that prevented their realization, whether financial, political, or technical. Some addressed practical needs through extraordinary forms; others explored how infrastructure, buildings, and cities could support new ways of living. New visualization technologies offer ways to revisit these works and examine ideas that, in some cases, anticipated the scale and complexity of contemporary megaprojects. A series being developed by Gendo explores this possibility through historical proposals that were never built or underwent significant changes before completion. Using archival drawings, models, and descriptions as starting points, it considers how generative tools might bring these ideas into view for contemporary audiences, while making clear where documentation ends and interpretation begins.

Among the earlier proposals is Charles-François Ribart de Chamoust's colossal triumphal elephant for the Champs-Élysées, conceived in 1758 with elaborate interiors, including a dining room with trees and a stream. A later elephant monument proposed under Napoleon for the Place de la Bastille reached the stage of a full-scale plaster model, which remained on the site for decades. Claude-Nicolas Ledoux's work similarly extended beyond his built tollhouses for Paris to more unusual proposals, including classical structures on boats intended to collect tolls from river traders. In his cenotaph for Isaac Newton, Étienne-Louis Boullée imagined a monumental sphere whose interior would evoke the night sky.

Early twentieth-century proposals explored the possibilities of new materials, infrastructure, and urban organization. Antonio Sant'Elia's Città Nuova imagined a layered city of elevators, bridges, and power stations, with a visual language that invites comparison with the later worlds of Metropolis and Blade Runner. Mies van der Rohe's Friedrichstraße skyscraper explored the architectural potential of a glass envelope, while Erich Mendelsohn's sketches and Hermann Finsterlin's proposals pursued expressive, almost biological forms.

Other projects challenged architectural conventions more directly. Adolf Loos, known for his criticism of ornament, entered the Chicago Tribune competition with a skyscraper shaped like a giant Doric column. The Hotel Attraction, a soaring tower proposed for Lower Manhattan and attributed to Antoni Gaudí, introduces a different question: how to interpret a project whose authorship and surviving drawings remain subjects of debate.

Postwar proposals expanded these ambitions to entire urban systems. Arata Isozaki's Clusters in the Air organized spaces around tree-like structural cores above Tokyo. Ron Herron's Walking City for Archigram imagined mobile structures crossing landscapes on telescopic legs. Rem Koolhaas's Bangkok Hyperbuilding proposed concentrating an entire urban environment within a single structure. Some of these projects were intended to be built on specific sites and others used drawings and models to explore ideas about architecture and urban life without aiming for construction.

The choice of medium also affects what an image communicates. A watercolor can suggest atmosphere while leaving details unresolved. A section can explain spatial relationships without describing every surface. Photorealism gives those surfaces and details greater apparent certainty, which can be useful when presenting a developed proposal but misleading when decisions are still provisional.

At the evidence-based end, the proposed visualization of Willson's pyramid uses survey data and defined camera positions to place the structure within aerial views of Primrose Hill and the wider London skyline. Drawing on methods used for verified views in planning applications, the exercise aims to test technical accuracy as well as visual persuasion. Mies's Friedrichstraße tower receives similar treatment within its Berlin site, while Wren's Warrant Design will be shown alongside the cathedral that was built, allowing direct comparison.

Open-ended provocation deliberately moves further from literal reconstruction. Finsterlin's and Mendelsohn's expressive forms can become starting points for experiments that follow the spirit of their work, potentially in dialogue with the visual worlds of H. R. Giger or M. C. Escher. Loos's column-shaped tower invites another kind of response: covering it in graffiti and tattoos would revisit the architect's own associations between ornament and bodily decoration. None of these images constitutes a definitive completion of another architect's work. Their labels help explain how much rests on historical evidence and how much comes from contemporary imagination.

The series also places historical proposals in dialogue with later projects and visual cultures. Boullée's cenotaph, for example, can be considered alongside the LED-covered Sphere proposed for Stratford in London, a counterpart to the venue in Las Vegas that was refused planning permission in 2023. Although radically different in purpose, the two spherical monuments invite a comparison of what different periods choose to monumentalize: scientific understanding and the cosmos in one case, entertainment in the other.
