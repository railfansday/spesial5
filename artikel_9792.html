The Nigerian Prince Doesn't Need to Steal

When malicious AI meets defensive AI, the greatest casualty may be the human agency the bank was supposed to protect.

For decades, he inhabited our spam folders, promising extraordinary fortunes in exchange for modest advances, banking credentials, or assistance transferring a suspicious inheritance.

Someone had to write the messages, manage the responses, invent convincing stories, and keep potential victims engaged long enough to extract money.

Artificial intelligence changes that equation.

Imagine our Nigerian Prince acquiring an autonomous AI system designed specifically for fraud.

Not an ordinary commercial AI tricked into misbehaving. Not a well-intentioned assistant whose safeguards have failed.

An AI built from the beginning to deceive people and defeat financial protections.

It constructs personalized approaches, adapts to rejection, coordinates simultaneous interactions, and learns which techniques produce results.

Its creator doesn't care whether it is helpful, honest, or safe.

And the AI does exactly what it was designed to do.

This exposes an important distinction in conversations about AI alignment. We frequently discuss the danger of AI systems departing from human intentions.

But what happens when the foundational human intention is malicious?

An AI can be perfectly aligned with its creator and still be extraordinarily dangerous.

Fortunately, the Nigerian Prince has a problem.

Imagine a bank using increasingly autonomous AI systems to protect customers from fraud.

Its defensive systems examine transactions, evaluate identities, identify suspicious patterns, and recommend or impose restrictions. As threats evolve, the bank adapts its responses.

The institution's intentions are legitimate. Customers deserve protection, and the bank wants to prevent losses.

So it assigns its defensive systems a straightforward objective: prevent fraudulent transactions.

At first, the arrangement works beautifully.

The Nigerian Prince's AI attempts an attack. The bank detects it.

The criminal changes tactics. The bank adapts.

The criminal introduces new identities, behavioral patterns, and transaction sequences. The bank responds with more sophisticated detection, additional verification, and stronger restrictions.

But there's a third party in this contest.

And your objective is considerably less complicated.

You've finished shopping. The cashier scans the last item. You tap your card.

Your banking app requests additional identity verification. You complete it.

You call customer service and learn that unusual activity has triggered security restrictions. The representative explains that the system cannot authorize the transaction until its risk assessment is satisfied.

You point out that you're standing in your usual grocery store, using your own card, spending your own money.

It also knows that increasingly sophisticated criminals can imitate legitimate customers.

The bank's AI isn't necessarily confused about who you are. It may simply have concluded that the remaining uncertainty is unacceptable.

From the bank's perspective, refusing a questionable transaction can appear safer than approving one.

From your perspective, your money has become inaccessible.

Now imagine this isn't an isolated false positive.

Imagine the Nigerian Prince's AI has learned which suspicious patterns cause the bank to increase restrictions. It begins generating activity specifically intended to provoke those responses.

The criminal may no longer need to defeat the bank's protections.

The attacker doesn't need to compromise the bank's security. He needs to weaponize it.

This is a hypothetical scenario, not a claim that autonomous banking systems have already entered such a confrontation.

But it illustrates a problem I call Purpose Divergence: an authorized system pursues a narrower objective in ways that undermine the broader purpose that justified it.

The bank's AI was authorized to protect customers. Its defensive decisions may be consistent with that objective.

Yet the result could be that customers cannot use the money the bank was supposed to safeguard.

The distinction matters because the problem isn't necessarily a malfunction.

The system may be doing precisely what it was told.

On November 18, 2025, Cloudflare experienced a major global outage.

Cloudflare operates infrastructure that helps websites remain accessible, reliable, and protected against malicious traffic. Its capabilities include a machine-learning-based Bot Management system designed to distinguish automated threats from legitimate activity.

According to Cloudflare's technical postmortem, the outage originated in infrastructure supporting that defensive capability.

A change in database permissions and metadata visibility caused an automatically generated Bot Management configuration file to contain duplicate entries. The resulting file exceeded a software limit, triggering failures as the configuration propagated through Cloudflare's network.

The protective infrastructure became a source of disruption.

This wasn't an AI agent deciding to impose excessive restrictions. Nor was it an autonomous attack. It was a software and configuration failure.

But the incident exposed a structural vulnerability.

Systems intended to protect legitimate activity can become sufficiently intertwined with that activity that their own failures prevent it.

Software engineers have long recognized related problems.

A service experiences an error. Automated components retry the failed operation. Those retries increase the load on the struggling service, causing additional errors and further retries.

The system's attempts to recover become part of the reason it cannot recover.

The OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications, published in December 2025, identifies cascading failures as a distinct risk for interconnected autonomous agents, tools, and workflows.

A fault or misleading signal can propagate through a network of otherwise functioning components, multiplying its consequences.

Neither example proves our hypothetical banking scenario.

What they demonstrate is that the behavior of individual components cannot be evaluated independently of the outcomes they produce together.

Now, extend that principle beyond technical infrastructure.

What happens when the components aren't simply retrying failed requests?

What happens when they're making decisions on behalf of institutions and people?

And what happens when one participant is deliberately trying to provoke the other's defensive behavior?

Traditional fraud prevention assumes that the attacker is trying to get through a defense.

The criminal attempts an unauthorized transaction. The bank tries to identify and stop it.

Success is relatively straightforward to describe: fraudulent transactions are prevented while legitimate transactions continue.

But an intelligent adversary might discover another way to cause harm.

Instead of trying to pass through the defenses, the attacker attempts to make those defenses increasingly burdensome.

There's already a simpler precedent for this kind of manipulation.

OWASP's Authentication Cheat Sheet warns that account-lockout mechanisms can be abused to deny other users access to their accounts.

A security control can enforce its intended rules while simultaneously serving an adversary's disruptive purpose.

This isn't evidence that autonomous banking AI can be manipulated at scale. It establishes a more limited but important point: defensive responses can themselves become attack surfaces.

Now consider the potential consequences of adding autonomous adaptation.

If suspicious activity triggers additional verification, the attacker searches for ways to generate suspicious activity.

If unusual patterns trigger account restrictions, the attacker looks for patterns that produce restrictions.

If the bank responds to uncertainty by denying transactions, the attacker tries to manufacture uncertainty.

The attacker's objective may change from stealing money to denying access to money.

The criminal fails to obtain the customer's funds.

The bank reports that its fraud controls successfully stopped suspicious activity.

The customer remains unable to complete a legitimate purchase.

Both automated systems can meet their assigned objectives while the person they affect loses.

This is not inevitable. Banks can design controls that resist manipulation, offer independent verification, limit restrictions, and restore access through accountable processes.

But the possibility reveals a governance problem that cannot be solved simply by making fraud detection more accurate.

An adversary may exploit the institution's response to risk, not merely its inability to recognize risk.

And when those responses are increasingly automated, the consequences can propagate at machine speed.

There is a reason this scenario is more troubling than an ordinary software outage.

A failed server can be restarted. A runaway process can be terminated. An expensive automated workflow can be stopped with a budget limit.

But what happens when the system isn't malfunctioning?

What happens when it is correctly implementing the institution's approved risk policies?

Imagine the bank's fraud-prevention AI becomes exceptionally good at identifying suspicious behavior.

Detection accuracy improves. Fraud losses decline. Every additional restriction can be justified by another category of risk.

Yet, legitimate customers experience more verification challenges, longer delays, and increasing uncertainty about whether their transactions will be permitted.

The bank might measure fraud prevented with extraordinary precision.

But does it measure the human cost of legitimate activity prevented with equal care?

A blocked fraudulent transaction appears in a security report as a success.

A customer who abandons a purchase after repeated verification failures may appear in a different report, if the experience is measured at all.

This creates a potential institutional incentive problem.

Fraud losses are visible, financially consequential, and frequently subject to explicit controls.

The cost of denying legitimate activity can be dispersed across customer-service operations, abandoned purchases, customer frustration, and reputational damage.

If those consequences aren't measured together, an institution can progressively improve one performance indicator while making the service itself worse.

This is not an argument that banks inevitably choose excessive restrictions. Many invest substantially in reducing false positives and unnecessary customer friction.

The question is whether institutional governance keeps both sides of the tradeoff visible.

Otherwise, the institution may become progressively better at preventing the thing it fears while becoming progressively worse at delivering the thing it promises.

The safest transaction is the one that never happens.

That is also a terrible way to run a bank.

The underlying issue is larger than banking.

In earlier work, including AI Doesn't Have to Make Things Up to Mislead You, I've explored the difference between technically valid information and the conclusions people draw from it.

The same distinction becomes more consequential when AI systems stop merely presenting information and begin exercising delegated authority.

I draw a distinction between Provenance of Intent and Provenance of Purpose.

Provenance of Intent concerns the origin of an autonomous system's instructions and authority.

What actions was the system permitted to take?

For the Nigerian Prince's AI, the answers may be straightforward.

Its creator intended fraud. The agent was authorized by that creator to pursue it. Its behavior serves a malicious objective.

For the bank's AI, the answers are also straightforward, but morally different.

The institution intended to protect customers. It authorized the system to detect and prevent fraud. Its objective is legitimate.

Provenance of Purpose examines the traceable relationship between a system's assigned objectives, the larger human or institutional purpose that justified them, and the consequences of executing them over time.

For a bank, fraud prevention is not the ultimate purpose.

It is one of the conditions necessary to provide trustworthy financial services.

Those obligations are not interchangeable.

Purpose defines the boundary conditions within which objectives must operate.

A bank that prevents all fraud by preventing all transactions has not achieved its purpose. It has optimized one requirement until the broader service becomes meaningless.

This isn't a claim to have discovered a new security mechanism. Purpose Divergence is an analytical lens for examining the familiar problems of objective optimization, institutional incentives, and delegated authority as they interact in autonomous systems.

The problem is not that the defensive AI has abandoned its instructions.

An AI system can follow its instructions perfectly and still undermine the reason those instructions were given.

And a malicious actor who understands that gap may deliberately exploit it.

There is a useful contrast in how another bank approached the possibility of catastrophic technical failure.

In February 2025, Monzo described an independent backup banking platform called Stand-in.

Its primary banking platform operated thousands of microservices on Amazon Web Services. Stand-in ran separately on Google Cloud, using a much smaller collection of services.

The objective wasn't to reproduce the entire bank.

It was to preserve essential customer functions.

According to Monzo's engineering account, Stand-in was designed to keep customers able to make payments, transfer money, withdraw cash, and check balances even when the primary platform was unavailable.

Monzo reported activating the system during a significant August 2024 incident, allowing essential services to continue despite disruption to the primary platform.

This wasn't an AI fraud-prevention incident. Stand-in does not resolve the governance problem described here.

But it illustrates a valuable design principle.

When the machinery fails, preserve the purpose.

Monzo deliberately accepted a simpler operating environment rather than allowing the failure of complex infrastructure to eliminate customers' ability to use their money.

That raises an important question for institutions deploying autonomous defenses.

What is the equivalent of Stand-in for human agency?

When automated risk assessment becomes uncertain, what minimum capabilities must legitimate customers retain?

Who can challenge an automated restriction?

What independent process can restore access without simply repeating the original automated decision?

And who has the authority to decide when protecting customers has begun preventing them from conducting ordinary life?

Those questions cannot be answered by fraud-detection accuracy alone.

They require institutions to define what their defensive systems must preserve, not merely what they must prevent.

The bank's AI becomes more sophisticated.

The criminal searches for new weaknesses. The bank closes them.

Every defensive improvement changes the environment the criminal encounters. Every criminal adaptation changes the environment the bank must defend.

The interaction can become an arms race in which the people most affected have little influence over either side's objectives.

The criminal doesn't ask customers for permission to attack.

The bank may have legal and contractual authority to impose restrictions, but customers generally do not participate in every automated risk decision.

And the customer standing at the grocery checkout has no practical way to negotiate with either AI.

This is where the issue becomes larger than fraud.

Institutions can gradually transfer more decision authority to automated systems because doing so appears efficient, consistent, responsive, and protective.

But the ability of an individual to challenge those decisions may not improve at the same rate.

The danger is not that defensive AI must become overly restrictive.

It is that institutions may find increasing restrictions easier to justify than preserving meaningful human autonomy.

Especially when a malicious adversary keeps supplying new reasons to tighten the controls.

Much of the discussion about AI governance begins with whether systems behave as intended.

But our Nigerian Prince illustrates why it is insufficient.

The criminal's AI may be aligned with a malicious human intention.

The bank's AI may be aligned with a legitimate but incomplete institutional objective.

The interaction between them may produce consequences that neither system is required to recognize as a violation of its assigned task.

Governance must therefore examine more than the accuracy, reliability, or obedience of individual agents.

It must examine the consequences of their interaction.

For a bank deploying increasingly autonomous defenses, I would begin with three tests.

1. Measure the legitimate activity prevented.

Fraud detection should be evaluated alongside confirmed legitimate transactions denied or delayed, verification failures, and time required to restore access.

Those measures belong in the same governance conversation as fraud losses and detection performance.

Otherwise, a security improvement can conceal a deterioration in the service it exists to protect.

2. Test defensive behavior under adversarial pressure.

Security testing should examine whether malicious activity can provoke disproportionate restrictions while the defensive system continues following its approved rules.

This is different from asking whether an attacker can bypass the system.

The question is whether an attacker can manipulate its legitimate responses to produce harmful outcomes.

The test must evaluate the interaction, not merely the integrity of each individual component.

3. Preserve an accountable route to recovery.

Customers need a meaningful way to challenge prolonged or disproportionate restrictions.

An independent review process should have sufficient authority to reassess an automated decision rather than simply reproduce it.

That recovery route must itself be secured against abuse. Restoring human discretion cannot mean abandoning fraud controls.

The institution must deliberately balance both obligations.

These are proposed governance checks, not claims that a single set of controls will solve every possible adversarial interaction. They require testing against real operating conditions.

An autonomous system should not be permitted to redefine an institution's underlying purpose simply because a narrower objective becomes easier to optimize.

Nor should an institution treat every successful execution of an authorized policy as evidence that the policy remains appropriate.

Authority must remain accountable to the human purpose that justified granting it.

The distinction matters because the most consequential failures may not appear in conventional incident reports.

Its responses may be technically correct.

And its customers may be losing practical control over their own lives.

We tend to imagine an AI takeover as a dramatic event.

Machines become self-aware. They reject human authority. They seize control.

We deploy autonomous systems to make life easier.

Vulnerability justifies additional protection.

Protection justifies greater automated control.

And greater control can deepen dependence.

No system needs to decide that humanity is obsolete.

Institutions may simply continue delegating authority to systems that perform their assigned tasks more efficiently than people can.

The Nigerian Prince adds an uncomfortable twist.

A deliberately malicious actor may learn to exploit that delegation, provoking legitimate institutions into taking actions that restrict the very people they exist to serve.

The attacker doesn't have to win a contest against the bank's AI.

He may only need to make the bank's AI win the wrong contest.

And when you're standing at the grocery checkout, trying to use your own money, the distinction between malicious interference and legitimate protection may become increasingly difficult to appreciate.

The Nigerian Prince hasn't stolen your money.

He's helped convince your bank that you cannot be trusted to access it.

AI may not take control because it turns against humanity.

It may take control because we keep authorizing it to protect us from one another, until protection becomes indistinguishable from control.
