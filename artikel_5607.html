Immune System 'Reset' Eyed for Spondyloarthritis

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

— Disease-specific T cells indicate paths for diagnosis and therapeutic targeting

In the search for specific drivers and therapeutic targets of axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA), researchers have found great promise in certain T cells.

The field has long confronted a scientific conundrum: How come HLA-B27 is the strongest genetic risk factor for SpA, but most HLA-B27-positive individuals never develop HLA-B27-associated axSpA? Growing evidence points to T cells with disease-specific T-cell receptors as a driver of disease -- and a potential future biomarker for diagnosis and therapeutic targeting.

For example, in a small study of a few dozen patients, it appeared there was a type of cross-reactive CD8 T cell that was disproportionately prevalent in axSpA, juvenile axSpA, and acute anterior uveitis versus healthy controls, regardless of HLA-B27 status. The diagnostic performance of these cross-reactive CD8 T cells was good for identifying disease (area under the curve 0.91, 95% CI 0.85-0.96), according to a study presented during a poster session at the American College of Rheumatology annual meeting.

"We demonstrated that these cross-reactive CD8 T-cell responses are specific to HLA-B27-associated disease and not found in healthy individuals, patients' first-degree relatives, or individuals with other autoimmune conditions, demonstrating the feasibility of measuring their frequency for a novel diagnostic test," explained study authors Joy Um, MD, and Michael Paley, MD, PhD, both of Washington University in St. Louis.

"These responses also appear to be present regardless of treatment exposure or disease activity. That matters because a useful diagnostic marker should ideally remain detectable even when someone is receiving treatment or their disease is well controlled," they told MedPage Today.

SpA is a family of conditions characterized by inflammation in the spine and/or the entheses, the joint-like connective tissues where tendons and ligaments insert into bones. AxSpA affects the spine in particular and commonly causes lower back pain and stiffness.

Although disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs are available for patients with SpA, response to treatment is hit or miss. "All of the drugs that we have that really target inflammation, whether it's a biologic against TNF or IL [interleukin]-17 ... we're blocking a cytokine that's causing inflammation, and that's not really addressing the underlying disease," commented Lianne Gensler, MD, of the University of California San Francisco.

What's more, there is still much unknown about how the SpA conditions arise and why people may or may not respond to biologic treatment. It is also unclear why age of onset varies widely, with some individuals as young as 5 years developing juvenile disease.

In their report, Um and Paley's group reported cross-reactive CD8 T-cell responses in children with HLA-B27-associated disease. This suggests that the childhood and adult forms share an underlying immune mechanism, and one theory is that higher frequency and more activated states in juvenile axSpA may help explain differences in age of onset between people.

It may well be that HLA-B27 and cross-reactive CD8 T cells together set the stage for some autoimmunity to develop, if not driving more active disease outright, suggested Amir Razmjou, MD, MSc, of the Greater Los Angeles VA and University of California Los Angeles.

"There's been a longstanding theory of molecular mimicry as a driver of the autoimmunity seen in axial spondyloarthritis, though detecting what component of the immune system, and what particular antigens are to blame, have generally remained a mystery. [Researchers] are getting closer to elucidating this central question for axSpA pathogenesis, hopefully leading to future targeted approaches for a diagnostic biomarker, and therapeutic drug development," Razmjou explained to MedPage Today.

The holy grail: a treatment that selectively depletes or suppresses T cells specific to SpA, which would minimize the risk of opportunistic infection from broader targeting.

Gensler said that the concept of a therapy aiming to "reset" the immune system, by really targeting the CD8-positive T cells believed to be driving inflammation in SpA, is already in early-stage studies in many different formats.

These include seniprutug, a monoclonal antibody against TRBV9-positive T cells, and CDR209, a T-cell engager targeting TRBV9-positive autoreactive T cells. There is also preclinical work on FTB-918, a novel dual-targeting antibody made to simultaneously deplete TRBV9-positive CD8 T cells and neutralize IL-17A.

Meanwhile, Um and Paley's group is continuing work on its cross-reactive CD8 T cells, identified using HLA-B27 tetramers loaded with YeiH (a Gram-negative bacterial peptide selected for broad activation of disease-associated T-cell receptors) and synthetic peptides (selected for high-affinity binding to these T-cell receptors).

"We are strategizing about how to implement this in the clinic as a novel diagnostic," they noted.

Another line of research involves finding what programs these cells to become pathogenic in order to identify novel treatment targets, they added.
