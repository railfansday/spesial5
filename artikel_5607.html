'He Should Talk to His Primary Care Doctor About That'

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

— We should treat the patient in front of us whenever possible

One spring day during my neurology rotation, a man in his nineties came to our clinic. He was walking with a cane but otherwise remarkably independent, with the air of distinguished gentlemanliness a brown tweed jacket lends. We had an instant connection. Talking for just a few moments, we realized he hailed from my own family's ancestral town. As I spoke with him in his native language, he soon began calling me "pota" for "grandson." Quickly though, my resident and I also realized our patient was with us by mistake. Perhaps due to a miscommunication, or an administrative error, he thought he was at his primary care physician (PCP). He had been experiencing constipation.

After we clarified the confusion, my resident -- someone who taught me a great deal -- asked me to walk our patient out to the front desk and help him schedule a primary care appointment. The front desk said the next PCP appointment wouldn't be available for 7 weeks. I went to ask the resident if we could do something to help our patient; after all, while constipation may turn out to be a complex symptom either due to a serious condition (colorectal cancer, sigmoid volvulus) or as a precursor to downstream pathology (diverticulosis, fecal impaction), it is also quite common among older adults.

Both the resident and I had rotated on internal medicine and knew the questions to ask to identify any red flags and suggest a bowel regimen. And we had the time -- our patient didn't have a neurological concern, but he still had the appointment slot. Yet, when I returned to the clinic room to ask my resident if we could give our patient the relevant advice, he looked up from the queue of notes he was finishing and replied: "He should talk to his PCP about that."

Economics tell us that workforce efficiency is maximized when everyone spends time on what they are uniquely best at doing: labor arbitrage. As a management consultant prior to medical school, I spent years evangelizing this concept of "top of license work" to clients -- mostly large academic and nonprofit hospital systems. And I still believe in the principle broadly, albeit with perhaps a bit more reticence and understanding more nuance than before.

Top-of-license work in medicine doesn't only apply vertically across levels of training; it also operates horizontally, across fields. Patient outcomes are generally maximized when neurologists focus on diseases of the nervous system and gastroenterologists the digestive system because each specialty is doing work only they can do. Yet, that day in the neurology clinic taught me a nuance to this perspective.

At the end of the day, all physicians should consider themselves doctors first, and specialists second. Medical specialization shouldn't absolve specialists of the duty to help the patient before them -- especially when doing so requires minimal time and resources -- even if the patient's concern is not directly within their field.

Of course, there are challenges to this perspective. I can empathize with the medico-legal concerns of a neurologist who is served a subpoena and deposed by a lawyer: "Firstly, why did you manage this constipation? And why did you not order XYZ tests for this patient with constipation who turned out to have colorectal cancer?" And I recognize the confusion medical teams may feel when checking charts and seeing who prescribed certain medications: "Why is the prescribing doctor for this patient's cetirizine (Zyrtec) an orthopedic surgeon?"

These concerns are real. But primary care is a limited resource too. The dumping of basic tasks on primary care -- a field with its own specialized expertise that is often not recognized -- has been discussed ad nauseam; specialists should help relieve this burden when possible.

Labor arbitrage and top of license work still apply when specialists help patients with "primary care problems." However, because of the design of our healthcare system, there is little structural incentive for physicians to work outside of their typical scopes. If, for instance, we financially incentivized all of a patient's physicians by the same quality metrics, specialists would be incentivized not just in their field, but across the spectrum of the patient's health. Or even if we had less insurance churn, payers would perhaps better leverage incentives within their networks to promote overall health.

Responding to incentives does not mean physicians have moral failings. It shows they are human. But if we continue to depend on our system as currently designed, we will continue to have rampant "moral alibis" -- a term coined by Sachin Jain, MD, MBA, to highlight how physicians at times emphasize problems with the system to escape responsibility for personal decisions -- that limit the impact of our care.

The vast majority of physicians enter medicine to help patients. It is the design of our system that has forced practice to occur in unnatural ways. But perhaps while we solve these structural problems, we can start changing things by treating the patient in front of us -- one case of constipation at a time.
