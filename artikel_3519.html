The Role of the “Queen Mother” in Monarchies Around the World: Queen Sofía, Sonia of Norway, and Beatrix of the Netherlands

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Queen Sofía celebrated her 88th birthday on November 2 and remains a pillar of the Spanish royal family, with a demanding schedule that includes at least one official engagement per week. Queen Sofía has never set aside her institutional role, unlike some other European “queen mothers” (this official title is used only in the United Kingdom but is commonly used to refer to the mothers of kings), who, to a greater or lesser extent, continue to work for the monarchy in their respective countries.

This week, Queen Sofía will be in San Fernando, Cádiz, where she will serve as patroness of the ceremony to present the National Ensign to the Marine Corps, a special unit within the Navy and the world’s oldest marine corps, created in 1537 by Emperor Charles I. Far from retiring from public life, the Queen Emerita continues to play an important role within the Crown; she retains her office at Zarzuela and receives an official stipend.

She also remains closely involved in the day-to-day activities of the Queen Sofía Foundation and regularly visits the provincial offices of the Spanish Federation of Food Banks to learn about its charitable work, among other projects. Always discreet, Felipe VI’s mother has weathered the controversies surrounding Juan Carlos and maintained a professional image dedicated to serving the Spanish people—one that her family circumstances have not tarnished.

Queen Sonja, a great source of support today for her son, King Haakon.

Following the death of her husband, King Harald, in late August, and with the newly crowned Queen Mette-Marit on medical leave, Queen Sonja of Norway has become a pillar of support for her son, Haakon VIII. Sonia of Norway is 89 years old and in recent months has had minor health scares that led to her being hospitalized for a few days in the hospital. But despite everything, she continues her duties as a member of the Norwegian royal family. Just a few days ago, Queen Sonja traveled to Málaga to attend the opening of an exhibition on Picasso and Munch at the Picasso Museum in the Andalusian city, and was all smiles during a visit to a Norwegian school in Benalmádena.

On November 3, Queen Sonja accompanied her son Haakon to an official dinner to welcome the provincial governors at the Royal Palace—an important event at which the king will have his mother’s support in the absence of his wife and his daughter, Crown Princess Ingrid Alexandra, who is currently focused on her university studies in Oslo. Two days later, Queen Sonja will visit the Furniture Maker School in Askim, attend the opening of a furniture exhibition at the Center for Democracy at Fort Høytorp, and meet with members of the Indre Østfold Women’s Group and young people at the Re:læx youth center in Askim.

After more than three decades as queen, Sonia of Norway has stayed by her son’s side, at a time when he needs her support more than ever. Since her accession to the throne, Queen Sonia—whom Haakon stripped of the title “Her Majesty,” now simply Queen Sonia—has become a cornerstone of institutional and family support for the new monarch.

Beatrix of the Netherlands served as queen for 33 years.

At 88 years old, Beatrix of the Netherlands does not have a schedule as busy as those of Queen Sofía or Queen Sonja of Norway, but she has continued to attend institutional events and fulfill some official engagements since her abdication in 2013. King Willem-Alexander’s mother actively participates in award ceremonies linked to the Crown, such as the Appeltjes van Oranje awards presented by the Oranje Fonds foundation—awards whose bronze statuettes she designed herself and which she attends each year alongside King Willem-Alexander and Queen Máxima.

In addition, Princess Beatrix continues to make official trips to the Dutch Caribbean territories (such as her recent visits to Bonaire, St. Eustatius, and Saba) to preside over events dedicated to nature conservation, sustainable development, and education. She also continues to attend certain cultural and charitable events in her home country, such as the Dutch Ballet Gala hosted by the Dansersfonds Foundation, which she attended in mid-October.

Beatrix of the Netherlands, who now resides at Drakenstein Castle, was Queen of the Netherlands for 33 years, from 1980 to 2013. Following the coronation of her son Willem, she regained the title of princess, and today her official title is Her Royal Highness Princess Beatrix of the Netherlands, Princess of Orange-Nassau, and Princess of Lippe-Biesterfeld. Beatrix of the Netherlands remains deeply loved and respected by the people, and within the Orange-Nassau royal family, she continues to support and guide her son, King Willem-Alexander.

Princess Beatrix is a great role model for her granddaughter, Princess Amalia.

Queen Sofía, Queen Sonja of Norway, and Beatrix of the Netherlands all embody the key institutional values essential to preserving the monarchy in their respective countries. Loyal to the crown in one capacity or another and having dedicated their lives to public service for decades, all three are great examples of institutional loyalty for their granddaughters— Princess Leonor, Princess Ingrid Alexandra, and Princess Amalia—who will one day become queens.
