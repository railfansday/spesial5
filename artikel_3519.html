Did O.J. Simpson Have an Accomplice?

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

About five years ago, I started receiving a string of increasingly frenzied emails from a man named Todd Guzze. He was trying to clue me into an explosive theory about the most famous murder case in American history: O.J. Simpson had an accomplice on the night he allegedly murdered Nicole Brown Simpson and Ron Goldman in 1994. The messages tended to be written solely in the subject line: “Lot of history at VANITY FAIR on OJ CASE…”; “Let me know if you want to chat...” On another occasion, he wrote, “IPPOLITO MOB FAMILY OF NEW JERSEY COVERS UP OJ SIMPSON MURDERS FOR 27 YEARS!! THE REAL SOPRANO’S!!”

The story sounded tantalizing. But my editor and I agreed, after I engaged with Guzze a bit, that given the tenor of his outreach, it was best to leave this lead alone. Judging by the past few weeks, we couldn’t have been more wrong.

A recent New York Times investigation and a separate Peacock documentary, OJ’s Accomplice, each make a more rigorous case for the theory that has circulated among Simpson case obsessives and celebrity conspiracists like Guzze for decades. The foremost additional suspect in this new batch of reporting is Joey Ippolito, a friend of Simpson’s and a drug trafficker turned federal informant who, as Guzze told me in 2021, was connected to New Jersey’s Italian Mafia. (Like the Times, in outlining Ippolito’s larger-than-life biography, Guzze also highlighted Ippolito’s relationship with The Godfather star James Caan.) Guzze has also, to this day, intermittently sent me photos of Charlie Ehrlich, a Miami strip club manager who was convicted alongside Simpson in a 2007 armed robbery case, accompanied by mostly inscrutable theories of Ehrlich’s alleged involvement in the murder. (Ehrlich has not been accused of any wrongdoing in connection with the killings.) The highlight of OJ’s Accomplice is an ostensible hot mic revelation provided by Ehrlich. Following his interview, as he walks to the bar of his strip club, he says, “You didn’t bring it up, so I’m not going to bring it up.”

“I was very good friends with [Ippolito’s] brother Lou,” Ehrlich continues. “Do you know what Lou told me? He told me that a month before Joey died, he took him to Italy…. Joe admitted that he was involved.”

Versions of the accomplice theory have ratcheted up ever since the release of If I Did It, a 2007 book that offered what was described as Simpson’s “hypothetical” account of the 1994 killings.

Guzze has all sorts of theories—he’s also written to me about assorted Jeffrey Epstein matters and a mysterious murder in Utah. Our most extended engagement came in 2024, when he independently published Kim’s Lost Words, a 59-page book billed as a memoir by Kim Porter, the deceased ex-girlfriend of Sean “Diddy” Combs, that has been dismissed by Porter’s friends and relatives as a fabrication. (It ends with Porter, who died of pneumonia in 2018, appearing to predict her own murder at Combs’s hands.) The book rose to the top of Amazon’s best-seller list after Combs was indicted on sex-trafficking and racketeering charges, and conspiracy theories surrounding his sex life and celebrity connections flooded social media. But while that case ultimately fell well short of Guzze’s ideas—Combs was convicted only on prostitution-related charges—I felt obligated, in light of the recent Simpson reporting, to hear his reaction.

“It’s very difficult to watch this happen and to see the world listen to The New York Times and NBC when I’ve already been saying this for seven years,” Guzze told me this week. “Every single news outlet you see right now, every reporter, I already came to them before and they blocked and censored me.” (The broken clock adage comes to mind.) Following the Times’ reporting, a sister of Nicole Brown Simpson has called for an investigation into whether an accomplice was involved in the 1994 murder, as has Alan Dershowitz, who helped represent Simpson during his 1995 trial. While Simpson was found liable for the deaths in a civil suit, according to the Los Angeles Police Department, a lack of conviction in a criminal trial means that the case remains open.

Guzze learned about the accomplice theory from a Los Angeles film producer named Erik Laibe. Laibe, in turn, had learned about it from Ehrlich. Laibe told me this week that he met Ehrlich around the time that Simpson was being paroled from prison in 2017 after serving part of his sentence for armed robbery. They decided to work on a movie telling Ehrlich’s life story, but the project went sideways after the Miami New Times obtained a copy of the script and published a story noting that Ehrlich shared a first name with the accomplice described in If I Did It. “During that time,” Laibe said, “I was contacted by, I’ll say, dozens and dozens of news outlets…and literally from all over the world: ‘Is this Miami New Times story real?’ And I was no comment. The only guy that I called back was a gentleman named Mike Baker.” (Baker and another New York Times reporter, Tim Arango, published the paper’s Simpson investigation last month.)

“One of the challenges early on for us,” Baker told me, “was trying to navigate this world of what is a conspiracy theory and what might be rooted in fact and what are people’s just general theories that they’ve come up with over the years.”

“There was initially a big instinct,” he added, “for people to say, ‘No, I’ve heard this all before. It’s not true.’”

There was a parallel path from these celebrity backwaters to Peacock’s OJ’s Accomplice. “Once I heard the term ‘OJ having an accomplice,’” the film’s codirector Ari Mark told me, “I’m not going to lie, I thought it was really, really juicy and something that I really wanted to serve up to people because I like being part of that energy. But at the same time, it gets you into this weird underbelly of people where you’re not really sure who to believe, what to believe.” For his own dive into the Simpson-adjacent underworld, Mark enlisted Alex Tomé as a consulting producer: a food truck owner from North Carolina and “self-proclaimed O.J. Simpson expert” who had never worked on a documentary before.

“My goal is not to solve the case,” Mark said. “My goal is to say, ‘Can I get underneath this subculture and can I get people to tell me their point of view in a way that feels authentic?’”

I took my own brief dip into the subculture to get a sense of what I’d missed out on by neglecting Guzze’s outreach. Iroc Avelli, a former bodyguard for Simpson, has claimed to have had thumb drives with audio recordings in which Simpson confesses to the murders and says he had help. (Police only found recordings of Avelli talking to himself.) Mark told me that, as he conducted his reporting, Avelli pelted him with antisemitic remarks. When I called Avelli this week, he was less incendiary but just as bombastic, immediately launching into an account of how he first connected with Simpson while he was dealing cocaine.

“I’m only going to deal with facts,” Avelli told me. “I don’t deal with bullshit. I’m not looking to make money off of this because I sold more cocaine than anybody on the West Coast or the Midwest or the East Coast.” He denied making antisemitic comments to Mark, saying, “I’ve been to prison three times; I don’t make careless, frivolous remarks.” He repeated his account that he was certain that Simpson had an accomplice, though he wouldn’t or couldn’t specify who.

These are the sorts of conversations required to produce a story like Mark’s or Baker and Arango’s. Everyone in the Simpson murder orbit, even all this time later, had a harried and sordid quality that made trust difficult to come by. In its way, it was a testament to Simpson’s cultural footprint. I asked Mark how working on his film made him reevaluate the singular personality of one of the most notorious figures in American life.

“I think it reaffirmed my cynicism around optics, celebrity, entertainment,” he said, “and just how far you can fall.”
