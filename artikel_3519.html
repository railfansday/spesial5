Mississippi Goddam: Scott Colom Is the Answer to Anyone Who Looked Down on the South

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

“Mississippi is not who you think we are.”

This is what an older Black woman said to MS NOW’s Nnamdi Egwuonwu as he interviewed Scott Colom supporters on the Tougaloo College campus Thursday night. The crowd was waiting for Colom to speak after his historic victory, becoming the first Black Mississippian elected to statewide office since Reconstruction, and its first Democratic senator in nearly a half century. The woman did not give her name, but her simple statement has tattooed itself inside my brain.

“To understand the world, you must first understand a place like Mississippi” is a more famous cautionary quip that scratches at the same sentiment. If you’ve known anyone from Mississippi, you’ve probably heard it. Everyone cites William Faulkner, even though nobody has definitively sourced it. But as the world looks with fresh eyes on my home state, the wisdom checks out.

Onlookers, including longtime national political strategists, seem surprised by Colom’s win. Mississippians are not. The extremes of the moral universe have always been duking it out down there, which makes Mississippi not strange or anomalous, but illustrative.

There’s no avoiding the facts: Human beings have committed the most brutal violence imaginable against their fellow humans in Mississippi, as a matter of law and legacy. One of the consequences of that horror is that it has become prophecy. It is impossible not to feel a sense—from within the state and from without—of frustration and dread that it is taking way too long to emerge from a racism that has raptured the entire culture.

But to people who do not know Mississippians, who do not understand Mississippi—to those who suggest any right-minded or good people simply up and leave, or think the rest of the country should abandon all hope, or worse, look away entirely—you are missing one of the most important threads in the future of American politics and culture. Colom got at this rub when he said that outlets like Cook Political Report, who prognosticate a “solid R” conclusion, are destructive. “They made it much more difficult to fundraise and are part of the reason the South has been neglected for so long,” he wrote. And if you dismiss possibility in Mississippi, you are compounding the neglect. You are part of the destruction.

You think John McCain was a maverick? Scott Colom is a maverick.

During the race, he drew national attention not only for his prospect of flipping the unflippable, but also because he was doing so through one of the most vivid, creatively engaged, and original campaigns in modern politics. He built a coalition virally, with his twin brother, Andrew Robert Colom, as collaborator, offering “Sheeeeit” to the world (yes, that’s how many white Mississippians really talk), along with a Hollywood-worthy Sinners send-up, and Andrew’s searing spoken-word poem set to the strains of Fannie Lou Hamer. Colom even stood in line at a Trump rally to speak earnestly with voters who would otherwise be against him—I guarantee he won some of them over.

His entire strategy was built on making people recognize Mississippi, in all its underdog idiosyncrasies. He asked Kamala Harris to campaign with him, even as his advisers reportedly told him it would be an error, because he knew she would draw media attention to underfunded hospitals. And he took the phone call informing him that he’d won while wearing a Richard Pryor hoodie. Because we like to laugh in Mississippi, okay?

As Kiese Laymon, novelist, memoirist, and modern-day Mississippi bard, told me, “Once I saw the protein ad, I knew he’d win.”

History doesn’t echo in Mississippi; it rhymes. You can draw a straight line from the murders of Medgar Evers, or those of James Chaney, Andrew Goodman, and Michael Schwerner, to the killings of Jorge Gomez or Renee Good or Alex Pretti. You can draw a straight line from Theodore Bilbo to Tate Reeves to Donald Trump.

As Kiese wrote in this magazine in 2020, Mississippi politics flow from its culture. (Incidentally, Kiese and Colom both attended Millsaps College, about 10 years apart, and had the same thesis adviser.) In “Mississippi: A Poem, in Days,” Kiese describes how he got into “an altercation with two fraternities on Bid Day”—when the students who want to be in the Greek system receive offers from frat and sorority houses. “Some fraternity members wore Confederate capes, Afro wigs, and others blackened their faces.” They called Kiese and his girlfriend the N-word.

I have never written about the heartbreak of seeing the future governor of Mississippi in that group of white boys, proudly representing the Kappa Alpha fraternity and its confederate commitment to Black suffering. I have never admitted that after playing basketball against Tate all through high school, and knowing that he went to a public school called Florence, not a segregation academy, like so many other white boys we knew, it hurt my feelings to see Tate doing what white boys who pledged their identities to the Old South ideologies were supposed to do.

When I saw Tate in that confederate cacophony of drunken white boyhood, doing what they did, I knew he could one day be governor of Mississippi and president of the United States.

That is still the most damning thing I can ever say about a white boy from Mississippi.

Damning things still happen, and will again. Just days ago, as the votes continued to stack up for Colom, the old ugliness reared its head. White women in my hometown, students at the University of Mississippi, were captured on video making racist gestures—gesturing and making sounds like monkeys—at their Black neighbors’ video doorbell. The school, still embarrassingly insistent on calling itself “Ole Miss,” continues to investigate, while the offenders get so much more grace than they deserve from the victims and their families, as you can see in their CBS interview. I feel deep shame in these moments, because I know this is what so many people think Mississippi is. There is no excuse for it, and no explanation beyond the obvious. The shame should be obvious, too, but a white person from Mississippi is compelled to express it.

The women are not from Mississippi, according to a source familiar; one, Alaina Kunes, is from Wisconsin, where a car dealership that bears her family’s name is now being sued for a separate allegation of racist actions. This is not an excuse, but rather a fear: that Mississippi’s reputation for the worst parts of history continues to attract the worst sorts of people.

But this is partly what Faulkner, or whoever it was, meant by understanding the world through the lens of Mississippi: Everywhere we must hope our better angels prevail.

I was born and raised in Mississippi among a large, politically engaged family who have wanted change for generations. When Sargent Shriver turned up unexpectedly at my grandparents’ house for dinner one night, on a swing through the South in a failed presidential bid, my grandmother whispered the urgent directive of “FHB”—“family holdback”—so her five sons would leave enough food for Shriver. This story, and that guest, was a point of pride her whole life. She was an unforgiving George McGovern campaigner; in our family photo album, among the pool parties and Christmas mornings, are pages of Polaroids she snapped of Richard Nixon’s televised resignation.

Local private schools, as I understood them, were inherently racist. Public school was a choice of one, and I got a world-class education in the Oxford school system. (Hyde-Smith attended Lawrence County Academy and she sent her own child to Brookhaven Academy, both segregation academies.) My class of 2000—I came up with the same kids from kindergarten through high school—was roughly half Black and half white, with the skinniest pie slivers of anything else. I left for college and never moved back, but my parents, brother, and extended family are still there. Today, Mississippi’s public-school students are doing better than most of their peers across the nation; my own children in the New York City school system are getting a more segregated, less excellent education than I did.

My father, a green-energy mayor and a Barack Obama appointee to the Tennessee Valley Authority (fired by Trump amid a Republican board takeover), pushed throughout my childhood to take the Confederate flag off the state flag and to remove the Confederate monument that still mars the Oxford Square. I was about eight years old when someone keyed “LIBERALS” into the trunk of our Honda Civic, probably because of the Ray Mabus for Governor bumper sticker. I recall being worried about it, until I saw that my parents were amused.

As white Southern liberals, we were in a tiny minority. And though the mantle was worn proudly, it was more existential than that—you were proud to be a Democrat not because it was one party over another, but because it was a stand-in for compassion, common good, and humanity. And nowhere could this be truer, or more necessary, than in Mississippi. (By way of disclosure, I see no conflicts in telling you all this. As former Time editor Rick Stengel says, “You should be non-partisan, but not neutral. Journalists must be biased in favor of the truth and facts. Biased in favor of democracy itself.” And this is the truth, in favor of democracy.)

Scott Colom and I were born a year apart in the earliest 1980s, during a sort of second-wave new New South rebranding. More jobs were coming into the state thanks to manufacturing; more national attention was being paid to Black Mississippians’ cultural contributions (the Delta Blues Museum was established in 1979). William Winter was governor the years of our respective births, and he gave us free kindergarten. William Allain, whose moment was virtually destroyed by a racist, homophobic GOP slander campaign, followed. And Allain was succeeded by Mabus—who won on the slogan “Mississippi will never be last again.” Now, with the exception of Ronnie Musgrove, the governor has been a Republican since 1992. We’ve never had a Black person, or a woman for that matter. Have you had many good thoughts about Mississippi in all this time? I don’t think you have.

Heavy, Kiese’s devastating and raw memoir of coming of age as a Black boy in Mississippi, written in address to his activist and educator mother, gives his own view into 1980s and ’90s Mississippi: “You worked on Governor Mabus’s campaign and kept talking about how much was possible politically in Mississippi because it was the blackest state in the nation,” he writes. And his mother says to him: “Do you understand what’s possible if we actually get effective radical politicians in place down here?” Kiese responds, “I’m glad Mabus won but hearing about it every day is just kinda mee-guh.” Meager. It was not enough then, and we have plenty far to go.

Heavy was published in October 2018, just before Stacey Abrams turned Georgia purple, signaling to the rest of the country that it was time to take notice of the deeply disenfranchised Southern states.

When I reached out to Mabus, who went on to become Bill Clinton’s ambassador to Saudi Arabia and then Obama’s Secretary of the Navy, he identified the dominoes that knocked toward Colom’s win: the Supreme Court’s gutting of the Voting Rights Act, rampant corruption, a welfare scandal featuring Brett Favre, and “office holders taking constituents for granted” over decades. Mabus was a young man working for Winter when Colom and I were little, and this election, he said, “brings hope for the future.”

It doesn’t really matter which party you’d like to claim. Repulsive policies will eventually push everyone away. Mississippi Republicans are not moderate, are not descended of gentle governance. (See: Bilbo et al.) This is the state that put “personhood” on the ballot as part of a national antiabortion strategy; fortunately for all y’all, Mississippians rejected the idea of life beginning at conception. Lynn Fitch, the current attorney general, not only effortfully attempted to overturn the results of the 2020 presidential election, but she is the signed counsel for the petitioners in the Dobbs brief, which ultimately reversed Roe v. Wade. (Because I keep underscoring to you the infinite refractions of Mississippi, Fitch also went after Colom for one of his ads that used AI; he removed it.) Even the affidavit ballots that have taken so long to count are a result of strident, generational attempts by Republicans to disenfranchise Black voters. Which is why Mississippians knew, as the Colom–Hyde-Smith race came down to those affidavits, exactly in whose favor they would tally up. Were it not for the dedication of young Black canvassers—especially Black women—and homegrown mobilization efforts, things would have gone differently.

“There is a radical history of Black academics, activists, lawyers, and judges who have really been working to see a Mississippi where this was possible,” Kiese emailed me over the weekend. “I am thankful that someone like Leslie McLemore, who was a young member of Hamer’s Mississippi Democratic Party, can see what they worked and died for.”

What does Colom’s win mean for Kiese? “It means that the people, not any party, get what they organize and fight for,” he replied. “Our state taught the world that again last week.”

My privileged position occluded my own understanding of Mississippi’s true potential until I was older. Growing up, I mostly just clocked defeat—every presidential election, every midterm, Mississippi was red as red could be, as Mississippi’s poverty levels ratcheted toward a 21st-century high of 24%. In 2008, John McCain beat Barack Obama in the state by a 13% margin (the two staged a debate in Oxford). In 2016, Trump bested Hillary Clinton by almost 18 points.

After Trump won in 2024, an old childhood friend who’d gone MAGA texted me a GIF of a penis ejaculating stars that reconstituted in the shape of a patriotic eagle. Flash forward a year later, he’s angry about gas prices, and, more striking to me, horrified by the war in Gaza. Colom’s protein ad is for him, and that nerve of enthusiasm it struck is going to keep firing.

Colom didn’t run on pronouns or any numerical strain of woke. He has said he is a fan of Trump’s border security, agrees with him on certain tax policies, and supports the Second Amendment. When asked about improving wages for government employees, he has said he wants to take care of teachers—and cops. As Colom told Jen Psaki in a televised interview, “We gotta be a party that represents our whole country, that understands all parts of the country.”

Now that people outside Mississippi are waking up to Colom, leftists may find his centrism irritating or even offensive. You may find a new reason to look down your noses at Mississippi. It all but promises to provide an opportunity.

So I will just leave you with the brothers Colom.

“They lied about Mississippi. Said that evil lived in one state, but the cotton cow fed the nation. Europe eating off the same plate. They lied about Mississippi. Said the torture was only here, but the banker’s desk in northern mills still dripping blood from hostage tears. They lied to us all. That’s propaganda machine on both sides, running on slander…. Denying the nation condoned it, denying the nation owned it, denying the nation fed off pain…. They lied about Mississippi. Said that evil lived in one state.”

He says, invoking Hamer, “Mississippi Freedom Party began again.”

And the vision of Scott: “There is nothing wrong with Mississippi that can’t be fixed by what’s right.”
