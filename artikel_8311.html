Natural Wonders: Crowley Lake Columns

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

It is low tide on Crowley Lake in Mono County, California. The water is still, the trout abundant, and one or two kayakers paddle about, enjoying the lack of tourists. But as you walk along the shore, something out of place appears: spiral pillars and arches that seem left over from some ancient civilization.

Crowley Lake is a man-made reservoir, administered by the Los Angeles Department of Water and Power. It was named after an important figure in the community, affectionately known as the Desert Padre. This beloved Catholic priest worked tirelessly to help a community that experienced water shortages throughout the 1920s and 1930s. Water from the nearby Owen Valley was diverted to Los Angeles, leaving very little for local residents.

Father Crowley made it his mission to bolster tourism in the area. The publicity led authorities to help locals by drawing up plans for a dam and reservoir. When Father Crowley died in a car accident, authorities completed the Long Valley Dam and named the reservoir Lake Crowley. If it weren’t for his intervention, the lake wouldn’t have existed.

As the years passed, visitors continued coming to Lake Crowley to fish, kayak, camp, and hike. Then something weird started to appear on the cliffsides. The banks appeared to be receding and hollowing out, revealing hundreds upon hundreds of spiral columns. Many onlookers believed they were man-made. Some compared the formations to Moorish architecture from North Africa and Spain.

Though the reservoir is man-made, the area’s history dates back millennia. Crowley Lake lies in the wider Long Valley Caldera, one of Earth’s largest calderas.

The caldera sits atop one of three ancient supervolcanoes in the United States. Around 760,000 years ago, this supervolcano erupted and spewed 650 cubic kilometers of rhyolite rock and ash into the air for a week. It covered the land in a blanket of ash and debris over one kilometer thick. And it left behind a 32km long, 18km wide, and 900m deep depression in the earth.

The depression formed when the subterranean magma chamber emptied onto the surface and the remnant shell of a roof collapsed. The blast and pyroclastic flow were so powerful that ash fell as far as Kansas. Along the rim are the lava dome complexes of Mammoth, Glass, and Bald Mountains. The caldera also hosts a cluster of hydrothermally active lakes called the Mammoth Lakes.

The main feature left by the eruption is the Bishop Tuff, a few square kilometers in area. A tuff is a rock of compacted volcanic ash. At the base of this tuff stand the Crowley Lake Columns. They range in thickness, average about six meters high, and are generally reddish, beige, grey, or white with a dark water line at their base. Some lean while others stand straight.

Attached by arches, the columns are so evenly spaced that to some visitors, it resembles an ancient temple. Horizontal cracks about 30cm apart add further detail to the columns. They look like they hold up the cliffs but they simply remained after the softer cliff rock started to erode.

The high levels of silica, sodium, and potassium in the columns make the rock conduct heat well and cool suddenly. This led to the cracks. Similar formations exist in Guatemala, Mexico, and New Mexico.

The few people who frequent Crowley Lake have long known about the columns. Only recently, social media has started to highlight them. This is due to its seclusion on the lake’s shoreline. The only ways to access them are on foot or by boat, kayak, or 4×4. The dirt road on which you turn from Highway 395 is so steep and uneven that it can bog down vehicle. Visitors tend to drive halfway, then walk.

Despite popular speculation that these columns are an example of columnar jointing, they are not. The geological process is different. With columnar jointing, the spaces are not so far apart. A study by geologists at UC Berkeley used a combination of computer models, X-ray diffraction, and electron microscope imagery to determine how they formed. It turns out that the culprit is simply…water.

“We hypothesize that the columns are a result of instabilities at the liquid water and steam interface as cold water seeped into the still-cooling Bishop Tuff,” says Noah Randolph-Flagg, the study’s lead scientist.

The columns formed through hydrothermal convection. Photo: Shutterstock

Essentially, rainwater seeped down into the hot ash and started to boil, producing a mineral glue called mordonite. The boiling water formed a column of steam which carried the mordonite upward through the rock. Thus, the mineral hardened the rock, leading it to remain standing while the surrounding rock eroded. This process is called hydrothermal convection or circulation.

According to Randolph-Flagg, it only took 100 years to form these columns. The study also states that “the liquid water–steam interface probably is the source of the instability that generates columnar flow.”

As for the erosion of the cliffside, the reservoir’s water levels fluctuate throughout the year with the seasons. After the completion of the reservoir, the columns started to reveal themselves. The constant rise and fall as well as a repeating freeze-thaw cycle caused the tuff to recede. It revealed the columns and also hollowed out the cliffside to form shallow caves. Take care when going into these little caves or pushing on the columns, as they might not be as stable. As the cliff continues to erode, we should see more of these columns.

The arches resemble Moorish architecture. Photo: Shutterstock

In terms of the Long Valley Caldera, scientists have closely monitored its activity, which has been increasing since the 1980s. Since it is a one of only 20 supervolcanoes in the world, they have paid close attention to its potential for eruption, despite its status as inactive.

There have been small earthquakes and signs of movement in the earth as if it were “breathing.” This is seen in Yellowstone, another supervolcano. While there are no immediate signs of an impending eruption, the caldera’s history and current activity suggest that it is possible.
