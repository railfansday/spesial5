Why the Alamodome's real NFL legacy endures in cities other than San Antonio

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

Through the bus window, a young NBA player saw something that caught his attention. And as the Golden State Warriors rolled past downtown San Antonio on their way to Frost Bank Center, he asked a longtime member of the team's traveling party an obvious question.

"Why is there a football stadium here?" the player wondered.

That was two Fridays ago, and it was far from the first time in its 33-year history that the Alamodome's mere existence has served as a source of consternation. On that Warriors bus, for instance, not even those who'd been coming here for decades were sure they understood the building's intended purpose.

Had they stayed through the weekend and secured a local newspaper, they might have learned a thing or two. Last Sunday, a front-page story by Express-News reporter Madison Iszler provided an in-depth look at the dome's uncertain future, which won't include another significant overhaul until at least 2035, when demolition might be one of the options on the table.

The Alamodome never became the permanent home of an NFL franchise, despite the unrealistic dreams of the officials and voters who approved its construction. It eventually became obsolete, not only by the standards of the world's richest sports league, but also by those of the College Football Playoff and men's basketball Final Fours now spurning it to set up shop elsewhere.

Let it not be said, however, that the Alamodome lacks a real NFL legacy. In its own way, it reshaped the geography of professional football. And across this country there are more than a couple of billionaires who owe the odd-looking structure on Montana Street a debt of gratitude, not to mention a chunk of their wealth.

After all, nobody loved the Alamodome more than the owners who used it as a threat.

Look around the NFL today. More than 12% of its franchises play in palaces funded at least in part by public money that was approved amid rumblings that San Antonio might be willing and able to steal those teams away.

The Arizona Cardinals play in State Farm Stadium, which was built with the help of $455 million provided by Maricopa County voters in 2001. The New Orleans Saints play in the Caesars Superdome, which was renovated after 2005's Hurricane Katrina with the help of a $41 million bond package.

The Minnesota Vikings play in U.S. Bank Stadium, which was built with a combined $500 million in state and city funds approved by the Minnesota legislature and Minneapolis city government in 2012. And the Las Vegas Raiders play in Allegiant Stadium, built after the Nevada legislature approved a plan in 2016 to use $750 million in public money.

In each of those cities, there had been media reports that the teams were considering a move to San Antonio, where a 64,000-seat dome was sitting empty.

Some of the flirting was overt. Before he sold the Vikings, local auto magnate Red McCombs scheduled a preseason game at the Alamodome while pushing for a new Minnesota stadium in 2001. Owner Tom Benson openly tried to keep the Saints here beyond the four games they played in the dome on a temporary basis in 2005, before then-commissioner Paul Tagliabue stepped in.

Then there were the truly shameless publicity stunts. In 2014, Raiders owner Mark Davis met with city officials as part of the charade that eventually led him to Las Vegas. Nine years earlier, Major League Baseball got in on the act, with Marlins president David Samson making a much-publicized trip to San Antonio even though there was no non-ridiculous way the Alamodome could have been configured for the sport, even on a short-term basis.

It didn't matter. State and local officials in Florida fretted about losing the team, and Miami-Dade County agreed to build what became LoanDepot Park with funds that one report showed eventually cost the public $1.2 billion.

Throw all that together, and one might argue that a dome built for less than $200 million in 1993 has wound up helping teams in other cities to secure more than 10 times that amount in public money.

Without the giant bargaining chip across the highway from the Tower of the Americas, maybe the Cardinals would still be in Glendale, Ariz., and maybe the Vikings would still be in Minneapolis, and maybe the Raiders would still be in Las Vegas. But maybe voters and legislators in those places might have been a little more willing to force owners to foot their fair share of the costs.

At any rate, there might come a time when a bus drives by one of those megastructures, and a rider poses an obvious question.

"Why is there a football stadium here?" someone might ask in Glendale, or Minneapolis, or Las Vegas.

And maybe someone else on the bus will know the answer.

It's at least in part because of the Alamodome.
