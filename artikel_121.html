Meanwhile, a senior Russian diplomat outlined a series of proposals that could form a part of any future security guarantees for Russia, Reuters reported.

The second possibility he cited included political agreements not to help each other’s adversaries in ​the event of aggression, like the pact Moscow has with Iran.

And the third option he presented were bilateral mutual non-aggression pacts.

Meanwhile, we are getting some lines from those present at the Abu Dhabi talks participating in the Ukrainian, Russian, and US delegations.

Zelenskyy also turns to Russia’s demands for security guarantees.

He points out that is not obvious given “no one has attacked Russia for many decades,” and yet “since the second world war, Russia has not gone through even one decade without starting a war or using military force against another country.”

“So when the Russians now talk about a security architecture for themselves, or about security guarantees for Russia, they are hardly talking about real security and peace.

What they really want is to freely interfere in the affairs of other countries, and to use force whenever they want, and to make sure that no one can support liberty inside Russia, because Putin wants to keep his personal power forever and pass on his imperialist and in many ways, simply fascist views to the next generations.”

He also says clearly that Europe “must be at the table” of any talks on ending the war in Ukraine, warning that “whenever Europe does not believe in its own strength, others try to impose their decisions on us.”

He gets warm applause and a standing ovation at the end of his speech.

Zelenskyy also talks about the need for Europe to develop “its own anti-ballistic defence programme,” Freyja to become independent of deliveries from overseas (mostly the US).

He also mentions the importance of drone technologies and learning from Ukraine’s real-life experience.

Zelenskyy brings up the Abu Dhabi discussions, but in quite general terms.

“Yesterday and today our Ukrainian team together with American team has been talking to the Russians in Abu Dhabi.

Putin started this war thinking he could break Ukraine in just days or few weeks. But here we are in the fifth year of the full scale war. The Russians have to sit across from our representatives and have to listen to Ukraine and to talk to us as equals about what comes next. They wanted to decide Ukraine’s future without us, without Ukrainian people, but they failed.”

Zelenskyy says the strait of Hormuz crisis creates a dangerous precedent, asking “what if Russia tries to do the same in Baltic Sea”?

“What if someone tries to repeat what happened in the Strait of Hormuz, this time in other major shipping routes and straits around the world? And who will protect European interests?”

He says the Iran crisis also has an effect on Ukraine, as is experiencing “the shortages of weapons we need for our defence.”

“Today, everyone in the world can see just how connected everything is in our world. Nothing stays local. Nothing. Especially wars and injustice,” he warns.

Zelenskyy then begins his speech proper, with a much more sombre point about the war in Ukraine.

He says that on 11 November, “people in many European countries remember those who lost their lives in war on the anniversary of the end of the first world war.”

“This is not just some distant history, unfortunately. What is happening in the world today brings our thoughts, our feelings, our fears back to those times when Europe and the whole world were turned upside down.”

He says “Russia has fallen for the same illusion as back then more than a hundred years ago. The illusion that it could simply grab more land, a few more colonies, more power and influence at the cost of Ukrainians independence.”

He also points to the events in the Middle East as an additional destabilising element, warning against “rising energy prices” and “one of the worst cost of living crises.”

Zelenskyy also jokes that given the warm relations between Denmark and Ukraine, he regrets the two countries “do not share a border.”

“You have much better neighbours,” he says in a swipe at Russia.

“After all, we have Russia as our neighbour and having a neighbour like Denmark is something any country would dream of right now.”

He then lands another joke about the US and Donald Trump:

“Maybe when someone in America wanted Greenland, he didn’t even realise that what he really wanted was not the land, but to become a little more Danish. And who knows, maybe that was exactly what he needed.”

Zelenskyy begins with words of thanks to PM Frederiksen and to Denmark on behalf of the Ukrainian people.

He opens with an interesting US-themed dig, as he notes he is the first foreign leader to address the Folketinget.

“I‘ve heard that many leaders wanted to be here, to speak here. One of them was even the president of the United States.

That was back in 1997. In the end, the American president spoke to the Danish people in the different place.”

Zelenskyy is now getting ready to address the Danish parliament.
