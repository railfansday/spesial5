OpenAI’s Codex helps amateur astronomer uncover two possible planets NASA’s data had missed

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

A SETI Institute astronomer independently recovered both signals after an OpenAI engineer used AI-generated software to search observations of 100,000 stars.

An AI-assisted search of NASA's archived telescope data has uncovered two possible planets, with preliminary checks by a professional astronomer recovering both signals.

In a Nov. 24 report, OpenAI detailed how two amateur astronomers used its Codex and Astra tools to search observations of millions of stars for previously overlooked planetary candidates.

Kevin Bond, an OpenAI engineer, used Codex to develop software that detects recurring declines in stellar brightness, a potential indication of a planet passing in front of its host star.

Bond combined existing detection methods into a program that deployed two AI agents. One identified promising signals, while the other subjected those findings to further examination.

Running the software against archived observations of approximately 100,000 stars from NASA's Kepler Space Telescope produced more than 200 signals, including 10 associated with previously confirmed planets.

Vishal Gajjar, an astronomer at the SETI Institute, independently recovered two of Bond's findings.

The first involved a relatively cool star, KIC 5689121, whose brightness declined every 13.8 days. OpenAI said the signal was consistent with a potential planet approximately 2.4 times Earth's diameter. The second, KIC 6691030, exhibited recurring dips every 4.8 days, suggesting a potential planet smaller than Neptune.

OpenAI says SETI Institute astronomer Vishal Gajjar recovered both dips in the same Kepler data Kevin Bond searched.

Each repeat interval gives astronomers a pattern to test in follow-up observations.

“Our initial checks are encouraging, and follow-up observations will tell us more.”

While Bond focused on Kepler's archived observations, another amateur astronomer, Ethan Byrd, used OpenAI's Astra to examine telescope data covering approximately 19 million stars.

Byrd developed two searches, including one targeting planetary candidates within habitable zones, where atmospheric conditions could allow liquid water to exist on a planet's surface.

His initial search identified 49 candidates. Byrd has submitted one to researchers working on NASA's Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS) mission, and two others are awaiting submission.

Astronomers have used artificial intelligence to identify planets for years. In 2017, NASA announced the discovery of Kepler-90i using a neural network trained to recognize planetary signals in telescope observations.

General-purpose AI tools now let amateur astronomers develop their own detection software and search vast archives for signals earlier searches may have missed.

However, recurring declines in stellar brightness can also result from eclipsing stars or fluctuations in telescope instruments.

Astronomers could investigate Bond's candidates by observing their host stars at the predicted transit intervals and measuring whether gravitational forces from potential orbiting planets cause detectable stellar movement.

Establishing whether either signal represents an undiscovered planet could take months or years, OpenAI said.
