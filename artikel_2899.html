An early breakdown in the polar vortex is threatening already depleted gas stocks

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

An unprecedented early breakdown of the stratospheric polar vortex and a record-breaking El Niño event has put energy traders on alert this winter, with analysts warning the weather and climate phenomena could have an outsized impact on Europe.

Meteorologists are currently observing a record early breakdown of the polar vortex, a large-scale circulation of strong winds that typically keeps extremely cold air over the North Pole.

Changes in the strength and shape of the polar vortex can influence weather patterns much lower down in the atmosphere, potentially bringing in colder weather to parts of Europe and North America.

This year, however, the collapse of the stratospheric polar vortex, or SPV, is taking place at the same time as an unusually powerful El Niño, a climate event that can boost global temperatures and disrupt rainfall.

The collision of these two events raises the risk of a prolonged cold spell that could lift heating demand and force European countries to draw down already-low reserves faster. That would leave the continent more reliant on imported liquefied natural gas (LNG) at a time when supplies from the Middle East are disrupted.

To be sure, Europe's natural gas storage levels started the winter at their lowest level in more than a decade.

The International Energy Agency warned earlier this month that low European storage levels and limited storage options in several LNG-importing countries in Asia and South America could intensify the competition for cargoes and put upward pressure on prices during periods of high demand.

Simon Lee, a lecturer in atmospheric science at the U.K.'s University of St Andrews, said the combination of a sudden stratospheric warming (SSW) with an El Niño event is not necessarily unusual, but meteorologists had been expecting a polar vortex breakdown in February — not as early as November.

The early SPV disruption is an "extremely rare" event that could influence winter weather in northwest Europe and the eastern U.S., Lee said, increasing the chance of colder conditions although likely not to the extent that a polar vortex event caused parts of Niagara Falls to partially freeze in January 2014.

"Geopolitics and weather are the two key volatility drives for energy and now they seem to be happening at the same time — and that's always the biggest risk for markets," Florence Schmit, senior energy strategist at Rabobank, told CNBC.

For months, meteorologists expected an El Niño event would result in a milder winter in Europe and relatively muted heating demand, Schmit said, which was broadly seen as welcome news given ongoing concerns over the continent's tight regional supplies for gas and crude oil.

The prospect of a colder-than-expected winter due to an early breakdown of the polar vortex could be "incredibly difficult" for Europe to navigate, Schmit warned. She noted that the region's gas storages have seen "very, very strong withdrawals" over the last two weeks, "which is quite unusual for this time of year."

"If you continue to see the same storage withdrawals over the next couple of months, that means energy prices will spike again — and they won't spike because of geopolitics. This time it will be the weather driving that spike and that volatility," Schmit said.

European gas prices have soared to levels not seen since the 2022 energy crisis in recent weeks, notching 103.9 euros ($113.99) per megawatt hour in late October. European benchmark Dutch TTF futures for December delivery stood at around 87.28 euros on Friday afternoon.

"We have never seen an early breakdown of the polar vortex and a super el Nino in the same winter so we have no historical precedent on how this will impact global gas demand," David Lewis, principal research analyst of gas and liquified natural gas at Wood Mackenzie, told CNBC by email.

El Niño is commonly associated with milder winters, while a SSW event typically leads to colder temperatures and higher snowfall, Lewis said, noting this could materialize in December and January within Europe.

"So far, the start of the European winter heating season has been quite cold but more importantly has seen low wind speeds across Northern Europe," Lewis said.

"The drawdown in stocks in the first half of November has been amongst the highest on record. With inventories starting the winter at their lowest level on record, any further deterioration in temperatures or wind speeds will have an outsized impact on European balances and lead to higher prices across Europe," he added.
