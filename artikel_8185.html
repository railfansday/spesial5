Hike of the Week: Explore the Dramatic—and Threatened—Big Bend on This Desert Peak

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

The first time I visited Santa Elena Canyon, the gorge in Big Bend National Park that straddles the border between the U.S. and Mexico, I was amazed not just at how beautiful it was, but how peaceful. The buff and black-streaked limestone walls soared 1,500 feet over the water, the afternoon sun streaming through the gap. The river was running low and slow, trickling over the mud and rocks, no more than knee deep. I rolled up my pant legs and waded into the water, toward that invisible line in the middle of the river that split two countries.

Nine years later, Big Bend is still beautiful. Covering an area bigger than Rhode Island, this stretch of West Texas desert is home to some of the state’s most moving scenery, from river canyons to rocky peaks, with animal and plant life to match. Hikers willing to put in the work on the area’s rugged trails may spot javelinas rooting in the undergrowth or nocturnal, raccoon-like ringtails climbing up the cliffs and trees of the Chisos Mountains; they’ll see prickly pear cactus and ocotillo, a spindly, Seussian shrub equipped with vicious spines, growing wild in the desert flats. (That border, however, may soon be far more concrete. Read on for more on that.)

One of the best ways to see it today is the hike up Emory Peak. At 7,825 feet, Emory is the highest point in Big Bend and the Chisos, and enjoys commanding views of the terrain for miles around. Getting there, however, takes some muscle: The rough, 9.7-mile hike to the top ascends singletrack and rock stairs through oak, piñon, and juniper forest before ending with a final, dramatic scramble to the peak’s exposed summit. November, when summer’s broiling temperatures finally cool down, is an ideal time to hike it. (Note: There is no water at all along the Emory Peak Trail, and the NPS suggests hikers carry at least one liter per person per hour.)

The round trip takes about 6 to 8 hours, and you’ll want to leave some time to relax afterward. Santa Elena Canyon’s picnic area is an ideal spot for an early dinner, and cap off the night at the park’s free riverside hot springs. (Due to recent flood damage, reaching both of these highlights is currently harder than usual. The paved road between Cottonwood Campground and Santa Elena is closed at the time of writing; drivers with a true 4-wheel drive vehicle and high clearance can get there on Old Maverick Road. Due to another closure, getting to the hot springs requires a 4-mile round-trip, relatively flat hike along a gravel road. Consider it part of the adventure.)

Starting at the Chisos Basin Visitors Center, take the Laguna Meadows trail for about a third of a mile to a fork, where you’ll hang a left on the Pinnacles Trail. Follow it as it steadily climbs, traversing desert flats and rocky slopes with the sheer top of Emory looming in the background. After another 3.4 miles, the trail reaches a saddle below the top of the peak. Continue on the Emory Peak Trail for another 1.5 miles, then tackle a final, somewhat exposed 30-foot scramble to the top of the mountain and 360-degree views of the park below. Retrace your steps to the trailhead, or break up the hike by reserving one of the Pinnacles campsites ahead of your trip.

In an 8-1 decision on November 9, the Supreme Court approved a request by the Trump administration to resume construction of a border wall through Big Bend, ruling against plaintiffs who had argued that the barrier would decimate rare plant species and cut off migratory routes for animals, and that it risked damaging ancient rock art and Native heritage sites. In Santa Elena Canyon itself, the Department of Homeland Security plans to install vehicle barriers and surveillance infrastructure and clear patrol roads through the area. Conservation groups and state officials like Governor-Elect Gina Hinojosa say they’ll continue to push to end construction in the park.
