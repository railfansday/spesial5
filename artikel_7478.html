Stations in New Hampshire, Wisconsin, Massachusetts, and Utah all climbed in our regular ranking of public radio stations between July and September.

More than a year ago, NHPR reporter Todd Bookman started asking questions about “an irregular personnel maneuver” involving the chief justice of New Hampshire’s Supreme Court. Bookman’s reporting ultimately became “Exhibit A” in the ethics probe that led to the justice’s resignation in September.

The bombshell resignation news was NHPR’s biggest story in September — and of 2026 to date. The story helped push NHPR up several spots in our regular ranking of public radio websites. NHPR saw visits rise 22% that month, according to Similarweb visit figures compiled by Nieman Lab.

The Chicago Sun-Times, owned by Chicago Public Media, held onto the top spot, followed by Gothamist (from WNYC) and Minnesota Public Radio. As Nieman Lab senior writer Joshua Benton wrote when introducing the rankings, which we’ve also done for local newspapers and local nonprofit news sites, not all markets are created equal. Public radio stations serving major metropolitan areas with lots of college graduates have a bigger potential audience than stations serving smaller communities and areas with fewer resources. And, of course, Similarweb provides an estimate; internal metrics may differ. Still, the rankings are a valuable window into some industry-wide trends, and often an opportunity to highlight local news organizations punching above their weight.

Like several public media sites on our list, election season provided a bump to NHPR. New Hampshire held its primary on Sept. 8 and the statewide public radio station saw a traffic boost from results pages and campaign coverage, said NHPR director of audience engagement Casey McDermott. She described September as a solid month, not an outlier. “But what makes September an exceptionally cool case study is that it reflects two fundamentals at the heart of our mission: a commitment to fresh, local reporting and a renewed internal investment in digital publishing,” McDermott said. “[Bookman’s piece] wasn’t just a standard breaking news item — it was the culmination of months of meticulous accountability reporting,” McDermott added. On the audience side, McDermott said that, in addition to a boost from NHPR’s partners at NPR and Google News, the chief justice story benefited from X (formerly known as Twitter). WBUR had the biggest raw gain of any station in September. Tiffany Campbell, WBUR’s executive editor for digital audience and news, said organic search traffic and Google Discover helped. “We’ve been working on building out many referral streams,” Campbell said, “but in early September Google delivered a lot of organic search audience via a couple of strong stories.” “Our referral diversification aims to be less reliant on Google organic search but it’s also important to take advantage when it delivers,” she added. Those strong stories? An explainer on the insanity defense during jury deliberations in Lindsay Clancy’s murder trial, results from the Massachusetts primary held on Sept. 1, and a Here & Now interview with Associated Press photographer Richard Drew — who took the “Falling Man” photo — on the 25th anniversary of 9/11. All three stories drew both local and national readers, Campbell said. Campbell credited the steady rise in traffic to “things we’re always doing: in-depth local coverage, comprehensive reporting on a closely watched election (which was an early indication of interest in the general election in November) and relevant enterprise reporting.” The Milwaukee-based WUWM was the fastest climber in the top 25 this quarter. The station nearly doubled its visits between June and September, rising in our ranking from No. 31 to No. 15. According to Similarweb, the station’s visits rose 32% in August and by another 17% in September. Michelle Maternowski, WUWM audience innovation manager, said the surge started with Wisconsin’s August 11 primary. “We published our Voter Guide in time for that and saw a decent amount come to our meet the candidate posts,” she said. Readers were especially interested in news and information on the lesser-known races. “We see lots of traffic to the down ballot, statewide races — like state treasurer and secretary of state.” And then there’s the gift that keeps on giving. Published in 2019, WUWM’s segment on the 150th birthday of the periodic table still ranks “really high in search results.” “I think once the school year started up again, a lot of students were searching their periodic tables,” Maternowski said. “It is very silly that this story continues to get so much traffic.” If you only look at the top 25 stations, you’ll miss some impressive jumps. The Salt Lake City-based KUER, for example, took the biggest leap in ranking of any station in July. The station’s visits rose 122% that month, according to Similarweb’s numbers. “The numbers don’t lie,” Jim Hill, digital manager in the KUER newsroom, confirmed. “July was a monster for us.” Hill had expected a “plateau or small retreat” after a record year in 2025 — which included the Charlie Kirk killing and a shooting at a No Kings Protest in Utah. Instead, the news site has been ahead of last year’s pace. Total users are up 19% over last year, even after attempts to filter out bots, and social media referrals falling by 54%. (Hill noted the station’s social media producer was laid off and that KUER is attempting to “pivot to video/reels.”) The station saw “some runaway Google Discover success” last year but, in 2026, clicks from Google Discover are “less than half” of its 2025 figures. Hill credited “engagement reporting and audience-valued topics” that have “kept audiences reading” despite the headwinds. KUER also grew its “owned” email audience and drove 34% more users to the website compared to 2025, according to Hill. All in all, July was “very hit-heavy,” Hill said. The top 10 stories — led by Donald Trump’s decision to dramatically shrink two Utah national monuments — accounted for 64% of the month’s views. The station also saw a major boost from NPR, including multiple stories from a week-long hearing in the Charlie Kirk case. “Our newsroom was the ground team for NPR, so the network highlighted web coverage nationally,” Hill said. Most of the audience came from outside Utah. Hill said NPR can be directly attributed for 32% of sessions, Google Search 14%, and Google News 13.5%. Two wildfire stories also gave major traffic boosts. But audiences especially loved hearing about Utah getting full-color specialty license plates. The story was KUER’s top Google Discover story in July and also got a significant bump (14% of visits) from Reddit. “We affectionately refer to stories like this as license plate news,” Hill said. “It always hits with the audience.” Sure enough, a second plate story, on Utah cracking down on the infamous “Montana loophole,” ended the month as the No. 2 Discover story. WBUR, one of two Boston-based public media stations, grew every month this quarter, moving from 1.45 million visits to 2.1 million in September, according to Similarweb. WBUR had the biggest raw gain of any station in September.

Tiffany Campbell, WBUR’s executive editor for digital audience and news, said organic search traffic and Google Discover helped.

“We’ve been working on building out many referral streams,” Campbell said, “but in early September Google delivered a lot of organic search audience via a couple of strong stories.”

“Our referral diversification aims to be less reliant on Google organic search but it’s also important to take advantage when it delivers,” she added.

Those strong stories? An explainer on the insanity defense during jury deliberations in Lindsay Clancy’s murder trial, results from the Massachusetts primary held on Sept. 1, and a Here & Now interview with Associated Press photographer Richard Drew — who took the “Falling Man” photo — on the 25th anniversary of 9/11. All three stories drew both local and national readers, Campbell said.

Campbell credited the steady rise in traffic to “things we’re always doing: in-depth local coverage, comprehensive reporting on a closely watched election (which was an early indication of interest in the general election in November) and relevant enterprise reporting.”

The Milwaukee-based WUWM was the fastest climber in the top 25 this quarter. The station nearly doubled its visits between June and September, rising in our ranking from No. 31 to No. 15.

According to Similarweb, the station’s visits rose 32% in August and by another 17% in September. Michelle Maternowski, WUWM audience innovation manager, said the surge started with Wisconsin’s August 11 primary.

“We published our Voter Guide in time for that and saw a decent amount come to our meet the candidate posts,” she said. Readers were especially interested in news and information on the lesser-known races. “We see lots of traffic to the down ballot, statewide races — like state treasurer and secretary of state.”

And then there’s the gift that keeps on giving. Published in 2019, WUWM’s segment on the 150th birthday of the periodic table still ranks “really high in search results.”

“I think once the school year started up again, a lot of students were searching their periodic tables,” Maternowski said. “It is very silly that this story continues to get so much traffic.”

If you only look at the top 25 stations, you’ll miss some impressive jumps. The Salt Lake City-based KUER, for example, took the biggest leap in ranking of any station in July. The station’s visits rose 122% that month, according to Similarweb’s numbers.

“The numbers don’t lie,” Jim Hill, digital manager in the KUER newsroom, confirmed. “July was a monster for us.”

Hill had expected a “plateau or small retreat” after a record year in 2025 — which included the Charlie Kirk killing and a shooting at a No Kings Protest in Utah.

Instead, the news site has been ahead of last year’s pace. Total users are up 19% over last year, even after attempts to filter out bots, and social media referrals falling by 54%. (Hill noted the station’s social media producer was laid off and that KUER is attempting to “pivot to video/reels.”) The station saw “some runaway Google Discover success” last year but, in 2026, clicks from Google Discover are “less than half” of its 2025 figures. Hill credited “engagement reporting and audience-valued topics” that have “kept audiences reading” despite the headwinds. KUER also grew its “owned” email audience and drove 34% more users to the website compared to 2025, according to Hill.

All in all, July was “very hit-heavy,” Hill said. The top 10 stories — led by Donald Trump’s decision to dramatically shrink two Utah national monuments — accounted for 64% of the month’s views. The station also saw a major boost from NPR, including multiple stories from a week-long hearing in the Charlie Kirk case.

“Our newsroom was the ground team for NPR, so the network highlighted web coverage nationally,” Hill said. Most of the audience came from outside Utah. Hill said NPR can be directly attributed for 32% of sessions, Google Search 14%, and Google News 13.5%.

Two wildfire stories also gave major traffic boosts. But audiences especially loved hearing about Utah getting full-color specialty license plates. The story was KUER’s top Google Discover story in July and also got a significant bump (14% of visits) from Reddit.

“We affectionately refer to stories like this as license plate news,” Hill said. “It always hits with the audience.”

Sure enough, a second plate story, on Utah cracking down on the infamous “Montana loophole,” ended the month as the No. 2 Discover story.
