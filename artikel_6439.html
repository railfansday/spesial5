Researchers warn that warmer weather is helping mosquitoes spread dangerous diseases like malaria and dengue

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

A century of data shows bed nets, drugs and growing cities explain much of why malaria and dengue went separate ways as the planet warmed.

Scientists have long predicted that a warmer world would bring more disease from mosquitoes, and dengue has kept rising along with the heat. Malaria went the other way.

Over a century of increasing temperatures, the area where malaria was a constant presence shrank to about half of what it had been, while dengue infections climbed to record highs.

According to a new review of the evidence, much of that split comes from outside the weather. People pushed malaria back with decades of bed nets, spraying, and drugs, while the growth of cities favored the mosquitoes that carry dengue.

Toby Cumming, a research fellow at the Health and Climate Initiative in Monash University’s School of Public Health, led the work with three colleagues. It is a review of studies that other teams have already published, not a new experiment.

In an interview with Earth.com, Cumming said the climate in many parts of the world is becoming more suitable for diseases spread by mosquitoes. “We cannot draw a straight line from climate suitability to rates of malaria and dengue transmission,” Cumming added.

Both diseases reach people through a bite. Malaria comes from a parasite carried by Anopheles mosquitoes, which bite mostly at night. Dengue comes from a virus carried by Aedes mosquitoes, which bite during the day.

Mosquitoes are cold-blooded, so their body temperature follows the air around them. At 59°F (15°C), a young Aedes aegypti takes 40 days to become an adult. At 95°F (35°C), it takes seven.

Warmth also makes mosquitoes bite more often. It also shortens the time the parasite or virus needs to develop inside the insect before it can infect a person.

There’s a ceiling, though. Past a certain point, heat starts to hurt the mosquito. Models suggest malaria spreads best at about 77°F to 82°F (25°C to 28°C). For dengue, it’s about 79°F to 84°F (26°C to 29°C).

Rain works in both directions. Heavy downpours can wash eggs and larvae away, while drought can leave stagnant pools in riverbeds and push families to store water in open containers near their homes.

Asked how people can protect themselves from dengue, Cumming said the best thing is to avoid bites, with long, loose clothing and insect repellent. Emptying containers that hold water around the home helps too.

The authors put numbers on how much warming has already shifted the odds. Between the 1950s and the decade ending in 2024, the climate’s estimated potential to spread dengue rose 12 percent for Aedes aegypti and 49 percent for its cousin, Aedes albopictus.

Over the same stretch, the area where the climate suits malaria grew by only about 2 percent. In Africa, the malaria map is moving.

One analysis cited in the review found that the elevation limit of malaria mosquitoes rose about 21 feet (6.5 meters) a year from 1898 to 2016.

Even so, the authors call the evidence for climate-driven shifts in malaria more contested than for dengue.

Dengue’s spread is easier to see. The disease has spread locally in places where it hadn’t before, including the southern United States, and it’s showing up more often in mainland France. Earth.com has also reported on rising dengue risk in California.

More than 14 million dengue cases were reported to the World Health Organization in 2024. That was more than double the previous annual record.

The longest view comes from a 2010 analysis in the journal Nature. Around 1900, malaria was a constant presence across an estimated 58 percent of the world’s land. By 2007, that figure had fallen to 30 percent.

Within the smaller area, far fewer people carried the parasite. In Africa, the share of people infected with the deadliest malaria parasite fell from 40 percent in 1900 to 1929, to 24 percent in 2010 to 2015.

Europe shows the same pattern. Malaria began to fade there in the 1800s while temperatures were rising, and the review credits better living conditions, better health care, and improved irrigation and drainage.

Cold didn’t stop the disease, either. Malaria was common in the marshy parishes of England from the 16th to the 18th centuries, during an especially cold period. In Finland, mosquitoes survived freezing winters by sheltering inside houses and stables.

The two diseases differ first in the tools people built to fight them. Efforts to wipe out malaria go back to the 1950s, with house spraying and mass drug campaigns, and later with bed nets treated with insecticide.

Global spending on malaria rose from $1.5 billion to $4.3 billion between 2000 and 2016. Over that time, the yearly rate of new cases fell 36 percent and the death rate fell 60 percent.

“What started as a climate story – the impact of temperature, rainfall and humidity on malaria and dengue – became a human story,” Cumming told Earth.com.

Dengue had no matching toolkit. There are still no drugs that treat the virus, vaccines are only now arriving, and bed nets do little against mosquitoes that bite in daylight.

And mosquitoes are building resistance to insecticides, a problem the authors flag for both diseases.

Cities are the second difference. Aedes mosquitoes lay eggs in gutters and water containers, and cities offer plenty of those, along with lots of people to bite.

Anopheles prefer more natural pools, which cities tend to lack, so urban growth tends to cut malaria.

The authors flag one important exception. Anopheles stephensi, a malaria mosquito from Asia that breeds in artificial containers, has now settled into cities in the Horn of Africa.

The good news on malaria has slowed. New cases per 1,000 people at risk fell from 79 in 2000 to 59 in 2015. By 2024, the figure was back up to 64.

In 18 countries, the rate rose by at least 70 percent between 2015 and 2024. Resistance to key malaria drugs is a growing concern too.

Extreme weather can undo progress quickly. After record flooding hit Pakistan in 2022, malaria cases there rose fivefold.

Asked what should be done first to curb the spread, Cumming said the answer is a renewed commitment from the global community to fight malaria and dengue.

That includes scaling up public health measures that work and speeding up research on new ways to prevent and treat both diseases.

The forecasts don’t agree on malaria. Some models predict it will spread into new areas as the planet warms. Others predict that hotter, drier conditions will shrink it in parts of Africa where it’s common today.

Adding wealth to a model changes the picture. Using climate alone, one model estimated that 5.2 billion people would be at risk of malaria by 2050. When it also counted each country’s economic output per person, the estimate dropped to 2.0 billion.

Much of the temperature evidence also comes from lab studies that were never checked in the field. And mosquitoes may adapt to local heat, which fixed temperature rules can’t capture.

For dengue, newer tools are arriving. One vaccine has been licensed in many countries since 2022. The World Mosquito Program, a not-for-profit group owned by Monash University, releases mosquitoes carrying a bacterium called Wolbachia, which makes them less able to spread the virus.

It’s still unclear whether those tools can do for dengue what bed nets and drugs did for malaria. In order to find out, researchers will need years of dengue case records from the places that use them.

The full study was published in the journal Science Advances.
