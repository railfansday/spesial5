Two Overlooked “Forever Chemicals” Linked To More Than Double the Risk of Liver Cancer

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Two little-studied “forever chemicals” have been linked to substantially higher odds of liver cancer in a new University of Southern California (USC) study. People with the highest blood concentrations of either chemical had roughly two to two-and-a-half times the odds of developing the disease compared with those with the lowest concentrations.

The association was especially strong among people genetically predisposed to fatty liver disease. Published in Environmental Health Perspectives, the findings suggest that inherited susceptibility may influence how environmental chemical exposures affect cancer risk.

Researchers analyzed blood samples from 223 people who later developed hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), the most common form of liver cancer, and 223 matched controls. The samples were collected an average of 7.8 years before diagnosis as part of the Multiethnic Cohort Study (MEC), which has tracked more than 215,000 participants in California and Hawaii since the 1990s.

Of the 12 per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) measured, two showed significant associations with liver cancer: perfluoroheptanoic acid (PFHpA) and perfluorononanoic acid (PFNA).

“The concerning thing about our results is that the strongest associations were not in the most studied legacy PFAS,” said lead author Jesse Goodrich, PhD, assistant professor at the Keck School of Medicine of USC. “They are among the newer PFAS, and we don’t have much information about their effect on human health.”

The researchers also examined whether inherited susceptibility to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), commonly called fatty liver disease, influenced cancer risk. They found that the association between PFHpA exposure and liver cancer was considerably stronger among participants with high genetic susceptibility to MASLD. No statistically significant association was detected among those with low susceptibility.

“Our study shows that genetic differences in how the liver processes fats may play an important role in determining susceptibility,” Goodrich said. “You have this one-two hit: first, your genes and then PFAS impacting this pathway.”

PFAS exposure may disrupt liver fat metabolism, contributing to the fat accumulation associated with MASLD. Over time, the condition can progress to inflammation, scarring, and cancer. MASLD is an increasingly important driver of HCC, which has a five-year survival rate below 20%.

“PFAS have been linked to an increase in fat accumulation in your liver, and over time, that can actually increase the risk of liver cancer,” Goodrich said.

PFAS are found in products such as nonstick cookware, food packaging, stain-resistant fabrics, and firefighting foams. Many resist degradation and persist in the environment for decades. These chemicals have been detected in drinking water across roughly half of U.S. water systems, while an estimated 99% of the global population has detectable PFAS in their blood.

Most regulatory attention has focused on older compounds such as perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA) and perfluorooctanesulfonic acid (PFOS). PFHpA was developed as an alternative to these chemicals, which were phased out of many applications because of health and environmental concerns.

“PFOA and PFOS have received much of the scientific and regulatory attention, however, they represent only a small part of the PFAS family,” said senior author Vaia Lida Chatzi, MD, PhD, professor at the Keck School of Medicine. “Our findings show why we need to look beyond those legacy compounds. We can miss important associations with liver health if we study only the chemicals we already know best.”

The PFHpA finding also supports results from an independent National Cancer Institute study published two months earlier. However, these observational studies cannot establish that PFAS exposure causes liver cancer.

Identifying people with both elevated PFAS exposure and high genetic susceptibility could eventually help guide earlier liver cancer screening. Reducing exposure may also offer a potential prevention strategy, although its effect on cancer risk remains uncertain.

“Our study actually provides something that is actionable,” Goodrich said. “You can make lifestyle and diet changes and there are certain types of water filters you can put in that can reduce exposure. And if you have the information on genetic susceptibility, that could also provide additional evidence for things that you could do to mitigate risk.”

The study is the first publication from a USC Norris Comprehensive Cancer Center research program supported by a $3.4 million federal grant to investigate PFAS and liver cancer.

“This first paper from our NIH-funded study is an important step. Drawing on the diverse Multiethnic Cohort, it adds to the evidence that some PFAS may be associated with liver cancer,” said senior author V. Wendy Setiawan, PhD, professor at USC. “We hope this work will help build the evidence needed to reduce harmful exposures and inform prevention efforts for diverse communities.”

The team plans to investigate additional environmental exposures to better understand how chemicals encountered throughout life may contribute to cancer.
