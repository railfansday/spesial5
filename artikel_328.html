Rocket Report: A weekend of ups and downs; Stoke celebrates engine milestone

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

Russia launched a Soyuz rocket with satellites to help enable drone attacks over Ukraine.

Welcome to Edition 9.20 of the Rocket Report! Last weekend was a picture of the US launch business in miniature, and emblematic of the opposing trajectories of two of the nation’s most experienced rocket makers. First, on Saturday, SpaceX completed a perfect midair catch of its Starship upper stage at its launch pad in Texas after spending nearly a day in orbit. The Super Heavy booster was lost at sea, but SpaceX proved it can recover that piece of the rocket on previous flights. While SpaceX showed it is well on the way to fielding a fully reusable rocket, United Launch Alliance suffered its first-ever total launch failure with the Vulcan rocket. That is terrible news for not only ULA, but also the US Space Force and Amazon, which have booked some 70 launches on Vulcan over the next few years. Your faithful correspondents will take a break from the Rocket Report next week for the Thanksgiving holiday, so we’ll see you again the first week of December.

Europe’s leading satellite startup eyes Italian rocket maker. Finnish satellite developer ICEYE announced Tuesday that it is seeking to buy Italian rocket-maker Avio for more than $2.2 billion in a transaction aimed at creating Europe’s first true vertically integrated end-to-end space company, Ars reports. Avio builds the Vega C rocket, a solid-fueled launcher designed to carry up to 3.3 metric tons (7,300 pounds) of payload to low-Earth orbit. ICEYE is, by far, the most successful space startup in Europe, with a valuation of more than $12 billion after the company’s most recent fundraising round in June.

Fulfilling a need … In a statement, ICEYE said it wants to join forces with Avio to meet Europe’s need for “sovereign space and defense capabilities.” ICEYE manufactures and operates its own satellites for radar surveillance and sells data to commercial customers and national governments, including Ukraine, which uses ICEYE imagery for targeting and tracking Russian troop movements. Most of ICEYE’s satellites have launched on SpaceX Falcon 9 rockets. With Falcon 9’s retirement on the horizon, ICEYE’s satellites will need rides to orbit. Avio’s Vega C rocket has a healthy backlog of contracts for European governments, but it hasn’t been a hit commercially. Investment from ICEYE could shore up Avio’s Vega rocket program and perhaps accelerate development of a new rocket.

NATO unveils launch brokerage initiative. The North Atlantic Treaty Organization is contracting commercial rockets and spaceports to grow its satellite launch network and help neutralize risks to the space systems on which it relies, Bloomberg reports. NATO launched two initiatives on Tuesday: Starlift, through which participating countries can secure slots on commercial rockets, and the Global Data Marketplace, which will bolster the alliance’s military operations in space with commercial data and services.

Swimming in launches … Starlift will allow participating countries—Belgium, Canada, Finland, Germany, Italy, the Netherlands, Norway, Sweden, Turkey, the UK, and South Korea—to pool their military launch requirements and secure launch slots on commercial rockets. The NATO initiative will also prepare and test procedures for short-notice launches, as might be needed in a crisis. Bloomberg reported NATO’s tech agency will offer about $2.7 billion in contracts over the next two years, including to commercial space companies, AI, cyber, and command-and-control networks.

Stoke wraps up booster engine development. Stoke Space has completed development of its Zenith engine, the methane-fueled powerplant that will propel the company’s medium-class Nova rocket off the launch pad. Stoke made the announcement in a social media post Thursday. “As flight engines clear acceptance testing, we’re shipping them down to Cape Canaveral for integration with Nova’s first stage—every week,” Stoke said. Seven Zenith engines will power the Nova rocket’s reusable first stage booster, combining to generate about 700,000 pounds of thrust at full throttle.

All eyes on Stage 2 … Stoke announced it cleared a “proto-qualification” milestone on Stage 1 earlier this year. Stage 2 is undergoing cryogenic proof testing and a hot-fire test campaign at Stoke’s test complex in rural Washington. Nova’s second stage is the real innovation, with a unique arrangement of 24 thrusters surrounding a regeneratively cooled heat shield to protect it during reentry back into Earth’s atmosphere. With this, Stoke aims to fully reuse both Nova stages. The first launch is currently expected sometime next year.

Soyuz flies from Russia’s Far East. A Soyuz-2.1b rocket made its first and only launch from Russia’s Vostochny Cosmodrome this year, RussianSpaceWeb.com reports. The rocket’s Fregat upper stage deployed a new pair of high-resolution imaging satellites, along with a cluster of smaller payloads, following liftoff from Russia’s Vostochny launch base on November 14 (local time). Vostochny, located in Russia’s Far East, hosted its first space launch in 2016. By some measures, Russia spent more than $5 billion building Vostochny, only to use it for an average of two launches per year.

What was onboard? … Soyuz and Fregat carried two dual-use imaging surveillance satellites. A pair of CubeSat-class satellites for a Russian arms manufacturer were also onboard. These satellites are owned by NPO Kaisant, which specializes in drone communications and counter-drone weapons. NPO Kaisant says the satellites will join a constellation providing secure communications to Russian drones over Ukraine. A total of 12 secondary payloads were reported to be onboard, according to Roscosmos.

China’s LandSpace reports rocket reuse milestone. Three months after landing its first orbital-class rocket booster, Chinese rocket company LandSpace claims it has completed “ground acceptance and testing” of the nine methane-fueled engines recovered from the first stage of its Zhuque-3 rocket, according to Guandian News, a Chinese outlet focusing on the country’s financial and economic sector. The engines were disassembled, inspected, refurbished, reassembled, and retested after flying to space and back in August.

But wait … LandSpace’s Zhuque-3 is one of two Chinese rockets to successfully land its booster after an orbital launch attempt. The company did not say whether any of the nine engines will fly again, but don’t count on the entire Zhuque-3 booster stage from the August flight making another trip to space. The Zhuque-3 first stage toppled over soon after a seemingly flawless touchdown at LandSpace’s downrange recovery zone in northwestern China. Based on this week’s news, it appears engineers were able to salvage the engines.

SpaceX makes a historic catch. SpaceX, nearly a quarter of a century after its founding, took a hugely significant step toward developing and optimizing a massive, rapidly reusable rocket with the first successful catch of its enormous Starship upper stage on November 14, Ars reports. It’s hard to overstate the significance of the moment. SpaceX’s ability to recover and quickly relaunch Starships and Super Heavy boosters is central to all of the company’s lofty commercial ambitions and many of America’s high-level strategic imperatives, from national security to maintaining dominance in space exploration.

What was it like to be there? … Ars Space Editor Eric Berger covered the launch and catch of Starship from a nearby perch at Starbase, Texas. “Watching Starship launch from here a little less than a day earlier, and knowing it had orbited the planet multiple times, added to the sense of unreality in seeing this spaceship return to Earth under its own power and be captured by the tower,” Berger writes. “It felt like stepping into the future. SpaceX has dazzled us before by landing rockets on barges and returning humans to space with Crew Dragon. But this flight took things to another level. Starships are huge. They carry a huge amount of energy coming back from orbit. And until a few years ago, it seemed beyond fantastical to think about capturing a rocket with what are essentially outsized chopsticks. Nevertheless, it happened.”

Vulcan fails to deliver for Amazon. United Launch Alliance’s Vulcan rocket and 40 Amazon Leo broadband satellites fell back to Earth minutes after liftoff Sunday, a crushing setback that will ripple across all sectors of the US space industry, Ars reports. Aside from ULA itself, the fallout from the failure will strike the US military and Amazon hardest. Sunday’s mission was the first of 38 Vulcan launches booked by Amazon to build out its network of broadband Internet satellites aimed at competing with SpaceX’s Starlink. Vulcan is also on contract to launch roughly half of the US Space Force’s national security missions over the next few years.

Identifying the cause … ULA said Wednesday that the failure was caused by inadequate fuel supply to the first stage engines, Ars reports in a follow-up story. “ULA has completed an initial integrated review of flight data from the Leo 1 launch anomaly that occurred last Sunday,” the company said in a statement. “While work remains to determine cause, preliminary indications are that the low booster thrust was the result of inadequate fuel supply from the vehicle to the BE-4 engines, not from the performance of the engines.” This is fantastic news for Blue Origin, which builds the BE-4 and uses a similar design on its own New Glenn rocket. Vulcan’s solid rocket boosters also performed as expected Sunday after experiencing nozzle failures on two prior Vulcan flights.

Google taps Blue Origin for AI launch. Blue Origin announced Thursday that its New Glenn rocket will launch data center satellites for Google in 2029 as part of that company’s Project Suncatcher program, Ars reports. Google disclosed it was working on scalable orbiting data centers about a year ago and characterized the project as one of its research “moonshots.” The mission on New Glenn is designed to deploy satellites, each with computing capacity at roughly the scale of a data center rack, into Sun-synchronous orbit where they can receive nearly continuous solar energy.

Hitching a ride … Google snatched up a ride on New Glenn just as US commercial heavy-lift rockets are filling up their capacity through the year 2030. The Google mission will fly on the 7×2 variant of the New Glenn rocket, the smaller version of the vehicle that has launched three times to date. The purpose of the 2029 launch will likely be to test the ability to network solar-powered satellites equipped with AI chips and demonstrate machine-learning compute in space. Perhaps the bigger question for Google is not whether it can make this technology work—but whether it is tenable for a company to have ambitions for a large constellation of orbital data centers in the 2030s without its own launch vehicle.

Relativity makes progress with Terran R. Another new rocket is moving closer to the launch pad. Relativity Space’s partially reusable Terran R rocket could launch sometime next year if engineers can finish development with no major setbacks. Relativity posted an update on YouTube this week, detailing several recent achievements at several of the company’s locations across the country. At Relativity headquarters in Southern California, teams have completed Terran R’s first stage thrust structure with a full set of 13 Aeon engines, fluid lines, and avionics. The engine compartment is now being integrated with Stage 1 tankage. Terran R’s second stage recently completed its first all-up test firing at Stennis Space Center, Mississippi.

Meanwhile, at Cape Canaveral … Relativity is also moving forward with the Terran R launch pad at Cape Canaveral Space Force Station, Florida. The launch pad’s lightning protection towers are now complete, as is the steel framing for Terran R’s launch table. Work continues on the rocket’s transporter-erector, strongback, and the launch pad’s propellant storage tanks.
