2 Montgomery County magnet stories raise 1 question about access

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

“Minorities in Magnets” was the winner of the ABA Section of Civil Rights and Social Justice’s 2026 High School Civil Rights Film Competition.

A high school filmmaker saw a problem in Montgomery County, Maryland’s selective programs: Lack of representation of students of color had an impact on their educational experiences. At nearly the same time, a lawsuit challenging admissions to the county’s middle school magnet programs reached the U.S. Supreme Court.

Although these stories involve different programs and raise different questions, they share an important issue: How do we determine whether an admissions system provides fair access to educational opportunities, and what evidence should matter?

The ABA Section of Civil Rights and Social Justice recognized Minorities in Magnets, directed by Toftaa Fedhaa, as the first place winner of the section’s 2026 High School Civil Rights Film Competition. Her film explores the experiences of students of color in the Montgomery County Public Schools’ high school magnet programs and the effects of their underrepresentation.

Separately, on Oct. 19, the Supreme Court agreed to hear Association for Education Fairness v. Montgomery County Board of Education. The case concerns admissions to four selective middle school magnet programs, not the high school programs discussed in the film.

While the court did not rule on whether the district’s admissions policy is discriminatory, it agreed to consider a narrower legal question about the evidence plaintiffs must present when challenging a facially neutral admissions policy as racially discriminatory.

The Association for Education Fairness alleges that Montgomery County’s admissions policies intentionally disadvantaged Asian American students. The school district maintains that its admissions system is designed to provide equitable access to rigorous educational opportunities.

After initially challenging a “field test,” the plaintiffs challenged a revised lottery-based system introduced for the 2021-2022 school year. That system screened students for their potential to benefit from enriched instruction and then used a lottery to assign students to magnet programs or enriched instruction at their neighborhood schools.

The federal district court dismissed the challenge to the revised plan, finding that the plaintiffs had not adequately alleged discriminatory effect or intent. The 4th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals at Richmond, Virginia, affirmed, applying circuit precedent on disparate impact allegations.

The Supreme Court will now consider whether a plaintiff challenging a facially neutral admissions policy must demonstrate that the affected group experienced an aggregate “disparate impact” compared with its share of the applicant pool.

Put simply, “disparate impact” refers to a policy that produces unequal outcomes for a particular group, even when the policy does not expressly classify individuals by race. An equal protection challenge also raises the question of discriminatory purpose.

The court’s decision will address the legal standards governing these types of claims and whether a particular showing of aggregate impact is required before such a claim may proceed, not whether Montgomery County’s policy ultimately violates the Constitution. The court has not yet decided the merits of the case.

The film underscores why the conversation should not only focus exclusively on admissions policies, statistics and legal standards but should also consider the voices and experiences of students.

The file and the lawsuit should not be confused or treated as evidence of one another. They involve different grade levels, programs and legal questions. The experiences described in the film do not establish what happened in the middle school admissions process, just as the allegations in the lawsuit do not resolve the concerns raised by high school students.

Although selective educational programs can open doors to advanced coursework, specialized instruction and opportunities that shape a student’s academic future, simply making a program available to everyone does not necessarily mean everyone has an equal opportunity to participate.

We must also consider who knows these programs exist, how students are identified and encouraged to apply, what barriers may discourage participation, and whether students feel represented and welcome once they are admitted.

These are important questions for educators, families, policymakers and communities to consider. They may also inform legal analysis, although the applicable legal standards determine what evidence is relevant to a particular claim.

For lawyers, the Supreme Court case highlights the importance of understanding how statistical evidence, applicant pools and allegations of discriminatory intent intersect when challenging facially neutral policies. It also reinforces the need to examine the specific admissions process and how that process may have changed over time. The court’s decision may affect how courts evaluate statistical evidence in challenges to facially neutral admissions policies.

For school officials, families and advocates, the broader questions are equally important:

• How are students identified and informed about selective educational opportunities?

• What criteria determine eligibility and admission?

• Are admissions outcomes regularly examined across schools and student populations?

• Are the policies and measures used to assess access transparent and understandable?

These questions deserve attention regardless of how the Supreme Court ultimately resolves the legal issue before it.

The case is also a reminder that we must distinguish between allegations in a lawsuit, a school district’s stated reasons for its policies, procedural court decisions, and a final ruling on the merits. Understanding those distinctions is essential to an informed discussion.

The winning film, Minorities in Magnets, brings student perspectives into a debate that is often reduced to admissions criteria and demographic statistics. The Supreme Court will address a specific legal question about the evidence required in an equal protection challenge.

Neither alone answers the broader question of what meaningful educational opportunity should look like. But they invite a broader conversation about how communities define opportunity, how they measure access, and how they respond when students say the system is not working for them.

As lawyers and members of our communities, we have an obligation to listen, ask difficult questions, and examine whether the systems we create are providing genuine opportunities for all students.

Ultimately, educational access is about more than who gets admitted. It is also about who gets the opportunity to succeed, who feels that they belong, and whether every student has a fair chance to reach their full potential.

Mario A. Sulllivan is the immediate past chair of the ABA Section of Civil Rights and Social Justice and a name partner at Johnson & Sullivan in Chicago.
