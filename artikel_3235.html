How to steer clear of danger while using an AI assistant to shop

When you purchase through links on our site, we may earn an affiliate commission. Here’s how it works.

We’ve gotten to the point where people are relying on AI assistants to help them navigate all the madness associated with holiday shopping.

According to Bain & Company, nearly a quarter of US online shoppers plan to kick off their holiday shopping on an AI platform like ChatGPT or Gemini (which is a stat that’s up from 17% last year).

With that increase in AI tools being used to aid online shoppers comes the threat of scammers, who look to exploit those shoppers with fake online stores (which are now popping up at a current rate of one every three minutes). And what’s worse is that those same stores have now started showing up in AI assistants’ product recommendations.

In a bid to learn more about how to shop more safely when using AI for online shopping, how a holiday gift list given to AI could build out an identity map of your family and more, I spoke to cybersecurity expert Serena Sullivan.

Here’s hoping her advice makes your AI-assisted holiday shopping experience less susceptible to any sorts of scams.

Consumers often unintentionally disclose personal information. Depending on the way the user crafts their prompts into the AI engine, some information could be inferred.

For example, if the user is searching for personal care items to purchase as a gift, they could unintentionally reveal the gender and age of the person they are purchasing the gift for. If they search for ballet shoes, they could reveal the age range of the recipient, the gender, their hobbies and interests. If you are looking for gifts that are very expensive versus inexpensive, some conclusions could be drawn about the user’s disposable income.

If the person was looking for an experience instead of a gift, maybe a family trip to a theme park, AI could likely deduce the size of the family, whether any family members need accessibility options, which state or country they live in, and a generalization of how much income the family has access to.

Individually, these details might seem harmless. However, when combined, they can create a surprisingly detailed picture of someone's personal life. This is how a data aggregation attack works. An attacker can pull information using Open Source Intelligence (OSINT), meaning information that is freely available on the internet, to compile a detailed profile of the individual. This information can then be used to perform detailed phishing or other social engineering attacks through email, voicemail, phone, or text.

Treat AI assistants as you would any other online service. Only share the information you are willing to disclose, keeping in mind that other information could be combined to create a detailed picture of the user and those they are purchasing gifts for.

Just because AI makes a recommendation does not mean that AI found the correct website and does not indicate that the website has been vetted for safety or security. Additionally, AI could present the shopper with an outdated link.

When you click a link provided in AI results, the first thing to check is if it is a secure website. For example, if the shopper is warned that the digital certificate is out of date or illegitimate, do not continue using that website. Other warning signs, beyond security, are if the items appear too good to be true. For example, if you know an item normally costs about $100 and this website is selling it for $10, it could be a scam.

Shoppers can also identify potential signs of scams by checking the website address and determining if it matches what was expected. For example, the shopper wants to buy a nice piece of jewelry for their significant other; let’s say they wanted to go to ringsforspecialpeople.com (please note this is a fake website, just for the example). When the shopper enters the website, they notice the domain name is not related; maybe it automatically turns into jewelryforspecialpeople.com.

The website could be an exact duplicate of the website the shopper is accessing, but it is not. This should not be confused with related domains. For example, many companies will purchase all the related domains and have them redirected to their main website. The owner of the website could have purchased jewelryforspecialpeople.com and have it redirected to the main website.

Shoppers can also navigate to the suggested website without clicking the link in the AI response. Instead of clicking the link, the shopper could use a search engine to search for that website and click the website from the search results.

Prospective buyers can also review the payment options the website provides. One red flag is if the website requests payment in the form of money orders, wire transfers, gift cards, or cryptocurrency. Those forms of payment are not typical. Most companies would prefer a credit card or payments through other sites, like PayPal, Apple Pay, and others.

Shoppers should be aware of the company they are doing business with. They can research the seller by verifying it is a legitimate company, read user reviews, and identify if they have a customer service department. Most scammers will not have a customer service department. Review the policies on the website.

The warning signs mentioned above apply regardless of how someone arrives at a website. Whether a shopper clicks a link provided by an AI assistant or finds the website through a traditional search engine, the same precautions should be taken. However, as AI increasingly generates recommendations based on individual shopping queries, fraudulent websites and scams may become more difficult to recognize, particularly when the results appear relevant, personalized, and legitimate.

The advancement of AI has given scammers the ability to create highly convincing websites, customer reviews, product descriptions, and chatbots. As a result, it can be increasingly difficult for shoppers to distinguish legitimate businesses from fraudulent ones.

However, consumers can take steps to verify a business before making a purchase. Researching the company, reviewing its Better Business Bureau (BBB) rating and complaint history, and checking for any current or past consumer protection lawsuits or regulatory actions can help shoppers determine whether a business is trustworthy.

If a website appears to be professional-looking, it does not necessarily indicate that the website is trustworthy. If a shopper has even the slightest inkling that the website may be fraudulent, they should research using some of these suggestions or find a different website that does not contain any of the red flags mentioned.

Children would often like to be gifted technology items. However, parents should use caution when deciding what type of technology is appropriate for their child. I would stress that parents teach their children how to spot behaviors or platforms that might be attempting to collect information, or worse.

Many popular technology gifts have the capability of collecting personal information. Parents are the first line of defense for minimizing some of the risks associated with those types of products. If the gift includes options for using a microphone, cameras, location tracking, or has AI integrated, parents should pay particular attention to those capabilities and become familiar with parental controls.

AI-driven toys, like robots or interactive dolls, could record the child’s voice, save conversations and questions the child may ask. Other interactions could also be saved on the device, or in the cloud if it is connected to the internet. If the toy connects to the internet, there is potential that an attacker could access that information.

Video games are a very common gift for children to receive. Games such as Roblox have a feature where children can talk and type to other users they are playing with. A safer approach is to use parental controls to block that feature from children.

If children are using personal assistants or planners and add significant dates, such as birthdays, vacations, school hours, extracurricular activities, and similar, that could be dangerous if an attacker compromises that data. The attacker would potentially be privy to household schedules, how many members are in the family, potentially gender, sexual orientation of family members, religious beliefs, or when the child would be away from home and where they are.

Smartwatches and fitness trackers can also pose potential danger. These devices may collect location information, activity patterns, contacts, and other personal information. Many of these devices are not covered under HIPAA, so they do not necessarily have the same protection applied as medical devices do.

Parents can ask themselves a few questions when evaluating the potential technology gift:

1. What information does the device collect?

2. Is audio, video, or location information stored in the cloud?

3. Who has access to that information, and is it shared with third parties?

4. Can parents disable microphones, cameras, and location tracking?

5. Does the manufacturer provide regular security updates?

6. Can parents review and permanently delete collected information?

Users should be careful about what they self-disclose.

Sharing general information about a family gathering, planning a holiday list, or finding the best gifts for their loved ones is a reasonable use of technology. However, one does not need to include things like full names, addresses, phone numbers, birth date, financial circumstances, sexual orientation, ethnicity, and other identifiable information. Users should consider sharing the bare minimum of information with the AI assistant that will allow it to perform as expected.

Certain information should never be provided to any AI-enabled device, such as:

Personally identifiable information, for adults and children, like Social Security numbers, driver's license numbers, passport information, school schedules, extracurricular activities, or information that could allow an attacker to know where the user is physically located.

As a general rule of thumb, if you would not want anyone outside of your family and close friends to know the information, it should not be entered into the technology.

Anyone who is not familiar with technology or does not understand online safety practices can be especially vulnerable to scammers.

Older adults are often targeted because the scammers know their victims are unfamiliar with online safety and security measures and will use terminology to increase the likelihood that an inexperienced user might fall for the scam. Older adults make desirable victims because they may have more funds, like access to a retirement account, which could contain a large sum intended to last the retiree the rest of their life. Scammers will often try to create a sense of urgency, exploit trust, or play on emotions.

Scammers used to craft emails, websites, apps, and other methods of delivery, and they were often riddled with misspellings, logos that did not seem accurate, and incorrect grammar. With generative AI, scammers have reduced the likelihood of consumers immediately identifying the communication as a scam. By using AI and a few pointed prompts, scammers can now craft grammatically correct and spelling error-free communication. AI has made it much easier for everyone to develop websites and apps in a fraction of the time it used to take.

Creating more realistic content, combined with the ability to find publicly available information about their victim, has allowed scammers to develop realistic-looking communications that can be specifically targeted to their victim.

For example, Dr. Sullivan could have posted to an open Facebook account that she likes to take her corgi to the dog park. The attacker could create an email pretending to be from the city that owns the dog park, naming her dog, saying he was being banned because someone reported her dog as being aggressive. They could include an urgent deadline to have the situation rectified, increasing the chances that Dr. Sullivan would click on the link in the email.

When she clicks the link, it could take her to the fake dog park’s website that appears to be legitimate. Perhaps they will encourage her to pay a fine if she wants her dog to be allowed back in the park. Just that quickly, the attacker could now have a username and password she created on the website, along with her credit card information. That information could then be used to steal additional funds from her or start trying the username and password she provided to attempt to enter other accounts and websites.

Voice cloning is also easier to perform. All the attacker needs is a few-second clip of the voice, and they can generate illegitimate phone calls and other types of voice communications. Imagine a grandparent receiving a phone call in their grandchild’s voice, stating they are being held for ransom. The fake voice states that the grandparents must send a certain amount of cryptocurrency to see their grandchild again.

These situations have happened, and some have been tricked, while others knew it was not their grandchild, because the grandchild is sitting in the back seat of their car and could not be the same person on the telephone. If a familiar voice is saying unusual things, it is a great idea to double-check to determine if it was a legitimate phone call or not.

Families can protect themselves by using several precautions.

Tips on how to handle online scams are to slow down and think about the request, who is making the request, and how likely the request is to be legitimate. Scammers thrive on people making hasty decisions or acting out of emotions rather than thinking the situation through.

From the ransom situation described above to a fantastic and unbelievable deal on an item the person was researching as a gift, consumers should take a few minutes to ask for help from a tech-savvy family member or to think about the situation thoroughly before acting.

1. Protect your personal information: Do not share passwords, financial information, Social Security numbers, children's identifying information, or detailed travel schedules with AI assistants.

2. Verify every website and seller: Never assume an AI-recommended website is legitimate. Independently verify the retailer, domain name, seller reputation, and contact information before purchasing.

3. If a deal seems too good to be true, it probably isn’t true: Be cautious of unusually low prices, unexpected discounts, fast-approaching sales end, and requests for unusual payment methods. If something seems urgent, that is an indicator that consumers should slow down and independently verify the details.

5. Verify before you buy: Independently confirm the product, seller, total price, shipping costs, return policy, and payment method.

Details a consumer should verify before purchasing include ensuring the seller is legitimate, validating the details of the product being purchased, confirming the total cost, payment security, and available purchase protections.

Use credit cards for purchases whenever possible, as that allows consumers one more avenue to explore should they have followed all the safety tips and were scammed regardless.
