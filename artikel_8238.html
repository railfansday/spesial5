Howls, Growls, and Sounds of Hounds

Tanggal: 2026-11-11

Should you use an external loudspeaker while on trail? The internet has spoken: you should not. Should you use headphones? Conclusions vary, but in general, no. Sometimes the internet provides a good reason, and sometimes just an opinion.

Thru-hiking has its mundane moments. And I don’t just mean road-walks – several years ago, I was bored at least once on the John Muir Trail. A few thousand miles later, even though boredom is still very much a thing, I’m able to zone-out more easily. Unless, that is, I get an earworm. Then, my daydreaming gets interrupted.

It was a cold morning on the PCT. I broke camp, started hiking, and realized I’d left my MP3 player in the side pocket of my tent. Digging it out was too much hassle, so I hiked in silence. I noticed a patch of frost within a patch of shade, and that’s how the earworm gained entry. The song that got stuck in my head was “Frosty the S**wman”, and I’m hesitant to name it because it’s so virulent. The first couple of lines rattled around in my skull for the next ten miles. That was the day I learned that listening to music is a good defense against earworms.

The day after the bear incident, I mentally survived a hot, 15 mile road-walk, and physically survived the high speed dump-trucks on County Highway F. Unsettled weather arrived that night, and when I hit the trail the next day, distant thunder started approaching from the west after 30 minutes. The air remained warm even as the storm eventually passed overhead, but the torrential rain was extremely cold. The trail turned into a stream in some places, and a pond in others. Wet feet, no problem. When my music cut out, that was a problem.

My jacket pocket wasn’t as waterproof as I thought, and I discovered that my MP3 player was completely waterlogged. Despite the water sloshing around inside, I was hopeful it might eventually dry out. I shook out as much water as possible before putting it away.

The MP3 player spent that night inside one of my sleep-socks, on top of my foot, like a penguin egg. I incubated it in my shorts pocket the next day, while hiking the Mondeaux River eskers. There was a minor earworm infestation, but I’ve had worse.

At the beginning of the third day, I made a beeline for the town of Rib Lake. It was a full day of distractions.

At the end of the third day, resurrection! Kind of. The MP3 player was able to play music, even though one of its buttons didn’t work. And its display soon flickered and died. It was stuck in “Shuffle” mode, and had developed a couple of other quirks, but it worked well enough. It defended me valiantly against earworms for the remainder of my thru-hike.

I headed north out of town along Kennedy Street. During the 1.5 mile road-walk, three pickup trucks drove past. Each truck was outfitted with several kennels, and each kennel contained an excited, baying hound. As the noise faded into the distance, I reached the trailhead, headed east, and silence descended. With some background music, of course.

That afternoon, I met hikers Linda and Wendy, who were heading west. They were piecing together the entire IAT by parking a vehicle at each of two trailheads, and hiking from one to the next. I met them again at Wood Lake County Park campground, and they were very hospitable. They let me share their campsite, and that evening, Wendy cooked Gnocchi, Pesto, and Chicken of the Woods (mushrooms that she’d picked that day).

In the morning, after only two miles, I saw the three pickup trucks again, empty this time. They were parked at my first trailhead of the day, and I could hear the hounds’ sounds coming from the direction I needed to go. Whether they were training or hunting, I wasn’t sure. Randomly, my MP3 player chose that moment to select “Dueling Banjos”.

Not really. But it would have been appropriate.

I decided to walk the road for a few miles, heading south. Again, the noise faded into the distance.

Compared to western states, Wisconsin sounds different. This was day 17, and I was still noticing birdsongs I hadn’t heard before. The R2-D2 bird, for example. I’d recently discovered that the creatures snorting at me from afar were White-tailed bucks, who apparently have an excellent sense of smell. Mule deer out west have never snorted at me.

In camp, at sunset, three wolf-packs exchanged howls several miles away. It was the only time on the Ice Age Trail that I heard wolves, so they’re probably no more common in the midwest than the west.

The next day, I hiked north along the west bank of the Wisconsin River, then south along the other side. The Grandfather Falls dam and hydropower plant were interesting, but the subsequent out-and-back was not, at least according to FarOut commenters. I know the IAT is a work-in-progress, but I believe a three mile round-trip with no pay-off is optional for thru-hikers. I skipped it, and immediately started a 10 mile road-walk.

I dodged most of a heavy rain shower by sheltering in the pavilion at Tug Lake County Park, and was dry by the time I reached my campsite, which was, in this case, a shelter. It was my first ever night in one – cozy, even if the neighbors were noisy. I now know that the birds at Dog Lake that night were Sandhill cranes.

The next night, my neighbors were remarkably quiet. At the campsite on the other side of Chain Lake, about 300 yards away, were 16 teenagers and two camp counselors from Milwaukee. It sounded like everyone was fast asleep as soon as the sun went down.

I only hitched once on the IAT, going to Rhinelander. I stayed the night, and tried hitching back mid-morning. There were roadworks on the edge of town, which might be why state route 17 wasn’t very busy. An hour later, I had no takers. I tried Uber, also unsuccessfully, then called a cab. Five minutes later, they picked me up. I was back on trail at midday, and reached a suitable place to camp about five o’clock.

Actually, suitable is an exaggeration. I chose a flat patch of grass beside a five-way intersection of overgrown, disused dirt roads. I wouldn’t have camped there if this had been Grizzly territory.

As expected, the intersection was busy that night, but each disturbance didn’t keep me awake for long. Then, at 1:15 AM, something started growling at me, and I was wide awake in an instant, heart thumping.

I’ve been growled at by dogs and coyotes, and grumbled at by porcupines and raccoons. This sound wasn’t canine, and the animal making it didn’t sound small. In my imagination, the creature was bear-sized.

I used my outside voice, but there was no reaction. I unzipped my tent door and shone my flashlight around. Still no movement. I couldn’t see the animal because it was on the other side of my tent. Periodically, for about 15 minutes, it uttered a low, rumbling growl lasting several seconds. Each time, I replied with a few words.

Then, at last, it padded quietly away. I lay awake for 45 minutes while the adrenaline subsided. In the morning, when I had cell service, I researched my nocturnal visitor, and suddenly everything made sense. Wisconsin is the Badger State.

Fast-forward a few hours, and I had a silent night. Wholly unexciting night. All was calm, all was quite… normal. The morning was a bit chilly, but that’s hardly worth mentioning.

In the afternoon, near the tiny community of Polar, I arrived at my chosen campsite on the shore of Mueller Lake. On the water, I could hear a couple of speedboats, their stereos, and the shrieks of people enjoying themselves. The boaters soon went home, and for a time, it was peaceful.

Then the residents of the lakefront house 400 feet away fired up their megawatt outdoor sound system. For the next three hours, I was treated to a Spotify 70’s selection with commercial breaks. Full-size headphones with noise cancelling would have been useful. Fortunately, the loudspeakers fell silent just before sunset.

So, if someone were to suggest that earbuds disconnect me from the sounds of nature, I’d have to disagree. They don’t disconnect me from man-made sounds either.

Sometimes you’ll hear logging on the IAT.
