The Legacy and Evolution of Rockstar Games: How One Studio Redefined Open-World Gaming

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

I grew up with an unhealthy obsession with the Grand Theft Auto series. The game wasn’t obviously meant for an 8-year-old, but it showed me the greatest gift: my parents wanting to give me happiness and comfort throughout my upbringing, even if it meant unknowingly handing me a game that was clearly meant for older audiences.

But hey, it didn’t meddle with my behavior, alright? Playing San Andreas was one of my most endearing experiences, with countless playthroughs/save files. And I mean, some of my best video game memories even involved my dad helping me clear a couple of tough missions in both it and Vice City. So from those 3D-era entries to see how far Rockstar has come to be this industry powerhouse with their sandbox video game series is genuinely astounding to see.

Aaron Garbut, co-studio head of Rockstar North, explained that no single person has the entire game mapped out in their head.

Rockstar Games has spent decades building some of the most recognisable worlds in video game history. Whether it's the streets of Los Santos, the mob-boss-riddled underworld of Liberty City, or a quiet ride through the unforgiving American frontier with an on-the-run gang of outlaws, there's something remarkably special about the worlds this studio creates.

With Grand Theft Auto VI approaching in just over two weeks, it's worth looking back at how that journey unfolded. From the studio's transformation and the franchises that shaped its identity to the enormous expectations around its next release, Rockstar's legacy is about much more than the games it has sold. It's about how one developer helped redefine what players expect from open-world experiences.

We’re taking this back to the humble beginnings of Rockstar Games. See, before it became one of the most popular names in the gaming landscape, its story actually began with a much smaller development team in Dundee, Scotland. Founded in 1988 by David Jones and a group of fellow developers, DMA Design started as a relatively small studio with ambitions to make its mark in an industry still finding its footing.

Its early releases, including Menace and Blood Money, showed its interest in arcade-inspired action games, but the arrival of a quirky little game called Lemmings in 1991 truly put the developer on the map. This relatively simple puzzle game (unexpectedly) became an enormous success, selling an estimated 20 million copies across its various platforms.

However, a very different kind of project would ultimately change the studio's future, since in 1997, DMA Design would then release the original Grand Theft Auto, trading its familiar arcade experiences for a top-down crime sandbox that allowed you to steal vehicles, complete odd-job missions, evade the police, and wreak havoc across fictional cities.

As the OG Grand Theft Auto continued to gain momentum despite being an experimental first entry, DMA Design underwent a series of ownership changes before becoming part of Take-Two Interactive and the wider Rockstar Games family around the turn of the millennium.

Eventually, the studio relocated from Dundee to Edinburgh, and in 2002, it officially became Rockstar North. This transformation, or turnaround, whichever one you prefer, would soon carry forward the creative foundations established years earlier. And little did you or I know just how far that journey would take them; this small Scottish team was now at the heart of a studio that would go on to produce some of the most influential open-world experiences in gaming history.

Former Rockstar technical director Obbe Vermeij recalls how GTA 3’s small team made communication easier and gave developers room to experiment.

Funnily enough, I had the chance to revisit GTA 3 this past month as part of my replay of all the mainline entries before VI’s arrival. Forgive me if it was the Definitive Edition; it was dirt-cheap on sale, and I’d already played the OG version on my PS2 as a kid, so I thought, why not try things differently, especially after this much time has passed since this trilogy’s controversial release?

Now, contrary to what the header suggests, although open-world games existed long before Grand Theft Auto III arrived, few developers had managed to bring together the same level of freedom, exploration, and interactive storytelling that Rockstar would eventually become known for. In fact, this is also why I’d like to give a shout-out to my co-worker and friend, Maddie, for discussing how 1999's Urban Chaos, developed by Mucky Foot Productions, was already experimenting with a 3D urban environment, free-roaming exploration, and mission-based gameplay two years before GTA 3 hit the PS2.

While it wasn't the expansive sandbox Rockstar would then create in later titles, it showed the potential of this simple yet experimental idea: letting players explore and interact with a full 3D city. But because it went beyond the formulaic approach of the prior two GTAs, you could absolutely make a case that this game was a generational release for its time.

Instead of simply following a predetermined series of missions, players could steal vehicles, explore different neighborhoods, interact with pedestrians, engage in shootouts, and stumble upon all sorts of unexpected chaos along the way. Driving, shooting, and exploring were no longer separate gameplay modes, but seamlessly interconnected parts of a much larger experience.

Oh, and it didn’t stop there, obviously. The game's success would go on to influence countless developers, establishing a formula that would shape much of the open-world genre for years to come, since Rockstar wasn't simply offering players a large map to explore; it was creating an environment filled with activities, distractions, and opportunities for experimentation.

The studio continued refining that approach with Grand Theft Auto: Vice City in 2002 and then Grand Theft Auto: San Andreas in 2004. The former introduced a distinctive 1980s atmosphere reminiscent of Miami Vice’s stylized tone and an unforgettable soundtrack, while the latter expanded the formula considerably with multiple cities, character customization, and RPG-inspired mechanics.

So by the time San Andreas arrived, Rockstar had shown how much freedom and variety could fit into a single open-world experience. It is genuinely insane that these guys released three back-to-back bangers; it's an honest travesty that they outsourced the modern re-release of them to an external developer under their umbrella.

GTA 3 often ran at just 7 or 8 FPS during development, according to former Rockstar technical director Obbe Vermeij.

While Grand Theft Auto remained Rockstar's biggest commercial success, the company was never particularly interested in limiting itself to a single franchise; these guys were seriously trailblazing their way to the top. That’s because throughout the 2000s, its growing network of development studios experimented with different genres, storytelling approaches, and settings, resulting in some of its most memorable cult favorites.

Bully took the familiar open-world formula and turned it into a crude yet charming coming-of-age adventure. Meanwhile, Manhunt explored a much darker side of the company's creativity through its disturbing atmosphere (enough to give me the frights as a kid) and brutal stealth-based gameplay, and then Midnight Club brought open-city exploration into the street racing scene of the 2000s that other series like NFS also capitalized on with Fast and Furious’ popularity.

And don’t worry, like most of you, possibly longtime fans reading this, I still hold an undying grudge against Rockstar for never revisiting these games again, especially the high possibilities for a sequel to Bully, which I’m never burying the hatchet over because the Scholarship Edition is one of my most played and adored games on the Xbox 360. The bottom line is that these projects showed Rockstar's ambitions extended well beyond crime-driven satire sandboxes, allowing its various studios to establish their own identities across genres.

Of course, Grand Theft Auto wasn't going anywhere. And how ironic that I rolled credits on this just a week ago. With GTA IV in 2008, Rockstar took a noticeably different approach to its established formula for a grittier, more grounded portrayal of Liberty City akin to New York City.

It introduced a string of themes like revenge, organized crime overlords butting heads for power, and the often uncomfortable realities of chasing the American Dream. Plus, things like the ragdoll physics and weight-based driving model were the icing on the cake for an otherwise brilliant crime drama, one that I personally think is the best in the series in terms of writing. However, as much as I hate to admit it, Rockstar wasn't finished pushing those boundaries.

An interview with Rockstar reveals details about GTA 6's morality system.

Grand Theft Auto V arrived in 2013, taking you back to Los Santos and introducing three playable protagonists: Michael, Franklin, and Trevor. Its intertwining storytelling, enormous variety of activities, and remarkable sense of freedom made it a defining open-world release of its generation. Today, it is insane to think about how many records this game has broken: it's one of the best-selling video games of all time, second only to Minecraft, and has generated billions of dollars since its original launch.

Beyond the story mode, GTA Online (unfortunately) gradually grew into a massive multiplayer platform of its own, keeping players invested through years of updates, new missions, vehicles, heists, and activities. And I won’t lie: as someone who spent an unhealthy number of hours on it, this game was partly why I found two of my bestest friends. Also, this mode's longevity and the revenue it generated through microtransactions were low-key part of why GTA V became a cultural phenomenon that kept thriving across multiple console generations.

The wizards at Rockstar didn’t stop there, because the leap that came forward after would soon set the benchmark for people to compare most open-world releases to, even if most of those comparisons became insufferable to come across in social media posts and/or videos.

And that considerable leap came when Rockstar dropped the technical bombshell that was Red Dead Redemption 2 in 2018. You see, I could’ve put the original entry in the section above with the other projects these guys did, but it only felt right to include it here, since both these two Wild West open-world titles became games that almost felt like companion pieces to the GTA franchise. That was evident not only in the mission design, but also in newer additions like the Honor system, a fully realized wildlife ecosystem, and other gameplay elements that made it feel like a sister game to GTA.

Following the success of the 2010 entry, the sequel offered a story centered around Arthur Morgan and the gradual decline of the Van der Linde gang, one that slowly connected to the events of RDR1. Its greatest achievement, however, was arguably the extraordinary level of detail packed into its bygone world. From real wildlife behavior and daily NPC routines to dynamic world events that shifted depending on the protagonist or time passed, almost every aspect of its world contributed to an incredible sense of immersion.

Although years later, you’ll now find some recent verbal engagements about people arguing about its flawed mission design, and as much as I think it's a bit overrated (please put the pitchforks down), I will wholeheartedly agree on the fact that this was THE game that made Rockstar the rightful heir to the throne for creating one of the most immersive and compelling open-world games of all time. Most importantly, it also rewarded those willing to slow down and simply enjoy their surroundings, and I think that’s the best compliment to give RDR2 given the current attention span of most mainstream audiences.

After decades of refining its approach to open-world design, Rockstar Games now stands on the doorstep of what could be its most ambitious project yet, as GTA VI represents more than just another long-awaited sequel, especially considering how much the studio has accomplished since we first stepped foot into Los Santos by repo’ing cars with Franklin and Lamar or just cruising around Ocean Beach as Tommy.

I won’t bore your ear with a bunch of what-if bumbo jumbo, but all I want to sign off with is that, given everything Rockstar has showcased so far, there's plenty of reason to be excited about the possibilities they could improve upon here, particularly when considering the technical artistry demonstrated in RDR2.

We’ve already seen an extent of the full gameplay in action with the Extended Look, as well as tons of deeper details shared online across media outlets that make this entry a proper sim-like experience, which I honestly don’t mind since I genuinely wanted Rockstar to bring back the RPG stuff they introduced back in San Andreas. Heck, even if it’s not confirmed, throw in vehicle schools again to give Lucia and Jason even more stats to manage.

For me, one of the biggest challenges GTA VI faces is delivering a story that can stand alongside GTA IV because Niko Bellic's journey for me remains one of the franchise's most memorable narratives precisely because it went beyond the usual criminal escapade trope. Most of all, it made its protagonist feel like more than another criminal navigating a fictional city, and I'd love to see Jason and Lucia achieve that in their own way.

Their relationship already gives Rockstar an interesting foundation to explore, but what matters most is whether the studio can make players genuinely care about the people behind all the chaos. Give me another round of NPC social links calling either protagonist just for a round of bowling or, y’know, give me reasons to interact with them again via their special abilities/benefits.

Now, with Leonida and the newly imagined Vice City waiting just around the corner, it feels like we're about to experience the next chapter in a legacy that has shaped how an entire generation experiences video games.

While I generally have mixed feelings about the overly eccentric fanbase, I’ll put that aside for now since I’m also proudly blasting Flower in Hurricane and Inner Light on repeat these days to itch my hype fever for the game’s release on November 19th. Whether Rockstar can deliver another landmark open-world experience remains to be seen, but hey, to anyone who made it here to see it happen, I hope we eat good with whatever they’ve cooked up over the past 13 years.
