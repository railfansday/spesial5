Nvidia's Now-Former CEO, Jensen Huang, Just Broke One of Warren Buffett's 2 Unbreakable Rules, Making His Stock Uninvestable for the Foreseeable Future

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

Once trust is lost, the investment thesis is, too.

Last week, on Nov. 12, the face of the artificial intelligence (AI) revolution, Nvidia (NVDA -2.44%), rocked Wall Street. Jensen Huang, who co-founded Nvidia 33 years ago, announced his resignation following an admission of an affair with an alleged Chinese spy, and amid allegations of another extramarital affair with a co-worker.

While Nvidia's historically inexpensive valuation may be intriguing to some value-focused investors, the billionaire synonymous with value investing, Berkshire Hathaway's (BRKA +0.47%)(BRKB +0.53%) now-retired CEO Warren Buffett, would likely disagree.

Berkshire Hathaway's Warren Buffett was unwavering on two of his investing rules. Image source: Getty Images.

The Oracle of Omaha followed a laundry list of unwritten investing rules, many of which contributed to Berkshire's greater-than-6,000,000% outperformance of the benchmark S&P 500 since the mid-1960s. But only two of these "rules" were unbreakable in Buffett's eyes -- and Nvidia's now-former boss just broke one of them.

Warren Buffett had several unwritten rules that he occasionally broke. For instance, he preached the importance of long-term investing, but piled into gaming giant Activision Blizzard as an arbitrage opportunity shortly after Microsoft (MSFT -0.43%) made an all-cash offer to acquire it at $95 per share.

But there are two rules the Oracle of Omaha absolutely refused to break:

At one point, money-center titan Wells Fargo (WFC -0.09%) was one of Berkshire Hathaway's largest positions, totaling around 480 million shares (around $28 billion in market value at its peak). Buffett always favored big banks for their strong cyclical ties and their ability to generate larger profits over time through prudent lending.

However, a cross-selling scandal came to light in 2016, revealing that Wells Fargo employees had opened millions of unauthorized accounts to meet aggressive store-level goals. In the years that followed, Buffett and his team pared down and eventually exited this once-core position in its entirety. Once trust is lost, the investment thesis is, too, in the eyes of Warren Buffett.

Huang's resignation completely wrecks the trust that Wall Street, investors, and perhaps even businesses have placed in the company leading the AI revolution. Despite stepping down as CEO, federal investigations concerning national security are continuing.

Additionally, Jensen Huang's departure is bound to put the spotlight on the company's laundry list of large-dollar deals and the claims that many of its collaborations represent circular financing. Nvidia has pledged a lot of capital to hyperscalers and neoclouds, including CoreWeave (CRWV -4.78%) and OpenAI, which in turn agree to use some of this funding to purchase Nvidia's graphics processing units. Future collaborations may prove harder to come by.

Replacing Jensen as CEO of Nvidia could backfire on the ENTIRE AI boom. Why? Because of this chart. Jensen has been one of the biggest architects of circular financing. This has been propping up those valuations and nearly all of these deals were negotiated by him. Would a successor be able to engineer similar structures? And does uncertainty with Nvidia's direction call into question this broader gambit? Tenuous times.

Jensen Huang's departure likely also exposes Nvidia to a growing number of AI headwinds, including the prospect of higher borrowing costs, deteriorating corporate bond prices for AI debt offerings, and the possibility of an AI bubble-bursting event taking shape.

Based on one of Warren Buffett's unbreakable investing rules, Nvidia stock just became uninvestable for the foreseeable future.
