Is This a ‘Gut Feeling’ About My Relationship... or Just My Anxiety?

We’re often told that we should trust our gut in relationships—that when something is wrong, we’ll get a distinct feeling in our bodies. But what if that feeling isn’t distinct at all? What if these feelings of dread and alarm are constant? What if you can’t really trust your gut?

I’ve had anxiety for as long as I can remember. And when I get into relationships, I feel the symptoms tenfold. I create worst-case scenarios in my head, over-analyze every little reaction, and go into a panic when a partner has the slightest shift in attitude. A lot of this anxiety has to do with my past (surprise, surprise). I was in an emotionally abusive relationship, riddled with infidelity and manipulation, for six years. And although it’s been years since my breakup and I’ve moved on to an entirely new relationship that feels healthy and fulfilling, I don’t think my body and brain have quite gotten the memo.

“A person who has experienced neglect, abandonment, unpredictability or inconsistencies in past relationships may be unknowingly conditioned to scan for threat, even in a healthy, stable relationship,” Jessica N. Gordon, LCSW tells Cosmopolitan.

So if you come equipped with intense relational trauma, how do you know the difference between when something is actually wrong in your relationship—and when it’s just your anxiety acting up again?

When every little thing feels like the end of the world, it becomes hard to discern when there’s a real concern in your relationship. This is typically what Relationship Obsessive Compulsive Disorder (ROCD) looks like.

"Anxiety driven by ROCD tends to feel very intense and urgent,” Cameron Murphey, LMFT says. “It feels like you have to address it right now.” He explains that people with ROCD typically feel like they’re unable to focus on anything else until they've addressed what's making them feel anxious.

But the problem is what’s making them anxious isn’t exactly rooted in something concrete. People with ROCD constantly ruminate on thoughts like “How do I know I’m truly in love?,” “There have been no signs, but I think they’re cheating on me,” or “Is my partner doing enough?” There might not even be a specific action that triggers these thoughts.

“An [ROCD] thought tends to jump from topic to topic... and feels spontaneous rather than a consistent subtle thought,” Gordon says. A true concern in the relationship, on the other hand, is tied to real, observable behavior that shows up in a pattern: “every time we talk about my feelings, he picks up his phone and I feel ignored.”

But what if your gut is just screaming at you that something is wrong?

“I think we sometimes give our gut feelings a lot of authority without stopping to consider what's informing them,” Sonni Williams, LPC says. “Our intuition can be shaped by our experiences, values, and even the things we're afraid of repeating.”

She adds that we sometimes tend to oversimplify intuition as calm and anxiety as panic. The problem with this is that both of these things can feel convincing, and neither feeling alone tells us whether something is true.

Rather than torturing yourself with these thoughts as they come, Williams says it’s more effective and helpful to look at what happens afterward.

“Can you reflect on the concern and decide whether something needs attention?” she says to ask yourself. “Or are you repeatedly analyzing conversations and feelings, trying to find certainty?”

Now this doesn’t mean you need an immediate answer in order to take your feelings seriously. Williams explains that when it comes to concerns involving abuse, coercion, or controlling behaviors, these things deserve attention regardless of whether anxiety or ROCD are present.

It’s absolutely a good thing to reflect on your relationship and assess if it’s working for you. But Williams explains that what’s most important is knowing the difference between evaluating your relationship and feeling like you have to constantly prove that it’s right or perfect.

And you may not be able to trust your gut, specifically, but that doesn’t mean you shouldn’t trust yourself—you just have to learn what that looks like in the context of your anxiety.

"Self-trust is not about believing that you'll always make the right choice,” Murphey says. “It's about trusting that you can handle whatever happens, good or bad.”

He explains that we tend to feel fear in our stomachs, so it’s easy to confuse those very physical feelings with something grander like intuition. And guess what? That’s totally fine—so long as you don’t use those feelings to solely dictate your actions in your relationship. If you get those feelings, think about why or where they’re coming from.

You’re going to have second thoughts about your partner from time-to-time (or if you’re me, every three business days). Seeing a therapist for lingering, uncontrollable thoughts can help you work through whatever anxieties you may have. Otherwise, there are going to be times where you just inevitably question your relationship.

Gordon says, “The goal is to feel less controlled by doubt, not to never have it.”
