Unlike window air conditioners, portable air conditioners roll easily from room to room and take only minutes to set up—no brackets, no tools, no sweating through a complicated installation before you can start sweating less. Most just require a nearby window and wall outlet to get cold air flowing. Yes, they tend to cost a bit more than window units, but the tradeoff is real: you can’t beat the convenience their portability adds, which is why many people choose them over other models. Plus your view isn’t blocked by a giant metal box.

Portable air conditioners have an internal fan that moves hot, humid air over a refrigerant-gas-filled coil to cool and expel it into a room to lower the temperature. The moisture it strips from the air collects in an internal reservoir that you’ll periodically need to drain. Higher-end units either pump it out automatically or evaporate it entirely so you never have to think about it. Because the whole unit rolls on casters, you can take the cold air with you instead of running more energy intensive central a/c or buying multiple window units.

Coverage area is one of the most important factors in choosing an air conditioner. Manufacturers rate units in square footage, but that number assumes an average ceiling height (roughly eight feet) and room insulation. If the room is especially hot in the summer (for example, it’s a finished attic or it has large, south-facing windows) we recommend bumping up to the next size rather than buying a unit that will run constantly and struggle to keep up.

An air conditioner’s cooling capacity is typically measured in British Thermal Units, or BTU, per hour. To gauge coverage, figure roughly 20 BTU per square foot of space. Most portable a/c units range from 5,000 to 20,000 BTUs, which puts their effective range roughly between 350 and 700 square feet. So when in doubt, size up—an oversized unit cycles off when it hits your target temperature, while an undersized one never stops trying.

Single-hose units essentially recirculate the room’s air to cool it and have one line that runs outdoors to expel exhaust. The catch is that expelling air creates a slight negative pressure in the room, which pulls in warm air from other parts of the house and from outside through gaps around doors and windows. It works, but it’s working against itself.

Dual-hose units solve this by pulling outside air in through one hose for cooling and pushing hot exhaust out through a second hose. The result is a more self-contained system that’s noticeably more efficient, especially in larger spaces. The tradeoff is price.

Because dual-hose models are more complex, they tend to be more expensive. If you only have a small space to cool, a portable a/c with a single hose may suit your needs just fine. For anything larger, the dual-hose design is worth seriously considering.

Hose length determines how far away from a window you can maneuver your portable air conditioner. If you set it up in a large room, or one where the nearest window is far from where most people gather, hose length becomes more important.

It also plays a key role in room setup. If the hose isn’t long enough to set the air conditioner in front of large furniture pieces, you may have to rearrange the room for the most effective cooling. Most hoses range anywhere from 50 to 90 inches fully extended. This should give you enough space to make adjustments and pull the air conditioner closer to the center of the room if needed.

Most portable air conditioners hover between 50 and 60 decibels while they’re running—that’s roughly the noise level of a normal conversation. In most cases, that’s fine during the day, but if you’re planning to run the unit in a bedroom overnight, those numbers start to matter.

Units that dip below 50 decibels in their quietest modes are on par with most ceiling fans and are easier to sleep through; a few on this list get impressively close to whisper-quiet. Pay attention to the decibel rating at the unit’s lowest fan setting, not just its average operating level.

Much like larger ductless mini-split air conditioners, some portable air conditioners include a heat pump. “If you’re shopping for an a/c unit because you have a space not currently cooled or heated, one of these models may warrant consideration,” says gear editor Bradley Ford. “Similarly, you may have a room that your HVAC system doesn’t reach effectively. Rooms like this are typically either too cold or hot, depending on the season. If this is the case, a unit with heating capabilities may be able to fill your HVAC gap.”

Every portable a/c pulls moisture out of the air, similar to home dehumidifiers. The question is what happens to it next. Budget units collect it in an internal pan that you’ll need to manually drain, sometimes daily in humid conditions.

Other units use self-evaporating technology to push moisture out through the exhaust hose with no bucket to remember. A few high-end models go even further, offering a continuous drainage port where you can attach a hose and never think about water again. If low-maintenance matters to you, the drainage method is a factor to consider.

There isn’t a clear superior option between window units and portable options. Choosing between the two largely depends on your home, windows, and personal needs. That said, window air conditioning units are generally faster at bringing temperatures down and run more efficiently than their portable counterparts.

Portable air conditioners have certainly closed the gap over time in terms of cooling ability, but still lag behind if that’s your number one priority.

Portable units stand out because of their convenience and ease of setup. If you don’t have compatible windows, get a portable unit. If you need quick cooling in a spot that generally doesn’t need air conditioning, get a portable unit. If you want just one machine for your entire apartment, get a portable unit.

Portable air conditioners are compatible with a lot more window openings than window units, so you can use them regardless of your apartment or home fixtures.

New portable a/c units are relatively energy efficient across the board. That said, it’s certainly a sliding scale. There are several ways in which energy efficiency is calculated for air conditioners, but the shorthand is if your a/c unit is Energy Star-certified, it’s worth getting.

For items that don’t have Energy Star certification, pay attention to the CEER, EER, and SEER numbers. In short, look for an air conditioner with a CEER above 10, with anything above 12 qualifying for Energy Star certification. Similarly, look out for a SEER above 14 in the north and 15 in the south as well as an EER above 10. These are general numbers, but serve as a basic guide when choosing between different options.

One of the biggest issues to solve with portable air conditioners is cooling efficiency. While most units generate a ton of cold air, leakage from the hose or exhaust port can work against that. There are two key things you can do: insulate the opening in the window and insulate the hose itself.

Many portable units provide foam pieces that fit in and around the window insert to minimize the amount of hot air that leaks in from outside. The hose itself won’t come with foam insulation pieces, but you can perform a similar trick by buying one online or by making one yourself. Some people use blankets while others have used everything from reflective sun shades to Amazon plastic packaging. The goal is to prevent heat from the tube from radiating into your living space.
