People blind since infancy are said to hear better, but the difference isn’t in their ears

Tanggal: 2026-11-11

Being blind from infancy didn't sharpen hearing in the ear itself, but it steadied how the brain judged pitch, and music lessons did the same.

People who lose their sight early in life are widely thought to hear better across the board. Yet, on a test of the ear itself, adults blind since infancy did no better than sighted adults at catching a faint tone hidden in noise.

Where they did pull ahead was in telling whether one tone was higher or lower than the next.

A computer model traced that edge to the brain, which seemed to respond to the same note more steadily from one moment to the next. Years of music lessons went with the same steadiness, in blind and sighted listeners alike.

Woon Ju Park, a researcher in the School of Psychology at the Georgia Institute of Technology, led the work with colleagues at the University of Washington. The group was small, nine blind adults and 18 sighted ones.

“Blindness is often assumed to lead to a general, across-the-board enhancement in hearing, but our findings suggest that improvements may be more specific to tasks that rely on how information is processed in the brain and not at the ears,” Park said.

The blind volunteers had lost their sight at birth or within the first two years of life. Seven could perceive no light at all, and two could sense a little light.

Each was paired with two sighted people of similar age and a similar number of years of music lessons. On average, partners were less than five years apart on both counts.

The idea that blindness sharpens hearing is old; the French writer Denis Diderot discussed it in a 1749 essay.

The pairing mattered, because many blind people have worked as piano tuners and musicians, and earlier studies that skipped it disagreed about whether blind listeners really had sharper pitch.

Everyone’s hearing was screened first at three pitches. The blind and sighted groups heard quiet tones equally well.

The first test was aimed at the cochlea, the snail-shaped part of the inner ear that sorts sound by pitch.

Listeners heard two bursts of hiss, and one of them hid a steady tone vibrating 1,200 times a second. They pressed a key to say which burst held the tone.

The hiss had a gap cut around the tone’s pitch, and the gap changed width from trial to trial. How quickly people improved as the gap widened shows how finely their ears separate nearby pitches.

Blind and sighted listeners scored almost exactly alike, after 80 practice trials and two sessions of 480 trials each. The gap between the groups was far smaller than the smallest difference the test was built to catch.

The tie on the ear test ruled out two other explanations. The team hadn’t simply recruited blind people with unusually good ears.

And the blind volunteers probably weren’t just paying closer attention, since both groups made careless misses at about the same low rate.

In the second test, listeners heard two short tones, one after the other, and said whether the pitch went up or down, 216 times each.

On a piano, neighboring keys sit one-twelfth of an octave apart. The pitch gap between the two tones ranged from one and a half keys down to about a tenth of a key.

The tones lasted anywhere from a third of a second to about two hundredths of a second.

Here, the blind listeners had the edge, and their lead grew when the pitches sat farther apart and the tones lasted longer.

So their edge was clearest when each sound gave them plenty to work with. The authors suggest that may help explain why earlier studies disagreed: one that used shorter tones found no advantage.

Next, the team built a computer model of how a brain might hear each tone, with two settings.

One was tuning, how narrowly the brain’s pitch-sensing cells respond around a single note. The other was jitter: play the identical tone twice and the brain’s response still wobbles a little. Researchers call that wobble internal noise.

Fitted to each person’s answers, tuning came out the same in both groups, and internal noise was lower in the blind listeners.

A 2019 brain-scanning study had found slightly narrower pitch tuning in blind people’s hearing regions, but only by about 3 percent. The authors judged that change too small to be the main cause.

When the researchers forced both groups to share one level of internal noise, the model’s fit got much worse. Forcing them to share one tuning width cost far less.

Most of the blind listeners’ advantage came down to steadier responses, not narrower tuning, and that result surprised the team.

In an interview with Earth.com, Park explained that hearing can become more precise in more than one way. The auditory system can respond more distinctly to different sounds, or it can respond more reliably to the same sound each time.

“We often think of better perception as being able to distinguish sounds more sharply from each other,” Park said. “But our findings suggest that responding more consistently may be just as important.”

Because the volunteers had a wide range of music experience, the team could also check what lessons did. People with more years of musical training had lower internal noise, whether they were blind or sighted.

Blindness still predicted lower internal noise after the researchers accounted for music experience. Music didn’t explain the blind listeners’ advantage away.

Neither blindness nor training predicted a better score on the ear test. That cuts against earlier reports of sharper tuning in musicians’ ears, and the team had enough people to catch an effect that size.

Blind listeners were still generally better at telling pitches apart than sighted musicians, Park said. What surprised the team was that both groups seemed to improve in the same way.

“There’s been a long-standing question about whether superior hearing in early blind individuals comes from the absence of vision or from their extensive auditory experience,” Park told Earth.com.

“Our findings suggest that both may play a role. Auditory experience appears to improve pitch perception in a way similar to musical training, while early blindness may provide an additional benefit.”

Asked whether everyday listeners could lower their own internal noise with training, Park said it is possible. Studies of vision have shown that learning to tell objects apart can reduce internal noise.

The noise can come from many different sources, Park added, and how or why it falls with training is still not fully understood.

“These findings suggest that learning may involve making our sensory representations more consistent and reliable,” Park said.

The study can’t tell whether the blind listeners’ steadier hearing comes from the loss of vision itself or from years of leaning harder on sound.

The internal noise in the model also isn’t a direct recording of brain cells. It stands for how much a person’s judgment wavers from one try to the next.

One explanation the authors offer is that practice teaches the brain which signals to trust and which to ignore. Their model can’t yet separate that from other possibilities, such as more precise timing in how the brain encodes sound.

Park said the study relied on relatively simple listening tasks and a mathematical model. The next step is to find out what is happening in the listeners’ brains.

“Do their patterns of brain activity reflect what we found in behavior? Are there changes in brain structure that help explain their improved hearing?” Park said.

To pull vision loss apart from heavy listening, the authors suggest checking whether blind people’s advantage is larger for hearing sounds that matter most in their everyday lives.

The full study was published in the Journal of Neuroscience.
