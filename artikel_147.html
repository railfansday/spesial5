Election date? Brexit reset outcome? Farage’s survival chances? Andrew Sparrow answers your questions

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

The Guardian’s UK politics blogger is conducting a live Q&A

From now on, I will be focusing on the Q&A.

To follow what is happening at the Coalition of the Willing summit, do read Jakub Krupa’s Europe live blog.

double quotation mark@Andrew - gov must call a GE by August 2029. Burnham has ruled out a 2027 GE, so do you think that means we’ll have one in 2028? Because it’s inconceivable they’d wait till 2029 when gov has to cut spending isn’t it?

The conventional wisdom in British politics is that prime ministers call elections on the first Thursday after they have been in office for four years and when polling suggests they will win. Often this ends up as the first Thursday in May, when local elections happen anyway.

That is because a PM with a decent chance of winning at that point normally does not want to risk ‘events’ taking away his or her lead, and leaving no room for manoeuvre, as the five-year hard deadline looms.

On that basis, 2028 would be a reasonable guess.

You are right to say Burnham has ruled out an election in 2027. Theresa May also ruled out an election in 2017 – until she changed her mind. It is not impossible that Burnham might do this. But voters don’t like early elections, and dozens of Labour MPs would probably lose their seats, and so I can only see that happening unless Burnham can come up with a very, very good pretext to justify one. I think that’s unlikely. (And I hope it won’t happen too – I’ve booked a holiday for next spring.)

As Jennifer Rankin reports, one of the issues still being haggled over at the last minute ahead of tomorrow’s UK-EU reset deal has been how many young EU nationals would be allowed to come to Britain under the youth mobility scheme.

In a report for the i, Arj Singh says the EU seems to have won this one. He says:

double quotation markThe UK and EU have provisionally agreed there will be a cap of 100,000 visas on a landmark Brexit reset deal to allow easier travel, work and study for under-32s between the two areas, The i Paper has learnt …

It means 100,000 Brits stand to benefit from easier arrangements to travel, work and study in the EU from as early as 2028, with a final deadline of 2030, and vice versa.

The i Paper has also learnt the UK and EU have agreed the scheme will be open to those aged 18 to 31 on the first day of their intended stay on the other side, despite initial suggestions it would only be open to under-30s.

Singh says this still needs to be signed off by EU member states. He adds:

double quotation markThe final 100,000 represents a late compromise from the UK, which had been pushing for 80,000, but British negotiators are likely to regard it as a fair one given the EU’s initial push for an uncapped scheme.

In addition to 1,000 long-range strike drones (see 12.03pm), Downing Street has also announced that the UK is buying two advanced fast attack boats for Ukraine. The UK and Norway are jointly paying the £100m cost, and they will be made in Britain, No 10 said.

double quotation markFunded with Norway, built here at home in the UK, and delivering game-changing capability to the Ukrainians, this project will not just strengthen Black Sea defences, but supports European security too.

Stable and secure sea lanes are arteries of global trade, and Russia’s ongoing indiscriminate attacks in the Black Sea are not just hurting Ukraine and our European Allies but also hitting the world’s most vulnerable.

Downing Street has said the talks about the UK-EU reset deal due to be announced at a summit tomorrow are “going down to the wire”.

At the No 10 lobby briefing, the PM’s press secretary said:

double quotation markThe talks with the EU are going down to the wire.

If we secure a deal at tomorrow’s summit, the government will bring forward legislation to parliament next week.

There is a consensus in the country that change needs to happen.

The choice over the coming days is clear: stand with Boris Johnson and Kemi Badenoch defending a broken status quo, or with Andy Burnham delivering change, cutting costs for business, helping with the costs of the weekly shop and creating opportunities for young people.

In an article for the Daily Mail today, Kemi Badenoch claimed the deal being unveiled tomorrow would take the UK backwards. She said:

double quotation markThe three central purposes of Brexit were control over our money, over our laws and over our borders. On each of those, Burnham is moving us backwards.

Consider his proposed agreement on food and agricultural products. The farmers and businesses exporting to the EU will welcome fewer checks and less paperwork.

But what about the 90 per cent of businesses and farmers who don’t export to Europe? They are going to be forced backwards on innovations like precision breeding and GM crops, on which Britain has been able to surge ahead of the EU since leaving.

But, at the lobby briefing, the PM’s press secretary defended the decision to seek a reset. He said:

double quotation markThe status quo is not working. Brexit has done more harm than good.

It has not fixed what was broken a decade ago and those problems have become even more severe.

The cost of living has risen, it’s harder for British people to travel and businesses to trade, and the small boats crisis has reached our shores.

We ended up with a harder Brexit than was promised at the referendum. The hard Brexit that was negotiated has impacted people’s everyday lives, longer queues at the airport and young people missing out on the chance to live, work or study abroad.

The world has changed. Russia poses a much more direct threat to European security, tariffs have disrupted trade and the war in Iran has hit people’s pockets.

The lack of international border controls at the start of the pandemic left the UK in an “extremely vulnerable position”, the Covid inquiry has concluded. Jessica Murray has the story.

Robert Jenrick has been mocked and criticised after giving an interview critical of Margaret Thatcher.

At the Conservative party conference just two years ago, before he defected to Reform UK, Jenrick astonished party members by revealing that, such was his admiration for the former PM, he had burdened one of his daughters with Thatcher as a middle name.

Even by Tory standards that was seen as excessive – although Thatcher is probably the only former party leader, other than Winston Churchill, who has always been venerated by the vast majority of its members.

Nigel Farage, the Reform UK leader, was also a huge Thatcher admirer in the 1980s, and he only left the Conservative party after she was ousted and John Major signed the Maastricht Treaty.

But Reform UK are now polling well in former Labour seats in the Midlands and the north of England and Jenrick’s comments about Thatcher seem to be part of a deliberate tilt to appeal to this audience.

In an interview with the Sun, Jenrick said:

double quotation markI grew up in the Black Country, and my mum and dad worked in heavy industry and those industries were destroyed in the 1970s by out-of-control trade unions, bad management, and by the policies that Labour and Tory governments pursued at that time.

And yeah, the Thatcher government turned that around. But they also did it in a way which left a deep scar on these communities.

Businesses went under. There was a loss of pride and identity which has taken a long time and perhaps has never fully recovered.

Jenrick also criticised Kemi Badenoch, the Conservative leader, for “wrapping herself in the mantle of Margaret Thatcher”. Pointing out that Burnham also defines himself in relation to Thatcher (the PM argues that many of the problems facing Britain now started with policies she introduced), Jenrick said Westminster should “move on” and talk about the future. He said:

double quotation markMargaret Thatcher is as dated a reference to my kids as Winston Churchill was to me when I was growing up, and that doesn’t feel that long ago.

In a post on social media, Luke Tryl, the More in Common pollster, said that disowning Thatcherism was a logical electoral strategy for Reform UK.

double quotation markFurther evidence I think Reform is leaning into where their actual potential voter pool actually is and if you’re not going to be able to squeeze affluent Tories, your voter pool of socially conservative/economically statist voters dont want Thatcherism 2.0

But Jenrick has also been widely mocked for his comments, which will do nothing to quell claims that – even by MP standards – he is a shameless opportunist.

Quite what Jenrick’s daughter thinks of this all remains a mystery. However, the Tories have issued a comment. A Conservative spokesperson said:

double quotation markWhether it’s Brexit, the border or the Iron Lady, Robert Jenrick’s political compass is all over the place. Having named his daughter after Margaret Thatcher, Bobbie now wants to disown her.

You cannot believe a word he says. He has no fixed principles, no convictions and no consistency. He is a political weather vane, pointing whichever way he thinks the wind is blowing.

It is impossible to keep up with which version of Robert Jenrick will turn up next.

Andy Burnham is going to announce details of a £260m package of support for Ukraine at the Coalition of the Willing meeting in Berlin this afternoon. It will include more than 1,000 long-range strike drones. No 10 says:

double quotation markArriving in Berlin today, Andy Burnham is set to announce a new £260m package, including long-range drones, which can travel more than 1000km - the equivalent to the distance between London and Berlin.

The package will include strike drones able to carry heavy warheads capable of flattening targets in a single hit, enabling Ukraine to target military production facilities and other legitimate military targets far behind the front lines.

The UK will also build and deliver vital maritime, logistical, and surveillance drones to allow Ukraine to resupply front line forces, ensuring stocks such as blood and fridges for medical stores reach those on the front line …

Today’s announcement of further support won’t just boost Ukraine’s front lines; it will also bring jobs and opportunity around the country, with more than 80% of this funding to be spent in the UK – including with small and medium-sized businesses across Wales, Northern Ireland, and Scotland.

Over in Brussels, EU ministers are arriving for foreign affairs talks, with questions already popping up about tomorrow’s EU-UK summit, 10 years on from Britain’s vote to leave the bloc.

Ireland’s EU minister Thomas Byrne said the main areas to be discussed on Friday “will have very significant benefits” for all parties, with the sanitary and phytosanitary agreement set to resolve “one of the biggest problems out of Brexit”.

But he was cautious talking about the future relationship with the EU, saying that while he had “no doubt that the EU and Britain will want to go further on that path, we have seen how complex even dealing with the issues for tomorrow is and how long it takes.”

Sweden’s Jessica Rosencrantz stressed it is “up to the citizens of the UK to decide how close you want to work with the EU”.

But, again, asked if Britain could rejoin the bloc and keep its historical opt-outs and rebates, she dismissed it as “a highly speculative question”. She added:

double quotation markLet’s remember how the discussion was at the time of Brexit. In that light, I think it is amazing that we now can have a big summit together, with discussions on how we can come closer together when it comes to trade and mobility.

Poland’s EU minister Ignacy Niemczycki said Warsaw’s approach “has always been very pragmatic,” as he stressed that the EU after Brexit is “weaker.” He said:

double quotation markI would like us, wherever possible, to work out such mechanisms so it works for both of us. … This is a country that thinks similarly to us in Europe on many issues and there is no reason for us to treat each other like enemies. We are friends and let’s find the right formula for that.

double quotation markI would encourage everyone to think seriously about the benefits of being in the EU, … and I think the people of the UK see it [too], now that they are outside the union.
