A Former DOGE Operative Won a Federal Contract. Then Things Got Messy

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Yet another former member of the so-called Department of Government Efficiency (DOGE) appears to have exited the government only to return as a contractor.

In September, the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) put out a call for a contract worth $200,000 for “modernization discovery, prototyping, and implementation-planning engagement.” Four days later, the department closed the bidding process and awarded the contract to Yat Choi LLC, a company owned by a former Airbnb engineer and Canadian citizen named Yat Choi, whose company also goes by the name GovMaxxing.

At the time, Choi had left his role at the National Design Studio (NDS)—the group tasked with redesigning government websites—just a few weeks prior. Before that, Choi worked at DOGE.

The bid was for a project set to run for a little under six months, and the contract was awarded as sole-source, meaning that it did not go through a normal competitive bidding process. In the documentation announcing the award, HHS justified this decision by saying that its “market research and source rationale found that conventional consulting firms generally would need to assemble a multidisciplinary team or obtain additional technical resources” to provide the required skills around “business-process modernization, artificial intelligence, automation, technical architecture, and rapid prototyping.”

In October, a company called CoA Solutions lodged what’s known as a bid protest with the Government Accountability Office (GAO) contesting the agency’s decision to award the contract to Choi. CoA Solutions is a small business that builds AI software for the government and received another contract with the Treasury Department in 2024.

Ari Perez, the CEO of CoA Solutions, tells WIRED that his company made a bid below the $200,000 award price, and it proposed two experts doing the work full-time, including Perez himself.

Perez says that CoA asked HHS for an explanation of the award on October 8 and, when it did not receive one, filed its protest with the GAO the next day. Perez says that his company’s sole contestation was about how the bids were evaluated by HHS, not about Choi or his work with NDS.

“I'm a service-disabled veteran. I've served this government since I was 17 at West Point. At the US Digital Service I helped deliver for veterans at the VA, and later I worked on asylum and immigration systems at DHS. I started CoA to keep doing impactful government work,” says Perez. “We want to win on merit, and if HHS reopens it, we'll bid again.”

According to CoA, in response to the bid protest, HHS terminated the award to Choi and canceled the call for contractors. With the cancellation, the GAO dismissed the protest without investigating it further. A GAO spokesperson referred WIRED to HHS.

“HHS remains committed to modernizing its systems to improve efficiency and better serve the American people,” says HHS spokesperson Grace Davis Jamison. “Following a bid protest challenging the award, HHS voluntarily took corrective action, terminating the contract at no cost to the government and canceling the solicitation.”

Choi’s role in the US government has not been exactly clear. In his bio on X, Choi writes that he was part of both DOGE and NDS, and said in a post that he was recruited by billionaire Elon Musk and Joe Gebbia, Airbnb’s cofounder, to help modernize the retirement system at the Office of Personnel Management (OPM). Gebbia now leads NDS, which was created after Musk left government and which has served as a landing pad for several DOGE members, including Edward “Big Balls” Coristine.

In December 2025, Choi contributed to a blog on the NDS website about automating the federal retirement system. But on his LinkedIn, Choi lists himself as a senior technologist at OPM. On November 5, just two days after Republicans lost the midterm elections, Choi announced in a post on X that he had left OPM and was launching his company, GovMaxxing, through which he plans to continue to contract with the government. GovMaxxing operates as Yat Choi LLC federally.

In response to questions from WIRED about his role in government, Choi says that he “was recruited to support federal modernization efforts and subsequently onboarded as an OPM contractor.” There, Choi says, he “contributed to modernization efforts associated with DOGE and subsequently NDS. I was not, and have never been, a federal government employee.” Choi claims he left OPM in mid-August, though OPM did not respond to questions confirming whether this was the case. “I began actively pursuing the contracting opportunity after my engagement with OPM ended,” says Choi.

Choi confirmed that he is a Canadian citizen and a “lawful permanent resident” of the US. Foreigners are not generally allowed to hold full-time roles in government or serve as special government employees. The rules are more flexible for contractors. During the height of DOGE’s activity, a close Musk ally, Baris Akis, was prevented from joining the group as a special government employee because he is a Turkish citizen, according to The Atlantic. Akis, however, continued to serve as a recruiter for the group.

On his website, Choi labels the “client” for his work in government as “US Office of Personnel Management / DOGE / National Design Studio” and describes himself as the “modernization lead.” However, documents indicate that Choi’s company was not registered in the state of New York until April 2026, and in the System for Award Management, the procurement website where US government contractors bid, there are no other contracts for Choi’s work. “None of my work at OPM was performed through my LLC. My website references my prior professional experience at OPM and does not attribute that work to my company,” says Choi.

Choi says that the HHS contract was for a “separate modernization discovery project. It was not a continuation of any work with OPM, DOGE, or NDS.”

According to the code of federal regulations, except in very specific circumstances, government contracts cannot be awarded to government employees or to organizations they fully or partially own. But Jessica Tillipman, associate dean for government procurement law studies at George Washington University, says that even though Choi was not a regular government employee, the fact that he was part of DOGE and NDS, whose work has spanned across government agencies, could create the appearance of a conflict of interest with regard to the HHS contract. “The baseline posture of the federal acquisition regulation is to strictly avoid conflicts of interest or even the appearance of them,” says Tillipman. “So agencies all have a duty, and the contracting teams have a duty, to make sure that they are considering these things when they're awarding these contracts.”

Choi tells WIRED that HHS “initiated contact and expressed interest” in his “modernization experience” before the call for bids was announced. HHS did not respond to questions about whether the bid had been written specifically for him.

Tillipman notes that while the government may sometimes create a contracting opportunity designed for a specific person or company, “it’s generally niche or highly specialized work." Tillipman says that the fact that there were 75 bids placed on the contract indicates that this is “not a unique service.” She notes that the government’s justification for a sole-source contract is “thin.” “Usually, when there's only one responsible source, there's a technical reason,” she says. This could be something like a special tool that is not interoperable with outside software, or when there is only one company that can provide a specific service or that makes a certain physical part that might be used for weapons or specialized machinery.

On the GovMaxxing website, Choi notes that potential government clients can work with the company as a prime contractor or “subcontract through one of our partner primes your agency may already be working with.” Unlike prime contracts, there is no way to publicly see which subcontractor a prime contractor works with. Choi declined to name the companies or current “prime” contractors he subcontracts under.

But Tillipman says that even if Choi were to take on another version of the HHS contract for a subcontractor, similar questions could still be raised within the agency and externally should it ever come to light. “In certain instances, your subcontractors’ conflicts can be imputed to the prime contractor,” she says. “The whole thing is highly irregular overall.”

Choi is not the only former DOGE or NDS member who appears to be hoping to benefit from federal contracts after leaving the government. Earlier this year, WIRED reported that Ethan Shaotran, one of the young engineers who formed the early core of DOGE, had started a defense tech startup. The startup has been listed on the System for Award Management since April. Former DOGE members Jack Stein, Gavin Kliger, Luke Farritor, and Marko Elez launched an AI defense technology company called Cathedral, which has been listed on the System for Award Management since July. Neither have received “prime” contracts with any federal agency, but whether they have been taken on as subcontractors to other prime contractors is unclear.

Despite the HHS contract’s cancellation, Choi appears undeterred. Earlier this week, following the nationwide grounding of flights by the Federal Aviation Administration, Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy called for funding to modernize air traffic control in a post on X. In response, Choi wrote, “Secretary Duffy, I’m the DOGE engineer who led the modernization of federal retirement processing at OPM last year … I’ve since started a company to work with more agencies, and we’d love to help with the FAA’s modernization efforts. Put us in, coach!”
