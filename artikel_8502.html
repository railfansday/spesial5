Garage Sale Pricing – A Third of Retail to Start, Half Off by Afternoon

Garage sale shoppers expect thrift store prices, so price to sell everything rather than to get back what you paid. Tag most items at a third of what they’d cost new, take offers down to a quarter, and mark everything half off at noon so little goes back inside.

The money you spent on each item is gone whether it sells or not, so it shouldn’t set the price.

Shoppers compare your price with what the same thing costs new and what a thrift store would charge for it. For something used, with no warranty and no returns, they’ll pay only a fraction of the new price.

Your own tags will still feel low, since owners tend to value their things above what a buyer would pay, a bias called the endowment effect.

Do the tagging the week before, while you plan the sale, since you’ll be busy making change once shoppers arrive. Start from what each item would cost new today. Tag most things at a third of that price, and accept offers down to a quarter of it. A blender that sells for $60 new gets a $20 tag, and you can take $15 for it. A $600 sofa in good shape is tagged $200, with $150 the least you’ll take.

Before you tag anything that could bring $50 or more, that sofa included, search eBay for the same item and filter the results to Sold items, which shows what it sold for in the last 90 days. If it went for far more than your tag, keep it out of the sale and sell it online.

Put a price on every item where a shopper can see it. Most get their own sticker, but a box of things that sell for little secondhand, like books, can share one sign, such as $1 for any hardcover. Skip color-coded dots, which make shoppers check a chart for each price, and one price for a whole table of mixed things, which sells the best pieces too cheap. Bundle small things, like four mugs for $3, and put odds and ends in a free box.

Make a sign before the sale opens announcing either half off everything or $5 for whatever fits in a grocery bag, and put it up at noon.
