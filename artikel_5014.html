Wrapped Monero Token Collapses 99% After $13 Million Hack Blamed on Web Host

A wrapped version of Monero lost nearly all its value Friday after an attacker drained its deployer wallet and dumped the tokens on Hyperliquid.

XMR1, a token issued by cross-chain swap service Wagyu that represents Monero on Hyperliquid, fell more than 99% in roughly half an hour, from about $500 to around $1.

The account that deployed the token sent 5.2 million XMR1 to a newly created account early Friday, according to an analysis by onchain data firm Bitquery.

That account sold about 364,000 of the tokens to 388 buyers for $13.5 million, then converted the proceeds to ETH. At Bitquery’s last check, about 7,350 ETH sat in three wallets.

Wagyu’s pseudonymous founder, PerpetualCow, blamed a breach at 1984.hosting, an Icelandic web hosting provider.

He wrote on X that the XMR1 deployer key had been stored on an old server there that he had deleted, but that the host apparently kept its logs and files. “Lesson to learn here is that these hosting providers keep your data/logs even after you delete your server with them,” he wrote.

1984 ehf., the company behind the service, said it had suffered an extensive and serious cyberattack on its IT infrastructure, Icelandic outlet DV reported Friday, citing a joint statement with CERT-IS, Iceland’s national cybersecurity incident response team.

Early findings point to a well-organized and technically sophisticated attack, and the company said it couldn’t rule out that more systems were affected than it had confirmed. It temporarily shut down all services. The statement didn’t mention Wagyu or whether customer data was taken.

PerpetualCow said he would personally refund everyone who lost money and that Wagyu would relaunch later. “Give me some time please,” he wrote, but didn’t give a timeline.

Bitquery said onchain data alone can’t show whether the deployer’s key was stolen, and that it can’t see how much Monero backs the remaining XMR1, since the Monero side of the bridge isn’t visible.

The attacker’s account received its first funds from the crypto mixer Tornado Cash minutes before it was set up. The same deployer account still controls nearly all of ZEC1, a Zcash token. Wagyu also disclosed a separate data leak in October, saying at the time that funds were unaffected.

Wagyu surfaced earlier this month in ZachXBT’s tracing of a suspected $106 million theft from an unnamed European entity, in which part of the funds were converted to Monero through the service.
