Scientists discover that the heart doesn't age the way we thought

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

As the heart gets older, inflammation increases, but scientists have not fully understood where these changes occur or which parts of the organ are most affected. Researchers at The Jackson Laboratory (JAX) have now uncovered a surprising pattern: Certain regions of the heart appear to be hotspots for aging-related inflammation, offering new clues about how the heart changes over time.

In a study of mouse hearts published in Aging Cell, the researchers discovered that signs of inflammation and tissue scarring were especially concentrated around the aortic valves and aortic root. These areas contain immune cells and connective tissue cells whose interactions change with age, potentially affecting the surrounding heart muscle. The findings provide a new picture of cardiac aging and could guide future efforts to understand cardiovascular disease.

"Some of the markers associated with inflammaging were there from the very beginning in young hearts, but they were focused specifically in areas no one had defined before, in the valves and the aorta rather than in muscle tissue," said study author Nadia Rosenthal, scientific director at JAX. "We really have to think of aging as something that is not linear, because just like we know that people don't age at the same rate, what we know now is that organs and tissues don't age at the same rate either, even within one individual."

Much of the previous research on heart aging has centered on cardiac muscle, particularly the left ventricle. This chamber performs the essential task of pumping blood into the aorta and throughout the body. By studying the heart as a whole, rather than concentrating primarily on its muscle, the JAX team uncovered important changes in other tissues and the ways their cells interact.

"The valves and the major blood vessels are parts often thought of as plumbing rather than the main engine, but this is a very complicated little machine," Rosenthal said. "It turns out they're home to many of the cells we're interested in, and that's where some of the most important aging-related signals were concentrated."

Growing older is the single greatest risk factor for cardiovascular disease, yet many of the cellular processes responsible for the heart's decline remain poorly understood. One challenge is that mammalian heart muscle cells have very little ability to regenerate or replace themselves. To investigate what happens as the heart ages, Rosenthal and her colleagues used advanced techniques that allowed them to study thousands of individual cells from young and old mice while also identifying their locations within the heart.

Scientists already know that aging is accompanied by a persistent, low level of inflammation throughout the body. Earlier studies have found similar changes in the heart, including an increase in immune cells that regulate inflammatory activity (macrophages) and cells that produce and maintain connective tissue and contribute to scar formation (fibroblasts).

The new analysis showed that aging macrophages became more active in genes involved in inflammation and changes to tissue structure. Fibroblasts, meanwhile, increasingly displayed molecular characteristics associated with fibrosis (or scarring). Crucially, the researchers went beyond identifying which genes were active. They also mapped the locations of these cells, making it possible to see how different regions of the heart change as they grow older.

This approach revealed that inflammation and fibrosis (or scarring) were not distributed uniformly. Instead, the strongest signals appeared around the heart valves and aortic root. The researchers believe these regions may act as important centers of cardiac aging, where changes in supporting cells could influence the health of nearby heart muscle.

"To keep muscle cells happy, the other cells in the heart have to continue supporting them throughout life," Rosenthal said. "What this study does, for the first time, is provide a comprehensive picture of how those support cells, particularly fibroblasts and macrophages, change with age and contribute to what we call inflammaging."

Surprising Signs of Aging in Young Hearts

Another unexpected discovery challenged assumptions about how scientists identify aging cells. The team examined molecular markers frequently associated with cellular senescence, a state in which cells permanently lose the ability to divide, often because of aging or stress. As senescent cells accumulate, they can contribute to ongoing inflammation, tissue damage, and diseases associated with older age.

Surprisingly, some of these supposedly age-related markers were already present in young mouse hearts. Their presence suggests that the markers alone cannot reliably establish whether a cell has entered senescence, and that their meaning may depend on where they appear and the condition of the surrounding tissue.

"What surprised us was that some of these markers that have been associated with aging were already present in young hearts. They weren't where anyone expected to find them, and that forced us to rethink what those markers are telling us," Rosenthal said. "That turns the usual interpretation of cardiac aging on its ear because these markers alone don't mean a cell is senescent. There's nothing senescent about a six-month-old mouse heart."

The researchers also identified genes that became more active with age and were especially prominent in the same regions. Among them were Ccl8, which helps transmit inflammatory signals, and Cdkn1a (also known as p21), a gene widely used as an indicator of cellular senescence. Their locations provided further evidence that the valves and aortic root may be particularly important areas for understanding how the heart ages.

"We're not talking about single genes. We're talking about an entire conversation between immune cells and fibroblasts. As the heart ages, the way those cells communicate changes, and that shift appears to be linked to the inflammation and tissue remodeling we see in older hearts," Rosenthal said.

Male and Female Hearts Show Different Aging Patterns

The study uncovered another important distinction: Aging did not produce exactly the same molecular changes in male and female mouse hearts.

In males, aging fibroblasts showed stronger connections to biological processes associated with fibrosis. In females, the corresponding changes were more closely related to pathways involved in heart development and protein production.

Rosenthal noted that these differences may offer clues about cardiovascular aging in humans. Heart disease tends to become more common in women after menopause, while its development in men generally follows a more gradual pattern. The distinct cellular changes observed in male and female mice could help researchers investigate the biological processes behind these differences.

However, the results describe differences in molecular activity, not demonstrated differences in heart function or disease risk. Further research will be needed to determine how these patterns influence cardiovascular health.

To explore whether their findings might also apply to people, the team compared the mouse results with previously published data from human hearts. Several molecular processes associated with aging, including pathways involved in responding to cellular stress, appeared in both species. This overlap suggests that at least some of the biological changes identified in mice may also be relevant to human heart aging.

"Our findings change the focus from aging across the entire heart to specific anatomical hotspots, helping us better understand how inflammation and fibrosis are connected and which regions of the heart are involved in the aging process," said study author Amalia Sintou, a postdoctoral associate at JAX.

Could These Discoveries Help Protect Aging Hearts?

Although the research does not yet identify a new treatment, it offers scientists a more detailed understanding of where aging-related changes occur and which cells are involved. By revealing these cellular patterns, the findings could help guide future studies aimed at reducing cardiovascular decline, maintaining healthy heart function, and improving health later in life.

Rosenthal's team is now examining whether exercise, diet, and disease alter the interactions between immune cells and connective tissue cells. The researchers hope to distinguish the normal effects of aging from changes that may leave the heart increasingly vulnerable to cardiovascular problems.

The experimental design also helped the team isolate aging itself as a contributing factor. Because the mice lived under the same conditions and received the same diet, the researchers could investigate age-related changes without the additional variation introduced by different environments or eating habits.

"Valve replacement is common in older adults, and one of the big questions is how much of the underlying damage is driven by aging itself," said study author Aleksandra Aljakna Khan, a postdoctoral associate at JAX. "Because all of the mice in our study lived in the same environment and ate the same diet, we were able to isolate the effects of aging and show that aging alone can initiate some of these changes in the valves and aorta."

Together, the findings suggest that understanding heart aging requires looking beyond the muscle responsible for pumping blood. The valves, major blood vessels, and supporting cells may play a much larger role than previously recognized. Learning how these structures change and communicate over time could eventually help scientists identify ways to preserve cardiovascular health as people age.
