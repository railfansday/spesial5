What happens in a dog’s brain when its owner smiles but sounds angry?

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

A small study recorded dogs’ brain activity to explore how they respond when their owners’ facial expressions and vocal tones conflict.

A dog’s brain may notice within half a second when its owner’s happy face comes with an angry voice.

Researchers in Japan recorded electrical brain activity in three pet dogs as each viewed and heard recordings of its owner. The response was larger when the emotion in the face clashed with the emotion in the voice.

A reaction of such speed could not have been captured by the brain scanners used in earlier dog studies.

With the new method, the timing of a dog’s brain response to conflicting human signals can be measured.

The team was led by Takatomi Kubo, an associate professor at the Nara Institute of Science and Technology in Japan.

Hitomi Koike of Azabu University, the study’s first author, collected the recordings.

The work was described by the authors as a pilot. With only three dogs, it shows the method works but says little yet about dogs in general.

Dogs have lived alongside people for about 30,000 to 40,000 years. Several kinds of evidence suggest they respond to human emotions.

In a 2016 experiment, dogs tended to look longer when a human face and an emotional voice matched.

Brain imaging has also shown stronger activity near dogs’ hearing regions when they hear a happy voice.

Imaging of that kind is limited, however, since activity shifting within a fraction of a second cannot be followed. Small sensors resting on the scalp can, because the brain’s electrical activity is recorded directly.

The test is called an EEG. Kubo’s team took 1,000 readings a second.

In 2024, another group used the test on dogs. Brain responses changed when a spoken word did not match the object the dogs were shown.

Until now, emotion in a face and a voice together had not been tested in EEG research on dogs.

The sample consisted of one Brittany spaniel and two standard poodles kept as pets. Their ages ranged from 5 to 10 years.

The dogs were trained to remain still on a mattress with their chins supported. Each wore an individually fitted cap holding seven recording sensors.

The dogs were not physically restrained and received rewards for remaining still.

Each owner remained outside while the dog viewed a photograph of that owner’s happy or angry face for three seconds.

A recording of the owner’s voice was then delivered through a speaker at the midpoint of that interval.

A single word, “really?”, was spoken in every recording and was one used often by the owners in daily life. Only the tone was altered so that it sounded happy in some and angry in others.

In half of the pairings, the emotions agreed, and in the remaining half they differed.

A panel of 28 people had rated each photograph and recording before any dog was tested. Significant differences between the happy and angry versions were confirmed by those ratings.

Analysis was restricted to trials during which the dog had been looking at the screen.

When the face and the voice disagreed, the brain’s electrical response was larger. This increase was observed between 0.25 and 0.5 seconds after the onset of the voice.

The difference in response between matched and mismatched pairs was found to be small. The greatest consistency was observed at a single sensor positioned at the front of the head.

No reliable difference between matched and mismatched pairs was found in the first quarter of a second.

All three dogs showed the larger response in the later period, though not at the same spot. For the 10-year-old poodle, it appeared at the top of the head instead of the front.

The two kinds of mismatch, a happy face with an angry voice and the reverse, also produced somewhat different patterns.

The time periods analyzed were borrowed from human studies. It is not known whether a dog’s response at those moments means the same thing.

Kubo said the larger response may reflect a dog paying closer attention. It may also reflect the work of combining two signals, or a reaction to something unexpected.

The recordings cannot establish whether the larger response specifically reflected emotional processing.

A dog may be combining the emotion in a face with the emotion in a voice. It may also be reacting as it would to any event that breaks an expected pattern.

Every face and voice belonged to the dog’s own owner. So the study cannot say whether a stranger would produce the same response.

The photographs were still, while a real expression changes from moment to moment.

One feature of the study had often been avoided in earlier EEG work on dogs. Negative expressions tend to make a dog restless, and electrical noise is added by even slight muscle activity.

Usable signals were recorded by Kubo’s team with both happy and angry faces and voices.

“With further validation, our approach could help researchers understand how human communication affects dogs in everyday care and training,” Kubo said.

Testing larger groups of dogs with both familiar and unfamiliar faces and voices could help clarify what drives the response.

Tracking where each dog looks during a trial has also been suggested.

A further suggestion involves smell, since human emotional states are known to be detected by dogs through odor.

The full study was published in the journal Scientific Reports.
