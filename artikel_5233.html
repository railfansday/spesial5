Tokenizing an asset is only the beginning; financing it is another problem entirely. Lombard Systems announced $4 million in pre-seed and seed funding on November 5 to expand a standard it calls Delivery-versus-Lock, or DvL. The company wants institutions to use tokenized assets as collateral without relying on a different bespoke legal and technical process for each transaction. This is infrastructure funding, not evidence that the proposed standard has already become widely adopted.

Traditional secured lending often involves transferring collateral to a lender or an agent, or creating control arrangements that are expensive to negotiate. Lombard proposes a standardized approach for controlling and enforcing an interest in tokenized collateral. Its technology aims to coordinate asset control with financing obligations rather than treating the blockchain transfer as the entire legal contract. The company says its first institutional deployment is expected by the end of 2026. The announcement therefore describes a system being developed for real use, not one whose risk and cost savings have already been proven across many jurisdictions.

Lombard says funding will help pay for legal analysis in the United States, England and Wales and several additional jurisdictions. That is not a peripheral expense. A lender needs confidence that a collateral right survives insolvency and can be enforced if a borrower defaults. The blockchain might show that a token was locked, but it cannot by itself establish the legal priority of competing claims. Different securities, fund interests and property rights may require different treatment. Standardization is valuable only if the legal rights behind the tokens are recognized where the counterparties operate.

A tokenized collateral arrangement must specify who can authorize transfers, who can release collateral and what happens after a technical failure or disagreement. Removing one intermediary could make the process more efficient, but it can also concentrate operational risk in software, key management or a control location. Participants need escalation procedures, audits and clear rights in insolvency. The phrase delivery-versus-lock is attractive because it sounds simple; the genuine work is ensuring that the economic asset cannot be improperly moved while protecting the borrower from unjustified enforcement.

Tokenized Treasuries, funds and shares may become more useful if institutions can finance positions against them. That could support liquidity and capital efficiency beyond simple buy-and-hold exposure. But widespread collateral use also creates leverage, liquidity and contagion risks. If the same underlying asset is financed through multiple structures, participants need clear controls against double pledging and inconsistent records. Standards can reduce ambiguity, yet they do not remove the economic consequences of falling collateral values or forced liquidation.

The useful next milestones are a live institutional deployment, the jurisdictions covered by enforceable legal opinions, independent technical review and transaction volumes. Watch whether lenders and custodians from different organizations can actually interoperate. A financing protocol succeeds when it makes collateral safer and easier to use, not simply when it receives venture funding or publishes an ambitious architecture diagram.
