Toledo Museum of Art Brings Bobby Anspach Home

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

An improbable inventory of pom-poms, hand-blown glass, tapestries, and chandelier-like domes fills the immersive sculptures Bobby Anspach began building as a graduate student at the Rhode Island School of Design. Gathered under the title Place for Continuous Eye Contact, the enclosed environments are each designed for a three-minute visit, during which a participant wears a patch over one eye and studies the reflection of the other in a small mirror or, in the version made for two, holds the gaze of a second person seated opposite. Inside, light, color, and ambient compositions by Eluvium, the artist’s longtime collaborator, are calibrated to produce subtle shifts in depth perception.

Bobby Anspach in his Studio. Photography courtesy of Katie Hogan.

Three works from the series, along with a newly adapted version for visitors of varying physical abilities, anchor Losing Ourselves Together: The Art of Bobby Anspach at the Toledo Museum of Art through March 2027. Curated by Paige Rozanski, the museum’s associate curator of modern and contemporary art, in collaboration with the Bobby Anspach Studios Foundation, the exhibition marks a homecoming for the Toledo-born artist, who died in 2022, as well as the first presentation of his work at a major American museum. Rozanski organized the galleries as a sequence of spaces that keep Anspach’s voice and his belief in human connection at the center.

‘Machine 1’ by Bobby Anspach. Photography courtesy of Ethan Bond-Watts.

A short documentary opens the show, introducing Anspach through archival footage and his own words. From there, visitors enter the main gallery, where they can enter two enclosures: one made for a single person and another for a pair. Rozanski planned the layout with newcomers in mind.

“Because the ‘eye machines’ are experiential and require a guide to engage with a participant through a set of protocols established by the artist, creating a welcoming environment was essential,” she says.

Installation view of ‘Place for Continuous Eye Contact.’ Photography courtesy of the Bobby Anspach Estate.

The machines are also displayed together in a separate space, allowing visitors to study their individual construction and materials side by side. Throughout the exhibition, the museum’s design team incorporated Anspach’s presence into the details, translating his handwriting into the typography, reproducing his quotes at large scale, and stocking a closing reflection room with books that inspired him, offering visitors a place to decompress after experiencing the sculptures.

Installation view of ‘Place for Continuous Eye Contact’ at the Bobby Anspach Foundation opening in 2024. Photography courtesy of Gabrielle Beaumont.

Anspach’s interest in Buddhism and Eastern philosophy runs through everything on view. “His experience with meditation brought a sense of peace that he hoped to share with others by creating artworks that provide a place of momentary retreat that transcends physical limits,” Rozanski says. He also saw a direct link between human connection and the fate of the environment, a concern made explicit in paintings such as Climate Change and The Beautiful Nothing, both from 2014–2015. Smaller pieces, including Pom Sculpture Study 1 (2020) and Most Beautiful Thing in the World (2014–201515), show him working through ideas at a more intimate scale.

Installation view of ‘Place for Continuous Eye Contact’ at the Bobby Anspach Foundation in 2024. Photography courtesy of Gabrielle Beaumont.

Rozanski singles out one passage as a summary of the artist’s outlook. “I have had very deep experiences in this world which have shown me something profound,” Anspach said. “These experiences have shown me that there is a way of relating to this world that, if everyone could see it, we would all live our lives in a very different way. We would see that what we have is incredibly precious and we would go to great lengths to care for each other and for the planet.”

Portrait of Bobby Anspach. Photography courtesy of the Bobby Anspach Estate.

The exhibition occupies the museum’s Glass Pavilion, a single-story building of layered, curved transparent walls designed by Pritzker Prize-winning architecture firm SANAA. The museum describes the building as a fitting setting for work concerned with dissolving boundaries. Rozanski hopes the presentation introduces Anspach to a wider public while celebrating his roots in Toledo.

“It’s a great honor to present Anspach’s work in his hometown of Toledo,” she says, “and I hope this show will resonate with our local community and bring people together.”

Installation view of ‘Place for Continuous Eye Contact’ at Bobby Anspach Studio. Photography courtesy of Brendan Burdinski.

Losing Ourselves Together: The Art of Bobby Anspach is on view at the Toledo Museum of Art through Mar. 14, 2027.
