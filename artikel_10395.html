What working as an EMT taught Pie and Bonobos founder Andy Dunn

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

I got my first job the way a lot of teenagers do: through my mom.

She ran the ultrasound department at Good Samaritan Hospital in my hometown of Downers Grove, Illinois.

The summer I was 18 she introduced me to her counterpart in x-ray and helped me land the least glamorous job in the entire hospital: darkroom technician. I was a straight-up mole, developing x-rays in a pitch black room, alternating between Chicago Cubs sports radio debates on Sammy Sosa and the song “Peaches” playing on Q101 on repeat.

The easy move the next summer was to go back to that job that no one else in Chicagoland wanted. My boss liked me well enough. But even as a teenager I knew getting comfortable wasn’t the move. Instead I applied to the emergency room. The job paid better. It also required an EMT certification I didn’t have. So I took the course: nights and weekends, learning to splint fractures and say “airway, breathing, circulation” in my sleep.

That’s how, at 19, I ended up at the front desk of an ER at 2 a.m., asking a man what brought him in tonight and hearing the two words every ER greeter dreads: “Chest pain.” It can mean everything from heart attack to heartburn. Either way, it’s now your problem.

That summer taught me four things incredibly relevant to my day job as a founder and CEO.

1. Don’t stay where you started

Why leave a sure thing in x-ray? Because I already sensed something I’d spend decades confirming: Get uncomfortable, then comfortable, then get uncomfortable again. The whole point of being young and unproven is that you can switch lanes. Every move costs you something; you have to prove yourself all over again to a boss who’s never heard of you.

Startups are this on repeat. Every new investor is a new boss you have to prove yourself to. Every new potential team member is someone you need to sell. Every actual new team member is someone you need to activate, and then retain. Every quarter you graduate into a new job—more growth, more capital, more people.

You never graduate from auditioning.

The people who learn to love that fact compound who they are, and those who don’t can get stuck.

Doesn’t matter what you do—don’t stay comfortable for long.

2. Take the worst shift

They gave me the shifts nobody wanted. Nights. Weekends. Holidays. The 11 p.m. to 7 a.m. stretch when the ER alternates between zombie zones of nothingness and then a gunshot wound that wakes the whole place up.

You better be ready.

Here’s what I learned on the night shift: Taking the worst shift is the fastest way to earn the right to a better one. Not by complaining about it. Not by treating it as a stepping stone while visibly looking past it. By doing it well, at 4 a.m., when nobody important is watching.

Every startup has night shifts—the support queue when it’s just you, the unglamorous grind that doesn’t demo well, keeping the site up which no one appreciates . . . until it falls over.

Somebody has to do it.

In a meritocratic organization, the person who raises their hand without being asked is the person everyone remembers when it’s time to dole out the good stuff. Do whatever it takes. Start somewhere. Earn the right.

3. Influence without authority

Here is the org chart of an emergency room, as experienced by a 19-year-old at the front desk: Everyone outranks you. The registration staff outranks you. The PAs outrank you. The nurses outrank you. The doctors outrank everyone.

And yet the job requires getting all of these people to work with you, at speed, when the stakes are actual human lives. You cannot make a nurse do anything. What you have instead: energy, a smile, and usefulness. I made it my job to be someone people were glad to see at hour six of an overnight. I wanted the triage nurse cheering for me, not tolerating me.

Business books call this influence without authority. In the ER we called it not being a pain in the ass. Founders lean on this muscle constantly. You can’t make investors invest, candidates join, or potential customers give a shit. Early on, almost nobody has to do anything for you. Your entire company is built on people choosing to—and they choose the person they’re cheering for.

4. Quit the path, keep the ingredients

Here’s the twist: After two summers, the EMT course, and a completed premed course load, I realized—one week before the MCAT—that I didn’t want to be a doctor.

On paper, that’s a lot of wasted time. Except it wasn’t. Those summers taught me what I actually loved, which had nothing to do with Good Samaritan specifically: hard work when no one is looking, doing something that matters, and doing it with people I love.

The ER had all three.

But I didn’t want to wait 12 years to be an ER doctor. So I pivoted. Kept the ingredients, changed the recipe. Founders learn this the hard way or not at all. Some of my most valuable Bonobos years were spent on things we later killed. The tuition was worth it.

Then the twist.

Two summers after that job, I came back to Good Samaritan—not as an employee, but as a patient, in that same emergency room, with nurses and doctors who knew me from my summer shifts. One summer I was checking people in. Two summers later, someone was checking me in. That manic episode that I experienced became the opening act of my memoir, Burn Rate: Launching a Startup and Losing My Mind, and it made mental health a matter of life and death for me personally. I’d now seen the ER from both sides of the desk.

Today, as founder and CEO of Pie, I’m in the business of rebuilding the social fabric. The surgeon general tells us that lacking social connection is as dangerous as smoking 15 cigarettes a day. I’m not in an emergency room anymore, but I am working on an emergency.

I thought I left healthcare a long time ago.

Turns out I just took the long way back.
