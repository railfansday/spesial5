Lone Wolf Custom Gear Crossover Climber Review: The Most Ingenious Climbing Tree Stand Ever Made

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

The Lone Wolf Custom Gear D'Acquisto Series Crossover Climber is the top choice for mobile hunters who prioritize ultralight portability and dead-quiet performance over comfort. At 11.5 pounds and less than 2 inches wide when packed, it excels for run-and-gun public land deer hunting. Hunters wanting a roomy, comfortable stand or a budget-friendly option should look elsewhere.

I have been testing climbing tree stands for some 30 years now, and in that time, I’ve set up and sat in just about every model made, or at least some version of every base model made. For a long time—probably more than a decade—if you asked me, “What is the best climbing tree stand on the market?” I would have immediately answered, the Lone Wolf Hand Climber.

Ironically, that climber was not the easiest to climb with, and you couldn’t say it was built for comfort, but it was absolutely brilliant in every other way. It weighed nothing, folded down flat as a pizza box, was dead quiet, and bit into the tree like was made of beaver teeth. For run-and-gun—and especially run-and-bow—hunting, there was nothing better.

Well, now there is. The old Hand Climber is no longer produced, and Lone Wolf is now Lone Wolf Custom Gear, or LWCG, but their D’Acquisto Series Crossover Climber is everything the original was, only better. Here’s a close look.

The first thing to understand about the Crossover Climber is that it is uniquely designed for the mobile hunter—for folks who plan to walk far and frequently, often on public land, to get on bucks that other hunters may never know about, and for those who prioritize portability over comfort in a climbing stand.

So, if you’re looking for a cozy climber to scale the same handful of trees on a single property not too far from the truck or ATV—and there’s nothing wrong with that—then don’t waste your money here. There are far-less-expensive climbers that will do that job just fine and, in some ways, better. But if what you want is a climber that you can throw on your back and take anywhere with minimum effort, that accommodates your day pack so you don’t have to stuff your pockets and lash gear, that goes up quick and easy, and whose every detail is purpose-built for your style of hunting—if, in short, you want the absolute state of the art in mobile deer hunting gear, then there is only one climbing stand for you, and you might want start saving your pennies.

The D’Acquisto Crossover Climber is made of machined aluminum with a hammered titanium finish, which makes the stand ultralight and surprisingly quiet, even if you accidentally do bump the metal parts together. Although it is nearly the same size as the old hand climber, it is a full 5 pounds lighter at just 11.5 pounds, and it packs even slimmer.

LWCG kept the original traction belt and cam system for tree attachment, which works great and allows quick adjustments of the seat portion as you climb. Speaking of which, the seat portion now has a recessed, half-moon shape combined with a sliding web seat and removable cushion, an update to the Hand Climber’s rigid seat, which sort of forced you out toward the end of the platform. This new seat system allows you to get closer to the tree, giving you more room to maneuver when standing. The seat portion also adds a micro sit-and-climb bar, which you can use, not use, or remove completely. This is where the “Crossover” name comes from, because whereas Lone Wolf used to sell both Hand Climber and a Sit-and-Climb models, this one offers both options in one unit.

The bottom platform replaces the original’s stabilizing bar with a folding stabilizer that has integrated climbing stirrups, and the whole thing folds down and disappears into the platform for transport. Both the top and bottom portions also have newly designed recessed hinges that fold down perfectly flat. The stabilizing straps that connect the top and bottom portions while climbing have also been updated with metal, instead of plastic, buckles. Finally, there is a new tightening T-bolt that fastens the top and bottom portions together for transport, locking them in place—a huge improvement on the original stand’s bungee cord.

Tree stands aren’t like rifles or binoculars. I can’t report my group-size measurements or scores in a resolution test. What I can tell you is that I have used the stand for a good part of the 2026 bow season so far, and, if you couldn’t tell already, I’m impressed.

The real genius of the Crossover Climber is that everything folds or hinges or meshes or integrates ingeniously to create the most compact and packable (not to mention the lightest) climbing stand on the market. By a mile. As I said, the original Hand Climber packed up as thin as a pizza box. The Crossover packs up as thin as a laptop computer, and the new tightening T-bolt system snugs the two portions of the stand together, so there’s no clinking or clanging while you walk. The stand comes with very good padded shoulder straps, and it is designed so your daypack can attach to and ride along with the stand. The mini sit-and-climb bar even doubles as a support for your pack.

There are a few things I don’t love about this stand. The mesh seat is a bit of a pain. Yes, it slides out of the way to give you more room when standing, which is great, but I found myself having to fiddle with it to reposition for sitting. And I’ll repeat that it is not as roomy or comfortable as many other climbers. You can buy an extension for the bottom platform that adds a few inches, which helps, but this is a small stand. (Personally, I would love it if LWCG came out with a slightly larger version, as I would happily carry a few extra pounds for more room—and it would probably still be lighter than any other climber on the market besides this one.) All of that said, this not a knock on the Crossover, per se, as it is designed to be minimalist. It’s just a fact that you should be aware of before you drop more than a grand on a climbing stand.

Which brings me to the one other thing I don’t love: the price. Oof. Maybe I’m a bad judge, given that I still can’t wrap my head around so many hunters wearing $200 pants. But $1,200 is a price I never thought I’d see on a climbing stand. Of course, I never thought I’d see $8 a pound for beef chuck, either. Everything, it seems, costs three times what it used to. So, I’ll leave it to you to decide if it’s worth it. What I can tell you is no other climbing stand on the market gives half as much attention to detail—details that matter when you’re hunting—as this one. It is brilliantly engineered. And if you’re a mobile hunter who likes a climber, it will be your constant companion and one of your favorite pieces of gear for many deer season’s to come.
