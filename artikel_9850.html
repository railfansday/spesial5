UK Home Office waved through border system without a working fix for bad data

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

ICO reprimand follows failings that risked wrongful detention, with a temporary correction process taking more than a year to arrive

The Information Commission's Office (ICO) has reprimanded the UK Home Office over a border watchlist system launched without an effective way to correct inaccurate personal information.

The regulator said the failings risked people being wrongly detained for additional checks at the UK border, potentially causing distress and other harm.

The platform in question, Helios, was slated to replace its predecessor, Warnings Index, in September 2024 and despite an initial delay due to concerns over data quality, went live the following month.

Both Helios and Warnings Index are watch-listing systems, designed to allow law enforcement officials – mainly but not exclusively Border Force – to check people's data against official records at the UK border.

Helios's federated operating model, whereby the data sources are not centrally managed, yielded quicker results than Warnings Index, but contained a critical flaw in that operational staff could not alter incorrect records.

Platform operators, such as Border Force officers, could not amend the data that feeds into it, neither after the fact nor in real time. This was not the case with Warnings Index, which had a dedicated team responsible for correcting data entries in real time.

Under Helios, staff were supposed to report inaccuracies to the organization responsible for the relevant source data, which would make the correction.

One slight problem: there was no established reporting system at the time Helios was launched on October 2.

The platform went live anyway, as officials deemed the data integrity and security risks presented by Warnings Index to be greater than those of Helios.

Just nine days passed before the Home Office's data protection officer had to submit a breach report after Helios operational staff said they didn't know how to request data corrections.

During the time, Helios operators sent 87 data rectification requests to the Warnings Index correction team, despite it having no capability to make the required changes to the data.

The Home Office later said that many other requests likely weren't logged as the proper processes were not in place, and that staff should have logged the issue through a ServiceNow IT ticket, as any other departmental civil servant with any kind of IT issue would have.

But the Home Office also said that including personal data in those helpdesk tickets would itself constitute a data breach.

The ICO said in its reprimand document [PDF] that it is "unclear" how such a reporting arrangement could realistically work in practice, given that a report would require details from a subject's record that required rectification as well as the new, accurate information that should be used to replace it.

The Home Office's proposed workaround was to get operational staff to verbally read out said personal data over a phone call with the IT helpdesk – a procedure it described as possible "but cumbersome."

It took the Home Office more than a year to implement a temporary data-rectification method, which involved a BX Data Query tile on the IT Now platform, allowing Helios operators to securely report data accuracy issues.

The new method went live on October 25, 2025.

By March 18, 2026, the Home Office said the new process had received more than 6,500 correction requests and around 5,000 records had been removed.

The ICO said it "remains concerned" about the efficacy of this method, given that operators cannot include personal data in the tile report, but the regulator is satisfied the problem is at least in part fixed.

The Home Office also said it was aware of an increase in customer complaints following Helios's launch, and around 800 cases in which people were delayed at the UK border pending additional checks.

It was unable to attribute a specific number of these to Helios's technical issues, however.

"The Home Office further stated that it was not aware of any data subjects suffering distress as a result of using its services at the UK border," the ICO's report stated.

A Home Office spokesperson told us: "We take data protection extremely seriously. That is why we have co-operated fully with the Information Commissioner's Office. "We immediately removed all outdated records to minimize delays to passenger journeys and strengthened mechanisms to speed up updates."Our processes will continue to be kept under review, ensuring we meet data protection obligations while protecting border security."

Joanne Stones, investigations manager at the ICO, said: "People must be able to trust that decisions about their immigration status at the UK border are made with accurate, up to date information." She added: "They also have the right to expect that when information is inaccurate, there is a swift and efficient way for it to be corrected.

"We recognize that federated systems like Helios present unique challenges and welcome the steps the Home Office has taken since this issue was identified. However, the Home Office must ensure that its permanent solution provides a robust and effective way to identify and correct inaccurate information without delay."

The ICO's public sector enforcement policy favors guidance, reprimands, and other interventions, reserving fines for the most egregious cases. Its rationale is to avoid diverting money from public services and effectively punishing those affected twice.

Fines have included £350,000 for a 2021 MoD email that exposed details of Afghan evacuation applicants, and £750,000 for the PSNI's 2023 disclosure of workforce information, including surnames, initials, ranks, and roles. The PSNI penalty was reduced from £5.6 million under the public sector approach. ®
