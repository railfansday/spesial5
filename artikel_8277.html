To The Hospital — John Muir Trail Days 7 and 8

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

After a night in the Motel 6, I got up, took the car keys Sam left me, and went to the Northern Inyo Hospital.  It was a sunny day in Bishop, California.  My stomach was still full of barbecue from the night before.

My hospital! (Photo credit to India M. Clamp)

The people at the hospital were really nice.  They checked me in.  I thought I packed my insurance card but could not find it.  Fortunately, I had my insurance card on my phone and was able to email that to them.  The nurse took my temperature and other vital signs.

A moment later the doctor came in, and I told her about my health, and the John Muir Trail, and how I wondered whether my hike was finished.  Then she put her stethoscope on my chest for about five seconds.  I was puzzled, but she said, “Look, we’re going to take a chest x-ray to rule out pneumonia.  But regardless of the outcome of that x-ray, we’re going to put you on antibiotics.  The x-ray will tell us which particular antibiotic, but that’s about it.”

I agreed (what else was I going to do?) and they wheeled me off for the x-ray.  Then, after the chest x-ray, nothing happened for a long time. I waited in my little cubby in the Emergency Room for about an hour and a half.  I began to wonder what was going on.

In the meantime, I went to the bathroom.  I began to feel cold and asked them for a blanket.  Finally, the doctor returned and said my chest x-ray looked okay.  She said, “We are going to give you Z-pak.”

According to Google AI: “Z-Pak (azithromycin) is a common brand-name prescription antibiotic (Zithromax) taken over a 5-day course to treat bacterial infections like bronchitis, pneumonia, and ear or throat infections.”  Sounded about right.

She said, “Go to Duane’s Pharmacy in town.  You will get six tablets.  Take two today and then one a day for the following four days.”  She also prescribed some codeine cough medication as well as cough pearls.

Then I said, “Well, doctor, you know what my next question is going to be, right?”

She paused for a few seconds and then replied, “You want to know whether you can go back out on the trail.”

I nodded and she said, “I’ll tell you what.  Take off today and take off tomorrow.  See how you respond to the antibiotic.  If you’re feeling a lot better, I think you can go back on the trail.”

Sam and I checked out of the Motel 6 and drove south, stopping for lunch back at the Copper Top BBQ.  We each had the same order:  Tri-Tip hamburgers and chocolate cake.  Oh, it was so good!

Right at that corner in Big Pine was a sign for the Ancient Bristlecone Pine Forest turnoff.  Having the free time, we drove 45 minutes up into the White Mountains of Inyo County on the east side of the valley (the Sierras are on the west side).  I had never heard of “ancient bristlecone pines” but these trees are apparently the oldest living things on earth.

Bristlecone Pine (thanks to Wal Saravia and Pexels)

Some of these trees are over 4,000 years old.  They live at high altitude in rocky soil under arid conditions.  These factors make them incredibly hardy.  Some are able to continue growing even with just a tiny strip of bark left on them.

Sadly, the location of the oldest tree of all, “Methuselah,” is kept secret to prevent damage from vandals.  Who would do something like that?  But it is not a hypothetical fear.  According to Wikipedia:  “On September 4, 2008, an arsonist set fire to the Schulman Grove Visitor Center and several bristlecone pines. The building and all the exhibits within were destroyed.”  Appalling.

On our way back down, we tried searching for fossils.  We did not have much luck, but we did find a beautiful yellow flowering plant.

Can anyone identify this? Beautiful in any case!

Back in Independence, we checked into the Eastern Sierra Motor Lodge, where we had stayed before setting off the previous week.  It felt like it had been a much longer interval than that.  Sam’s room was for one night; my room was for two nights.

I started to think about unpacking and seeing what I had in the sealed resupply bucket I left in Sam’s car.  No surprise, I had too much food, though not as much as I feared.  The Mount Williamson Motel (of Lauralyn fame) was across the road and had a hiker box for excess food.  So, it would not necessarily go to waste.

That night we went south into Lone Pine and had a steak dinner at Seasons Restaurant.  They ran out of filet mignon, so I had to suffer through a sirloin steak.  What a sacrifice!  I stayed away from the wine for health reasons.  Instead, Sam and I each had a couple of Heineken Zeros.

Sleeping through the night was going to be the big test of the antibiotics.  I had codeine cough medicine and pearls but decided I would not take any – at least at first — because I had taken my two Zithromax at lunch and I wanted to see how I was responding.

Yes, I woke up several times in the night to pee.  But each time I did, I didn’t cough more than two or three times before falling back asleep.  Basically, I slept through the night.

Sam planned to leave for the Las Vegas Airport around mid-morning and asked me to set my alarm so we could go for breakfast together.  When it went off, I immediately asked myself, “How did the night go?”

The answer was promising.  Yes, I felt weak, but I produced much less mucus and coughed much less.  The improvement was dramatic.

Sam and I drove down to Lone Pine to eat at the Alabama Hills Café and Bakery again.  This time we had eggs Benedict with bacon and avocado.  Yes, delicious.  Then we took a drive through the namesake Alabama Hills, famous as the location site for many old Western movies and TV shows.  Many of the boulders are named for images they (ostensibly) suggest and one or two are actually painted.  You can learn more about this area (with pictures) here.

A sample of Alabama Hills (thanks to Allie Feeley and Pexels)

Then we drove back to Independence and soon thereafter, Sam left.  He was headed home.  I was on my own.  Despite my illness, I really enjoyed our time together and appreciated his kindness during my ordeal.  At the same time, I am very used to hiking solo, so there was no anxiety surrounding the change.

After that I loafed about trying to decide how I was feeling, how much impact the antibiotics were having.  I asked myself, “Should I go back on trail tomorrow, or try to take one more day before going back?”  It was a balance between being cautious with my health and being brave enough to get back out there.

I sat outside my room at the motel pondering my options.

The Eastern Sierra Motor Lodge with my socks drying in the foreground and the High Sierra in the background.

Before deciding, I elected to take a walk through town.

First, I dropped off my excess food and gear at the Mount Williamson Motel.  They told me they would not provide transportation to the trailhead for non-guests, but they gave me a name and number for “Mark.”   They said hikers often hired him for that purpose.

I continued down the road, past a stand selling “miel” (honey), and I fell. My first fall on the trail!  It was kind of weird.

The town’s configuration at street crossings entails bright yellow indicators painted on the ground to show where the crosswalks are.  These are at right angles (depending on which road you are crossing at the intersection) and, in between the two, there is a raised concrete block right on the corner.  That block has no marking on it at all, making it kind of invisible, particularly with sunglasses.  I didn’t see that there was a step.  Suddenly I was just  flying onto the ground in the bright sunshine on the hard concrete sidewalk and trying to protect my head to avoid hurting myself severely.

Although it was a dramatic fall, and somebody came running over right away from the miel-selling place, I was actually okay.  Maybe I bruised my elbow just a little bit.  But how funny that my first fall on the John Muir Trail should occur while walking without a pack in the bright sunshine of downtown Independence, California.

There are two places that advertise Mexican food in Independence, but the first one I visited was only open Monday to Friday and this was a Sunday.  The other was a taco truck pop-up next to the Shell station.  I had two carnitas, one asada, and one chorizo there, all really good tacos with a Jarritos de Mandarina (a Mexican tangerine-flavored soft drink) to wash them down.  It was wonderful.

Since I was still close to my motel, I ordered dinner and breakfast to go.  I got a dinner burrito of carnitas asadas, and a breakfast burrito of chorizo for the morning.  I put those in my hotel room and then headed out to the Eastern Sierra Ice Cream Company.

I swear the pure lime they sell there is absolutely the best.  I tried the orange one, which is supposed to be similar, but the lime was just better, so very tart.  This flavor was especially welcome because it was hot.

The temperature was “only” in the low to mid-90s, but the sun was just blasting down.  No one else was foolish enough to walk around town like I was.

Still, I enjoyed the chance to sight-see and I took some pictures of the town.  It was still the Inyo County seat, attesting to a former dynamism.  My picture of that distinguished building — the Inyo County Courthouse — tops this blog post.  But now the town was just dead.  It was weird.

It felt like a ghost town.  Here we were, in peak tourist season too, so it was very strange.  Though I did encounter an attractive young woman and almost persuaded her to come back to my room with me.  At least she let me take a photo of the two of us together which you can see below.

Back in my room (alone) I took stock.  No, I was not ALL better yet.  But I was much better than I had been and was finally trending toward recovery.  I had taken two ‘Zero’ days and would be falling out of alignment with my John Muir Trail permit if I took any more.  With some trepidation, I called Mark and arranged for a pickup at the motel the next morning at 6 a.m.

“Here I come,” I thought.  “I’m going back on the trail tomorrow.  At least, I’m going to try.  I have a 2,700-foot pass to climb right off the bat, so that will be a good test.  And if I can only make 2,500 feet of that, I can still turn around.  I can still come back down.”

I realized also that once I got over the pass, and headed into the High Sierra, I would be kind of committed to keeping on.  The “bail-out” points were few and challenging.  A couple of days earlier I did not expect to be on my way back to Kearsarge Pass, that was for sure.  But now I was going to try.

There was a lot to do before turning in.  I ate the dinner burrito.  It was enormous.  I guessed my hiker hunger was abating, because I couldn’t even finish it.

In assembling my gear, I could not find one of my canteens.  I looked everywhere.  Maybe it was in Sam’s rental car?  Sam gave me his Smartwater bottle, so I still had two water containers.  But I wanted to keep that canteen as it was easier to fill with a filtering squeeze bottle.

What else?  I took a shower, texted, sent emails, and called a bunch of people:  wife, son, friends.

I trimmed my nails a little bit.  They weren’t too long, but the shower presented an opportunity to trim them now.  Who knows whether or when I would be able to do it again the rest of the trip?

The hair tie holding my ponytail I did not even try to remove.  I accepted that my hair was already a complete rat’s nest and it was only going to get worse.

In my supply bucket was an alternate finger splint.  I wore a splint religiously on trail, trying to recover from a mallet finger sustained on my longest and hardest training hike.  In Independence I threw out the old splint and replaced it with the new one.

I packed my backpack.  That was challenging.  I love my backpack, but it was actually just a little too small for what I was carrying on this trip.  Adding the bear cannister crossed the tipping point.  Somehow, I was still making it work.

I tested my umbrella.  I thought, “If I can get that sun umbrella to work, it could actually help me a lot.”

With my new-and-untested 20,000 mAh power bank charged I had taken care of my electronics (except for the “untested” part).

I even spent a little time memorizing lines for my role in “Almost, Maine.”  Lee and I were slated to perform in that play after my return.

All this activity was exhausting me.  So, I completed these trail notes, set my alarm, and hit the mattress.  As I dozed off, I thought, “I hope my next set of trail notes convey a successful first day back on the trail…”
