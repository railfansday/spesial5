These days, many athletes play at the highest level past their late 30s and 40s. NBA icon LeBron James is still riding high at age 41, while Aaron Rogers continues to play great football at 42. Then there’s Serena and Venus Williams, who are still picking up the competitive tennis racket at 44 and 46 years respectively. In fact, there are countless examples of mature athletes who are dominating their field.

“Ah, but they have money, and coaches, and access to the best doctors,” you may argue. But would you be right? To find out, we asked a top doctor if physical longevity is reserved for the super-rich and famous. You may be surprised by what he shared.

“I think this is one of the biggest misconceptions about athletic longevity,” says Jordan D. Metzl, MD, who is a Sports Medicine Physician at Hospital for Special Surgery (HSS) in New York City. Dr. Metzl is also the bestselling author of titles including Running Strong and The Athlete’s Book of Home Remedies, not to mention being the founder of the IronStrength  fitness community, where he serves as an educator and advocate for exercise as medicine. Dr. Metzl is also pushing his own physical limits at 60. “Certainly, professional athletes have advantages that most of us don’t have, like access to personal trainers, nutritionists, physical therapists, sports scientists, physicians, and recovery specialists. They have people monitoring their training loads, sleep, nutrition, and performance. That level of support makes a difference. But the fundamental principles that allow these athletes to remain competitive are available to everyone.”

Rich or poor, famous or not, the drivers for longevity are broadly the same, shares Metzl. “The real secret is consistency,” he tells M&F. “Maintaining muscle mass, developing cardiovascular fitness, prioritizing sleep, eating well, and managing training loads are not exclusive to professional athletes. They are available to all of us.”

As we age, there are new challenges to overcome as relates to staying physically active. “I see a lot of tendon problems, particularly involving the Achilles tendon, patellar tendon, and rotator cuff,” says Metzl. “We also see plenty of knee arthritis, meniscus injuries, hamstring and calf strains, and stress injuries in bone. As people get older, the tissues that allow us to move and compete undergo biological changes. Tendons can become less tolerant of sudden increases in training load, muscle recovery may take longer, and cartilage changes can make certain activities more symptomatic. But I think it’s important to distinguish between getting older and becoming less capable. I see people in their 40s, 50s, 60s, and beyond doing extraordinary things.”

So, with all this in mind, how should we train to mitigate our maturity? “I think one of the biggest mistakes people make as they get older is assuming that they can train exactly the way they did when they were 25,” shares Metzl. “That doesn’t mean we need to stop running, playing tennis, skiing, or lifting weights. It just means we need to become smarter about preparation, progression, and recovery.”

Metzl is also keen to point out the importance of high-quality sleep and nutrition, and making a habit of listening to what your body is telling you. “Pain that persists or progressively worsens is a signal worth investigating,” advises the medical professional. “Think about what professional athletes do. They train consistently, they work on strength and mobility, they pay attention to nutrition, and they prioritize recovery.”

“Athletes (like LeBron James) also understand that training at 40 is different from training at 20,” says Metzl. “They may modify their workloads, change their recovery schedules, and adjust their approach to competition. They are not necessarily doing more; they are doing things more intelligently. I think there is a tremendous lesson here for the public in that you don’t need LeBron’s resources to adopt his habits. You need consistency, discipline, and a willingness to adapt as your own body changes.”

If you’ve let your training lapse entirely, Metzl says that it’s never too late to make progress. “My advice is to start small and make exercise part of your daily life,” he explains. “If you haven’t exercised in years, don’t start by trying to run five miles or spend an hour in the gym. Start by walking for 10 or 15 minutes. Build from there. Add resistance training twice a week. Work on balance and mobility. Gradually increase the duration and intensity of your activity. The World Health Organization recommends 150 to 300 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity each week, along with muscle-strengthening activity at least twice weekly. But I always remind people that something is better than nothing. Even short bursts of vigorous activity during the day can be beneficial. The most important thing is to get started. I often say that the best exercise program is the one you enjoy enough to keep doing. We don’t need everyone to become an athlete. We need everyone to become a little more active.

“One of the things I emphasize with my patients is that aging doesn’t mean you have to accept a steady decline in physical function. We lose muscle, strength, and aerobic capacity as we get older, but much of that decline is modifiable. The goal isn’t necessarily to compete with LeBron James. It’s to be the strongest, healthiest, most capable version of yourself at every age. Longevity is not about having the most expensive recovery equipment. It’s about doing the right things consistently over decades. I am still running marathons and doing triathlons, and I have changed the way that I train and compete over time. But I’m still out there and plan to be for years to come!”
