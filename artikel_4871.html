The Harm of Trying to Change Your Sexual Orientation

Posted November 20, 2026 | Reviewed by Tyler Woods

When people hear “conversion therapy,” they may picture a teenager forced into treatment by parents or religious leaders. But efforts to change sexual orientation often begin privately: someone prays for their attractions to disappear, suppresses sexual thoughts, or avoids relationships in hopes of becoming heterosexual.

In March 2026, the Supreme Court ruled that a Colorado ban on conversion therapy may violate free speech rules. Proponents of the ban argued that people who want to change their sexual orientation should be allowed to try. This raises an important question, answered by recent research—what happens when a person sincerely wants this change?

A new study offers a troubling answer: self-initiated efforts to change sexual orientation were associated with greater depression, less social support, and greater disconnection from oneself—without evidence that they changed sexual orientation. Choosing to pursue change does not establish that the pursuit is safe or effective.

Psychologist G. Tyler Lefevor and colleagues followed current and former members of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints over two years. Their study included 315 participants with information about change efforts at both assessments. Unlike research that asks people to reconstruct experiences years later, this study followed participants forward in time. The researchers also registered their research plan in advance and assembled a team with differing perspectives on sexual orientation change efforts. This is an important way the researchers demonstrated commitment to objectivity and research ethics.

Nearly three-quarters of participants reported trying to change their same-gender sexuality at some point. Forty-three reported ongoing or recently ended efforts at the beginning of the study.

Those participants were struggling more than people who were no longer trying or had never tried. They reported more depressive symptoms, lower well-being, poorer self-rated health, and less support from friends and family. They were also less open about their sexuality and more alienated from their own experiences.

Two years later, the group initially pursuing change still reported more depression and less support from friends. However, the overall test combining health outcomes was not statistically significant, and the researchers found no significant difference in suicidality. The depression difference averaged about two points on a commonly used questionnaire, with both groups’ averages falling within the mild range.

These findings deserve attention. This was an observational study, and participants were already experiencing distress when it began. It cannot establish whether change efforts caused depression, whether distress encouraged people to pursue change, or whether both reflected other pressures. Most participants were White adults from one religious background, and relatively few were actively pursuing change.

Nevertheless, the findings challenge the reassurance that personally desired change efforts are harmless and should be allowed.

A person can sincerely choose something while carrying painful beliefs about what makes them acceptable. “I want to become straight” may coexist with “I am afraid I will lose my family,” “I cannot imagine belonging in my faith community as I am,” or “My attractions mean there is something wrong with me.”

This study did not establish that these change efforts were entirely free of outside influence. “Self-initiated” describes who undertakes the effort; it does not establish where the desire originated. Nor did it mean everyone worked alone: participants’ experiences could include personal practices, groups, or other assistance.

That distinction matters. An external demand can become an internal rule, enforced without anyone else in the room.

One possible cost is persistent self-surveillance. Every attraction or sexual thought becomes something to monitor. Every unwanted erotic desire or impulse becomes evidence of failure. When attractions persist, a person may conclude that they have not prayed enough, worked hard enough, or shown sufficient commitment.

Another possible cost is isolation. Avoiding people who evoke attraction—or communities that offer acceptance—may reduce opportunities for friendship, intimacy, and honest conversation. The study’s findings of lower support and greater self-alienation fit these concerns, although they do not prove these particular pathways caused the distress.

The promised benefit also failed to materialize in the study’s comparisons. Participants pursuing change showed no significantly different changes in same-gender or other-gender attraction, sexual aversion, or sexual behavior over two years. Nearly 63 percent of those initially classified as pursuing change were no longer doing so by follow-up, having given up trying to change their sexuality.

Sexuality can show natural variation over time. People can also change their behavior, relationships, or identity labels. None of those changes, by itself, demonstrates that an intervention can reliably turn same-gender attraction into heterosexuality.

Respecting personal autonomy remains essential. People deserve to make their own decisions about faith, relationships, sexual behavior, and the language they use for themselves. A therapist should not prescribe a particular identity or relationship.

But respecting someone’s choices also requires being honest about the evidence and helping them examine the costs of their goals. Someone seeking help can explore religious commitments, relationship decisions, grief, and fear without being promised that their attractions can be eliminated. Choosing celibacy or setting boundaries around behavior is a different goal from trying to change the attractions themselves. Sex therapist Jack Morin famously described that when people go to war with their own sexuality, they are likely to end up worse than they started.

The clinical task is to help people build a life they can inhabit with dignity, connection, and less self-rejection.

A sincere desire to become heterosexual does not guarantee that pursuing that goal will help. This research adds to concerns that the pursuit can accompany continuing distress while delivering no demonstrated change in orientation. People experiencing that conflict deserve support that takes both their values and their well-being seriously.

Source: Lefevor, G. T., Rosik, C. H., Beckstead, A. L., Mansfield, T. R., & Yarhouse, M. A. (2026). A preregistered, prospective, longitudinal examination of the impact of self-initiated sexual orientation change efforts on health and changes in sexuality. Archives of Sexual Behavior.
