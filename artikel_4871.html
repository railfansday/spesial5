Five Reasons Marsha Linehan Will Always Be a Legend

The field of clinical psychology lost a giant this week with the passing of Marsha Linehan, the psychologist who developed dialectical behavior therapy (DBT) and fundamentally changed how we understand and treat borderline personality disorder (BPD).

For decades, people with BPD were considered among the most difficult patients to treat. In fact, many clinicians believed that these patients were untreatable. Linehan proved them wrong by developing a better treatment. Since its publication in 1993, DBT has saved countless lives and inspired generations of clinicians and researchers.

As a clinical psychologist whose own career has been shaped by her work, I've been reflecting on what made Linehan such an extraordinary figure. Here are five reasons her legacy will endure.

When Marsha began her work, people experiencing chronic suicidality and severe emotional dysregulation were often viewed as too difficult or too risky to treat. Rather than accepting that these patients couldn't be helped, she set out to develop a treatment that could get the job done.

The result was dialectical behavior therapy, a structured treatment that combined cognitive-behavioral strategies with acceptance-based approaches drawn from Zen practice. DBT taught people practical skills to manage overwhelming emotions, navigate relationships, and build lives worth living.

Marsha was also a scientist who understood the importance of testing whether her approach actually worked. Through rigorous clinical trials, she demonstrated that even people experiencing severe and persistent suicidal behaviors could benefit from treatment.

In doing so, she challenged the longstanding assumption that BPD was untreatable and helped establish a new standard of care.

Patients with BPD were frequently described as manipulative, attention-seeking, or resistant to treatment.

Marsha challenged these labels by encouraging clinicians to understand the function of behavior. Actions that seemed manipulative or irrational often served a purpose, such as escaping overwhelming emotions or getting important needs met.

Rather than blaming patients for these behaviors, therapists could work to understand what was reinforcing them and teach more effective ways to achieve the same goals.

In 2011, Marsha publicly shared her own history of severe suffering, suicidality, and psychiatric hospitalization in a New York Times profile.

This revelation had a huge impact. Here was one of the world's most influential clinical psychologists disclosing that she had once experienced profound psychological distress herself.

Marsha later shared more of her story in her memoir, Building a Life Worth Living. She recalled a patient asking her, “Are you one of us?” For people experiencing profound psychological suffering, knowing that someone who had faced similar struggles could go on to build such an extraordinary life offered a powerful source of hope.

She was living proof that recovery was possible.

One of Marsha's most important contributions was her emphasis on dialectical thinking, the ability to recognize that two seemingly contradictory things can both be true.

You can accept yourself as you are and work to change.

You can validate someone's suffering and hold them accountable for their behavior.

You can do your best and need to do better.

This was a major shift in how therapists approached treatment. Rather than choosing between validating someone's experience and encouraging them to change, Marsha showed us how to do both. This allowed therapists to approach even the most challenging behaviors with compassion while still helping clients take responsibility for building better lives.

Marsha's influence extends well beyond the people who received DBT. She trained and inspired generations of clinicians and researchers who have continued building on her work.

DBT was the first treatment I learned as a graduate student. Today, I supervise DBT in that same clinic, and Marsha's work continues to shape my own work, BPD Compass.

I first met Marsha when she stopped by my poster at a conference. I was a graduate student presenting BPD research, and I completely fan-girled. It was really cool that she took time to look through the BPD-themed posters and offer encouragement.

Years later, I interviewed with Marsha for a postdoctoral position. Although my career ultimately took me in a different direction, she still agreed to co-mentor my first National Institute of Mental Health grant.

Marsha generously gave her time and encouragement—multiply the experience by the generations of clinicians and researchers she mentored, and you begin to appreciate the reach of her influence.

Marsha Linehan fundamentally changed what clinicians believed was possible for people with BPD by giving them tools that actually worked for these clients.

Despite these advances, too many people with BPD still struggle to access effective treatment. Making effective BPD treatment available to more people is one way we can honor her legacy.

Marsha's influence will live on through the people whose lives she saved and the generations of clinicians and researchers she inspired.

Marsha's influence on my work continues through BPD Compass, a treatment my colleagues and I developed to help make evidence-based care for BPD more accessible.
