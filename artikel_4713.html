When Someone You Love Dies, Who Do You Become?

When someone we love dies, we can feel like we’ve lost the person we share our good news with, the person who knows our true selves, or the person who remembers earlier versions of ourselves and our relationships. We may wonder what our future will be without them.

We don’t just grieve the loss of the person; we grieve the future we won’t be spending with them. Underneath these feelings is another concern, though, and one that may be harder to name: We may wonder who we are without that person.

Marvel’s new series, VisionQuest, gives this question an unusual form. Its central character is a synthezoid trying to understand his memories, his relationships, and himself. Beneath the vibranium is a recognizable human experience: the struggle to make sense of a life that no longer feels like our own. (Note: There are spoilers ahead.)

In VisionQuest, a rebuilt Vision carries memories of his relationship with Wanda Maximoff, but he consistently clarifies that he is not the old Vision, the “real” Vision. This rebuilt Vision’s search for meaning becomes a search for identity. Given the complexities of the technology that created and rebuilt Vision, viewers may be likely to believe that this version is not the “real” Vision. Read through the lens of grief, however, there is another possibility.

When we grieve, we may question how the life we remember with our loved one feels so far away. We may remember our old selves while struggling to reconnect with how we felt then. A video may show us laughing with the person we loved and have since lost. We remember the occasion, the conversation, perhaps even what happened just after the video was recorded, but we may struggle to recognize the person we were then. That person lived in a world where a particular future still seemed possible.

Vision’s experience offers viewers a way to understand this experience. Having access to the past does not automatically tell us how to live in the present.

In the finale of VisionQuest, Ultron states that “grief isn’t love, it’s fear.” Vision confirms that he believes the same, but adds that it’s “fear that without them, you’re no one. Inconsequential. An untethered thing that can all too easily float away and disappear. Alone.” This exchange asks Ultron, Vision, and viewers to confront the fear that losing someone we love also means losing part of who we are.

As a family scientist, I am drawn to the relational question at the center of this story. Our identities develop within relationships. We become someone’s partner, parent, child, sibling, or closest friend. Over time, those relationships help organize our routines, responsibilities, and expectations. When someone dies, we may lose a person who knew how to comfort us, challenge us, or make us laugh. We may also lose everyday opportunities to be a particular version of ourselves. Grief can unsettle our identities. We may ask who we are when we no longer care for our parents each afternoon, what it means to be a spouse when our partners are gone, or where we put the affection that once had a destination.

Research on meaning reconstruction examines how people make sense of loss and rebuild their identities afterward. One study of bereaved young adults found that positive identity change was associated with fewer grief complications. This does not mean people must find a benefit in someone’s death. It suggests that understanding who we are becoming can matter alongside understanding what we have lost.

A story about an android or synthezoid might tempt us to imagine grief as a problem that could be fixed, if only we had enough information or faster processing speeds. If we could understand everything, perhaps we could stop hurting, but understanding a death and adapting to its consequences are different tasks.

Margaret Stroebe and Henk Schut’s dual process model describes movement between attending to loss and attending to the demands of a changed life. We might spend an evening immersed in memories, then spend the next morning learning to manage a responsibility our loved one once handled. Taking a break from grief is part of the model, too.

That perspective makes room for experiences that can otherwise feel contradictory. We can miss someone and enjoy dinner with friends. We can feel capable one day and overwhelmed the next. For viewers, Vision’s search offers a useful perspective on what it means to participate in life, like creating a relationship with his son, while carrying an unfinished relationship with the past.

We may find ways to carry that relationship into our present lives through small, everyday actions. We repeat a loved one’s joke or favorite phrase. We teach a child something our loved one taught us. We make a decision informed by their kindness—or decide to live differently from them. We may never become the person we were before the loss, but the relationships that helped shape us can remain part of who we become.

Marvel says: Grief can be frightening because losing someone we love can leave us questioning who we are without them. Vision gives voice to the fear that we might become untethered without the relationships that helped define us.

Psychology says: Fear can be part of grief. Losing someone can unsettle our identity, but adapting to the new identity does not require erasing the relationship. We can move between mourning what we have lost and building a changed life. We can carry parts of that relationship into who we become.

The Verdict: For a story about synthezoids, Marvel’s portrayal of grief feels deeply human. I’m still reflecting on this episode and series, and on how my own experiences with grief have shaped who I am.
