“I Was Scared There’d Be More Eye Rolls Than Optimism”: Gaten Matarazzo on Making the West End His First Stop After ‘Stranger Things’

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

He's swapped the Upside Down for late '80s New York in Luke Sheppard's refreshingly sparky 'Rent' revival and is ready to talk about child fame, platforming the HIV/AIDS crisis and Lin-Manuel Miranda's 'Octet': "I love weird shit."

Before he was Dustin, Stranger Things‘ adorably dorky, cap-wearing kid whose knack for science would help defeat interdimensional villains Vecna and the Mind Flayer, Gaten Matarazzo was a real theater kid.

Aged nine, his first-ever professional credit was in the Broadway production of Priscilla, Queen of the Desert, and his second sizeable stage spell came just two years later, when Matarazzo joined the U.S.’ 25th Anniversary Tour of Les Misérables as neglected street child Gavroche. He would reprise the role in 2014’s Tony-nominated revival in New York (when the Connecticut-born actor was still only 12 years old). Matarazzo’s household-name status wouldn’t come via these live performances, but through Netflix’s most successful original series of all time. At 24, Matarazzo is both hyper-aware of this fact and refreshingly liberated by it: Dustin, Stranger Things and its franchise-spawning, billion-streams success have allowed this now-very-adult actor — and astonishingly brilliant singer — to return to the art form he grew up entrenched in.

“It’s really lovely to have [Stranger Things] in our back pockets as we go forward in whatever we choose to do,” says Matarazzo over Zoom about his co-stars Millie Bobby Brown, Finn Wolfhard, Caleb McLaughlin, Noah Schnapp and Sadie Sink. “I’ve gravitated towards theater for now, just because it’s a reminder of my childhood, and I love it very, very much.”

It’s fitting then that Matarazzo’s West End debut — and first major role since the final outing of the Duffer brothers’ Netflix tentpole — is in a show so close to his heart. Director Luke Sheppard (Paddington: The Musical) has seemingly bottled the energy of a late 20th-century New York City and unleashed it on the Tom Stoppard Theatre stage through an invigorating young cast. Jonathan Larson’s musical chronicles a year in the life of a group of impoverished 20-something artists and activists in the East Village. Their creative dreams are kept alive through song and a sense of punk enthusiasm as drug addiction, poverty, gentrification, and the HIV/AIDS epidemic loom large. Larson’s creation itself comes with built-in tragedy — the prodigious composer, lyricist and playwright died at 35 on the morning of Rent‘s first preview performance.

Below, Matarazzo dives into his masterful portrayal of aspiring filmmaker and story narrator Mark Cohen in this epic Rent rebirth. He tells The Hollywood Reporter about championing the “essential” stories of those with HIV/AIDS in late ’80s New York, why he believes he’s come out of the Stranger Things bedlam in one piece and what to expect from Lin-Manuel Miranda’s hotly anticipated Octet movie with Rachel Zegler, Amanda Seyfried, Tramell Tillman, Jonathan Groff and Sheryl Lee Ralph: “The things I want to do are probably things that I read and say, ‘I’ve never read anything like this before,’ and that’s what Octet was.”

How did this all come together? Did the production approach you for Mark?

Yeah, they did. Luke specifically, which is very nice. It was very unexpected. […] I remember I was in the shower, and I got a notification as I was stepping out, and I wasn’t even dressed by the time I was on the phone and saying, “Yes, yes, yes!” because all I saw [was] the subject title: “Mark West End” and I didn’t know what it meant. I saw Luke’s name and I didn’t really know what the implications were, how long it would be, if it was truly a West End run, or if it was just going to be a London concert. But I knew that an opportunity to jump into the show was one that you don’t pass up, especially when it’s a director like Luke. So it was just a no-brainer. After I had given an immediate yes to being a part of it, then [came] the implication of moving away from home for an extended period of time, which I’ve done before, but never for this long and never by myself. […] Thankfully, I was working on some other stuff in the city leading up to it, so I actually got to meet some of the creative team for Rent.

You have a long-running relationship with Broadway. What were your preconceptions about the West End? Did you always want to perform here at some point?

Absolutely. London is the birthplace of what modern theater looks like, and if you want to have a real grasp of, I think, the epitome of theater culture — and if you have the opportunity to — you should see shows in London. It’s easier said than done. I was always here for press for Stranger Things, which was very nice, but that’s really the only time I was ever able to come here. And on press runs and junkets, you rarely have any time to really enjoy the city. I knew how intimidating a process it was going to look like. I know how seriously it’s taken. I know how seriously audiences take it in New York and in London, and there is a standard that is expected to be met, which is warranted, so there is a pressure that comes along with that, especially coming from overseas. […] There’s always going to be a curiosity about somebody who isn’t necessarily known for having a bulk of work in theater jumping into something as intense as this. I was scared that there were going to be more eye rolls than optimism, and I was met with nothing but love, which was really, really wonderful.

The energy in the room was just lovely. There were no eye rolls — only enthusiasm.

And what was your relationship like with this show? Do you remember seeing Rent for the first time?

I remember being introduced to the show at a time when seeing it in its best form was hard to do. Because I was too young to see it when it was running on Broadway, but by the time I was old enough, not enough time had passed for a big professional production to really hit New York again. A lot of time, after shows close, they need time to breathe. They need some time to simmer with new audiences so you can find new opportunities. You can gauge culturally what is resonating about a piece. […] But something that’s so special about Rent is that it was the modern musical. It was one of the first defining young, pop-rock shows that launched a new wave of theater kids. Rent was essential in bringing in a new wave of people who were going to love this form of art. And I remember when I first started getting into it, the movie had already been made. The show was already very famous. It had already closed on Broadway, so really, my introduction was scouring the internet to try to find as much information about it and as much footage as I could. And thankfully, they had a really great proshot [a high quality-recording] of the closing company, and it was stunning. […] That’s what led me to really feel an intense, real desire to one day get to do it at some point.

I recall reading that you have an uncle who was a closeted gay man in New York in the ’80s and ’90s, when the HIV/AIDS crisis was rife. We’re past that stigma now, but this is a show that manages to really capture the sentiment around that awful time.

Yeah. It’s essential that stories about it and stories about the people who endured it, people we lost, people we still have, [are told]. It’s essential that it’s a continuation because the people that suffered through it and endured through it and thrived through it are still very much here, very much contributing to every walk of life, especially art and the theater. And that’s why this show just continues to work — because people don’t forget. As far as a musical goes, 30 years is a long time. But as far as a pandemic goes, 30 years is not a long time. […] They don’t cover it in American public schools as much as they should. I think they should. There’s a different lens that you learn about when you’re growing up in a time when it is significantly more treatable than it was.

In places where modern medicine is accessible, an HIV diagnosis is always going to be scary, but there are so many options. There are so many ways to prevent. There are so many ways to protect. It’s incredible that we’ve gotten to this point, but to hear it from people who were genuinely just terrified at not knowing how one gets it, how one spreads it, and what the timeline looks like for the rest of their life… Because there are people who would receive a diagnosis, and of course they could live 30, 40 years and never even experience symptoms. And then there were people who were dead in six months, and there was really no way to tell. I remember hearing about that and having this pit of dread. And even at nine or 10 years old, learning about that, that’s always scary, no matter what. But then you look around at people you respect and admire, and love very, very much, who can tell you personal stories about their own, not just watching their loved ones go through it, but then personally experiencing that. That is something that, even at that age, I didn’t take for granted.

And what’s amazing about Rent is that it’s framed by such radical optimism and a real punk enthusiasm. And it still resonates with audiences, which I’m sure you see every night.

Right? It’s a show that’s so full of love and so full of optimism, and it doesn’t talk down. It’s so beautiful to watch because it was a crisis and is a crisis, so there is always going to be a very large, very sad group of people who insist on making shame the central point of the conversation around that crisis. For this show to release at a time when that was still very present, where mass media wouldn’t cover it. […] Hindsight’s 2020. We can look back now and say, “Insane that it ever happened.” […] But to have the characters suffering through it [and] be some of the most vibrant, loving, enthusiastic, incredible people you could ever… Everybody has their issues. But after you watch the show and see the family that they create, all you want to do is be a part of it.

You’ve had a little space from Stranger Things and Dustin now. When you reflect on it all, what feelings come up for you?

I don’t feel very far removed from it in the slightest. I know it’s been some time. [… But] there’s always a new wave of people being like, “Oh, there’s that gem of a show that people talked about.” So I’m seeing friends of mine and people around my age, or even a little younger, even older, start this new wave of experiencing it for the first time, and that’s an incredible reminder because that’s something I had forgotten that was so special — seeing people be consistently surprised by the show in those early years, and that was before a standard was set, before an expectation had been made after the second or third season, which there always is going to be when a show does well.

It’s great to hear you talk positively about it all. We’ve seen how difficult child fame can be. It sounds like you’ve come out relatively unscathed.

Thanks. Kids can’t do this personally, but it’s a matter of ensuring that you are surrounded by people who have your best interests at heart, which I do, and others don’t. And a lot of times, that’s luck of the draw, and I was very lucky. My parents always made sure that I was doing it for the right reasons. My parents would always not just tell me but show me that they were invested in that process for the right reasons. Because they knew I loved it. And we were lucky with the team on the show specifically. There was a lot of prioritization of our safety and security and our education, and ensuring that we were enjoying ourselves throughout that process. I can only speak for myself, but I would assume that I can speak for [others]. Collectively, we had a really great experience, and we loved the show, and it was the best learning experience in the world that one could ask for. It’s like the best film school you could ever ask for.

To work on a production in a show as intense as that was, you got to see every single day how something that massive is made. You gain a literacy in film and TV that you wouldn’t really get to learn otherwise, and we didn’t even know we were learning it. We didn’t even know that it was cementing itself into the backs of our brains. So now that we enter our early 20s, when most people are starting their careers in this world and just getting a gauge, there’s a weird little kind of mentality. I’m like, “Ah, back in the day…”

(Laughs.) Right! Jaded and sad. No, it’s really lovely to have that in our back pockets as we go forward in whatever we choose to do. I’ve gravitated towards theater for now, just because it’s a reminder of my childhood, and I love it very, very much. But I very much expect to continue working in film in different capacities. I see all of my friends doing incredible work in such different, wonderful ways, and none of us would really be able to do that without the opportunities we were given from the show.

I read that some people have been coming to Rent in Stranger Things costume and you’ve had to set a boundary?

Oh, goodness gracious! That was more of a thing in passing that I think was blown out of proportion a little bit. It was said in a more sarcastic, joking manner. But I’m okay with the way the sentiment was perceived because what was highlighted at the end was the emphasis on 20 other people being up on the stage that I’m on. […] That was more something that had happened once or twice, maybe in some other shows that I had done, and it’s more of a funny aside rather than this issue that keeps becoming apparent.

I understand. There’s an entire ensemble alongside you. So people have to be considerate of that if they’re showing up just for you.

The gang that they have assembled is essential to why the show works. I remove myself from that completely. The people that I watch every day do the show are absolutely astounding, and they bring new life [and] spontaneity. They take the history of what the show means and transform it into something really, really fresh and beautiful. [We] were always going to be on the backs of the original company that did this show, but the minute we all entered that room, we understood that there was going to be a new dynamic. We would have to develop a history that we, as individuals, all had with each other, and it was so surprisingly easy because of how engaged and full of life and excitement this whole company is. That’s before even mentioning that I’ve never heard this show sound the way it sounds when I’m listening to that group singing.

How have you found a West End audience compared to a Broadway one?

The thing that’s different about this is [that] Rent will always feel like a Broadway show. It feels like big, booming Broadway, and I think that transfers over here, and audiences come in with that energy. So it feels like a very New York audience when we’re doing the show. I was warned that you have to earn [a wild reaction] more here. But there’s this energy with this show being here and being created here. I think that almost feels like there’s this brewing excitement about it when we go in.

It feels like one of the only times when it’s really cemented itself as a new type of show, a new type of Rent. I think we’re very early in the run, but succeeding pretty substantially here… This feels like one of the ones where it’s like, “Oh shit, they tried something, and that thing that they’re trying is working.”

It’s clicking. It must be so satisfying to know that it’s landed. Because those first few weeks are nervy, right?

In terms of what’s next… You have Lin-Manuel Miranda’s Octet coming up. Does Rent mark a new chapter for you?

I think so. I don’t know. It would be a weird thing to label. I just want to take things in [my] stride and go with the flow of what presents itself over time. I don’t really want to force anything. I’m doing a lot of theater right now because those opportunities have presented themselves, and it [was] a no-brainer that I wanted to continue doing it. But it’s not like I’ll finish up this run and be like, “Now I’m doing movies, so don’t fucking ask me to do [anything else].” You know what I mean? Like, whatever comes up that I find to be an opportunity for growth and learning… I really only want to learn. I rarely want to do things I’ve done before, and that’s why I decided to do this because it’s a new experience in a new city, and a workload that I hadn’t really tackled that intensely yet.

I knew it was going to be a physical challenge, and that’s what I was going to learn from this process. But as far as film and TV goes, I heard this once, and I can’t attribute this thought to myself because Rob Pattinson said it once in an interview. (Laughs.) He really considers whether he can read a script and say, “If I say no, I have to be okay with never doing the script again.” Because there’s a lot of things that come through. and it’s like, I’ve seen this before. It’s great. It’s good. I just saw this yesterday, or I’ve done this before, [or] that’ll probably be very good, and there’s going to be good people making it, but I don’t think I’ll learn much from that experience. The things I want to do are probably things that I read and say, “I’ve never read anything like this before,” and that’s what Octet was.

It was a blending of worlds that I love so much. It’s a beautiful score. Dave Malloy is an incredible writer. Lin’s an incredible director. The cast that was involved — working with each individual in that group has been a goal and dream of mine for a long time. So for them to all be in the room at the same time… And as far as a movie goes, I love this. I mean, it’s so weird, and I’m obsessed with it. I love weird shit. I like filming things that are weird, and I feel like permission is granted if you’re doing something that is very, very absurd, which, at times, this movie is. It grants you personal permission to be as absurd as you can, and that’s when I feel most comfortable jumping out of my comfort zone — when everybody else is also doing it.
