Before you plan an early exit from work, read two or three of the books behind the financial independence movement, one for the investing math, one for how you think about money, and one on the path you’d take. Borrow them from the library first, and favor authors who invest in low-cost funds over ones selling a shortcut.

A forum thread on FIRE, short for financial independence, retire early, gives you one stranger’s spreadsheet. A book gives you a whole plan and the reasoning behind it, so you can see where it fits your life and where it doesn’t.

You don’t need all eight. A book on the math shows how much saving and investing it takes to leave work early, one on the mindset helps you keep at it for years, and one on a single path shows what those years look like. One of each gives you a plan to test instead of a pile of tips.

Judge any FIRE book you find later by how its author invests. A plan built on low-cost index funds, which own every stock in a market index, has nothing extra to sell you. One that only works with the author’s course, coaching, or trading system is selling a shortcut.

The eight below start with the math and the mindset, move on to five different paths, and end with a counterpoint on spending.

For more on investing itself, a few investing books for beginners go deeper on index funds, investor behavior, and stock picking.

Before you buy any of them, check what your library card covers, since thousands of libraries lend e-books and audiobooks free through apps such as Libby and hoopla. Then test whichever plan wins you over against your own numbers, starting with your savings rate, before you change anything at work.
