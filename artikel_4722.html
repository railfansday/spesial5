What Poison Ivy Teaches Us About Environmental Grief

Poison Ivy may be the only Batman villain whose cause many readers quietly agree with.

In her origin story from Batman comics, Dr. Pamela Isley (Poison Ivy) was once a passionate scientist. However, she was injected with toxins that merged her with plant life. She can control vegetation and feel the pain of plants directly.1 Driven by empathic distress, she fights environmental abuse and political negligence. Archetypally, she embodies both the grief and the fury of a wounded Earth.

Her grief reflects emotions many of us share; the research literature captures how people commonly feel sadness, fear, anger, helplessness, and profound grief in response to ecological loss.2 This is closely tied to the experience of solastalgia, or the anguish of watching a cherished home environment deteriorate until it no longer offers the solace it once did.

Environmental grief and climate-related trauma are natural and instinctive responses to climate breakdown.3 They arise from tangible losses of ecosystems, wildlife, homes, jobs, and communities. They also arise from losses that are harder to see, such as diminished trust, a loss of faith in the future, and a deeper psycho-spiritual disconnection from Earth itself.

Poison Ivy is also a figure of political grief. She is driven by moral outrage at governance that permits deforestation, pollution, and the exploitation of the natural world. Environmental grief often intersects with political grief, especially as climate change and environmental breakdown places a far greater burden on disadvantaged communities and developing countries.4 Subsequently, growing numbers of people are questioning values that give personhood to corporations but not to the natural world.

Poison Ivy accurately shows how these losses can be felt on an intensely personal level, one that penetrates us to the core of our being.

Grief, hopelessness, and despair can push us into survival states marked by defensiveness, psychological inflexibility, and absolutist thinking.5 Over time, these experiences can lead people toward extremist positions, social alienation, and political polarization.6 Poison Ivy’s impulsive, destructive choices do not create meaningful collective change. Her over-identification with plants causes her to lose touch with her own humanity.

In troubling times, it can be tempting to imagine that we need a figure like Poison Ivy. This supervillain fantasy will not solve the ecological crisis, though, or meaningfully comfort our grieving hearts.

The world has already experienced enough destructive authoritarianism that violates all traditional cultural wisdom. Violence will only make problems worse, and coercion entrenches polarization and resistance.6 Tragically, supervillains embody the same cycles of harm they claim to oppose. Without an ethical framework, supervillains impose rigid ideologies that oppose cooperation, respect, dignity, and mutual understanding.

The environmental crisis and the ongoing tear in our political systems evoke a great deal of fear, which is exactly why we need superheroes. The Justice League from DC Comics is a diverse group of superheroes who unite against threats too large for any one of them to handle. Despite differing ideologies, they preserve a sense of common humanity.

Adopting a stance of common humanity does not require compromising deeply held values or excusing abuses of power.6 Instead, it means focusing on our intention to connect rather than our need to defend. Many Indigenous worldviews express this through four guiding values: relationship, responsibility, reciprocity, and redistribution.7

Over 40 years ago, Roger Walsh urged us to face collective existential threats from a place of psychological self-awareness rather than fear or shadow.8 External change is most effective when it is fuelled by inner transformation and by tending to our own unresolved losses. Furthermore, crises benefit from level-headed individuals who are respectful, strive to minimize harm, utilize ethical frameworks, and maintain unwavering integrity. In other words, people who can embody and psychologically integrate the qualities of a superhero are what the world needs.

When we can metabolize our widespread environmental and political grief, we create inner space and presence. That space awakens us to life, love, and interdependence, and to the ability to re-imagine the future. No one is also meant to face this grief alone. Our collective moment of reckoning has arrived, and we need all the superheroes we can muster.

This piece draws on my book chapter, “From Poison Ivy to the Justice League: A Psychological Approach to Environmental and Political Grief,” in Superhero Grief and the Multiverse of Loss: Considerations and Case Studies, edited by Jill Harrington and Darcy Harris (Routledge, 2026). This new collection uses superheroes and the multiverse to explore how non-death loss impacts our psychology.9
