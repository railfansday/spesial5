The Safest Way to Handle a Kitchen Grease Fire

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

A grease fire is one kitchen emergency in which instinct can work against you. Here's how to handle one safely.

One of the most valuable things I learned in culinary school was how to work with fire without losing control of it. So much of cooking depends on knowing how far we can push the heat—getting a pan hot enough to sear steak or bringing oil just to the right temperature for a batch of fried chicken. I’ve yet to lose the little thrill of flambéing a steak au poivre or a rum-laced bananas Foster, but after years in kitchens, I have a healthy respect for high heat.

That respect is particularly warranted around the holidays, when every burner on the stove seems to be occupied, and our attention is divided between the cooking and the guests milling about in the kitchen. According to the National Fire Protection Association (NFPA), Thanksgiving, Christmas Day and Christmas Eve are the three peak days of the year for home cooking fires.

What makes a grease fire especially dangerous is that many of the things we’d instinctively do to put out a fire can actually cause it to spread. Fortunately, handling a grease fire safely is a fairly simple matter of knowing what to reach for and what to leave alone.

Whether you’re sauteing vegetables in a little olive oil or frying chicken in a Dutch oven, it’s important to recognize when your cooking fat is getting too hot. Different oils have different smoke points (the temperatures at which they begin to smoke) but if an oil continues to heat well beyond that point, it can eventually ignite without ever coming into direct contact with a flame. Overheating isn’t the only culprit, though. Oil that splashes onto a hot burner or an open flame can also catch fire, as can grease that has accumulated around the burners.

I find a thermometer invaluable when I’m deep-frying, but it’s important to keep an eye on the pan even when sauteing. It’s frighteningly easy to get distracted chopping the next ingredient or chatting with your dinner guests. If you notice smoke rising from your pan, turn off the burner immediately.

Should the oil catch fire, leave the pan where it is. As instinctive as it may be to move it to the sink, carrying burning oil through the kitchen is a serious burn hazard, and risks spreading the fire.

The most important thing to remember about a grease fire is that water will only make it worse. Most of us are familiar with the rather unpleasant sensation of hot oil spattering onto our hands when moisture finds its way into a sizzling pan. The science behind that reaction is the same here: Water sinks and turns rapidly to steam, agitating the oil. When the oil is burning, that sudden expansion can send flames and droplets of burning oil well beyond the stovetop.

A fire extinguisher may seem like the obvious thing to reach for, but not all extinguishers are suited for grease fires. Class K extinguishers are designed specifically for fires involving cooking oils and fats, and they can be useful if you have one and know how to use it safely. For a small grease fire contained within a pan, though, there’s a simpler (and considerably less expensive) piece of kitchen equipment that can do the job: a metal lid.
