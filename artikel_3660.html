How the ‘Glee’ and ‘Girls’ Revivals Can Avoid Mistakes Made by Other TV Reboots

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

The blue-tinged afterglow of the 2026 midterm elections, which saw Democrats seize control of both the House and Senate, unleashed a burst of online millennial optimism. Some have credited a pair of newly revived Obama-era coming of age TV shows that often got political themselves, Ryan Murphy’s Glee and Lena Dunham’s Girls, with being a precursor to the Blue Wave.

But for all of the internet’s excitement for new episodes of Glee and Girls , no one said aging beloved characters (who is Hannah Horvath at 40?), or addressing the current sociopolitical moment (will Sue Sylvester in a MAGA hat be inevitable?!) would come without risk. How do the Girls and Glee reboots meet nostalgic expectations while delivering something new and prescient about where the world is now? Perhaps Dunham and Murphy can draw lessons about aging gracefully in adaptation from revivals that have aired before them.

Ahead, a guide to do’s and don’ts for the return of generational TV, from the second coming of Sex and the City to Roseanne’s rocky renewal.

Riding a high from his Emmy-nominated shows including Ted Lasso and Shrinking (Apple TV), plus Rooster (HBO), series creator Bill Lawrence relaunched Scrubs, now airing its second season on ABC, into a post-Abbott Elementary landscape—where network TV craves workplace comedies for its airwaves. It seems that the folks behind Glee and Girls, respectively, have found the right moment to return to TV. Murphy has spoken about Glee’s recent streaming resurgence for younger audiences, and Dunham is coming off a headline-making book tour for her bestselling memoir Famesick, written about her time making Girls 1.0.

Rather than write themselves out of the show’s controversial final season, in which most of the show’s central cast, including Zach Braff, Donald Faison, Sarah Chalke, were sidelined in favor of an entirely new group of characters, the creative team opted to retcon the entire ninth season and start fresh for the reboot. That means Glee can freely pretend its own flashforward—in which Rachel Berry is a surrogate mother for fellow Glee Club pals Kurt and Blaine could be abandoned. And maybe Shoshanna never decided she was too good for the rest of the Girls and they can all be dysfunctional friends again…right?

A political sketch titled “Oh Honey,” which reunited original cast members Debra Messing, Eric McCormack, Sean Hayes, and Megan Mullally in support of Hillary Clinton ahead of the 2016 presidential election, may not have prevented a Donald Trump presidency. But it did garner millions of views online, earning the attention of top NBC brass who hoped to capitalize on the nostalgia by relaunching Will & Grace in 2017. While the Glee Club has yet to release their own teaser, Dunham announced the Girls revival via four-way video call with cast members Allison Williams, Jemima Kirke, and Zosia Mamet that promptly sent the internet into a tailspin.

While the Will & Grace revival lasted for three seasons largely because of its ability to engage with the day’s political issues, particularly as they related to the LGBTQ+ community, some felt the show became too steeped in Trumpland. In one episode, a Trump-supporting Karen even gets staunchly liberal Grace a job decorating his Oval Office. Girls and Glee will have to strike a similarly delicate balance with their own returns—incorporating the current political landscape in ways that feel natural to the narrative, as much as one can when often tone-deaf show choir kids and Brooklyn hipsters are involved.

Just as And Just Like That made a splash by murdering Mr. Big in the first episode via Peloton-induced heart attack, Glee and Girls should aim for maximum impact upon arrival. Maybe Adam Driver’s character meets his end while going method for a role and learning a dangerous new skill. As for Glee, perhaps the character played by Grammy-winning Finneas O’Connell (brother and music producer of Billie Eilish), could go up in a blaze of glory. Despite getting his start with a four-episode arc in the show’s final season, in 2020 O’Connell claimed that he’d never seen Glee, including the episodes he was in.

Taking a show outside of its comfort zone in the right ways raises the stakes—and highlights core tensions from the original series (see also: Miranda shown riding the subway for the first time). But some viewers felt And Just Like That… showrunner Michael Patrick King’s inclusion of diverse voices into the narrative veered too far outside of the shoebox with Che Diaz, the divisive podcast-cohost–slash-comedian–slash–veterinarian-office-worker that took the brunt of audience ire over freshly sober Miranda’s decision to divorce her husband Steve. New characters can become de facto villains for a series revival, particularly if they interfere with cherished dynamics from the show’s first run.

The return of Kelsey Grammer’s sitcom hero Frasier Crane was undercut by the absence of breakout supporting characters Niles and Daphne, as actors David Hyde Pierce and Jane Leeves did not reprise their roles for the reboot. Luckily, the core four of Girls have already signed on for their five-episode revival, and several Glee alums are in talks for its reboot, including series lead Lea Michele.

In addition to some of the creative challenges facing Frasier 2.0, the former NBC sitcom was welcomed back on Paramount+, a lesser-known streaming platform in comparison to Netflix or Peacock. At least it wasn’t Helen Hunt and Paul Reiser’s Mad About You, another ’90s NBC comedy that got its grand return on…Spectrum (?!) in 2019. Girls will air on HBO’s streaming partner, Max, and Glee is in the works via Disney+ and Hulu—barring any major industry mergers.

The Roseanne reboot earned new resonance in the zeitgeist—and major ratings on ABC—for its authentic depiction of a working-class Middle America family under Trump’s first presidency. Like the original 1988 series, the revival focused on the economic and social realities of surviving as a lower-income family in America. And like Will & Grace before it, Roseanne organically, even comically, depicted familial political divides by portraying Roseanne Conner as a Trump voter and her sister Jackie as a Hillary Clinton supporter.

After series lead Roseanne Barr tweeted a racist slur about Valerie Jarrett, a senior aide to President Barack Obama, ABC Entertainment president Channing Dungey terminated the series on May 29, 2018, following its renewal for an 11th season on the network. Then, the following month, ABC announced it was ordering 10 episodes of a spin-off about the family titled The Conners, which regenerated by killing off the character of Roseanne, ending just last year after seven seasons on the air. If anything, the show’s unlikely staying power proves that if a show sticks to its DNA, it can outlast even the messiest of scandals.

The four-episode return of Lauren Graham and Alexis Bledel’s mother-daughter dramedy was rightfully docked for its extended, gratuitous Stars Hollow musical involving Tony Award-winning performers (and ex-spouses) Sutton Foster and Christian Borle. But a bit of superfluous song-and-dance naturally feels right at home for Glee, and even Girls—an encore version of Marnie singing Kanye West’s “Stronger” would reach cringeworthy new depths.

The pregnancy revelation that ends Gilmore Girls: A Year in the Life should have kicked the revival off, not concluded its story. Instead, A Year in the Life emphasizes just how little Rory and Lorelei have grown in the years since Gilmore Girls ended, only to leave the revival’s most compelling leap forward for its final moments—and totally unresolved. After a decade, the bait-and-switch still burns, which speaks to the divisive, and enduring, nature of TV returns themselves. As for the next chapters of Glee and Girls, may the writers’ rooms hear our post-election pleas.
