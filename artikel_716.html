Scientists discover a hidden 100-million-year-old volcanic system

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Scientists have uncovered evidence of a previously unknown volcanic system that formed roughly 100 million years ago in what is now northwestern Iran. The discovery reveals a missing chapter in the region's geological history and offers new clues about the powerful tectonic forces that shaped ancient oceans and continents.

Mountain ranges contain valuable records of Earth's distant past, preserving traces of vanished oceans, shifting continents, and the geological processes that transformed the planet over millions of years. However, these records are often difficult to interpret because later tectonic activity, erosion, and layers of buried sediment can conceal evidence of much older events.

An international team led by Istanbul Technical University (ITU) has now identified remnants of an ancient continental magmatic arc that had previously gone unrecognized. Named the Azerbaijan Continental Magmatic Arc, the system formed during the Late Cretaceous period in a region that is now part of the Arabia-Eurasia collision zone.

The research, published in Geoscience Frontiers, indicates that the volcanic system was active approximately 101 to 97 million years ago, when the ancient Neo-Tethys Ocean was undergoing major geological changes. Over time, tectonic deformation, erosion, burial beneath sedimentary basins, and a younger episode of volcanic activity during the Eocene obscured much of the evidence of its existence.

Ancient Rocks Reveal a Lost Volcanic System

To reconstruct this overlooked chapter of Earth's history, the researchers examined volcanic and plutonic rocks collected from multiple locations across northwestern Iran. Much of their investigation focused on formations along the Siah Cheshmeh-Khoy-Misho-Tabriz Fault.

The team used several advanced techniques to determine when the rocks formed and where their original materials came from. These included zircon U-Pb geochronology to establish their ages, Hf isotope analyses to investigate their sources, and whole-rock geochemistry to examine their chemical composition.

Together, the results revealed signatures consistent with continental arc magmatism, a geological process that occurs when one tectonic plate sinks beneath another, generating magma that can rise through the overlying continental crust.

Dr. Ali Mohammadi, the study's corresponding author at Istanbul Technical University, explained: "This study shows that the magmatic history of northwestern Iran is more complex than previously recognized. By integrating geochronological, isotopic, and geochemical evidence, we identified a Late Cretaceous continental arc that was subsequently obscured by later tectonic processes."

The international research effort also involved scientists from the Institute of Tibetan Plateau Research and Xinjiang Institute of Ecology and Geography, Chinese Academy of Sciences; ETH Zurich, Switzerland; Karadeniz Technical University, Türkiye; and Damghan University, Iran.

A Missing Piece of Iran's Geological History

Previous reconstructions of Iran's ancient magmatic activity have largely centered on two major geological features: the Sananda-Sirjan Magmatic Arc and the Urumieh-Dokhtar Magmatic Arc.

The newly identified Azerbaijan Continental Magmatic Arc adds another important piece to that history. Its discovery suggests that northwestern Iran experienced a separate episode of continental arc activity during the Late Cretaceous, expanding scientists' understanding of how the region developed.

The findings also point to a potentially important role for the Siah Cheshmeh-Khoy-Misho-Tabriz Fault. Researchers suggest that this fault may mark a major tectonic boundary connected to changes in the ancient Neo-Tethys Ocean system.

Those changes may have included slab rollback, a process in which a descending tectonic plate retreats as it sinks into Earth's interior, as well as the migration of volcanic arcs to new locations. Such movements could help explain why this particular episode of magma formation lasted for a relatively short period.

New Clues About the Disappearance of an Ancient Ocean

Beyond Iran, the discovery provides a broader lesson about how Earth's geological history can become hidden over immense stretches of time.

Ancient magmatic arcs do not always survive as continuous, easily recognizable formations. Tectonic movements can break them apart, erosion can remove exposed rocks, and sedimentation can bury what remains. Later volcanic activity can further complicate the record, making older systems particularly difficult to identify.

By recognizing the surviving fragments of these ancient structures, scientists can better reconstruct how tectonic plates descended beneath one another, how volcanic arcs shifted, how continental crust grew, and how once-vast oceans eventually closed.

The Azerbaijan Continental Magmatic Arc offers new evidence for understanding the Late Cretaceous evolution of northwestern Iran and the larger Neo-Tethyan region. It also helps researchers refine geological models of an area shaped by repeated episodes of ocean closure and continental collision.

The ancient Neo-Tethys Ocean played a major role in the geological development of the Eastern Mediterranean, the Middle East, and Western Asia. Its eventual disappearance helped shape the modern landscapes of these regions.

By uncovering a volcanic system that had been largely erased from the geological record, researchers have added another piece to the complex story of how Earth's continents and oceans evolved over millions of years.
