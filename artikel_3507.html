12 Best Robert Downey Jr. Movies To Watch Before Avengers: Doomsday, Including Oppenheimer

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

There are some essential Robert Downey Jr. performances everybody should enjoy before he returns to the Marvel Cinematic Universe in Avengers: Doomsday. Robert Downey Jr. has enjoyed one of the most remarkable career transformations in modern Hollywood. Yet his ability to reinvent himself, take creative risks, and deliver memorable, distinctive performances has made him one of the most celebrated actors of his generation.

RDJ’s career reached an entirely new level when he joined the MCU timeline, becoming the face of a franchise that would dominate blockbuster cinema. However, reducing Downey's filmography to his superhero appearances would overlook some genuinely extraordinary performances, including roles that demonstrate just how versatile he can be.

His Oscar-winning work in particular proved that he could command an entirely different kind of screen presence. Now, with Downey preparing to return to Marvel in Avengers: Doomsday, audiences have an excellent opportunity to revisit the performances that established his reputation.

Richard III is an inventive adaptation of Shakespeare's tragedy, relocating the story to an alternate 1930s England where fascism has taken hold. Downey plays Earl Rivers, Queen Elizabeth's brother, whose political position makes him a target of Richard's murderous ambitions.

It's a relatively small role, and Downey doesn't have nearly enough screen time to make the same impression as some of his fellow cast members. Nevertheless, the role flaunts his acting talent and ability to convey sophisticated material – even among such an all-star cast.

The Judge reunites Downey with the kind of morally complicated protagonist he plays particularly well. He stars as Hank Palmer, a successful Chicago defence attorney who returns to his Indiana hometown following his mother's death. The film's strongest material comes from the uncomfortable relationship between Hank and his father, Josopeh.

Unfortunately, The Judge struggles to balance its courtroom drama with its family melodrama. Its legal proceedings become increasingly conventional, and the emotional revelations occasionally feel engineered to produce a reaction rather than emerge naturally from the characters. Still, Downey gives the material considerably more personality than it might otherwise have had.

A Scanner Darkly is one of the strangest films in Downey's filmography, and that's precisely why it's worth revisiting. Adapted from Philip K. Dick's novel, the animated science-fiction thriller uses rotoscoping to create an unsettling visual experience in which live-action performances are transformed into constantly shifting illustrations. Downey plays James Barris, a paranoid drug user who lives alongside Keanu Reeves' undercover narcotics agent.

Barris is an unpredictable presence, constantly talking and inventing elaborate theories. It allows Downey to indulge his gift for eccentric dialogue without turning the character into a conventional comic-relief figure. Downey's expressive delivery fits the material beautifully, even if A Scanner Darkly’s deliberately disorienting approach won't appeal to everyone.

Natural Born Killers is one of the most confrontational films of the 1990s. Its deliberately chaotic editing, exaggerated performances, and constantly shifting visual styles create an abrasive satire of America's obsession with televised violence and celebrity criminals. Downey plays Wayne Gale, a television journalist whose program turns the murderous Mickey and Mallory Knox into media sensations.

It's an especially interesting performance because Downey makes Gale both charismatic and deeply unpleasant. Natural Born Killers can be excessive, and the satire sometimes gets lost in its own stylistic aggression. Nevertheless, Downey's performance is an important part of its success.

Guy Ritchie's Sherlock Holmes gave Downey another opportunity to demonstrate his ability to make eccentric, intellectually gifted characters thoroughly entertaining. Rather than reproducing the familiar image of Arthur Conan Doyle's detective, the films present Holmes as an impulsive, physically capable and frequently antisocial investigator.

Downey makes an excellent Sherlock Holmes. His physical comedy, rapid observations and mischievous delivery create a detective who feels distinctly different from previous screen interpretations. With a third installment having been discussed for years, these films remain among Downey's most enjoyable blockbusters outside Marvel.

Ben Stiller's Tropic Thunder features one of Downey's most outrageous performances. He plays Kirk Lazarus, an Australian method actor who undergoes an extreme "pigmentation alteration" surgery to darken his skin for his portrayal of the black character. Even when the production descends into genuine danger, Lazarus continues behaving as though he is starring in an elaborate film production.

The role is deliberately provocative, and its humor depends on Downey's commitment to an absurd character who takes his own acting process far too seriously. His interactions with Stiller's increasingly frustrated director and Brandon T. Jackson's aspiring rapper Alpa Chino produce some of the film's funniest exchanges.

Chaplin gave Downey one of the most demanding roles of his early career. Playing the legendary Charlie Chaplin required him to recreate not only the comedian's distinctive physical performance but also the complicated personality behind one of cinema's most renowned performers.

Downey's transformation is remarkable. He captures Chaplin's movements, facial expressions and carefully controlled physical comedy, demonstrating an understanding of silent-film performance that goes beyond simply imitating the famous Little Tramp. Chaplin isn't entirely successful as a biography, but Downey's performance is consistently compelling.

David Fincher's Zodiac is one of the finest crime thrillers of the 21st century, and Downey delivers one of its most memorable performances as San Francisco Chronicle journalist Paul Avery. Based on the real investigation into the Zodiac Killer, it depicts the journalists and detectives whose lives become consumed by the search for an unidentified murderer.

Zodiac does not (and could not) provide the comforting resolution expected from a murder mystery, instead examining the damage that an unsolved case can inflict on the people pursuing it. Downey's performance is a crucial part of that approach, giving the film an unpredictable human dimension.

Kiss Kiss Bang Bang is arguably the film that first demonstrated how entertaining Downey could be after his career-threatening personal difficulties. The brilliantly irreverent neo-noir comedy stars him as Harry Lockhart, a small-time criminal who accidentally auditions for a Hollywood production and finds himself caught up in a murder investigation. Harry is an unconventional protagonist, frequently breaking the fourth wall to explain the increasingly ridiculous events unfolding around him.

Downey's sarcastic delivery and impeccable comic timing make the character immediately likable, even when his decisions are spectacularly questionable. More importantly, Kiss Kiss Bang Bang gives Downey the freedom to play a charmingly unreliable character without sacrificing the emotional vulnerability beneath his humor. Its success showed that audiences were still interested in Downey as a leading man, making it an essential stop before his Hollywood comeback.

Christopher Nolan's Oppenheimer provided Downey with arguably the most significant dramatic showcase of his later career. Playing Lewis Strauss, the former chairman of the Atomic Energy Commission, he delivers a controlled and calculating performance that feels completely removed from his familiar screen persona.

Downey is particularly effective during the film's Senate confirmation hearings, where Strauss' carefully maintained public image begins to unravel. Rather than relying on his usual quick-witted delivery, he communicates Strauss' insecurities through small expressions, measured dialogue, and an increasingly apparent desperation to control his own narrative. Downey won the Academy Award for Best Supporting Actor for the performance, alongside a BAFTA and Golden Globe.
