The Hunger Games Officially Changes Genres With a Near-Perfect New Movie

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

It’s also one of the best films in the franchise

The Hunger Games has never been afraid to put its young characters through the wringer. Despite being one of the most successful book and film franchises in history, the source material always stays true to its bleak premise: this is a story where teen bodies are used to keep the public under control.

More than a decade after the first film debuted, the franchise remains as relevant as ever, especially as our real world continues to look more and more like the dystopian nation of Panem. In 2023, we got a movie, The Ballad of Songbirds & Snakes, exploring the backstory of the franchise’s main villain, President Snow. Francis Lawrence’s Sunrise on the Reaping, his fifth Hunger Games movie, is a direct sequel to that story, and while it’s still YA, he and his team push the boundaries of what we can expect from the genre. The result is an incredibly entertaining blockbuster with the feel of a bleak, existential horror movie.

Sunrise on the Reaping follows 16-year-old Haymitch Abernathy (Joseph Zada), a charming and hard-working boy from District 12, the poorest district in Panem. To make ends meet and take care of his mom and little brother, he illegally distills and sells moonshine. He spends his free time with his girlfriend, Lenore Dove (Whitney Peak), even though her family doesn’t approve of him.

The story picks up on Reaping Day. This year is special, marking the 50th annual Hunger Games. To celebrate, the Capitol is demanding four tributes per district instead of two. After a boy’s name is called out and he runs off in fear, the reaping devolves into chaos. The boy gets shot, and Haymitch gets involved, trying to protect Lenore Dove from some Peacekeepers. To keep the show going and capture footage the Capitol can air, Haymitch is selected as the substitute tribute, and his journey begins.

If this all feels familiar, it should. While the Capitol comes off as particularly calculating, with the film paying close attention to how Snow (Ralph Fiennes) and his associates doctor footage for their own ends, Lawrence still follows the formula established by the original films. It isn’t until kids start dying before the Games begin that you realize Sunrise on the Reaping is doing something else. This is not a survival story; we all know where Haymitch ends up. This is a story about watching a character lose everything they care about.

While The Hunger Games movies always feature amazing actors, the cast for Sunrise on the Reaping is almost comically stacked. Fiennes brings his particular brand of menace to Coriolanus Snow, a character who develops a fixation on Haymitch. Glenn Close plays Drusilla Sickle, a Capitol escort sent to accompany the District 12 tributes (twist: she hates her job and the kids). Jesse Plemons takes over Plutarch Heavensbee from the late Philip Seymour Hoffman, playing him as aloof but quietly committed as he juggles making the show the Capitol wants and keeping Snow happy, all while fostering a rebellion among the tributes. At the center of it all is Zada, featured in almost every scene as he communicates an escalating sense of doom, loss, and despair.

Panem also gets a ’70s-style makeover. The Capitol is pushed decades into the past without losing its futuristic sheen, landing somewhere close to The Jetsons (complimentary). The best set is saved for what matters: the arena. It’s an open green field, lush and full of color, clear rivers, and adorable bunnies and deer. It’s a trick, though. The water is poisonous, and the animals are under the Capitol’s control. One tribute is killed by a swarm of squirrels. Another is impaled through the throat by a bird with a long, needle-thin beak. Haymitch’s plan to enter the arena and humiliate the Capitol on live TV is stymied at every turn, and Snow punishes him by making sure Haymitch gets a front seat to his friends’ deaths, one by one.

Sunrise on the Reaping is a tragedy. If you’ve seen the previous films, you know that Haymitch is an alcoholic from District 12, reviled by his community, and that it takes him meeting Katniss and forming a friendship with her for him to finally thaw. Sunrise on the Reaping effectively fills in the blanks of his character. At times, I found myself thinking, “There’s no way this could get worse,” but the film consistently proves me wrong.

It’s no secret that Sunrise on the Reaping also features the return of Jennifer Lawrence, Josh Hutcherson, and Woody Harrelson. It’s a sweet moment for fans, but also the weakest part of the film, muddling an otherwise thoughtful and engaging blockbuster with bad wigs and nostalgia. The cameos feel like reassurance that there’s a light at the end of the tunnel for Haymitch, undercutting the dread that Lawrence was so committed to building in the first place.

Still, Sunrise on the Reaping is the most depressing Hunger Games movie yet; Katniss and Peeta had it easy. And while it has the franchise’s blockbuster magic, it adds a welcome dose of dread and tragic inevitability, easily elevating it to one of the series’ best movies.

The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping premieres in theaters Nov. 20
