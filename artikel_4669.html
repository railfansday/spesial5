How to Self-Actualise

Self-actualisation is one of the oldest themes of therapy. It is present in all the founders, especially Jung’s notion of individuation, Carl Rogers’ sublime book On Becoming a Person, and Maslow’s hierarchy of needs, with self-actualisation on top. Yet few have ever outlined how to do self-actualisation in any systematic way.

I have a go at it in my book Beyond Happy, drawing on perspectives from humanistic psychology, existentialist philosophy, and more recent ideas in quantitative psychological science. I’ll sketch my approach here.

A useful place to start is self-discrepancy theory. It argues that we have a sense of three self concepts:

Begin by developing a thorough understanding of how each of these manifests in you. A journal can help.

Be mindful of what in your ideal and ought self is authentic to you versus a product of your environment or socialisation. The influence of family and trusted peers can be benevolent and beneficial, but you first need to get some distance from it if you want self-discovery.

The next step is self-creation: Bring your actual self into alignment with your ideal self, subject to the constraint imposed by your ought self.

This might require some tricky harmonisation. Consider a young woman who aspires to be a judge on the International Court of Justice. There’s a fast track there through commercial law, but she feels queasy about representing immoral companies. This is her ought self speaking, and harmonisation might require taking a slower route, perhaps through environmental law instead.

Your ideal self doesn’t need to be ambitious; indeed, that might be wholly unsuitable for you. Your ideal self is simply the person you’d be most proud to be. You might want to be a folksy type with lots of spare time to help friends and enjoy an array of hobbies. Stay true to yourself.

Now your first guess at your ideal self is likely to be wrong. Self-actualisation is an iterative process, and you’ll learn by doing.

That learning comes principally in the form of affective feedback. Joy, serenity, exhilaration and other positive emotions are signals to invest more.

What about negative feedback? Self-discrepancy theory finds that depression attends discrepancies between the actual and ideal self, while anxiety is associated with actual-ought self discrepancies. But you need to introspect to understand what these feelings mean for you in each case.

Are you depressed because your ideal self is meaningful to you and you’re not living up to it? Then that negative emotion is a signal to try harder.

But if you’re depressed because achieving your ideal is hard and feels like a drag, that’s a two-part affective signal. There’s depression, but also a lack of enthusiasm.

Here we get our next big self-actualisation puzzle piece from self-determination theory: There are five varieties of motivation running on a spectrum from extrinsic to relatively intrinsic.

Extrinsically motivated activities are done under duress. They require a great deal of self-regulation to enact because you don’t want to do them.

Introjected activities are done for a reason contingent to the activity itself. For example, many people put up with exercise because they want to be healthy.

We’re more autonomous here, but there’s still a lot of self-regulation going on, so these activities tend to drain our willpower and feel exhausting. If there’s no sense of achievement afterwards, then the activity is likely unsustainable.

The depressed and demotivated individual above is probably doing something introjected. The lack of enthusiasm is a signal that it’s not authentic to them.

They should switch to something with intrinsic motivation. These activities are self-determined, which means we do them spontaneously for their own sake. They’re associated with feelings of interest and vitality.

Not everything in the ideal and ought selves will be intrinsically motivated. Indeed, when self-actualisation begins, it usually involves a lot of identified motivation. This is where we value something but don’t have intrinsic motivation for it.

I value being honest, for example, but it didn’t come naturally to me. Indeed, it took at least a decade of deliberate effort to stop telling white lies.

Introjected and identified motivations can be internalised over time until they become intrinsic, but sustaining the necessary effort requires payoffs in the form of frequent positive feedback.

For example, I loathed mathematics in school. But training in it has made me a better social scientist, for which I have intrinsic motivation. Seeing the ways math would help me achieve this part of my ideal self kept me going through my statistics classes. Nowadays maths comes much more easily to me; it’s not a joy, but I don’t notice it enough to loathe it anymore.

This is an example of integrated motivation – where something identified becomes connected to something intrinsically motivated, and so is easier to engage in.

Let me bring this all together in a simple model of self-actualisation, shown in the image below. Imagine a young woman who dreams of being an astronaut. She identifies two activities as central to this ideal self – science and athletics, so she enrols in chemistry and a gymnastics club.

As she goes about these activities, she gets feedback. Maybe she likes gymnastics, but this particular club is full of overly competitive people. Introspecting on this feedback, she realises that gymnastics is right for her; she just needs to join a more casual club.

She loves chemistry, but her casual interest in gymnastics means she won’t be fit enough to be an astronaut. She needs to recalibrate her ideal self. Maybe she can be a terrestrial scientist and study samples sent by the astronauts to her lab.

This cycle of goal, feedback, introspection, and recalibration takes place across all facets of your life, slowly bringing them into harmony and yourself into actualisation.
