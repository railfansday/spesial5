Thanksgiving Isn't Over Yet

There’s a riddle in Alain de Botton and John Armstrong’s book Art as Therapy: What’s the most important part of a museum?

Consider that for a second. Maybe it’s the architecture, the explainers on the wall, or the audio tour that helps you make sense of an abstract creation.

This is not about surrendering to late-stage capitalism or Black Friday consumerism: It’s about the importance of slowing down to take in the experience you just had. And following a holiday dedicated to gratitude, that’s why this weekend might be the most important part of Thanksgiving.

I love museums. They’re amazing places to take in ideas and beauty with other people. But after a world-expanding hour or two, we step back into traffic and chores and, little by little, the magic dissipates.

Enter the gift shop. It’s a chance to travel back in time, to revisit what you just saw and buy a little trinket to keep that experience with you.

That’s the argument for museum gift shops: you’ve just had a lovely experience, but unless you slow down to make meaning of it and take it with you, you risk missing some of its goodness.

Maybe you’ve noticed that Thanksgiving also goes by fast. After all the logistics, planning, meal prep, traveling, and settling in… blink, it’s over. But it doesn’t have to be over, at least not in our minds.

I suspect your Thanksgiving wasn’t perfect, but I imagine it had some lovely moments. Here’s one of mine:

On our way to visit my cousin’s family in rural Maryland, we learned that they’d lost power. When we got there, it was still out. So, looking for a way to finish cooking, my cousin’s two Gen Z kids and I ventured out in the pouring rain and gusty wind to find some propane (because, of course, they were out of that, too).

After two failed attempts (one store was out, and the other wouldn’t sell it to us because their power was also out), we miraculously found some at the dollar store. Somewhere in the middle of this little adventure, I found myself sitting in the backseat, expanding my musical horizons with Slayyyter and having a silly time with my cousin’s kids, who, I might add, were being responsible and safe drivers.

It’s the kind of moment that’s easy to miss: the joy of watching the people you love grow up and appreciating how easily we made the most of a power outage.

Oh, and I forgot to mention the best part. Literally the minute we walked in with a full propane tank, returning heroes that we were, the power came back on.

After writing Sometimes You Should Be Late, I’ve become even more fixated on the question of when experiences truly end.

Does yoga class end the moment 9 a.m. hits and we race out and unlock our phones? Or can we take a few minutes to luxuriate in savasana, bask in the glow of sweat, and appreciate that we took time to slow down?

It’s the same with the rest of our lives: we can slow down to complete an experience, or we can rush past it.

I used to do a lot more things back to back to back, at work, on weekends, and on vacation. Here’s what I noticed: sure, I did a lot of activities, but it was harder to fully appreciate them. As I write in my book: “If our timelines leave no room to appreciate the experience we’re having or the people we’re having it with, why bother?”

Slowing down helps us pay attention. It helps us, as psychologist Rick Hanson says, to “take in the good.” He explains that our brains are often like Teflon for positive experiences but magnets for negative ones. Slowing down with the positive helps us rebalance.

What’s particularly interesting about taking in the good is that we don’t have to do this during experiences; we can do it afterward, too.

Here’s the Turkey Day upshot: Thanksgiving Day is over, but the experience of it isn’t.

So, instead of just moving on to the next holiday plan, here’s my invitation: dedicate a little time this weekend to taking in the good of what just happened, muddling imperfectly and all.

The movie Ferris Bueller’s Day Off offers this reminder: “Life moves pretty fast. If you don’t stop and look around once in a while, you could miss it.”

And here’s the thing: we do have a choice to slow things down at the end, or even a few days afterward, to bring a little more appreciation to the moments of our lives.

That could be a museum visit, a good book, or a day of gathering together—holiday traffic, TSA ground stops, and power outages be damned.

Perhaps the most important part of Thanksgiving is what we choose to do with the experience once it’s over.

Here’s me slowing down with my Thanksgiving gratitude: thanks to my lovely cousin Ann for hosting, for muddling through a Thanksgiving feast without power and keeping it all together, and for raising two responsible kids… er, adults!
