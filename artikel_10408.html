Turn individual experimentation into organizational learning

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

AI adoption is accelerating, but adoption doesn’t equal workplace transformation.

According to Gallup’s State of the Global Workplace 2026 report, 65% of workers in U.S. organizations that have implemented AI say the technology has had some degree of positive impact on their individual productivity. That’s an encouraging sign, until you consider that only 12% strongly agree that AI has transformed how work gets done.

That gap points to the next challenge for leaders. Many of AI’s early gains have centered on helping individuals complete existing work faster. The bigger opportunity is figuring out how what one employee learns can change how an entire team, function, or organization works.

Today, two employees sitting directly across from each other can have dramatically different experiences with the same AI tool. While one might use the technology to write an email, the other may have discovered a way to redesign a process that saves significant time and resources. If those use cases stay with the individuals, the organization never captures their full value.

As AI becomes increasingly embedded across organizations, access to the technology itself is less likely to create sustainable advantage. The real differentiator will be how quickly organizations can turn what employees learn into shared knowledge, redesigned workflows, and better ways of working.

LEADERS SET THE DIRECTION

Because employees are closest to day-to-day work, they’re often uncovering the most practical ways AI can improve work or solve problems differently. They understand where existing processes create friction and where AI might enable a better approach.

Walmart provides one useful example. Rather than treating employees only as end users of AI, the company has involved them in shaping AI applications. One logistics manager helped create an AI process designed to improve truck efficiency and reduce empty trailers. The insight did not originate with someone studying the workflow from a distance. It came from someone close enough to the work to see what needed to change.

Leaders can create the conditions for that type of discovery by establishing the problems worth solving, setting the boundaries for experimentation, and defining what a better outcome looks like. Employees then have room to figure out how AI can change work.

At my company, Cengage, we introduced AI in phases. Our “Change Champions” across the company experimented with AI, identified meaningful use cases, and shared what they learned with their teams. This shared-learning model allowed us to build knowledge across the organization. As access expanded, employees had examples, practices, and colleagues they could learn from rather than asking every team to start from scratch. Now, nearly 90% of employees with access are actively using AI.

Experimentation creates far more value when the knowledge it produces can travel beyond the individual or team that discovered it.

MAKE LEARNING PART OF THE OPERATING MODEL

One employee finding a better way to perform a task creates individual productivity. Dozens of employees learning from that discovery, adapting it to their own work, and improving it creates organizational impact.

Organizations need a way for those discoveries to travel across departments. Employees should be able to share what they’ve tried and what they’ve learned, giving other teams a starting point for their own experimentation.

At Canva last year, 5,000 employees were given a dedicated week to learn and experiment with AI, and to explore how the technology could improve their work. The value of creating the space extends beyond what employees accomplished during that week. It created an opportunity for what they learned, to spread across the entire organization.

Traditional training still helps employees build a shared foundation and understand how to use AI responsibly. But training alone can’t keep pace with a technology that is changing this quickly. The most useful applications often emerge as employees try AI against the real challenges in their work. Organizations need to make it easy for those employees to share what they’ve learned, so a promising idea can become a starting point for others. At Cengage, we saw the value of that approach through our Change Champions. Employees with subject matter expertise learned by experimenting, then brought practical examples back to their teams.

MANAGERS BRIDGE EXPERIMENTATION AND SCALE

In addition to encouraging employees to experiment, a culture of shared learning requires giving employees the time and support to test new ideas and share what they learn without expecting every experiment to demonstrate an immediate return.

Microsoft’s 2026 Work Trend Index found that when managers created psychological safety around experimentation, employees reported up to 20 points higher AI readiness and value.

Senior leaders can set the expectation that AI experimentation is important, but employees experience culture primarily through their managers. That puts managers in an increasingly important position. They can protect time for experimentation, help teams identify worthwhile problems, set expectations for quality and responsible use, and make sharing lessons part of the team’s regular work. They are also close enough to distinguish between an interesting demo and a change that genuinely improves a workflow. They are the bridge between individual experimentation and organizational learning.

BUILD A COMPANY THAT CONTINUOUSLY LEARNS

The next phase of AI adoption is an organizational learning challenge. Models will change. Tools will change. What seemed like a leading-edge use case six months ago may quickly become commonplace.

Organizations cannot retrain the entire workforce from scratch every time that happens. They need a system that helps employees iterate to discover, test, share, and build on better ways of working.

The organizations that ultimately create the most value from AI will build the capacity to continually learn what the technology makes possible—and turn those individual discoveries into better ways of working across the organization.

Darren Person is EVP and chief product and digital officer at Cengage.
