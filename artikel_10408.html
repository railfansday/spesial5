Does the workplace have a 4 p.m. problem?

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

At 10 a.m., a meeting is just a meeting. You grab a coffee and get ready to listen to something that could probably have been summarized in an email. At 4 p.m., that same meeting can feel like a hostage situation. You’re smiling and nodding along as your coworker rattles off the quarterly goals. Meanwhile, you’re calculating: If this meeting goes past 4:55, can I make it downtown in time? Can my son use the bus service to get to baseball practice? And what exactly will happen if I’m the last parent to pick up at daycare?

This is what I like to call “high-cost hours”—those periods of the day when your job interferes with the complicated logistics of caring for your family. For parents, every hour is not equal. A 10 a.m. meeting and a 4 p.m. meeting may look the same on the shared office calendar, but they can have very different repercussions. One cuts into your productivity, and the other can derail your entire evening. Parents have always navigated this dilemma. The juggling act is nothing new. But even as work has become more flexible, one major problem remains: Scheduling can unravel someone’s childcare arrangements.


The invisible price of 4 p.m.

The real problem is not that parents don’t want to stay late. Most adults are willing to put in the hours they’re expected to work. The issue is that certain hours disproportionately impact parents, who often pay a hidden mental and financial price that employers don’t account for. Sticking around until 5:15 p.m. might mean paying for an extra hour of childcare, harassing another mom you’ve asked favors from before, or rushing home and arriving irritated and exhausted. And while you can make up that presentation after the kids are in bed, the aftercare center closes at 6 p.m. and they are not happy with you.

Parents don’t need research to understand this problem. They live it. But it can explain why it happens. A 2025 survey from KPMG found that 54% of working parents reported their work schedules often conflicted with caregiving responsibilities. Half of the parents surveyed said they needed more flexibility in their schedules. Another study from 2026 by the Pew Research Center found that parents with unpredictable work schedules were more likely to report difficulty finding childcare options that matched their scheduling needs. Flexibility, it turns out, is less about where and more about when.

Both mothers and fathers experience this stress, but because women still do the majority of household chores and caregiving, they often experience more issues with high-cost hours. They’re not just thinking about how long they will be stuck in traffic. They’re also mentally going over a to-do list that includes reminding their spouse about which kid needs a ride where and what’s in the fridge. Oh, and why hasn’t anyone responded to the class parent’s email. By late afternoon, many mothers are basically running two jobs. One just happens to actually pay her.

It’s time to rethink the clock

Does this mean we should ban all meetings after 4 p.m.? Of course not. Some meetings will always be necessary. Things come up. Clients have different schedules than your employees. Meetings will always be a part of work. But that doesn’t mean everyone’s schedule needs to accommodate all meetings. 

Managers: Figure out your employees’ high-cost hours before scheduling important meetings. Find out where the conflicts are, and ask your employees what times consistently create conflicts. Can you agree on core hours everyone should try to be available? If not, save afternoon meetings for only the times when everyone needs to be in attendance. 

Employees: It’s okay to be specific about your needs. If something conflicts with your hard stop, let your manager know you can’t make the meeting at that time. Try: “I need to be out of meetings by 5 so I can leave on time for childcare. If we really need to meet at that time, can we cover my portion first?” 

Everyone: Please stop equating staying late with working hard. Staying late means someone had to rush home or pick up a late fee. The problem isn’t that parents are less committed to their jobs as the clock approaches 5 p.m. It’s that companies still struggle to understand that being committed to work doesn’t require people to abandon everything else. We endlessly debate how many days per week employees should be in the office, whether working from home is destroying company culture, and how flexibility has ruined workplace morale. Maybe it’s time to pay closer attention to when we expect employees to work and what those expectations cost.
