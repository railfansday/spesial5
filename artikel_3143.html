Microsoft says China’s Salt Typhoon and North Korean hackers now use ARTEX

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Microsoft says the tool, now in Salt Typhoon’s hands, can reach privileged access within minutes. Its report explains how ARTEX works, who sells it, and the bursts that give it away.

Microsoft logo at the Microsoft Experience Center, New York

State-backed hacking groups from China and North Korea, including Salt Typhoon, have added the AI attack framework ARTEX to their tools, Microsoft says. Microsoft Threat Intelligence named the groups in a report published on Wednesday.

The list covers Crystal Typhoon and Salt Typhoon, two Chinese state groups, and Onyx Sleet and Pearl Sleet, which Microsoft tracks as North Korean. Two more clusters, Storm-0940 and Storm-3358, are also on it. Microsoft says state-sponsored and financially motivated hackers use ARTEX to speed up network scans, credential attacks, and attacks on web apps.

“Depending on the threat actor’s configuration and the target’s defenses, ARTEX can advance from reconnaissance to persistent, privileged access to a target organization in a matter of minutes,” Microsoft wrote.

The report landed hours after US media tied ARTEX to the hack of three US spy agencies, as Jonathan Greig reported for The Record.

US agencies believe Salt Typhoon breached at least nine telecoms groups in 2024, The Record noted. That hack allegedly reached phones used by Donald Trump and JD Vance. A US House report later said Chinese carriers were still inside American phone networks.

Crystal Typhoon is newer. It was first seen in late 2023 and goes after government networks and critical infrastructure through edge devices and firewalls, TechRadar reported.

Microsoft had already linked Storm-0940 to the ConnectWise supply chain attack. The new report adds numbers from its campaigns. In one, Microsoft logged more than 151,000 failed sign-ins, peaking at 2,900 a minute. In several intrusions, the attackers had privileged access less than three hours after they first went after a target.

In another case, an AI agent went from searching GitHub for client secrets to taking data from Azure Key Vault in five minutes.

A GitHub user called Autumn-27 published ARTEX as open-source code with a Chinese interface in July. The developer took it down in October, but other developers kept publishing their own versions. This month, the Chinese security firm ThreatBook claimed ownership and started selling access to “ARTEX Pro”.

The attacker sets the targets. One AI model breaks the job into goals. A planner agent then hands short tasks to worker agents with a real shell and hacking tools. A reporter agent writes up what worked. ARTEX can run on almost any large language model, including ones stripped of their guardrails. That means no single AI provider’s safety controls can stop it, Microsoft says.

The FBI warned about ARTEX earlier this month.

“ARTEX does not introduce new attack techniques; it uses common, pre-existing penetration testing tools and techniques,” Microsoft wrote.

Attackers can change the tool’s IP addresses and user agent strings, so blocking those alone will not work. The steadiest sign is behaviour: many varied requests in a short burst, and quick moves to the next step. ARTEX does nothing to hide that activity by default.

Microsoft also suggests honeypots, tarpits, and fake wins. A fake win shows the agent false proof that its job is done, so it stops. It recommends rate limits, phishing-resistant multifactor sign-in, and replacing any secret that leaks into code. The report also points customers to Microsoft’s own products, including Defender XDR and its Perception security agents.
