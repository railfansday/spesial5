When should women start thinking about heart disease? Earlier than you might expect

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

New guidance explains how first periods, pregnancy complications, and menopause can offer clues to a woman’s heart disease risk.

Picture a heart attack and you probably picture a man in his fifties clutching his chest.

Yet heart disease kills more women in the U.S. than breast cancer and lung cancer combined, and much of what sets a woman’s risk happens decades before menopause.

New joint guidance from four medical societies tells doctors to start reading that risk at a girl’s first period.

The guidance comes as three papers from the American Heart Association, the American Academy of Pediatrics, the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists and the Society for Maternal-Fetal Medicine.

It’s built from existing evidence rather than a new trial, so it reorganizes what doctors already know instead of adding a new finding.

Sadiya Khan, a cardiologist at Northwestern University’s Feinberg School of Medicine, chaired the writing group. In an interview with Earth.com, she compared it to saving money.

“You would never wait until retirement to start saving. We should bring that same thinking to heart health,” Khan said.

Standard cardiovascular risk calculators already account for sex, but they don’t capture every risk factor tied to a woman’s reproductive history.

The new framework splits a woman’s life into four stages set by her hormones. They are the years around the first period, the decades of regular cycles, the transition into menopause, and the years after it.

Pregnancy counts as a separate event, running from 12 months before conception to 12 months after delivery.

Estrogen and progesterone act on blood vessels, heart muscle and the brain, not only on the reproductive organs.

Because those hormones rise and fall about once a month, with no equivalent in men, blood vessel function and sleep vary in the same rhythm.

The authors wrote that risk in each stage is shaped by the stages before it.

Asked by Earth.com which stage is most overlooked, Khan said the biggest gaps are what happened earlier, such as irregular or heavy periods, pregnancy complications and a diagnosis like endometriosis.

Those shape heart health in later stages, she said, and belong in the conversation at every visit.

“Heart health for women doesn’t start at menopause,” said Amanda Perak, a pediatric cardiologist at Lurie Children’s Hospital of Chicago who worked on all three papers. “Puberty is a big turning point.”

A first period before age 10 is linked to higher heart disease risk later in life, and the 2026 cholesterol guideline already lists it as a risk enhancer.

Some irregularity is normal in the first one to three years. A pattern that persists can signal the hormone disorder that used to be called polycystic ovary syndrome, which often comes with insulin resistance and unhealthy cholesterol.

The disorder, now called polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome, affects an estimated 10% to 13% of women.

Heavy periods affect 20% to 30% of young women, and about half of those with heavy bleeding are low on iron. The authors wrote that menstrual patterns may be the earliest sign of heart risk a doctor can see.

Mental health turns in the same years. By about age 17, girls have at least twice the odds of a depression diagnosis as boys.

Teens with depression are more likely to develop obesity later, and the reverse holds too. Nearly one in five girls in organized sports consider quitting over menstrual concerns, the authors wrote.

“The goal isn’t to worry teens about the chance of a heart attack, which are decades away for nearly all of them,” Perak said.

“It’s that the teen years are when patterns get set, and it’s much easier to keep good heart health than to get it back.”

Estrogen levels in pregnancy rise to about 30 times their peak in a normal cycle, and the heart and metabolism both change to support the fetus.

The authors call it a stress test because it can expose risk that was already there.

Nearly nine in ten women ages 20 to 44 begin pregnancy with less-than-ideal heart health.

High blood pressure in pregnancy, gestational diabetes and preterm birth are each linked to higher heart disease risk later in life.

Mood and anxiety disorders affect up to 37% of women in the first three months after delivery.

The year after a birth is a vulnerable time for follow-up. Women move from obstetric care back to primary care, and that handoff is often missed.

The guidance notes that the obstetric threshold for hypertension, a systolic reading of at least 140 or a diastolic reading of at least 90, matches the cutoff for stage 2 hypertension in general adult care.

Perimenopause begins at a median age of about 47 in the U.S., and the authors describe it as a period of swings rather than a steady decline.

LDL, the so-called bad cholesterol, rises during the transition independent of aging. Hot flashes affect 50% to 80% of women in this stage, and about one in three report significant depression or anxiety symptoms.

Women then spend about 40% of their lives after menopause. Around 67 million American women are 50 or older, and an estimated 6,000 more reach menopause every day.

Menopause that arrives early carries extra heart risk of its own.

Hormone therapy can help manage menopause symptoms, but the authors advise against using it to prevent heart disease.

Years of unhealthy habits may begin to show in this stage as heart disease. Stacey Rosen, the association’s immediate past volunteer president, said it is never too late to do something about it.

“It’s always about opportunities,” Rosen said.

A woman may see a pediatrician, a primary care doctor, an obstetrician, a cardiologist and a psychologist over her life, and their notes rarely travel with her.

One of the three papers surveyed 70 leaders of women’s cardiovascular centers and heard back from 33.

Of those programs, 29 are in cities and one is rural, and the paper proposes a national network of centers that combine the specialties.

Khan told Earth.com the papers are a first step toward giving every clinician the same playbook.

The next change, she said, is connecting all the clinicians on a woman’s care team through the life stage framework.

Her advice for the next appointment was a single question. “Ask at every life stage: What is my heart risk, and what should I do about it now? It is never too early to talk about your heart health,” she said.

The framework rests on evidence that links these stages to risk, and the papers say plainly where the links are weakest.

It isn’t known whether treating severe hot flashes lowers heart disease risk, with or without hormones. Female-specific cutoffs for heart imaging tests aren’t yet in guidelines.

And none of the three papers reports a trial showing that sorting care by life stage, rather than by age, prevents more heart attacks and strokes.

Khan said she is especially interested in reaching younger women now that risk can be estimated up to 30 years ahead with the association’s PREVENT equations.

Whether clinics can be rebuilt around that forecast is the test the four societies have set themselves.

The research was published in the journal Circulation.
