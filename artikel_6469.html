3 Ways To Defrost Chicken Without Spreading Salmonella, According To An Expert

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Your dinner plans aren't ruined just yet.

Forgetting to take chicken out of the freezer is an all-too-common occurrence that can bring your dinner plans to a screeching halt. To get things back on track, your gut instinct may be to thaw it as quickly as possible. But there are more important things to worry about than a delayed dinner.

Raw poultry can carry Salmonella, so how you handle it matters. Nothing is less appetizing than giving your whole family a foodborne illness.

But before you give up and order takeout, we’re here to help. We chatted with Ellen Steinberg, PhD, RD, LD, and food safety specialist, to find the best (and safest) methods to thaw chicken. Whether you plan ahead or need to defrost it fast, here’s what you should do.

Defrosting chicken isn’t as simple as taking it out of the freezer. Salmonella is real, y’all—according to FoodSafety.gov, roughly 4% of chicken packages at American grocery stores are contaminated with the bacteria. That bacteria can multiply rapidly in the temperature danger zone, between 40°F and 140°F. Translation: Leaving your chicken out on the counter can actually make you sick.

To prevent that, choose the right method and protect the surrounding environment from cross-contamination. Even with supposedly airtight packaging, the plastic may have holes, or chicken juice may transfer from other packages at the grocery store. It’s especially difficult to spot when the meat is frozen, so Steinberg says it’s better to be safe than sorry.

“You should put your [raw] chicken in a roasting pan or a baking sheet so the juices won’t spread,” she says. Everything that touches raw chicken should be thoroughly cleaned with hot, soapy water. If you want to take food safety even further, Steinberg suggests keeping a separate cutting board in a different color that you use exclusively for raw meat. And above all else: Wash your hands!

If you’re not in a rush, thawing chicken in the fridge yields the most consistent results. Because refrigerators should stay at 40°F and below, frozen chicken can gradually defrost without entering the temperature danger zone where bacteria multiply more rapidly.

Because of this, Steinberg says it’s the only USDA-approved thawing method that lets you refreeze raw chicken without cooking it first. “If you end up deciding that you don’t want to cook the chicken or you only want to use half of it, you can place it back in the freezer,” she says. There is a caveat, however: Once your chicken has fully thawed, you should either cook or refreeze it within one to two days.

If you opt for the refrigerator method, keep these safety measures in mind. Beyond keeping your meat contained on a plate or tray, place it on the bottom shelf. If juice drips, it’ll contaminate the fewest ingredients. Steinberg also suggests storing the chicken toward the back of your refrigerator. “The temperature is more consistent, unlike the areas closest to the door,” she says.

If you need to defrost chicken fast, one of the best ways is with cool, running water. Unlike fridge air, water conducts heat much more effectively. Keeping the water moving also helps maintain consistent thawing conditions. But you don't necessarily need to leave the faucet running: The USDA also recommends submerging chicken in cold water and changing it every 30 minutes.

So if cold water thaws meat quickly, would hot water do the job even faster? It might, but the potential food safety risks aren't worth it. "If you thaw chicken under warm or hot water, the outside of the meat can enter the temperature danger zone while the center is still frozen,” Steinberg says.

For the running-water method, place the chicken in leak-proof packaging in a bowl and run cool water (70°F or lower) over it. Thawing times depend on the size and thickness of the meat, but smaller packages may be ready in an hour or less. Once thawed, cook the chicken immediately before considering refreezing it.

The final USDA-approved method involves using your microwave’s defrost setting. While it’s certainly faster than letting meat thaw in the fridge, we suggest relying on this method only if you have no other option.

Steinberg says this technique leaves the least wiggle room for planning your dinner. “You have to cook it immediately,” she says. “You can’t defrost it and then decide to cook two hours later.”

The need to cook immediately is certainly one downside of the microwave method. However, another compelling argument against using the defrost setting is that it can make your chicken taste worse.

Microwaves heat food unevenly, especially with thicker pieces of meat. Some areas can start warming up or even cooking before the center fully thaws. It's not a big deal when reheating leftovers, but it can nearly ruin frozen proteins.

By the time the center is fully defrosted, the outer edges may already be partially cooked. Can you imagine chicken that tastes worse than unseasoned microwaved chicken? We can't.

It’s a safe and fast defrosting method when followed by immediate cooking—but at what cost? So we'd only vouch for this technique if your chicken will end up shredded or submerged in enough sauce to mask the texture.
