The White House announced a raft of new tariffs on the same day inflation data came back at nearly twice the Fed’s target

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

The White House has announced a litany of new tariffs targeting imports from 14 economies, including key trading partners such as China and the EU. With consumers already stretched by an affordability crisis, the new duties come on the same day that the Bureau of Economic Analysis confirmed inflation hit 3.8%, according to the annual rate of the Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) price index, and nearly double the Federal Open Market Committee’s 2% goal.

The announcement has two important implications. First, though the new duties would be imposed on foreign economies, previous tariff studies indicate that 90% of the increased prices are ultimately borne by U.S. businesses and, more often than not, their consumers.

And second, it complicates the Federal Reserve’s next move. The Fed, now led by Kevin Warsh, raised rates in September for the first time since July 2023, to a range of 3.75% to 4%, and meets again Dec. 9–10.

Former Fed chairman Jerome Powell said that while tariffs represented a one-off price increase rather than broader inflationary factors, they nonetheless increased near-term inflation expectations. As UBS chief economist Paul Donovan told clients today: “The concern is the impact on U.S. inflation, and thus U.S. interest rates.”

And the Fed already has a data headache on its hands: The PCE increase to 3.8% compared with a year ago is nearly double the FOMC’s 2% target.

China faces a new 20% duty, and Vietnam, up to 35%, or 20% if it reaches a trade deal, according to Trade Representative Jamieson Greer, citing Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974. Section 301 allows the U.S. to investigate foreign practices deemed unreasonable or discriminatory, or that burden or restrict U.S. commerce. Both nations are among the top 10 importers to the U.S., accounting for 7.8% and 7.4% of imports, respectively, per Census Bureau data.

Through September, the U.S. imported $215 billion in Chinese goods and $204.8 billion from Vietnam, which accounts for the largest U.S. trade deficit with any country, at $190.9 billion.

The European Union is also facing tariffs of 15%, while India is facing rates of up to 25%, or 17% in the event of a trade deal.

The new revenue-generating tariff action comes after the Supreme Court struck down Trump’s sweeping emergency tariffs in February, forcing the administration to refund roughly $100 billion of the $166 billion it had collected.

For most of the 14 economies, existing Section 301 duties count toward the new rate rather than being added on top. (China is the exception: Its 20% stacks on existing levies.) “In effect, it’ll look more like a top-up,” Greer told CNBC’s Squawk Box. “When it all nets out, it really will just top up a few percentage points.”

That will do little to calm the nerves of consumers who have been battling above-target inflation for more than five years. The New York Fed’s October survey of consumer expectations revealed that households expect inflation to be at 3.9% this time next year, and at 3.3% in three years’ time.

For the Fed, these heightened expectations can be a problem. Consumers may adjust their spending and saving, and make wage demands in response to their inflation expectations. That can create a self-reinforcing cycle, making inflation harder to bring down.

“The tariffs replace the previously announced Section 301 tariffs with a marginal incremental impact higher on goods prices but, moreover, set the table for further increases if negotiations break down,” Bradford Smith, portfolio manager at Janus Henderson Investors, told Fortune in a note.

“The announcement serves as a reminder that the Fed’s inflation fight is exacerbated by tariffs. With inflation already an issue and the Fed awaiting the lapping of previous tariff implementations, additional tariffs will make the Fed’s job more difficult.”
