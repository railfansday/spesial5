In Brooklyn, an Intimate Diwali Dinner Party Bridging Two Easts

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

On a Sunday night, the elevator doors open onto Rachel Gurjar’s Brooklyn living room. Amaranthus blooms brush brass family heirlooms and fresh pomegranates on a credenza. A tiffin bearing a relative’s initials rests on draped brown fabric. The room could be a still from Gurjar’s debut cookbook, East to East: Recipes and Stories From Mumbai to New York, released October 13.

Barefoot in a pre-draped blue floral sari, Gurjar moves unhurriedly, labelling plates with scraps of tape. She then pulls a white platter from an immaculately organized glass cabinet. “I think this is for the duck,” she says, already moving on.

Since 2021, Gurjar, who grew up in Mumbai, has thrown New York Diwali parties that have acquired a certain lore. One year, she led magazine editors in choreographed Bollywood dances; another, a pani puri fountain presided over a party for 140, with handmade naan to match. “I didn’t have a place to celebrate Diwali myself, so I decided to throw these parties,” she says.

Tonight, the sixth annual gathering is quieter, and intentionally so: a dozen chefs, artists, and designers who helped bring the book to life, on the page or in spirit. If the official launch party was for the public, this one is for her people.

Diwali, the South Asian festival of lights, is widely associated with the triumph of good over evil. It is also an occasion to gather, feed one another, and take stock. Gurjar’s chosen family spans disciplines, with an emphasis on the Black, Brown, and AAPI creatives whose work, she believes, makes New York what it is. “I wanted to make sure that every person at the table had a medium through which they expressed their creativity,” she explains.

Guests include collaborators like designer Ivan Lam of Tender Touch Studios, who picked up flowers that morning; food stylist Sean Dooley, who styled the book’s visuals; and photographer Isa Zapata, a former Bon Appétit colleague who photographed it in Mumbai.

The evening begins with an actually spicy pani puri mezcalita and masala popcorn. Whipped masala and curry leaf butters sit in vintage coupes beside a sweet-sour pomegranate Chex Mix that took Gurjar 12 attempts to perfect. Bollywood music plays as guests pass around bindis and apply them to one another.

The menu moves between Mumbai and New York, yet feels unmistakably familial. Gurjar’s mother, Ranjana, brought potato and rice chips she made in India, sneaking them past TSA. She also spent the week helping her daughter prepare, kneading dough and frying gulab jamun.

“At her core, Rachel is a nurturer,” says chef Josh Osamor, who calls her goat curry one of the most delicious things he has eaten. The two recently collaborated on a Nigerian-Indian supper club. “The biryani jollof was a no-brainer. It was a perfect dance,” he says.

Leanne Gan, an art director at the ACLU, has attended Gurjar’s Diwali parties since the beginning. Adjusting her sea-green dupatta, she dips a rice chip into the Penang curry crab dip. “Rachel is someone who doesn’t settle for less,” Gan says. “It’s been amazing to see her put 110% of herself into these recipes.”

The room erupts with oohs and aahs as artist and fashion designer Misha Japanwala arrives in a gold-and-silver lamé sari and a custom silver breastplate she made using body casting, the same process she used for pieces for Cardi B and Lizzo. (Most recently, Lupita Nyong’o wore Japanwala’s work in The Odyssey.)

“I just felt like I was missing a silver breastplate in my wardrobe, and everybody needs a silver breastplate,” she jokes. She accessorizes with nipple-cast rings and silver jewelry from Karachi, her hometown.

“One of the most beautiful things about moving to New York has been expanding my South Asian circle and being able to celebrate each other in this way,” Japanwala reflects. When she hosted Eid at home for the first time last year, she invited friends from her wider South Asian circle, beyond Muslim Pakistanis. “It really was the most miraculous thing,” she adds. Next to her, fashion designer Sheena Sood of Abacaxi is wearing a mirrorwork lehenga with a matching knit shrug from a 2021 collection.

The contagious joys of Diwali extend beyond the subcontinent: For Manouska Jeantus, founder of the social events company Nous Love Club, the gathering recalls celebrating Haitian Independence Day with her family, even as she eats malai shrimp instead of the soup joumou she grew up with.

Guests take their seats, and Gurjar raises a dirty chai espresso martini for a quick toast. Dinner is a greatest-hits collection from East to East, starting with achari melon salad with tempered pickling spices and garam masala roasted squash slicked with brown butter and lime. Back in the kitchen, Gurjar works two cast-iron skillets at once, searing scored duck breasts for her paanch phoran duck—a crowd favorite—and finishing them with raspberry chimichurri. Platters of rice arrive with naan she and her mother made that morning.

Dessert brings homemade gulab jamun and cardamom sticky toffee cake, which Gurjar floods with caramel and finishes with flaky salt. Osamor shakes up another round of dirty chai espresso martinis. A little dancing gives way to conversation on the couch about the week ahead. It is, after all, a school night, and everyone is pleasantly overfed.

The meal mirrors the book’s central idea: a refusal to draw a neat line between East and West. East to East also makes room for what a cooking reel can leave out: the distance from home, building a career abroad, and watching parents grow older from afar, and other complexities that extend beyond homesickness.

In a rare chapter titled “Cultural Appropriation: Who Really Pays,” Gurjar asks who really benefits when food travels, and who gets left behind. It’s a question she and her creative peers have long discussed. To her, celebrating food’s ability to connect people also means asking who gets to tell its stories.

When I first met Gurjar two years ago at a party, I told her she’d taught me to make perfect short ribs, her Thai-inflected coconut braise. What I didn’t tell her was how rare it felt to see another Indian immigrant pursue a creative path, or how brave I thought she was. She has become that person for others, too, inspiring adventurous cooking and a willingness to take up space.

Some had made a career like hers seem out of reach. “I had experiences where people made me feel it wasn’t an option for me,” Gurjar laments. “Brown people, we’re often scared to dream big because we’ve never been permitted to. And I’m in a phase of life when I’m not saying no to anything.”

Asked about the highlight of her career, Gurjar doesn’t have to look backward. She sips her chai espresso martini and says, “I think I’m living it right now.”
