School meals decoded: What’s really on the lunch tray

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

The menu in your child’s cafeteria isn’t set by chance. It’s the result of a multi-year federal process, and you can weigh in on how it changes.

By Lauren Ramakrishna Updated Nov. 23, 2026

This article is sponsored by Center for Science in the Public Interest (CSPI). Thank you for supporting the brands that support Motherly and mamas.

In my son’s school district, entering middle school is a rite of passage. Sixth graders go to a new building, get their own locker, and experience freedoms they weren’t allowed in elementary school.

When my son came home from his first day of middle school, I couldn’t wait to talk about it. “What was the best part of your day?” I eagerly asked. “The food,” he replied.

I wasn’t surprised. Meals are a big deal for a growing boy. But it did make me wonder, what exactly will he eat for lunch every day?

Long gone are the days when I would pack my son a homemade lunch with a loving note tucked inside. In middle school, he wanted to walk into the cafeteria, review the day’s menu options, stand in line, and buy his lunch. I agreed to the plan because I wanted to support his thirst for independence. But I also hoped I hadn’t unwittingly given him permission to start a junk-food-only diet.

Luckily, there are guidelines schools are required to follow when it comes to serving our kids meals. Those rules aim to make lunchtime enjoyable and healthy for the 30 million students who eat school lunch every day in the United States through the National School Lunch Program (NSLP). Unsurprisingly, they’re a constant work in progress.

The U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA) sets federal requirements for school meals served through the NSLP and the School Breakfast Program. The rules attempt to align school food offerings with nutrition standards outlined in the Dietary Guidelines for Americans (DGA), the official dietary guidance published by the USDA and the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) every five years. When the DGA gets an update, school nutrition standard changes will follow, as required by law.

Schools are currently implementing changes developed following the release of the 2020-2025 DGA. In April 2024, the Food and Nutrition Administration (FNA, formerly Food and Nutrition Service) , a government agency responsible for administering domestic nutrition programs, like the NSLP, issued a rule requiring schools to make changes to foods served at school, including:

These updates join a comprehensive list of nutrition standards for school breakfasts and lunches that outline weekly serving requirements for fruits, vegetables, grains, meats and meat alternatives, and milk, as well as calorie minimums and maximums, and limits on saturated fat.

The USDA and HHS released the 2025-2030 DGA on January 7, 2026, triggering the next wave of school nutrition reviews in preparation for new recommendations and requirements. The USDA will compare the new DGA guidelines against existing school nutrition requirements, then draft a proposed rule on which schools, state agencies, advocates, industry experts, and consumer advocacy groups, like Center for the Science in the Public Interest, can provide feedback. The USDA will read and respond to comments, sometimes changing planned provisions in response to the feedback received, then publish its final rule.

At face value, this may seem like a good thing.

The USDA and HHS appoints a Dietary Guidelines Advisory Committee (DGAC) every five years to serve as an independent, expert panel of scientists and researchers in nutrition, health and medicine. The committee spends years reviewing current nutrition science to write a scientific report that’s typically used as a foundational resource for new dietary guidelines. DGAs have historically followed the vast majority of the DGAC’s recommendations. But, as CSPI notes, USDA and HHS rejected more than half of the DGAC’s expert recommendations. Instead, the agencies, according to CSPI,  “hand-picked authors whose work aligned with beef, dairy, and protein supplement industry interests and the administration’s preconceived policy goals.”

This group of hand-picked authors created a new “Scientific Foundation for Dietary Guidelines for Americans,” which includes contradictory advice, recommending folks stick to prior, science-based saturated fat limits, while suggesting high-fat foods like whole milk and beef tallow are part of a healthy diet.

CSPI has raised other concerns about the 2025-2030 DGA, arguing that it gives harmful guidance to emphasize animal protein; advises people to consume double or more of the scientifically-backed recommendation for daily protein; reduces clarity around how people can eat a balanced diet within vegetable and protein categories; and adopts an inverted food pyramid that doesn’t accurately visually represent the dietary recommendations found within the text of the guidelines.

As in cycles past, schools expect the USDA to use the most recent DGA as a guide to propose changes to school nutrition standards. It’s unclear exactly what changes will be put forth, but there will likely be a mix of nutrition wins and losses. It’s possible the new standards will reflect evidence-based nutrition guidance like increasing whole grains, protein, and whole fruit, and cutting back on added sugars, sodium, and refined grains in school meals.

However, it’s also likely the new proposed standards may stray from evidence-based guidance by calling for decreased vegetable variety and overall fiber intake due to a deprioritization of plant-based proteins and an overemphasis on high-fat foods like whole milk and beef (rather than a variety of protein sources).  For example, students could see more protein, like eggs and yogurt, at breakfast but miss out on a rainbow of vegetables and inclusive protein options like beans and tofu at lunch. Zooming out, meals lower in added sugars could be prioritized, while progress made on reducing saturated fat and sodium in school meals will be lost.

The best way to inform what your kids get on their breakfast and lunch trays at school is to make your voice heard. After proposing changes to nutrition standards, the government will open a public comment period where you can share your opinions about what should be included or excluded from school meals. When the proposed rule finally comes out, CSPI will make it easy to share your opinion through model comments and templates that will allow parents to comment directly on the proposed rule. Stay tuned for next steps by signing up for CSPI advocacy emails.

The regulatory red tape may seem overwhelming. That’s why non-profits like CSPI give parents tools to learn about school nutrition standards and enact change.
