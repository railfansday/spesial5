Finally, Data on Super-High Carbohydrate Doses

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Science often moves very slowly. But sometimes, when the real-world stakes are high enough, it zips along at an impressive pace. That’s what’s currently happening with carbohydrate fueling for endurance sports. It’s a hot topic with lots of open questions, and a big new study offers some useful—though not entirely final—answers.

Over the last half-dozen years, top cyclists, runners, triathletes, and others have been pushing back the limits of what was thought possible. The existing science said you couldn’t make use of more than 90 grams of carbohydrate per hour, but elite athletes are now routinely using 120 grams per hour and, in some cases, far beyond that. At last year’s New York Marathon, Joe Klecker reportedly downed 175 grams per hour—the sports gel equivalent of eating nine servings of cooked pasta during the race.

Whether these carb megadoses actually improve performance is a different question. So far, the research has been equivocal. And critics like the South African sports scientist Tim Noakes have claimed that big carb doses don’t help at all, at least as a source of fuel for your muscles. Noakes argues that a mere 10 grams an hour is enough to keep your blood sugar from crashing, which in his view is all that matters.

When I wrote about this debate last year, I thought it would be straightforward to dig up “dose-response” data showing what dose of carbs led to the best endurance performances. To my surprise, though, the existing evidence was sparser than I expected. What we needed, I concluded, was fresh dose-response data spanning the whole range from 10 to 120 grams and beyond, featuring well-trained athletes in a realistic test of endurance performance. That’s what a new study, from an international team of scientists led by James Morton of Liverpool John Moores University in Britain and published in Medicine and Science in Sports and Exercise, provides.

Morton and his colleagues recruited eight serious cyclists, and (somehow) convinced them to do a challenging cycling test on four different occasions. They rode for three hours at a pace just below lactate threshold, including three five-minute surges in the final hour. Then they launched immediately into a ride to exhaustion at a faster pace, simulating the all-out finish of a race, lasting on average about five minutes.

They did these rides while consuming drinks containing a total of 10, 90, 120, or 150 grams of carbohydrate per hour. The drinks, based on a formulation from Science in Sport, contained a ratio of 1 part maltodextrin to 0.8 parts fructose; the mix of carbohydrates helped maximize absorption from the gut into the bloodstream.

Throughout the rides, the scientists took regular blood tests and monitored respiratory gases in order to calculate exactly how much carbohydrate and fat the cyclists were burning.

The more carbs the cyclists drank, the more they were able to burn—but only up to 120 grams per hour. You can’t really see the data points for 150 grams per hour because they’re virtually identical to 120 grams per hour.

Here it’s quite clear that consuming 90 or 120 grams per hour produced better results than just 10 grams per hour. In fact, you’ll notice that there are only four data points above zero in the 10 grams per hour group: four of the subjects gave up in exhaustion before they even got to the end of the three-hour ride.

This is obviously not a good look for the “you only need 10 grams of carbs per hour to keep your blood sugar up” theory. There’s a caveat to this: the ride with 10 grams was framed as a “familiarization ride” to get the riders used to the protocol rather than as a control group—so unlike the other three rides, it was neither blinded nor randomized. The subjects knew they were only getting 10 grams of carbs per hour, so it’s reasonable to assume that they expected to perform poorly.

That said, the blood sugar (i.e. glucose) data tells a clear (and placebo-proof) story:

The average blood sugar levels in the three high-carb rides were 4.6, 4.6, and 4.8 mmol/L; in the lower-carb ride, it was 3.8 mmol/L (68 mg/dL), which is considered mild hypoglycemia. Notably, these low levels are already apparent just 30 minutes into the ride. There’s still a big gap between 10 and 90 grams per hour, but it seems clear that 10 grams isn’t enough to maintain blood sugar levels, at least for trained athletes like those in the study.

Interestingly, performance on 150 grams per hour was also poorer, despite the fact that it produced identical carb burning compared to 120 grams per hour. There are a few possible reasons for this, which we’ll get into below. But if you take the data at face value, the researchers calculate that for these particular riders, doing this particular endurance test, the optimal dose is 107 grams per hour.

There’s a mystery here. Consuming 120 or 150 grams per hour produced identical levels of carb burning. So where did the other 30 go? It’s possible it was converted to glucose or glycogen (a form of carbohydrate storage in the body) or even fat.

Or it’s possible it was left sloshing around in the stomach, undigested. If that was the case, it might cause digestive issues that would explain why performance was worse with 150 grams. The data didn’t show any significant differences in reported GI symptoms between rides, but with just eight subjects it’s possible such subtleties would be missed.

There’s another intriguing clue. Check out the data below, which shows the individual data on how much “exogenous” carbohydrate each subject burned during the third hour of each ride. (“Exogenous” means carbohydrate from the sports drink, rather than from the body’s internal stores. The sports drink carbs were labeled with a special isotope tracer so the scientists could tell the different carb sources apart.)

Look at the rider at the bottom. In the three rides (90, 120, and 150 grams per hour), he burned 1.41, 1.45, and 1.46 grams of carbs per minute. In other words, drinking more carbs made no difference to how many carbs he was able to burn. This suggests he wasn’t able to digest them. Tellingly, he was also the subject who reported the highest level of GI symptoms in the 150 g trial, and had the biggest drop in performance in that trial.

In comparison, some of the other subjects were able to take advantage of more carbs. One subject went from 1.56 grams per minute in the 90 g trial to 2.08 and then 2.30 grams per minute in the 150 g trial. That might—speculatively!—translate into a big metabolic advantage at the higher consumption rates for this individual.

So the simple final answer to the great carb debate is: maybe 107 grams per hour is the new magic number. But the real not-so-final answer is that the best dose is highly individual. Ever since the carb revolution kicked off, sports nutritionists have been warning us that we shouldn’t automatically expect to benefit from the super-high intakes of athletes like Tadej Pogačar or Joe Klecker. This data tells us we might not even get the same benefits as our own training partners—and for now, the only way to find your sweet spot is to experiment.
