PCT Week 20: Home Stretch

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

📍 Mile 2,594.3 | 🥾 5.88 km | ⛰️ +339m / -55m

Rain had gently drizzled all night, and was still going when I woke up. I was eager to make it to town ASAP, in case it worsened, so packed quickly and was on the way as soon as I could. It was only five miles to Rainy Pass – apparently, a very apt name – and they disappeared quickly.

Unfortunately, getting a hitch didn’t come nearly as quickly. Cars zoomed past us, and we had to laugh at the number of times the drivers pointedly looked at the mountains on the opposite side of the road, as if they didn’t see us. I began waving to every car that passed and finally we had some luck: a van passed us, but turned around and came back to give us a lift. The driver turned out to have once attempted to hike the PCT, but had broken his foot on day three. Rough go. He was heading to Winthrop, which was further away than Mazama but had a bigger supermarket, so that suited us perfectly. And, by pure happenstance, Catch, Sabretooth and Shakedown happened to be trying to hitch there right as we were driving past; the driver stopped and let them pile into the back with me.

This resupply was a little different than usual. It was our final one of the PCT! After eating instant potatoes and tuna for literally every trail dinner since Julian in the desert, I’d finally reached saturation point and had to switch to ramen. We all bought a few of our favourite snacks and treats, and then had a fun little debate over near the alcohol about what we’d need for the terminus. In the end, we all got some form of bubbly, and a few of us grabbed some extras as well. Then we went in search of a town meal.

After a delicious burger at Three Fingered Jack’s Saloon, we were just preparing to try our luck hitching to Mazama when the heavens suddenly opened and rain bucketed down. It was torrential. Immediately we decided to go and grab a coffee at the bakery and wait it out. Of course, as soon as we had our coffees in hand, the rain stopped. When we did make it around to hitching, we didn’t have to wait long and got a lift with one of the first cars that drove by.

The hostel was practically crawling with hikers who, like us, were waiting out the bad weather but planning to start their terminus push tomorrow. In some ways it was overwhelming. I didn’t want to spend energy making new friends when we almost certainly were never going to see them again – a lot of people were heading up to Harts Pass, whereas we were going back to Rainy. But on the other hand it was nice to see a few familiar faces pass through. Apparently Sass and I had just missed Pieces, who’d left that morning. But Alice was still there, and then some hikers we’d been leapfrogging since the desert swung by to say hi after bagging the terminus. More and more hikers trickled in throughout the afternoon as we lazed on the outdoor couches beneath the dry marquee, enjoying the gas-powered fire at our feet and cooing at a puppy belonging to one of the trail angels helping out at the hostel.

📍 Mile 2,619.5 | 🥾 42.15 km | ⛰️ +1,777m / -1,235m

We’d signed up for a shuttle to Rainy Pass, but there were so many hikers heading back to trail this morning that we missed out on the first run. So we decided to try our luck hitching, in case that would get us there faster than the second run. We left early, hoping to beat other hikers who had the same idea, but when we reached the road we discovered we weren’t that early after all. There was no point in so many of us trying to hitch at once, so Sabretooth and I volunteered to do a coffee run while we waited our turn.

Coffees in hand, we were just leaving the courtyard of the Mazama bakery, when I tripped on a crack in the concrete. Before I knew it I was sprawled on the ground, embarrassed by the sympathetic “ooh”s from onlookers who’d seen me tumble. It was quite a fall. I had an open wound the size of an Aussie 5 cent piece on my palm, and I’d scratched up my legs pretty badly, too. It was a little ironic that I’d managed to sustain my worst PCT injury in town rather than on trail. I had, at least, managed to save my coffee – the cup was a little bent, but the contents were fine. But I’d been carrying Catch’s coffee, too, and it hadn’t survived. As I cleaned myself up Sabretooth ordered him another, and then I very carefully picked my way across the concrete and back to the road. By the time we got there the boys had gotten a hitch, but luckily the first car that saw us stopped and delivered us all the way up to Rainy Pass.

It was significantly colder at the pass. Sass and Catch were running laps of the car park to stay warm while they waited for us. We got on trail quickly, climbing higher still and yet naively hoping the day would warm up. Instead, we walked into lightly falling snow. It was magical, coating the ground and the trees in a light dusting of white. Then we arrived at the larches, which are conifers with needles that turn an indescribably gorgeous gold. Unfortunately, Cutthroat Pass was pretty socked in when we arrived, but we were in good spirits when we hit the 2600 milestone – only 50 or so to go! And only a mile or so later, the clouds began to disappear, giving way to a clear azure sky. Snow lay across the mountaintops as if someone had draped a cotton sheet.

We ran into a group of five hikers, overtaking them. They soon caught up with us again and we offered to let them go ahead, but for some reason they decided to tailgate us instead. So we stopped for lunch to put some distance between us. And it turned out to be a good call; a few rays of sunlight warmed us as we gazed around the mountains, taking in the colours of early winter. And when we set off again we soon reentered forest, so we were glad we’d stopped when we had a view.

The walking was nice and easy for several hours, but the final few miles of the day were a climb. It wasn’t a difficult grade, and we were soon above the trees and enjoying the mountain views once again, but I grew tired and was relieved when we made it to the top. Sunset seemed to be kicking off earlier than usual and the colours were unreal. The fading light turned the mountains indigo, which seemed to make the larches glow.

We arrived at camp, pitched quickly, and agreed we’d all be eating dinner from the comfort of our quilts, inside our respective tents. But that didn’t stop us chatting for an hour as we ate.

📍 Mile 2,649 | 🥾 49.9 km | ⛰️ +1,761m / -1,888m

I slept terribly. It was positively freezing last night, and we all woke up to frost on our tents. And to make matters worse, my inflatable sleeping pad was barely lasting for two hours – so I’d had to blow it up constantly throughout the night. I was glad when morning came and I could get moving to stay warm.

I’d been tempted to night hike last night, just because the weather was definitely closing in and I wanted to make it to Canada and back out quickly. But when we’d seen the trail continued along a ridgeline all thoughts of night hiking had disappeared. And this morning I was very pleased with that choice. The trail wrapped around the mountaintops, beckoning me forward into the beautiful day. The larches, interspersed with autumnal ground cover and evergreen trees, popped vibrantly against the pale blue sky.

We made it to Harts Pass mid-morning, and with the sun properly shining now took the opportunity to dry out our tents before setting off on the final push to the border. We all walked together, chatting happily. And then we started to come across PCT hikers who’d tagged the terminus and were now almost done with the 30-mile stretch back to Harts. Vibes on all sides were high; we were excited to be nearing the end, while those heading back were clearly relieved to be almost done and on their way to civilisation.

I was glad we’d waited out the weather; the mountains were gorgeous once again. A light layer of snow remained in the shadows of the peaks, in the little spaces that never saw sun. We crested Woody Pass just before sunset and were treated to stunning golden hour views of far-off mountain ranges.

Not long afterwards we ran into a group of hikers we knew who had just tagged the terminus. Catching up with them was great fun, although it did put us behind schedule. The sun was sinking rapidly. We pushed on quickly, but progress soon stalled when we arrived at a ridgeline where the blazing orange sun was setting to our left, and a glowing moon was rising on our right. How could we not stop and take it in? It did mean that our last 30 minutes or so descending to Hopkins Lake were in the dark, but it had been worth it. We’d heard rumours that a lot of people were already camped there, and that tent sites were scarce. But we found enough pitches for the five of us pretty quickly, and went to bed feeling like kids the night before Christmas.

📍 Mile 2,636.2 | 🥾 44.58 km | ⛰️ +1,666m / -1,731m

Today was the big day: after four and a half months, and hiking well over the 2,650 miles the trail officially covers (thanks to all our alternates and side quests), we were finally going to reach Canada! It was only 7 miles away. But after we reached the border, we’d have to begin the 30-mile backtrack to Harts Pass; sadly, walking into Canada is no longer allowed. So we set off at 7am as usual, planning to enjoy our time at the terminus, while hopefully still getting some good miles in this afternoon. As we left camp, a pika meeped at us. We took it as a blessing from the trail gods to continue.

The trail was a pretty gentle downhill for the first four miles. We alternated between comfortable silence and brief chats, pausing only once: to pretend to be bears in front of a wildlife camera. Then we arrived at a 3 mile section of blowdowns that had been much fearmongered on FarOut. We thought the rumours must have been overblown, but the deadfall turned out to be kind of gnarly. On the rest of the PCT the blowdowns have at least had their limbs sawed off, but these ones hadn’t been given that treatment and tough branches poked everywhere. I was trying to be careful not to scratch my legs again – I’d done enough of that in NorCal – but in the end I did manage to crack my patella into a branch, right over the top of an old scab from NorCal. It hurt pretty badly for a few seconds, but at least I’d look hardcore in our terminus photo.

We arrived at the top of a short series of switchbacks, and caught our first glimpse of a line of felled trees in the dense forest: that was the border. We could hear other people already at the terminus celebrating. This was it! Just as we finished the last switchback and were on the home stretch Sabretooth, who’d been in the lead, suddenly stopped. She started combing through her pack. We all realised why: we’d picked up a wooden clothespeg at Mazama and had been secretly hiding it in each others’ gear over the last few days. Whoever had it on them at the terminus would have to do a shoey. Out of someone else’s shoe. Satisfied that it wasn’t on her, Sabretooth picked up her bag and we charged towards the terminus, where we were greeted with cheers from the hikers already there.

The other hikers were already preparing to head out, so we wasted no time making them our photographers for group photos at the terminus. As well as a couple of videos of us first popping bubbles, and then doing shoeys. We did a play-by-play of the peg’s movements and discovered that it was with Catch. He took his punishment shoey well. It was only when the hustle and bustle finally died down that we took a seat and had time to take it in. It was a huge accomplishment, and yet it didn’t feel real. Or like the end. It did feel absolutely freezing. We were clearly in a katabatic zone – I’d been perfectly warm when we left camp this morning.

We spent about two hours there, enjoying each other’s company and that of a couple more hikers who arrived shortly after we did. But at the two hour mark the cold really started to get to us, and we prepared to leave. “Don’t you want to hear my speech?” Catch asked. I couldn’t believe I’d forgotten. We’d been badgering him for days to prepare a motivational speech for us. He climbed atop the terminus and we all looked up at him expectantly. Then he began to recite the lyrics to Miley Cyrus’s ‘The Climb’, with a couple of small changes to make it more appropriate: “It kinda was about how fast we got there / It kinda was about what’s waiting on the other side”.

We left in good spirits – the early morning alcohol probably helped with that – and began the climb back up to Hopkins Lake. We synched up playback of our hype playlist, which helped move us along. The pika meeped at us once again as we passed, which we took as a good sign. At the lake we paused for lunch, but not for long. The day had started with a beautiful blue sky, but had turned overcast, cold and windy.

It still didn’t feel quite real that we were done. Probably because we were still hiking. It was nice to have a few miles to walk and reflect and ease ourselves out of trail life. But we all agreed it would’ve been nice if this 30-mile section had been at least 10 miles shorter.

We crossed paths with hikers heading up to the terminus and made sure to fist bump them and cheer them on. Then we started running into familiar faces we hadn’t seen since NorCal, which was a nice surprise. But it meant that we arrived at the campsite we’d chosen in the dark, since we’d spent so much time chatting.

📍 Mile 2,625.2 | 🥾 22.85 km | ⛰️ +815m / -714m

There were only 11 miles between us and our final steps on the PCT. But rain was forecast, and we were keen to get out of it ASAP, so we set off at 7am as usual. The miles practically flew by as we chatted easily all morning, planning all the food we were going to eat now that trail was over. It drizzled lightly, on and off, and the clouds hung low around us so views were non-existent. We spotted a pair of hikers ahead of us and made it our mission to overtake them, which we finally accomplished just before we arrived at a trailhead. We decided to walk the road from there back to Harts Pass, since it would make for faster progress.

Just as we arrived at Harts and signed the last trail log, the rain really started to fall. We’d hoped the ranger would be in so we could call for a ride – there was no mobile service – but we were out of luck. There was only one car in the parking lot, and in this weather we didn’t hold too much hope for getting a hitch from it. So, since a note on the ranger’s door said there was service at a campsite a mile or so away, we decided to head there. It was better than sitting still and growing increasingly cold and wet in the rain.

Arriving at the campsite, though, none of us had any usable signal. Luckily, some day hikers there offered to let us use their phone; but we couldn’t get through to anyone. They said they could probably squeeze a couple of us in when they finished their hike, which we were very grateful for. We also had a lovely surprise, running into a group of hikers we knew well: in fact, it was Shakedown and Sabretooth’s earlier trail family. We spent a good while catching up, but standing still I grew increasingly cold, and eventually had to say goodbye so I could get moving to warm up.

Thankfully, Shakedown managed to convince some other people out for a day hike to allow us all to squeeze into their sedan. The driver turned out to be a volunteer SAR helicopter pilot, so we really lucked out. With three other people and a dog already in the car, though, it was a tight squeeze and we had to surrender our packs to the weather, strapping them to the roof racks. I shivered the whole way down the mountain, cold from the wind and rain. When the driver mentioned they’d been planning to stop at the Mazama Bakery for a coffee, we encouraged the break and gratefully paid for their coffees and ordered our own.

Not long afterwards we were being dropped at the doorstep of the Airbnb we’d booked in Winthrop, where we left our packs and went in search of food. And a celebratory beer, of course. Then we headed to the supermarket to grab some supplies for the next couple of days in town, before making ourselves at home in the Airbnb. Too lazy to cook, dinner was an easy spread of cheese and biscuits, as we cosied up in front of the fireplace to watch movies, introducing Shakedown to the delights of the Twilight franchise.

We slept in as long as our body clocks would allow, and then cooked a delicious big fry up for breakfast. It was a gorgeous, warm, blue-sky day outside, so I went for a bit of a wander around town to take advantage and keep my legs moving at least a little. When I got back we had a lunch of tomato soup and grilled cheese – which we’d fantasised about yesterday.

Then we began a room-to-room beer crawl, which we’d also dreamed up yesterday. Catch kicked us off with another motivational speech – this time, the Aussie classic, ‘Get on the Beers’ by Mashton Kutcher. In the end we only moved between the outdoor space at the front of the house, and the outdoor space at the back, since it was such a nice day it felt wrong to be inside. Slow and steady was definitely the name of the game, and we made it to dinnertime with plenty of beer to spare. As all good dinners should, we started with dessert: s’mores – my first ever, but certainly not my last. Dinner was some easy, delicious homemade pizzas, and we settled into the couch once more, to continue the Harry Potter marathon we’d started at White Pass.

This morning was much the same as yesterday morning – a relative sleep in, a fry up for breakfast – but the air was tinged with a little sadness. Today was our last day together as a group. When we’d finished breakfast and had packed up, reluctant to say goodbye just yet we headed down the main street to grab a coffee, chatting about which book would be our next book club pick, and trying to figure out where and when we could do a reunion hike.

At midday, though, it was time to go our separate ways. Shakedown and Sabretooth were heading to Vancouver, to continue their big year of travel. Catch was going with them, to catch a flight home for a family event. Sass and I were heading to Leavenworth, and then who knows where, as we passed a week waiting for Catch to meet us in Bend – the three of us were going back to fill in the miles we’d missed when we skipped around the smoke from the Austin fire. We hugged goodbye for now, and split in different directions.

Sass and I headed out of town and tried to hitch. Our backup plan was the bus, but it didn’t arrive for a couple of hours. We got lucky quickly: a pickup offered to take us to Twisp. We discovered that there was an earlier bus we could take from there, which connected conveniently with other buses all the way to Leavenworth. We decided to take it; neither of us had the energy to chat with hitches for a couple of hours. We wanted some time to be sad and morose about finishing the trail and saying goodbye to our friends.

The buses ran smoothly and we were dropped in Leavenworth in the late afternoon. There were lots of events on in town this weekend – including Oktoberfest, which was half the reason we’d come – so we made our way to the KOA in the hopes they’d still have a section for PCT hikers. We were in luck, and soon had our tents set up. Then we wandered into town to take advantage of the free beer Icicle Brewing offered to PCT hikers, and enjoyed people watching for a while before heading back to the campsite for an early night.
