Reflections on my PCT Thru-Hike

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

It’s been about 2 months since I completed the Pacific Crest Trail. I’ve put off writing a post to wrap up this experience, maybe because the whole thing still feels a bit like a dream. Procrastinating a wrap up post means it’s not over, right?

I look back at my time on the Pacific Crest Trail with an overwhelming feeling of accomplishment. I miss the simplicity of life on trail, I miss the people, I miss the mountain air… but believe it or not, I don’t really miss hiking (yet).

Don’t get me wrong, I loved thru-hiking and will always have love for the Pacific Crest Trail. But thru-hiking is hard! You meet your limit of what feels challenging, then push the threshold further and further. I thought at some point it might feel easy, but that was never the case. Instead, my definition of easy and hard just shifted. 20 miles in the beginning felt like such an achievement, and at the end a 20 mile day felt like a nearo.

After getting off-trail, it took a while to feel well rested. My hips were sore and my feet were throbbing for weeks. The feeling is just starting to come back to my numb toes. I know I’ll be itching to get back to backpacking soon, but not quite yet. For now I’ll keep resting, celebrating this accomplishment, and looking back with nothing but joy.

Hiking in a group with the same 12+ people for nearly 2,000 miles was a unique experience. I fell into this group somewhat intentionally, but stuck together somewhat accidentally. Early into the Sierra, I had asked a few hikers if they wanted to link up and go over a few of the more technical passes together. Over time, we happened to fall into a natural rhythm, hiking similar paces and distances spread out over just a few miles, and ending up in towns on the same days.

Having so many people around meant I always felt safe. Someone was always nearby if I was in a precarious situation crossing waist-high rivers or traversing slippery snow banks. Yet, being spread out amongst a few miles allowed me plenty of time to hike solo, and the flexibility to cover however many miles I wanted to, and camp wherever I wanted to without the pressure of anyone else’s schedule. Then linking up with everyone in town, we were able to save costs with shared rooms and maximize on fun side quests.

Other than our decision to complete the victory lap from Hart’s Pass to the Northern Terminus together, there was never any tribal council-style group decision making. The bubble ebbed and flowed as other hikers figured out their personal needs, sped up, slowed down, had to get off trail for x,y,z, and I never felt pressure to go along with what others wanted for the sake of sticking together. I don’t know if that was an experience unique to this group and the reason why such a large group was able to stick together for so long, or if there is a similar experience between bubbles ahead of and behind us. Regardless, I think the positivity and fluidity of our little bubble is what made the massive undertaking of a thru-hike feel more attainable and kept it so so fun.

Hiking in a big group isn’t for everyone, but for me, the people I met are the reason this journey was as special as it was. I started the trail with a very individualist mindset. Looking back at my introduction post, I confidently listed the reasons why my “why” was for me. I thought hiking the trail solo would be the ultimate proof of independence.

There’s a lot of online rhetoric about hiking your own hike and praise for breaking up with your tramily in pursuit of a solo journey. And all of this holds some truth. I do agree, there is a lot of personal growth and development to be had by making your own decisions completely isolated in nature. However, I personally found that making my own decisions paired with the support and encouragement of a community around me challenged my individual-minded perspective, and enabled greater personal growth than a solo journey ever could.

It showed me that independence and community can coexist and even be harmonious when the two are in sync. This is a lesson I’ll carry with me forever.

So to Dromedary, Posh Spice, Flash, Birdman, Radio, Little Wagon, Geezer, Rolodex, Pole Dancer, Buenas Noches, Bear, Rascal, Straight Up, Juggernaut, Bubbles, Turtle, Scratch & Sniff, and everyone else I crossed paths with for a week or a day or a mile, you all will always have a special place in my heart.

Well I’m still not entirely sure. And I’ve learned that’s okay. I’ve spent the last two months doing a bit of traveling, a lot of resting, going back to work, settling into a new routine, and thinking about the answer to that very question.

My family purchased a little cabin in Idyllwild, so I’ll be spending a lot of time in my favorite trail town and may even bring a bit of trail magic to next year’s hikers. My next obvious hiking goal is to go back to Washington and fill in the fire closure miles I had to skip from Snoqualmie Pass to Hart’s Pass. I’m easing back into running and climbing, and want to set goals to work towards within those sports. And of course, I have a looooong list of places I want to see and bucket list trails I want to hike.

But if I’ve learned anything from my time on the Pacific Crest Trail, it’s that chasing goals while letting go of rigid plans and expectations is guaranteed to lead to adventure beyond my wildest dreams. I’m excited to let go and see how the next adventures unfold. This won’t be the last you hear of Gadget.

If you’re even contemplating the decision whether or not to take on a thru-hike, do it. Hiking the Pacific Crest Trail was the best decision I’ve ever made. I’m forever changed.
