Wanting a digital detox may reveal something unexpected about you

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

A new study explores why people want a digital detox and how their attitudes relate to self-control, mindfulness, and screen habits.

Eighty-one percent of people feel positive about the idea of putting down their phones and stepping away from screens for a while.

But here’s the twist: that desire to unplug has almost nothing to do with being addicted to technology.

People who score high on wanting a digital detox aren’t the ones doom-scrolling at 2 a.m. They’re the mindful, self-controlled types who’ve simply decided their attention is worth protecting.

A team from Goethe University Frankfurt and the University of Bath has developed and validated the first psychometric scale for measuring attitudes toward digital detox.

The Attitude toward Digital Detox (ADD) scale is a seven-item questionnaire. Participants rate their agreement with statements like “Reducing my digital activities improves my mental health.”

Rated on a 1-to-7 scale, the measure captures how favorably someone views the idea of temporarily dialing down their digital consumption.

Digital detox, as the researchers define it, is fundamentally different from technology avoidance.

Avoiding technology means permanently rejecting a specific device or platform because it’s too complex, too inflexible, or not worth the switching costs.

A digital detox attitude is something else entirely. It’s a conscious, temporary inclination toward balance.

The development process was exhaustive. The participants were a mix of US adults recruited online and Central European university students.

Across seven studies, the team started by asking 157 participants to generate statements describing both attitudes toward and behaviors around digital detox. That produced 628 candidate items.

Consumer behavior experts from universities including Tübingen, Bocconi, Cologne, Passau, and St. Gallen then rated each item for representativeness and comprehensibility.

The pool shrank to 72, then 27 after content validity screening.

Finally, it went down to seven items after exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses on 400 participants.

The resulting scale showed strong internal consistency and only weak correlation with social desirability.

People weren’t just saying what sounded virtuous.

Study 4 tested the scale against a web of related psychological constructs using 382 US adults. The correlations painted a clear picture  and some of it was genuinely unexpected.

Self-control correlated positively with ADD scores. So did mindfulness and holding a growth mindset about intelligence.

People who believe their cognitive abilities can develop were more favorable toward stepping away from screens.

All of that tracks with the idea that digital detox attitudes emerge from a proactive appraisal process. People consciously evaluate their digital consumption and decide they’d benefit from less of it.

What didn’t correlate was more revealing. Perceived stress showed no significant relationship with ADD.

Neither did pathological internet use or smartphone addiction. These findings suggest that the desire to unplug extends beyond people experiencing problematic technology use.

“Being able to reliably measure that attitude opens the door to understanding what makes people more receptive to stepping back from technology, how those attitudes develop and whether they ultimately translate into changes in digital behavior,” said study co-author Lisa Eckmann.

In a pre-registered study with 1,583 US adults, participants chose a video genre they enjoyed and then alternated between watching clips and doing word-search puzzles.

Afterward, they could freely choose to watch another video or do another puzzle. People scoring higher on the ADD scale spent significantly less time watching each video.

However, ADD scores didn’t predict whether participants chose another video or another puzzle.

More favorable attitudes were associated with shorter viewing times, suggesting a connection with how long people engaged rather than whether they started.

Two additional validation studies tested whether the scale could distinguish between groups with predictably different orientations toward screens.

Members of Reddit communities focused on reducing screen time or using paper journals, such as r/nosurf and r/bulletjournal, scored significantly higher on the ADD scale than members of technology-focused communities such as r/ebooks and r/SmartThings.

The gap was large: 5.33 versus 3.47 on the seven-point scale.

Among Central European university students surveyed during the pandemic, those who preferred face-to-face teaching scored markedly higher on the detox scale.

And their attitudes predicted real behavior. Higher ADD scores correlated with actually attending more lectures in person over the winter term.

The researchers grounded the scale in Lazarus and Folkman’s transactional model of stress and coping.

The framework proposes that when people repeatedly experience digital consumption as taxing, they begin to view disengagement as a way to cope.

Over time, those experiences may develop into a more stable attitude.

That’s what the ADD scale measures: the crystallized disposition, not the momentary frustration.

One practical implication is immediate. People who score high on digital device usage time score lower on the ADD scale.

That negative correlation was statistically significant. The attitude does seem to connect, however loosely, to how much time people actually spend online.

But the researchers are careful to note that the attitude-behavior gap in digital consumption is well documented. Screen-time tracking apps reliably raise awareness of heavy usage.

They rarely produce sustained behavior change.

The scale opens several doors. Marketers could use it to identify consumers receptive to intentional disengagement and design experiences accordingly.

Researchers can now investigate whether there’s a subjective tipping point where digital consumption tips from manageable into overload.

And in a landscape increasingly shaped by AI-driven personalization and algorithmically curated feeds designed to maximize engagement, the question of who wants to opt out – and why – is only going to get more pressing.

The study is published in the journal Psychology & Marketing.
