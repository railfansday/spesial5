Arctic winter sea ice is being overestimated by climate models

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

Ancient algae preserved in Arctic seafloor mud suggest climate models overestimate winter sea ice, pointing to less ice in the decades ahead.

Arctic sea ice loss usually gets told as a summer story, counting down to the first ice-free summer. Yet climate models disagree most about winter ice.

Together, they simulate nearly a million square kilometers too much March ice – about 386,000 square miles, close to Texas and New Mexico combined.

A new study used traces of algae buried in 6,000-year-old seafloor mud to grade 27 climate models.

The corrected projections show about a million square kilometers less winter ice than before, in every coming decade and under every emissions path.

Crystal Fu of the Scott Polar Research Institute led the work with Matthew Osman at the University of Cambridge, and Marco Aquino-López of CIMAT in Mexico.

The numbers are model projections re-weighted by evidence from the past, not new measurements of today’s ice.

In an interview with Earth.com, Fu compared the missing ice to a country.

“A million square kilometers is a huge amount of sea ice to go missing – that’s roughly 4x the size of the UK,” she said.

Winter ice matters because it’s a lid. Open water in the Arctic winter releases heat and moisture into the air.

That release has been linked to colder winters and heavier snow across Eurasia and North America. It has also been linked to shifts in storm tracks and extreme rain in the Mediterranean.

In March 2025, the Arctic’s annual maximum was the lowest on record.

Yet the models’ biggest disagreements and biggest errors fall in March, not September, and most of the testing has gone into summer.

Fu’s team wanted an exam the models couldn’t have been tuned to pass.

Between 5,000 and 7,000 years ago, a wobble in Earth’s orbit gave the Arctic more summer sunlight. The extra sunlight warmed the region and reduced its sea ice.

The extra heating was comparable in size to what future greenhouse gases are expected to add.

Algae that live inside sea ice leave a distinctive molecule behind, and algae that live in open water leave others.

The ratio of the two in a layer of seafloor mud records how much ice covered that spot. Fu’s team compiled 46 such records from sediment cores across the Arctic, the largest set of its kind.

“What really struck us was just how much information is preserved in tiny traces of algae buried in mud across the Arctic seafloor,” she said.

The team converted the 27 models’ ancient Arctic ice simulations into molecular ratios for comparison with the sediment records.

That let the models be compared with the cores on equal terms. Each model got a weight based on how well it matched, with related models sharing credit.

Weighted that way, the models’ average March ice for the recent past matched the satellite record. The bias of nearly a million square kilometers disappeared.

The range of disagreement shrank by about 6.3 million square kilometers (2.4 million square miles), or 85%. The remaining range was comparable to ordinary year-to-year swings.

The ancient records turned out to be the sharper test. Weighting by the mid-Holocene alone corrected the models more than weighting by 1950 to 1999 did.

The same weights applied to the models’ futures lowered the projected March ice by 4% to 6% for the 2090s, under low, middle and high emissions alike.

Fu noted that the cause of the ancient warming doesn’t change the lesson.

“It doesn’t matter if the warming is natural variation or caused by human activity: if it warms, sea ice is going to melt,” she said.

In the Barents Sea, the Greenland Sea and the Labrador Sea, the weighted maps put the winter ice edge farther north than the unweighted models do.

The shift reaches 5.5 degrees of latitude, about 380 miles (610 kilometers).

Asked by Earth.com where winter sea ice was most vulnerable, Osman pointed to the Barents Sea.

“The Barents Sea, north of Norway and Russia, is probably the place to watch most closely right now,” he said.

The Greenland and Labrador Seas, farther south, are the next places to watch, Osman said.

More ice loss there could affect marine wildlife, shipping, the Atlantic’s circulation and the stability of the Greenland ice sheet, he added.

The projections assume the ice will keep responding to warming the way it did 6,000 years ago and in the 20th century.

The weighting improved the combined projection, but it did not explain why individual models produced different amounts of ice.

Links between winter ice and weather farther south also need confirming. Under high emissions, the weighted models suggest late-winter snowfall over southeast Greenland may be underestimated by more than 30%.

Winter and spring rain in the Mediterranean may be twice what the standard models project, which would raise flood risk there.

Fu said exactly how the Arctic changes will affect Europe’s winters is still being studied.

Earlier work tied a weaker polar vortex and cold snaps to a bare Barents Sea, but those links still need verifying.

Osman said the bigger payoff lies beyond sea ice.

Decades of records of ancient climates can be paired with the right mathematics to test climate models and narrow their projections, he said.

The snowfall and rainfall estimates suggest that ancient climate records could help refine projections beyond sea ice, although those results still need further testing.

The full study was published in the journal Nature Geoscience.
