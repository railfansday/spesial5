Netflix’s ‘The Body’ Explores Queer Girlhood, Catholic Guilt, and the Teenage Search for Faith

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

“Confess!” yells a teen as she stares into a pack of wide-eyed Catholic schoolgirls.

“I made out with Katie’s dad,” one reveals. “I masturbate to Daddy Franklin!” another cries, a black cross still smudged on her forehead.

Welcome to The Body, a new Netflix series that recalls a holy trinity of inspirations: Carrie, Jennifer’s Body, and Heavenly Creatures. But while all three were reference points for the show’s star, Geena Meszaros, she also wants her character to stand alone. “I really wanted her to be her own thing,” she told VF in October.

“It was kismet,” Meszaros says of landing the role of Lisbeth. “I feel like she was my dream character to play before I even realized it.”

The Body is set in a Catholic girls school circa the early 2000s—complete with Juicy Couture pajamas, Yahoo! Messenger, teenage devilry, secret lesbian relationships, and plenty of Catholic guilt. Meszaros plays Lisbeth, a gawky reject whose desperation to join the dance team leads her to some fairly sinful decisions. On Halloween night, mutually pining co-captains Anya (Kristina Bogic) and Elise (Sara Boustany) stage a team initiation that goes wildly wrong—and maybe accidentally summons the devil.

Amid the chaos of their drunken houseparty, Lisbeth takes one for the team and buries a body—literally. Before long, high school devolves into Crucible-style mania as the girls start seeing visions.

Though showrunner Quinn Shephard has described The Body as a love letter to the female-centered horror of the early 2000s, and it arrives amid a similarly themed Carrie remake and the Nov. 20 return of Yellowjackets; the series might be less of a Y2K echo than something entirely new: a snapshot of Gen Z’s faith in 2026.

“There's a sense of faith in each other, that you need to have, that you just don't get if you're not part of a team,” Meszaros says about connecting with her costars.

Meszaros calls the show a mix of horror, comedy, and grittiness, all smushed together. There’s also a bit of romance overseen by Chala Hunter, the same intimacy coordinator who worked on Heated Rivalry. Meanwhile, a very pregnant Gabby Windey makes her acting debut as the team’s coach. (“I don’t know if I’ve met anyone who’s just so themselves all the time,” Meszaros says of her costar.).

Like Meszaros, almost everyone else in the cast is an up-and-comer: Kristina Bogic, who plays the popular high school sweetheart Anya; Sara Boustany, who plays her wicked best friend (and secret lover) Elise; Sofia Wylie, the doomed preacher’s niece; and Nnamdi Asomugha, an NFL cornerback turned actor who plays that very preacher (and the school’s principal): Father (Daddy) Franklin.

“I'm more of a spiritual person, but I think having faith in anything outside of yourself, whether that's God or whether that's something else, I think that's important,” Meszaros says.

In the months before filming, the team bonded just like their characters do: through dance team training, which sometimes lasted seven hours a day. “There was no pretending with the friendships on the show,” Meszaros says, even if the rehearsals were so intense that they cost her two toenails.

Unlike the other girls, though, Meszaros was playing a pariah. “I love a flawed human,” she says. “I love kind of being able to dive into the psyche of someone that maybe some people find awful.”

It wasn’t hard for Meszaros to feel empathy for Lisbeth. Her character has roots in a rural West Virginia congregation famed for their serpent handling rituals, where worshippers handle live snakes to prove their faith. Lisbeth’s Stephen King-esque arc kicks off when her dad dies and she and her mentally ill mother move to a new town to start over. Their bad luck follows.

On some level, Meszaros could relate. “My parents divorced when I was a baby, and so at my dad’s house, I was raised Christian, and at my mom’s house, she was just more spiritual,” she says. “I went through a dark period where I didn’t [believe]. I just kind of abandoned it all, which is very Lisbeth.” Her own experienced gave her a bit of a blueprint for the character: “I [kept] that feeling of like a hole left over from her world being turned upside down after her dad died, and just what it does to someone when you don't have some sort of faith, and you don’t really believe in anything. That desperation.”

Gen Z shares that desperation. Research shows that young people are plagued by mental health crises at higher rates than any previous generation. And although experts today differ on whether there’s an actual religious revival among Gen Z, there’s no denying that the way young people approach religion is rapidly evolving.

Meszaros’s feed is divided about the show, even before its premiere: “People are gonna love it or hate it,” she says, laughing. But she’s mostly just excited for people to see it, even though it’s coming out too late to inspire Body-themed Halloween costumes.

“I kind of wish people could be Lisbeth,” she says with a sigh. “But maybe next year.”
