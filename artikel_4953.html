Yoshitomo Nara Is Gen Z’s Favorite Artist—Even if They Don’t Know His Name

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

Four years ago, when Terrell Brown was a college student in Wheaton, Illinois, he spotted an oversized ashtray at a local Goodwill. It featured a cartoonlike illustration of a fierce-looking girl smoking a cigarette, along with the words “Too Young to Die.” It cost him $10. Brown recognized the imagery but didn’t know the name of the artist who created it. But when he posted a photo on Reddit, a reader commented, “Yoshitomo Nara is a really cool and famous artist, this is an amazing find!”

Brown, now a vintage reseller with almost 360,000 followers on TikTok, resold the ashtray, still in its original packaging, on eBay for $2,860. The episode says something about the powerful attraction—and afterlife—of Nara’s imagery. The artist’s signature figures—round-headed children with enormous eyes and expressions that oscillate between mischief, defiance, and melancholy—are deceptively simple. They often feature slogans like, “No nukes” and “Rock ’n’ roll is not dead.”

Fans include the world’s wealthiest—like Salesforce founder Marc Benioff, a longtime Yoshitomo Nara collector who supported a major retrospective of Nara’s work last year at London’s Hayward Gallery, co-organized by the Guggenheim Museum Bilbao and Museum Frieder Burda in Baden-Baden. His work has also become something of a fixture in homes featured in AD, from the Nara sculpture in Usher’s backyard to the painting hanging in Kris Jenner’s dining room. But his fans also include Gen Z’ers who love the characters without necessarily knowing much about the artist behind them. Nara’s artwork has attained a kind of cult currency, circulating through dorm rooms, online marketplaces, thrift stores, and social media, the characters becoming so familiar they seem to belong to everyone.

Stella McCartney x Yoshitomo Nara handbag

This week, as powerhouse art dealer David Zwirner unveils the first major solo exhibition of Nara’s work in New York in over a decade (Nara’s first at the gallery since leaving Pace last year), that duality is especially striking. The highly anticipated show is poised to be a museum-quality affair. Curated by Nara himself, it will feature paintings, drawings, and sculptures across the gallery’s two Chelsea locations. Yet the gallery will undoubtedly also draw a crowd of broader Nara fans—the public who adore his instantly recognizable characters, even if they know them best as decorative icons closer to the cultural territory of Hello Kitty or Miffy.

Now in his 60s and based in Tochigi Prefecture, about 100 miles north of Tokyo, Nara emerged on the international art scene in the 1990s. His creations’ simultaneously innocent and world-weary affect is central to their appeal. The characters “look cute on the surface,” says Takako Nagasawa, deputy chairwoman at Phillips auction house, “but if you read into them, they’re not cute, like the girl who has a knife hidden behind her back.” Many collectors, she notes, display Nara’s work in their children’s bedrooms. “I think that two-sidedness is the attraction, and very relevant now. We want to talk about what’s going on around the world, but we also don’t want to talk about it.”

Nara prizes accessibility to his art and has actively participated in extending his imagery into everyday products. While other artists have created characters that appear on merchandise, like Kaws’s Companion and Jeff Koons’s balloon dog, none have endured and proliferated quite like Nara’s, which have an underlying rebelliousness that speaks to disaffected youth across generations. Yoshiki, the frontman of the rock band X Japan, is working with Nara on the packaging for his Napa Valley wines, Y by Yoshiki, adorned with the artist’s characters. Stella McCartney, a close friend of Nara’s, has previewed her third limited-edition collaboration with the artist, ranging from $100 shoelaces to a $7,900 knit scarf. The merchandise Nara designs often connects to themes in his art, like the $12 bandages, $85 snow globes, and $1,200 table lamps available at the MoMA Design Store. (He is one of the most searched artists on MoMA’s website, says Chay Costello, the museum’s associate director of merchandising and product development.)

Content creator Nick Lowry, who bought one of Nara’s ashtrays 20 years ago on eBay for $250, posted a TikTok video a few months ago of himself removing the object from its longtime spot on his coffee table and hanging it on the wall. (The original acrylic painting on which the ashtray is based is in the collection of the Rubell Museum, in Miami.) “Objects like this are one of the only ways to own work by a living artist whose paintings now sell for more than I could ever afford,” Lowry says in the video, “so it deserved a better spot.”

Benioff describes Nara as “one of the most important artists of our time.” Like many others, Benioff came to Nara’s work through eBay. He purchased journals designed by the artist and his “little books” about the Zen Buddhist concept of Shoshin, commonly translated as “beginner’s mind.” Benioff, who met Nara through Yoshiki, now considers the artist a close friend. He owns multiple artworks by the artist, including a drawing of a grimacing girl that reads “I’m just alright,” a totemic fiberglass sculpture of two heads in a bowl, and a monumental bronze head. “It’s great that I picked them up early,” he says of the pieces. “It’s really almost impossible to get anything [on the primary market]. The best pieces all go to the museums.”

Numerous Nara works are coming to auction in conjunction with the opening of the Zwirner show. (Nara’s auction record is $25 million for his seven-and-a-half-foot canvas Knife Behind Back, sold at Sotheby’s Hong Kong in 2019.) His eight-and-a-half-foot painting Milky Lake / Thinking One, featuring a little girl with downcast eyes, is coming up for sale later this month at the online auction platform Fair Warning at an estimate of $10 million. Next week, Christie’s is offering an eight-foot-high bronze version of his character Miss Forest, for $1 million, in its 21st Century Evening Sale, and Sotheby’s is selling his painting Candy Blue Night, from 2001, at an estimate of $5 million, and a set of fiberglass sculptures, The Little Pilgrims (Night Walking), from a 1999 edition, at an estimate of $1.2 million. (The sellers, Theodore and Ruth Baum, bought the set at auction two decades ago for just $360,000.)

For the Zwirner show, Nara has paired works from his student days in Düsseldorf in the 1980s and 1990s with recent ones, demonstrating how he incorporated drawing into his practice throughout his career. He’s also exhibiting 13 oversized bronze heads, enlarged from palm-sized clay models inspired by the snow “monsters” from his childhood winters in northern rural Japan. The show coincides with the reopening last week of the Hirshhorn Sculpture Garden in Washington, DC, after a four-year renovation, where three larger aluminum versions of the heads are on view.

Nara sculptures at the reopened Hirshhorn Sculpture Garden in Washington, DC

For most artists, the distance between the audiences for Nara’s masterpieces and his mass-produced products would be difficult to bridge. But for Nara, it may be the very thing that makes him so compelling. His work can be both a million-dollar trophy and a teenager’s trinket. And while the Zwirner show draws the art world’s big collectors and curators, the next generation of fans may be discovering him somewhere else entirely: on TikTok, Instagram, or in a gift shop.
