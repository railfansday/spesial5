How the brain protects itself from Parkinson’s disease

Sarah DeWeerdt is a science journalist in Seattle, Washington.

The pianist’s hands are poised over the keys, his right hand trembles slightly. A drummer and bass player start up and, after a beat, the pianist joins in, both hands fluently producing the luscious, staccato rhythms of jazz.

The hands in this video from 2019 belong to Dutch musician René van Helsdingen, who had recently been diagnosed with Parkinson’s disease. He uses strategies that reduce stress and emotion to help quiet the tremors that affect his right side and enable him to continue playing the music he loves, he explains in the video. “It’s my improvised solution.”

Improvisation is an apt metaphor for the ways in which many people with Parkinson’s learn to cope with the tremor, stiffness and movement difficulties that are characteristic of the neurodegenerative disease. Some use an external source of rhythm, such as a metronome, to overcome the tendency to ‘freeze up’ when walking or adopt a sideways crab-like gait. “I’m very inspired” by people’s creativity, says Rick Helmich, a neurologist at Radboud University in Nijmegen, the Netherlands, who has worked with van Helsdingen.

Other improvisations take place in the brain, beyond a person’s awareness, to preserve function in the face of Parkinson’s pathology. If scientists can understand them better and harness them more effectively, they might be able to slow the progression of Parkinson’s disease, delay its onset and even prevent it entirely, say researchers, such as Helmich.

The motor symptoms of Parkinson’s disease are caused by a loss of dopamine-producing neurons in the substantia nigra — part of a set of structures nestled deep in the brain known as the basal ganglia. But these symptoms do not emerge until at least 30% of those neurons are lost, and sometimes as many as 70%. Two people with a similar degree of neurodegeneration might have very different symptom severity. And some people’s condition worsens quickly, whereas others remain stable for years. Such observations have led most researchers and clinicians to agree that brain-based mechanisms of resilience must be important.

But for a long time, this area received little research attention because scientists focused instead on unravelling the pathology of the disease and on developing an effective treatment, called levodopa, that remedied the underlying dopamine loss. The awareness that the brain has ways of making up for the neurodegeneration in Parkinson’s hasn’t led to clinical advances, says Thilo van Eimeren, a specialist in brain imaging at the University of Cologne in Germany.

This area is now garnering more interest. It is a focus of a three-year, European Union-funded project launched in 2025, known as TRACE-PD, to track the mechanisms of disease progression and functional compensation in early stages of disease1.

The first task for the team from across Europe was to define its terms. That effort is more than mere semantics, says David Pedrosa, a neurologist at Marburg University in Germany and co-coordinator of the project. More clarity in the vocabulary that researchers use to talk about protective mechanisms will enable them to probe the underlying biology with greater precision, and design better targeted clinical trials to investigate how to bolster these processes, he explains.

The TRACE-PD consortium has defined resilience as an umbrella term that encompasses multiple protective mechanisms. One, called reserve, refers to neuronal resources built up across the lifespan that mitigate the effects of the disease. Another, called compensation, involves changes to neurons and brain networks that stabilize function in response to the disease.

The idea behind reserve makes intuitive sense: when a neurodegenerative process takes hold, individuals who have greater neural resources in a particular domain will take longer to decline to a point at which their impairment becomes obvious. Cognitive reserve has long been a focus of Alzheimer’s disease research. And over the past few years, studies of Parkinson’s have examined the idea of motor reserve.

Most such studies have defined individuals with high motor reserve as those with milder motor symptoms than their degree of dopamine-neuron loss would predict. A 2020 study by researchers in South Korea showed that people with newly diagnosed Parkinson’s and high motor reserve have greater connectivity between areas of the brain involved in movement, planning and control2. Greater connectivity in this network is also linked to a lower need for levodopa treatment.

“Individuals with higher motor reserve levels also show greater grey matter volume in motor-associated areas,” says Merle Hönig, a neuropsychologist at the University of Cologne. She and van Eimeren contributed to a study3 that also found that the gap in disease trajectory between individuals with high motor reserve and low motor reserve takes more than seven years to close, suggesting that assessing motor reserve early could help to determine prognosis.

But could motor reserve be the basis for interventions to alter the course of Parkinson’s? The same study showed an association between higher motor reserve and higher levels of physical activity in one cohort of people with the disease — a suggestive but circumstantial link. The team now has unpublished data that strengthen the case for a causal relationship by showing that people with higher lifetime levels of physical activity also have higher motor reserve. Exercise is known to slow progression during the early phases of Parkinson’s, but there is much about this connection that is poorly understood, says Patrick Santens, a neurologist at Ghent University in Belgium. “Nobody really knows what is the best,” he says. What type of exercise is beneficial, at what intensity and for which individuals?

Another question is the relationship between the different types of reserve. “We don’t know whether promoting, for example, cognitive reserve also helps to preserve motor reserve,” or vice versa, Hönig says. Research in this area might, therefore, have implications for multiple neurodegenerative diseases.

Research involving compensation goes back to at least the early 1990s. But over the past few years, Helmich says, the concept has been refined. Whereas greater activity in a brain area or network was once seen as evidence of compensation, scientists are now looking for a link between stronger hyperactivity and greater symptom reduction, for example.

This approach has enabled researchers to zero in on the neural substrate of compensation. Helmich and his team have shown that the main brain areas involved are the parietal cortex and the premotor cortex. The less affected parts of the basal ganglia also contribute4.

Another group have investigated brain activity in people with dopamine-neuron loss on both sides of the brain, but symptoms on only one side of the body. The researchers identified several compensatory mechanisms in the brain hemisphere that controls the unaffected side of the body, including increased plasticity in motor networks5.

Helmich and his team have also shown6 that, over the course of two years, “the speed of progression relates to the collapse of compensatory mechanisms”, he says. The researchers have unpublished data from a separate cohort showing that people who have mutations in the gene encoding the enzyme glucocerebrosidase, long known to increase the risk of Parkinson’s, have reduced potential for compensation. Such studies place the loss of compensation at the centre of disease progression.

Researchers are now beginning to investigate how to intervene to stimulate compensatory mechanisms. Helmich and his team identified an ‘anti tremor network’ that comes online when a tremor reduces in intensity and consists of areas of the cortex involved in attention and in interpreting bodily sensations7. He is testing whether an intervention involving mindfulness training and meditation can help people to activate this network and control the tremor.

Stimulating compensatory mechanisms might also involve non-invasive brain stimulation — Helmich plans to launch a study later this year using transcranial ultrasound to activate the less affected parts of the basal ganglia to find out. Meanwhile, Pedrosa is investigating how to use advancing knowledge of compensation to improve deep-brain stimulation, a treatment in which electrodes are implanted in the brain to send electrical signals to areas involved in motor control.

Further research will be needed to help refine and guide these approaches, says Josefine Waldthaler, a neuroimaging specialist at the Karolinska Institute in Sweden. “We know where to look” for compensation in the cerebral cortex, Waldthaler says, but not the particular patterns of brain activity that define it, and therefore what brain frequency interventions need to stimulate.

Magnetoencephalography, an imaging method that is Waldthaler’s specialty, could fill in some of those details. And that’s where TRACE-PD comes in. The effort brings together researchers who use different approaches to investigate various aspects of resilience. Rather than collect new data, the idea is to share existing data sets and analytical methods. This will help the consortium to tease apart the mechanisms of compensation and reserve, validate definitions and refine applications, Pedrosa says. What the researchers find could have broader relevance as well. “The cool thing about the idea of compensation and reserve is that it’s not a Parkinson’s specific idea,” says Pedrosa. He contends that the group’s insights might shed light on other neurodegenerative conditions, such as Alzheimer’s disease, as well as on strategies for healthy ageing.

And although TRACE-PD focuses on early Parkinson’s, the processes it looks at might still be harnessed later in the disease. In May, van Helsdingen performed with his trio in Italy. In a video of the concert, his symptoms seem to have worsened since 2019. He rocks rhythmically back and forth as he plays, and at one point crosses his left wrist over his right to execute a particularly tricky sequence of notes on the upper keys with his less-affected left hand. But the result is syncopated, at times cheerful, music that makes it clear he’s still improvising.
