Finding Unexpected Kinship With Senior Travelers—And a Joyful New Outlook On Growing Older

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

On a grassy field in Don-Aryk, Kyrgyzstan, men on horseback were wrestling over a headless goat carcass. This is kok boru, the country's national sport, sometimes called dead goat polo. The riders violently wrenched the goat from one another's grip, then tore across the field toward a cauldron-size goal. When the game ended, a player invited me to take a victory lap with the goat. I was two weeks into a Smithsonian Journeys trip across five Central Asian countries with a group of mostly retirees. They knew I was genuinely considering his offer, and their reaction was unanimous: Oh, no you won't.

Standing there in the Kyrgyz countryside, being collectively talked out of one of my more impulsive yeses by a busload of surrogate parents, I felt like a teenager being scolded—defiant but also secretly touched that people I had just met cared enough to save me from myself.

On my first trip to Uzbekistan, I joined an Intrepid Travel tour and wondered whether I had booked the wrong departure. Most of the guests were seniors, so I assumed we would have little in common. Then I met Penny, a septuagenarian from Australia who survived a terrorist bombing in Bali that killed three people in her group. Her husband had suffered brain damage and withdrew from the world; Penny kept traveling, even when it meant going alone. We shopped for textiles in Khiva and bonded over meals; by trip's end, whatever distance I'd imagined between us disappeared.

That first trip changed more than my assumptions about whom I might enjoy traveling with. My own parents struggled with severe depression and agoraphobia, each condition feeding the other as their shared world contracted. Older travelers like Penny showed me another way to age: by staying curious, social, active, and engaged with the world.

I saw that same determination on a Lindblad Expeditions–National Geographic cruise through the Peruvian Amazon. Dan and Kathy, both widowed after 30-year marriages, met online, fell in love, and kept a list of trips to take. Another guest had cared for her husband through terminal brain cancer, then received her own cancer diagnosis. Hardship hadn't blunted their zest for adventure; if anything, it had magnified it.

I was on board and turning that contrast over in my mind when I found out my dad died.

The next few days bled together. Through the blur my fellow travelers showed up for me—listening when I wanted to talk and giving me space when I didn't. These folks knew firsthand what it meant to have life cleaved into Before and After. Yet here they were, swimming with piranhas, trekking through the jungle, and plotting where to go next. They were living proof that joy could still exist on the other side of tragedy.

Back in Central Asia my fellow travelers who had vetoed my kok boru ride embodied what I'd come to love about traveling with groups of older people. Linda, an 80-year-old retired psychoanalyst, shared my raunchy humor and skittering attention span during tedious museum tours. I suspected Susan, 83, disapproved when she first asked about my tattoos—until she rolled up her sleeve to reveal Betty Boop on her biceps, acquired at 81. She had survived two comas and a stroke, and still posed for photos in fire-engine red lipstick. A red lip is my signature too. This was my kind of woman.

The whole group was intimidatingly accomplished: pulmonologists, oncologists, former heads of ICUs and surgical departments. Ask for a doctor, and half the bus could raise a hand. They talked passionately about history and architecture. My PhD dad could turn any conversation into a lecture too; I hadn't realized how much I missed that until I was sermonized at dinner. Their adult children came up often: Would they find the right partner? Start a family? Live up to their potential? Beneath all the second-guessing was enormous tenderness.

Traveling with seniors requires patience, of course. Some use canes and walkers, so itineraries are deliberately less demanding. To me, that's a fair trade.

On a Viking river cruise that sailed this past summer from Bucharest, Romania, to Budapest, my husband and I hit it off with Bob and Jeannie, a couple from Long Island. They peppered us with questions about our work, our four-year-old son, and our life in Minnesota. That curiosity touched a nerve. My mother, who has dementia and lives with us, no longer asks about my job, my kid, or anything else. Soon we were seeking one another out, talking for hours and doubling over with laughter. We promised to look them up the next time we were in New York—and meant it. Bob and Jeannie weren't filling in for anyone. They were just friends.

At our farewell Smithsonian dinner in Almaty, the group toasted me for “keeping us young.” I was usually the one volunteering to dance with strangers, climb onto horses, or let an owl perch on my head. If I got parental supervision in return, they got to live vicariously through my harebrained antics.

Their gratitude caught me off guard. I knew what these travelers gave me—perspective, attention, a road map for the years ahead—but hadn't considered what I might be giving them.

Now, when I join a group trip, I genuinely hope I'm the youngest person on the bus. I look forward to seeing whom I'll connect with, hearing stories I'll carry for years, and, if I'm lucky, getting a glimpse of the person I might become.
