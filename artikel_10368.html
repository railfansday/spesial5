The button-up was never designed for women’s bodies. These labels are fixing that

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

In the 1870s, tailors in London and Paris raced to redesign the most fundamental garment in a man’s wardrobe: the shirt.

Until then, men pulled shirts over their heads, with fabric that billowed down to their waist. But innovative shirt makers in Paris and London—like Charvet and Brown, Davis & Co.—realized that men wanted closer-fitting shirts. The breakthrough came when they sewed buttons down the front, along with darted seams and shoulder yokes, creating a flattering fit. As a final flourish, they added a stylish collar with pointed tips. And so the button-up shirt came into the world.

Over the next century, it would become one of the most iconic garments of our time. It conveyed good taste, but, perhaps more importantly, social status. A crisp, spotless shirt made it clear that the man wearing it didn’t work with his hands. He belonged to the professional class—what we’d later call a “white-collar worker.” From Tokyo to New York to Mumbai, dress shirts became a uniform at the office.

But in a strange twist, the shirt’s original customers have lost interest. Two-thirds of men now forego traditional workwear, opting for polos and t-shirts instead. But button-ups have found a new audience: women, who are dressing more formally even as men are becoming increasingly casual.

Search interest in women’s button-up shirts in the U.S. has more than doubled since 2019, according to Google Trends, and this April, it hit a 10-year high. The timing may not be a coincidence. Last year, McKinsey found that companies are retreating from their commitments to gender equity, giving women less support and fewer opportunities for advancement. As women are forced to advocate for themselves, it makes sense that they’re drawn to a garment historically linked to authority and competence.

The fashion industry is taking note. New labels have entered the market with a single mission of tailoring the dress shirt to the female form. Designers are tackling this issue in different ways: With Nothing Underneath keeps the masculine silhouette but subtly engineers it to a woman’s body, Frank & Eileen turns fine shirting fabrics historically reserved for men into styles that accentuate women’s curves, and TWP perfects the aesthetic of the oversized borrowed men’s shirt.

A century and a half later, tailors are once again racing to redesign the shirt. This time, it’s for women.

A Man’s Silhouette on a Woman’s Body

Women’s dress shirts aren’t new. Since the 1980s, brands have designed button-up shirts for women. But often, they try to feminize them with darts and cinched waists, designed to accentuate women’s hourglass form. This style isn’t everyone’s taste. Many women are drawn to the button-up shirt precisely because it is so masculine and represents male power.

Take Pip Durell, founder of a women’s shirting brand called With Nothing Underneath (WNU). In her career as a magazine editor at Vogue and Tatler, her uniform was jeans and a men’s dress shirt. In magazine shoots, she would call in men’s button-ups for female models, taking inspiration from the many women who have worn mens’ shirts with panache. (Coco Chanel famously borrowed her boyfriend’s shirts; Patti Smith wore a men’s shirt on the cover of her 1975 album Horses; Sharon Stone paired a men’s Gap shirt with a ball skirt at the 1998 Oscars.) “Men’s tailoring is impeccable,” says Phoebe Minney, WNU’s head of brand. “Savile Row has spent years perfecting the menswear wardrobe.”

The problem with wearing a men’s shirt is that it doesn’t hang the same way on a woman’s body. The shoulders are too broad and the sleeves are too long. The fabric stretches across the hips and gapes at the bust. In 2017, Durell decided to launch a label that would give women shirts that that would have the drape, fabric, and cut of a men’s shirt.

She began with a single style, the Boyfriend, that has oversized proportions and boxy lines. But under the surface, the brand has reengineered the shirt for women’s anatomy. Men’s shirts are cut wider in the front than in the back, to make room for their wide shoulders; WNU makes the back wider, so there is enough fabric on the front to make room for women’s busts. Buttons are strategically positioned so there is never a gape; the hem has extra length so it clears women’s wider hips and tucks easily. And the bottom buttonhole runs horizontally, so it stays fastened when tucked in. And while women’s shirts typically place their buttons on the left side, WNU’s shirts have their buttons on the right.

“Our buttons are still on the man’s side of the shirt as an homage to our inspiration,” says Minney.

Minney says that many of the brand’s customers are just entering the workforce and trying to figure out what to wear to their first corporate jobs as lawyers or bankers. At a time when even the most formal professions are relaxing their dress codes, a starched, slightly oversized dress shirt allows women to feel confident without being overdressed. And a white button-up with wide leg trousers is more comfortable than a suit. WNU designs its suits for movement to accommodate women’s busy lifestyles.

“Wealthy men whose shirts we’re modeling ours on might not have had to get on the tube every day, or carry their kids,” says Sarah Stokes, head of design at WNU. The brand’s design team obsesses over every detail of their shirts, focusing on places where they might come untucked or feel too tight. “The customer might not see the details on the hanger, but they feel them when they wear the shirts,” Stokes says. “We really overdevelop product.”

All of this obsession has given WNU a cult following. Both Kate Middleton and Meghan Markle are fans of the brand. Its core customer is a professional woman between 30 and 45 who wants a shirt that can carry her from a board meeting to the weekend. Shirts are priced between $175 and $250, which sits between mass market and luxury pricing. “You have the high street shirt that is poorly cut and not designed to last that long,” says Minney, the brand manager. “Then there’s really high end, where you’re looking at thousands for a shirt.”

There’s clearly a demand for WNU’s approach to shirt making. In 2022, WNU opened its first store, and in 2024, it raised its first outside funding. The company is now growing quickly in the United States. While it began venturing beyond shirts two years ago—including trousers, blazers, and denim—its focus remains on the shirt. Over the years, customers have asked for variations on the Boyfriend. WNU now has a portfolio, including the Classic, with a shorter, neater fit that tucks into trousers and slips under a blazer; the Weekend is more relaxed; the Q adds ruffles without being overly feminine.

Fine fabrics reserved for men

Frank & Eileen takes a very different approach. When Audrey McLoghlin launched the brand in 2009, she was less focused on fit, and entirely obsessed with materials.

McLoghlin trained as an engineer, then pivoted to a career in fashion, taking jobs at Gap and J.Crew. While at a factory in Los Angeles, she came across a book of Italian menswear fabrics on a dusty shelf. “All of a sudden, my mind was just blown,” she says. “Nobody makes button-ups for women out of these kinds of fabrics.”

For more than a century, Italy’s best mills have reserved their finest cottons and linens for men. By contrast, women’s shirts tended to be made from cheaper, coarser cotton or synthetics. The industry seemed to assume that women wouldn’t appreciate—or be willing to pay—for the drape or feel of these high-end fabrics. “These textiles are not an efficient solution,” says McLoghlin. “It’s really about the quality of the yarns and the love of the processes.”

With Frank & Eileen, McLoghlin wanted to take these male-coded materials and design them for a woman’s body. She found a 150-year-old mill that has made fabrics for some of the best-known menswear labels, and created shirts tailored to women’s curves. “I loved the contradiction of the masculine fabrics and heritage, then combining it with an extremely feminine silhouette,” she says. Her inspiration was less the boardroom, and more the Italian Riviera: the soft, lived-in shirt a man might wear day, after day, sleeves rolled, collar open.

Her first silhouette, the Barry, named after her father, took six months to develop with a pattern maker. Where WNU disguises its engineering, the Barry celebrates the female form. It curves in sharply at the sides and back. But the real flourish is at the top. The Barry has an open neckline designed to frame the face, and the buttons sit low enough that the collar stands up and the placket falls open without exposing too much. It also allows necklaces to stand out.

Five years later, after selling thousands of Barry shirts, Frank & Eileen launched the Eileen, which is almost its opposite. It has no structure, a soft shoulder, and a rounded body that covers the seat. This new design was partly a response to changing fashions. When skinny jeans dominated, women wanted long, loose shirts. Now that high-waisted trousers are back, tucking is in, and so is the Barry.

McLoghlin sees the button-up as a middle path between the 1980s power suit and the hoodie. Women once felt they had to show up in a suit with heels to command respect, she says. The shirt offers a quieter kind of authority. “It says, ‘I know who I am and I’ve earned my place at the table,’” she says.

Frank & Eileen spent its first decade in wholesale, selling through specialty stores and department stores such as Barneys New York and Ron Herman. In the pandemic, McLoghlin pivoted to selling directly to consumers, and e-commerce has since grown fivefold since 2021. Frank & Eileen has never taken outside investment. Last November, it opened its first U.S. flagship store in New York. Like WNU, it counts Meghan Markle as a fan. The Eileen, which retails for $288, now accounts for 30% of the business.

The romance of the borrowed shirt

There’s a fantasy attached to a woman wearing a men’s dress shirt: She wakes up after a rendezvous and pulls on his shirt, sleeves dangling past her wrists. This is the romance around which Trish Wescoat Pound built her eponymous brand. Pound founded TWP in 2021 after working at Theory and Michael Kors. The label grew out of “a void I saw in the market for the perfect white shirt,” she says. “And my love of a woman in an oversized shirt.”

You can see that wink in the shirt’s names, which include the Next Ex, the Soon To Be Ex, the Night We Met, the Next Morning, the Brief Encounter, and Something Borrowed. Beneath the cheekiness is a deeper ideas that the garment, and the authority it represents, can be co-opted by a woman.

Where WNU engineers the men’s shirt to fit women’s bodies, TWP revels in the places where a men’s shirt is too large for the female form. The oversized styles keep volume through the body and the long sleeves are perfect for rolling. But Pound obsesses over how to make these proportions look flattering, rather than swallowing the wearer. “There’s something about the ease of it, the borrowed-from-a-man aesthetic that I keep coming back to,” she says.

But in some ways, the shirts are a metaphor for women’s role in the workplace. Many women are looking for the perfect white shirt that allows them to project authority at work. Meanwhile, men can show up in hoodies and T-shirts without worrying that their competence will be questioned. Researchers call this the “red sneakers effect”: Dressing down signals status, but only for people whose authority is already assumed. Women rarely get that pass. One study found that women senior managers were judged less competent when their blouses were buttoned just one notch lower.

Men spent 150 years perfecting the dress shirt. Now, as they abandon it, women are claiming it for themselves. But this time, the borrowing comes with a twist. The shirt that once marked a man’s place in the professional class is being remade for women: cut for their bodies, sewn from fabrics once reserved for men, and, in TWP’s case, named with a wink at the men left behind.
