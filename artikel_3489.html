Johnny Depp’s ‘Ebenezer’ Makes a Weak Case for His Post-Trial Comeback

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

For weeks, Los Angeles has been plastered with posters of a malevolent-looking man in a top hat and gray sideburns. It always takes me a few minutes to process that this elderly curmudgeon is Johnny Depp, doing his Hollywood walk of shame in Ebenezer.

“It’s good to be back,” Depp gurgles in the movie’s trailer. He’s either reveling in his return from infamy or trolling the audience, depending on your point of view.

I adored Johnny Depp back in his Edward Scissorhands/Gilbert Grape/Ed Wood heyday; he probably would’ve been my hall pass if I’d known about the concept back then. Now, though, it feels as if the once chiseled-cheeked heartthrob has found a character that suits him: a monstrous figure seeking redemption.

“Grant me the grace to prove that I am no longer the man that I once was,” Ebenezer Scrooge begs one of the ghosts haunting him in this rather plodding new version of Charles Dickens’s A Christmas Carol, directed by horror auteur Ti West. Scrooge is trying to make up for a lifetime of stinginess and cruelty, whereas Depp seeks to win back mainstream audiences after the ugly, drawn-out brawl that was his marriage and divorce from Amber Heard.

Heard alleged that he was an abusive addict. Depp denied abusing her and filed two defamation suits. He aimed one at the British tabloid The Sun for calling him a wife-beater (which he lost, since the court found the characterization to be “substantially true”). The other was leveled at Heard for describing herself in a Washington Post op-ed as “a public figure representing domestic abuse.” What started as a defamation trial ended up as a livestreamed referendum on post-#MeToo gender politics.

The saga spawned organized smear campaigns soaked in misogyny. Social media erupted with vile memes, hashtags, and videos targeting Heard, bearing hashtags like #AmberHeardIsAPsychopath and #DontBelieveAllWomen. One men’s group launched a “Ditch the Witch” protest in support of Depp. It seemed to echo one of Depp’s texts about Heard, released during the trial, in which he proposed to a friend: “Let’s drown her before we burn her!!!…I will fuck her burnt corpse afterwards to make sure she is dead.” And it wasn’t just ordinary guys taking up arms for Depp. Moments after the verdict was announced, the GOP’s House Judiciary tweeted a GIF of Depp in Pirates of the Caribbean from its official account, showing support for him.

“The craziness [aimed at Heard] was a kind of pent-up anger at women,” a film producer suggested to me back in 2022. “I think it really stirred up a lot of deep-seated resentment that hasn’t been allowed expression since #MeToo.”

Depp didn’t come out of the crucible with his career intact. He was asked to resign from the Fantastic Beasts franchise—though Warner Bros. still paid Depp his full $16 million salary—and his former agent claimed Disney dropped him from the next Pirates of the Caribbean movie. Still, he never really went away. In fact, like so many guys who have been supposedly “canceled,” he wormed his way back in quite easily. In 2023, he got a standing ovation at Cannes for his role as Louis XV in the French film Jeanne du Barry. The next year, he directed the movie Modì, Three Days on the Wing of Madness. Meanwhile, producer Jerry Bruckheimer confirmed in 2025 that he’s been talking to Depp about a sixth Pirates film after all. All the while, Depp has also kept his massive brand deal as the face of Dior’s Sauvage.

“I don’t feel boycotted by Hollywood because I don’t think about Hollywood,” Depp announced imperiously a few years back. “I don’t have much further need for Hollywood.” Enter Ebenezer, his official return to…Hollywood. Which he doesn’t need or think about. At all.

Call it Christmas-washing: What could be more wholesome and uncontroversial than A Christmas Carol? According to Variety, Paramount paid Depp just $5 million—a big drop-off from the days when he could reportedly command $50 million for a single film—and only spent $38 million to produce the movie, hedging its bets. Still, this film is a pretty warm welcome back to American movie screens, major marketing campaign and all.

Unfortunately, Depp’s mediocre performance in Ebenezer doesn’t dispel the sour scent of desperation that hangs over his career. Scrooge is one of the most unlovable characters in all of literature, and Depp’s charm does not shine through the gnarled facial prosthetics. He plays the archetypal miser in the broadest of ways, spending much of the nearly two-hour movie alternating between exaggerated expressions of cantankerousness and horror. Depp’s inspiration for the role might as well have been Oscar the Grouch, though one with less educational value who’s hollow at his core. At various points, the actor campily throws himself into physical comedy that is at odds with the horror-soaked vibe of West’s production.

Knowing this is a test run for Depp’s uncanceling, viewers will find it hard not to see Ebenezer as a wink-filled plea for absolution. “I’m not the man I was!” his character screams over and over. “Allow me my redemption.” To which the answer—or my answer, at least—is simple: Bah, humbug.
