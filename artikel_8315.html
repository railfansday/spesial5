Day 20: The Labyrinth of Terra de Luz

Tanggal: 2026-11-11

Sneaking out of the albergue before dawn, we were guided by the “smell of Galicia,” as Suzanne calls it, navigating by headlamp around the obstacle course of its source. It’s a tricky path.

Real or imagined, I feel a distinct shift in the morning air. An early mist hangs low over the green meadows, and before dawn we walk alone. First one village, then another, still dark—and then a German Shepherd attacks, which Suzanne counters by wielding her hiking pole like a sword. No coffee needed now; the adrenaline is pumping.

A four-mile easy walk, the clouds sailing past the sliver of a moon—it was the kind of easy that only a gentle downhill provides. The Way has shifted from the open mountain field trails to the fall-turning Galician woods. The chestnuts cover the edges of the path, their green, prickly round pods everywhere. The blue late crocuses are popping up. Then we hit a major road and The Way instructs us onto the shoulder. There appears to be an old path paralleling the busy road, and we risk it. And risk it was, we end up navigating a risky culvert full from the rain and thankfully don’t slip and fall into it.

Forest paths bounded by tall embankments.

First coffee is always a fun challenge, and when we reach Sarria, the cafés are just starting to open. They are in hotels, with long tables set for their turigrinos (tourist pilgrims) who join The Way after their provided breakfast, but they aren’t up yet, so their barista is available.

We’re deep in hórreos country, those ancient stone granaries I’ve been so fascinated with. I tell Suzanne I want to build one in our backyard. What I really want to do is transport one, stone by stone, from Galicia and reassemble it.

Good question. I am just taken by them, I say, and think: or maybe I’d like one of them to be taken by me.

We walk for a stretch with the second pair of Aussies we met back on our zero day. He is recovering from open-heart surgery and frequently runs out of breath. Should he even be doing this? I think. We talk while the ladies walk ahead, immersed in a much deeper conversation of their own. This is the third day we’ve passed them and slowed down to chat. Yesterday, I learned he is a Bible literalist and quite conservative—and not in the fun way, but as a displaced Kiwi right-winger. He speaks in anger, hate, and with an arrogance of confidence. I listen, do not counter, but I pray silently that the Camino can soften his heart toward those he fears. I decide he is not on my Camino, but I don’t want to be rude about it. The next time the ladies wait for us, I give Suzanne the look, and we pull away.

Otherwise, it’s been a quiet walk this morning past open fields and the occasional chime of a cowbell. Randomly placed stone hórreos look like they’ve stood watch for centuries. There are other pilgrims, but two or three, here and there.

Then comes Sarria.  I realize that this is the end of our Camino and the beginning of another—theirs. Yesterday, the new pilgrims seeped into ours slowly like a rising tide; today it’s a full-on monsoon. Our tranquil bubble has been popped. The place where we stop for first coffee is a wreck—the trail of turigrinos has left its mark; they haven’t learned a real pilgrim always buses their table. Outside looks like the lost luggage area at baggage claim, an abandonment of suitcases and backpacks lined up for transport.

Kindly, they seat us inside, away from the chaos. The place is huge, with room after back room, but the oddest thing is that the bathroom is up a long flight of stairs. My knees do not like this, but my bladder appreciates it. We order two Americanos, bacon and eggs, and our daily chocolate croissant to share. Known locally as a napolitana de chocolate, it’s a delicious rectangular, chocolate-filled pastry. Then, after getting a stamp, we push off into the current of the crowded way.

This massive 800-year-old chestnut tree is so old it had a name: Castiñeiro de Ramil.

We walk up the last big incline before Santiago and down a seemingly longer one, winding toward Barbadelo. Along the trail, we come upon the landmark, massive 800-year-old tree named Castiñeiro de Ramil. It spans nine feet wide with a hollow trunk. The historical marker notes it dates back to the 12th century. In those days, annually 25,000 pilgrims—real pilgrims—made their way past, and this tree must have seen it all. We’ve been walking beneath chestnuts for weeks, but this is something different. Suzanne lets me take her picture against the trunk for perspective, and we walk away wondering about what it has witnessed through the ages: the prayers, thoughts, and pain of pilgrims who rested at its base and took shelter in its shade.

chestnuts, ready for picking. Better wear gloves, the spikes are sharp.

It’s afternoon now and we’re well past our “take a rest every hour” threshold, so we start looking for the perfect bench. Or large stone. Or anything that we can sit on that isn’t crowded. None appear. During the last steep decline, something about the terrain feels intimately familiar. Are we near Simon’s place, Terra de Luz? This was the last significant sanctuary of my 2017 Camino. Will it still be here? I wonder.

Entrance to Terra de Luz, AKA Simon’s Place

Then I spy a break in the courtyard wall and walk in.

Suzanne stays outside on the road, looking at me skeptically, hands on her hips. I can tell she’s thining “You can’t just go into someone’s courtyard!”

“It’s Simon’s place,” I tell her. I’d mentioned Simon to her before. “Come on,” I motion, but she remains dubious.

I look around—it’s different than I remember, but clearly Terra de Luz. A volunteer welcomes me, and I ask, mostly to confirm: “Is Simon here?”

“No, he’s off helping a neighbor.” This is definitely Simon’s place!

Donativo table of food, much of it grown here.

There is donativo food, tea, and comfortable couches. I chat with one of the volunteers. “I stepped off my Camino for a few days to stay here,” he says. “Three months ago I started in St. Jean. I walk a while, I stop and help a while.” Simon and I had originally bonded over Ghana, where he had begun a motorcycle trip around Africa back in the early 2000s.

Suddenly, Irun, the service dog, comes bounding in. She sniffs us, turns around, and runs back out to Monse, the young señorita we’ve been seeing every few days. Irun runs ahead like a scout, and I wonder if the dog views us as her trusted friends. Finding us, she runs back to tell Monse, and five minutes later, Monse herself walks up to the gate, as skeptical as Suzanne was. We see her and call her name and she comes in.

Simon returns, and I re-introduce myself. “Welcome,” he says, wrapping me in a big hug. It’s okay that he doesn’t remember me right away; I just want him to know the significance he played in my story, the value of his ministry. I remind him of some of the words he shared with me years ago:

It is so good to see him. The more we chat, the more I feel him remembering me, genuinely curious about the impact that earlier version of himself had on my life. I ask about his mother, and he smiles. They had walked the Camino together four years before we met.

Monse comes back from contemplation on the benches overlooking the quartz labyrinth and garden.

“You need to speak to this man,” I tell her—though I probably should have said listen to him.

She stammers, “Wait, what? Why? Who is he?”

“Simon really helped me years ago,” I say. “He is wise, and I think he might have something for you.”

I introduce them and excuse myself so they can talk, though I hear Monse is doing most of the talking. Across the courtyard, I notice the second Aussie couple has arrived. The woman is highly engaged in deep conversation with a volunteer, while the unpleasant, conservative husband sits staring silently into his coffee, unengaged and looking uncomfortable in this hippe-type place. We wave but don’t go over.

Turns out Simon’s volunteers are all musicians and sing well together.

I look at Suzanne, sun on her face, resting. Remarkable. I am in such a different place now. I remember back in Africa thinking about the zero-sum nature of our situation, trying to imagine a scenario where Suzanne wouldn’t have to make the forced choice between me and the university. Thanks to Simon, I had questioned my own intent in wanting to help her: Was it for me, or for her?

And now here she is at Simon’s place; we are walking the Camino together. She chose me. She chose life. Remarkable.

We both walk the labyrinth. The instructions at the entrance read:

I feel Santiago telling me to release judgment, evaluation, and the constant comparison of past Caminos. Yeah, I’m still working on this. Looking back at what I’ve written today, I see where I’ve backslid into those old habits—and how much work I still have to do.

From 2017 – Simon drawing a stamp in my credentials

About five minutes out from Simon’s place, I remember I didn’t get a stamp. “You can go back,” Suzanne says, and I really think about it, but maybe this is a test, perhaps a trap. “No, its a different Camino, I already have one.” I have this picture of Simon drawing my stamp last time. “Those were such sweet days,” he says when I show him the picture. “I bet you have a lot of people who come back just to tell you the impact you had on them. “Yes,” he says, “but none so sweet at this. He is truly gracious.
