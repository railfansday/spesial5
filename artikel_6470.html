AI Helps Three Powerful Supercomputers Simulate The Milky Way In Extraordinary Detail

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

In the early days, astronomers kept sketchbooks close at hand when they looked through their telescopes. They drew what they saw and shared their drawings. Modern astronomy, with its enormous segmented lenses, gigabyte cameras, and cryogenically-cooled infrared space observatories, makes those days seem more distant than they really are. But one of the latest efforts in astronomy makes astronomy's sketch-pad days seem so far in the past, they're almost red-shifted.

In an impressive technological maneuver, astronomers have harnessed three different supercomputers together to simulate the Milky Way at a level of detail never before achieved. They used the supercomputers and AI together to simulate the Milky Way down to the level of individual stars. Up until now, the smallest level of detail in simulations, called particles, was clusters of stars with the mass of about 100 Suns. The behaviour of individual stars was averaged out in those simulations (Auriga, FIRE, etc.)

These results are in a paper titled "Architecture-Agnostic Exascale Star-by-Star Simulation of Our Galaxy," from the SC26: International Conference for High Performance Computing, Networking, Storage and Analysis. The lead author is Keiya Hirashima, from RIKEN in Japan.

In simulations, achieving a level of individual stars means that individually simulated particles have one solar mass. So it's like a proxy for the population of stars. But regardless of that important distinction, the new simulations are extraordinary.

To achieve this fine particle resolution, simulations need ever shorter time steps, and time steps are like simulation currency; the shorter they are, the more computing resources they demand. To supply the resources for that, the researchers harnessed the power of three exascale supercomputers together: Aurora, Frontier, and JUPITER. Each one ran the same software on different hardware: Intel, NVIDIA, and AMD graphic processing units (GPUs) and central processing units (CPUs).

But linking three supercomputers was only part of the solution.

"We overcame this problem by using an AI model to predict how gas around supernova explosions evolves," said Fujii. "This allowed us to carry out the simulation more efficiently and achieve a level of detail where individual stars can be represented."

By using this model, the larger simulation was able to skip over many small timesteps. It's called PHEASANT, and it was developed by postdoctoral researcher Keiya Hirashima from RIKEN. Supernovae are complex and require their own detailed simulations with very small timesteps. By plugging PHEASANT into the larger simulation, it relieved that simulation of a lot of work.

"With trillions of particles, we resolve individual stars and model their SN feedback self-consistently within a galactic-scale simulation," the authors write. "Such a simulation reveals how individual stars, such as the Sun, contribute to the overall structure of the MW through multiple physical processes."

We can't understand the Milky Way, or galaxies in general, without a detailed understanding of how its gas and stars behave. Stars are born, drift away from their clusters, and evolve. Depending on their masses, they can remain on the main sequence for trillions of years, leave the main sequence after a few billion years and then linger as white dwarf cinders, or exhaust their fuel rapidly and explode as supernovae.

Gas is also complex. It collects in clouds where stars form, can be funnelled into the galactic center and "recycled" by supermassive black holes, or blasted away by explosions. Stars and gas are linked, and there's an extraordinary amount of detail involved.

Supernovae are among the most complicated events. In some regions, the massive young stars that eventually explode as supernova are born in the same region. When they exhaust their fuel, they explode and carve massive bubbles in the interstellar medium. That can drive gas away to form more new stars elsewhere. They can even propel gas outside of the galaxy. It's all part of the life of the Milky Way, and simulations that struggle to recreate all of the detail in supernova explosions can lead astronomers astray.

The PHEASANT AI surrogate model plays a critical role in this work, and that's part of a broader trend in science.

"The surrogate modeling approach can also be generalized beyond astrophysical problems to other research fields," the authors write.

"AI surrogate modeling accelerates simulations and bridges wider physical scales, opening new pathways in various real-world applications, including climate modeling, space forecasting, and low-temperature plasma," the authors conclude.
