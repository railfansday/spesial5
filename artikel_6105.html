Emirates Is Stripping Its Airbus A380s To 'Bare Metal' While Qantas Retires Its Superjumbos 4 Years Early

While much of the airline industry is moving away from the Airbus A380, Emirates is taking a very different approach, stripping its Superjumbos back to bare metal and investing billions in rebuilding their interiors. The strategy reflects the aircraft's continued value on high-demand international routes, particularly at airports where limited takeoff and landing slots make replacing its enormous passenger capacity difficult.

Meanwhile, Australia's Qantas is bringing forward the retirement of its A380 fleet, turning instead toward newer, more fuel-efficient twin-engine aircraft. The contrasting strategies highlight why the world's largest passenger aircraft remains valuable to some airlines while becoming increasingly difficult for others to justify.

The Airbus A380, widely known as the Superjumbo, is an incredibly important aircraft for Emirates and has been part of its fleet since August 1, 2008, when it received its first aircraft of the type, registered as A6-EDF. According to Mogaz International, this elderly A380 is 20.5 years old and had logged 45,892 flight hours across 7,539 flights by May 31, 2026.

However, the average age of Emirates' A380 fleet is approximately 11.6 years. Airbus states that its "A380-800 offers several payload capabilities ranging from 400 passengers in a very comfortable multiclass configuration, up to 853 passengers in an all economy class configuration." Actual seating arrangements vary from airline to airline, depending on their individual requirements.

For Emirates, the A380 is a key connector and revenue generator due to its ability to concentrate large numbers of passengers within a single fuselage on high-demand routes and deliver them to key destinations. This capability is particularly important when serving airports with restricted slot availability, such as London Heathrow Airport (LHR), Amsterdam Airport Schiphol (AMS), and Sydney Kingsford Smith Airport (SYD). Without the aircraft's substantial passenger capacity, the airline would need to operate more flights to transport the same number of passengers, placing additional pressure on airports where takeoff and landing slots are already limited.

Regarding the highly lucrative nature of the Superjumbo, Aviation Week quoted Sir Tim Clark, President of Emirates, as saying, “Last year, the A380 was the most profitable asset in our fleet, and that was the most profitable year we’ve ever had,” he said. “It tells you how successful, how popular that airplane is.” According to Emirates' financial data released last May, the company recorded an eye-watering profit during its 2025–26 financial year, amounting to $6.6 billion.

Due to these factors, Emirates will not be retiring the iconic aircraft in favor of smaller, more fuel-efficient twin-engine aircraft, as Qantas is doing. Rather, Emirates reportedly plans to maintain its A380 fleet until 2041, as no other aircraft currently in production offers anywhere near comparable passenger capacity. To keep the vast majority of its massive fleet of 116 Superjumbos looking their best and maximize passenger comfort, Emirates embarked on an ambitious and extensive retrofit program, the likes of which the company had never undertaken before.

As noted in the previous section, the A380 is a massive moneymaker for Emirates. Keeping these aircraft looking and feeling their best for the passengers who travel aboard them is a major priority, particularly as the average age of the fleet is nearly 12 years.

In an effort to keep these aircraft in top shape, Emirates is roughly halfway through one of the most ambitious retrofit programs in commercial aviation, which, according to the company, is worth $5 billion (£3.75 billion). Once completed, the airline will have completely retrofitted the interiors of 219 aircraft, including 110 A380s and 109 Boeing 777s.

The project began in November 2022, with the first A380 undergoing its retrofit at the Emirates Engineering hangars in Dubai. Since then, Emirates has stated that 47 of its Superjumbos and 53 Boeing 777s have received a complete nose-to-tail refresh, with the company averaging work on 28 aircraft per year. Completing the first 100 aircraft was an incredible milestone in this ambitious program.

The detailed work has involved 400 engineers and technicians who have collectively invested 4.4 million man-hours in transforming these aircraft. Incredibly, each revamped A380 requires more than 4,000 new parts, while the 777 requires more than 2,500, according to Emirates. The completed A380s feature a Premium Economy section for the first time, with a 2-3-2 configuration, full leg and footrests, six-way adjustable headrests, side cocktail tables, and a 13.3-inch personal screen. Additionally, Emirates is installing Starlink WiFi across 232 aircraft.

Ahead of the A380's planned fleet retirement in 2041, Aviation Week reports that Emirates expects to begin receiving the Boeing 777-9 in 2027, although the aircraft will accommodate approximately 150 fewer passengers. However, this transition towards twin-engine aircraft is becoming a necessity, as A380 production has ended and the industry has increasingly shifted towards lighter, more fuel-efficient passenger aircraft.

Qantas, an international airline based in Australia, operates seven aircraft types, and the A380 has been an important component of the company's ability to transport passengers to high-demand markets, such as Western Europe, Asia, and the US. While these aircraft have performed admirably, Qantas has officially stated that it will begin the process of retiring its 10 A380s as early as 2028. However, it remains unclear exactly how long it will take to completely phase them out, although they are expected to remain in service until at least FY2032.

The company made the decision to retire its fleet of Superjumbos due to two primary factors: fuel expenses and the arrival of new, long-haul-capable aircraft. Regarding the former, strong travel demand has not been enough to offset the impact of jet fuel prices, which have been affected by instability in the Middle East. AeroTime reports that "Qantas estimated that the overall impact of the fuel crisis could have been as high as AU$610 million (US$438 million), although claims to have managed to reduce this figure to AU$420 million (US$301 million) thanks to mitigation measures."

Aside from the expenses associated with operating older four-engine aircraft, Qantas will be receiving new-build aircraft with improved fuel efficiency. These new aircraft will be able to take over some of the A380s' routes and capacity, giving the company greater confidence in its decision to begin retiring the aircraft earlier than originally planned. Regarding the arrival of these new aircraft, at the end of August, Qantas Group CEO Vanessa Hudson announced:

“Our fleet renewal continued at pace, with 17 new aircraft arriving during the year and up to 31 more to arrive in the year ahead, with the majority joining Qantas . . . With our first Project Sunrise A350-1000ULR to arrive in April, and more A350s and 787s on the way, it’s a new era for Qantas’ international fleet with these next generation aircraft set to transform the way our customers travel. This means we can commence the retirement of our A380 fleet from 2028."

Regarding long-distance travel, the Airbus A350-1000 and Boeing 787 Dreamliner will form the backbone of the company's international flight strategy. While neither aircraft can fully replace the sheer passenger capacity of the A380, both can still carry substantial numbers of passengers over long distances.

Committed to these aircraft, in 2023, Qantas placed a multi-billion-dollar firm order for 12 Airbus A350s and 12 Boeing 787s, with deliveries expected to begin in FY2027. The order also included "access to up to 500 million litres of sustainable aviation fuel; enhancing pathway to reach the Group’s 2030 emissions targets."

Airbus states that its A350-1000 has a maximum seating capacity of 480 passengers and can comfortably accommodate between 375 and 400 passengers in a standard three-class configuration. It also offers a 40% increase in space within the premium seating category. Regarding fuel consumption, the A350-1000's Rolls-Royce Trent XWB-97 engines provide a 25% reduction in fuel burn and CO₂ emissions compared with previous-generation aircraft. Qantas expects to begin receiving these aircraft by FY2028.

Regarding the Boeing purchases, Qantas will receive four 787-9s and eight 787-10s. According to Boeing, the former can accommodate between 250 and 325 passengers, while the latter can carry between 300 and 375. In line with Qantas' commitment to fuel efficiency, the new Boeing 787s will be powered by General Electric GEnx engines. These aircraft offer improved fuel efficiency, with the 787-9 delivering a 20% reduction in fuel consumption and the 787-10 offering a 25% improvement, alongside a 60% reduction in noise.

The contrasting strategies of Emirates and Qantas demonstrate that the Airbus A380 still has a place in commercial aviation, although its long-term viability depends heavily on an airline's operating model. Emirates continues to see substantial value in its capacity, investing billions to keep its Superjumbos flying for years to come.

Meanwhile, Qantas is transitioning toward more efficient twin-engine aircraft, reflecting an industry-wide shift. Although the A380's era is gradually coming to an end, Emirates shows that the Superjumbo still has plenty of life left.
