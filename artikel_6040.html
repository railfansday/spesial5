Strategic Depth for Thee, Not for Me?

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

It cannot be that the pursuit of strategic depth is a logical extension of Russia’s legitimate interests but a provocative extravagance when it comes to our own.

Brigadier General Christoph Huber salutes during a ceremony marking the expansion of Germany’s military presence in Lithuania, in Kaunas, February 4, 2026.

Michael Brendan Dougherty has written a piece (“Even Putin Is a ’90s Nostalgist”) that I mostly agree with as far as it goes. My disagreement is largely confined to a few of its asides that nevertheless carry considerable weight for its underlying argument: that NATO expansion in the 1990s and 2000s was a mistake, and that it propelled us toward the unhappy situation NATO finds itself in vis-à-vis an aggressive and acquisitive Russia.

I agree of course that Vladimir Putin would like the geographical disposition of NATO as it existed in 1997 but very much not its order of battle. But Michael sees the growth of NATO’s membership as unwieldy and strategically burdensome in light of the reduced military footprint of the alliance. I don’t think that’s right. Rather, the increased size of NATO and its reduced manpower were related and, more importantly, could be seen as strategically offsetting developments.

Daniel Foster is a contributing editor at National Review.
