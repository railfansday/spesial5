The ‘recession indicators’ visible at your workplace

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

People all over the U.S. have been sharing their “personal recession indicators” in recent months, from only being able to afford Goldfish crackers and water for lunch to filling up their cars with a few dollars’ worth of gas at a time.

According to employment attorney Craig Levey, a partner at Bennett & Belfort, there are recession indicators at your workplace, too—you just have to take a look around. And they could be a sign that bigger cutbacks, or even layoffs, could be around the corner.

Levey makes content on TikTok, where he tracks patterns in his comments and feed that highlight how people feel about the world of work.

“Over the last few months, I noticed that a lot of companies were suffering financial issues. There was a downturn in their numbers,” he tells Fast Company. “People were trying to assess whether a recession was happening in the United States.”

Levey posted a video about workplace recession indicators that employees should look out for, including companies slashing expenses on business trips, bonuses, holiday parties, and even office snacks. Companies often start with the little things when tightening their belts, he says, and these cuts could be an early signal that a business is preparing for tougher economic conditions.

“Upper management and leadership, they probably get together and they say, ‘Okay, we need to cut costs. What can we do? Let’s cut the low-hanging fruit,’” he says.

Cutting now to save later

Companies do this, Levey says, because they’re looking six, 12, or even 18 months ahead, and taking preemptive steps to put themselves in a better position for the next fiscal year.

These cuts may seem trivial to some. But for other employees, they can be incredibly important. Levey says he regularly hears from people who have full-time jobs but have to live in their cars because they can no longer afford rent.

“A lot of employees are really struggling because the day-to-day costs are so high that they can’t pay their bills,” Levey says. “If you’re somebody who can’t pay their rent, trying to figure out how you’re going to get your next meal, and you can go into your company’s kitchen and get a snack—that makes a big difference.”

According to Chris Marvel Davis, cofounder of Pivot, a training and development provider that runs workshops on wellness, burnout, and leadership, snacks may be what people notice first. But there are other, less obvious signs that a company is becoming more cautious with its spending. 

Travel and conference requests getting denied or delayed may not be immediately apparent, he tells Fast Company. Other signs include open roles staying unfilled, job postings disappearing, backfills for departed employees being paused, and contractors being let go before full-time employees.

“Small purchases need bigger approvals,” he adds. “When a $200 expense suddenly needs a director’s sign-off, someone in finance is watching cash closely.”

Is it routine or a sign of something bigger?

Companies make these kinds of cuts first because they’re easier to reverse, Davis says. On their own, they may simply suggest that leadership is being careful.

“They buy leadership time to see how bad things might get without alarming employees or customers,” he says. “One sign by itself doesn’t tell you much, but several at once are worth taking seriously.”

People should also pay attention to what happens the next time someone leaves, Chiquita Hall-Jackson, an employment attorney at Hall-Jackson & Associates, tells Fast Company.

“If the company leaves the position vacant, divides the work among the remaining team, and still expects the same output, that tells you more about its staffing plans than a cut to the travel budget,” she says. “When that pattern shows up across teams, it’s worth asking whether leadership is planning to operate with fewer people.”

According to Hall-Jackson, it’s also a good idea to look at what’s being cut and how long the cuts last. A canceled off-site meeting may save money once, but a hiring freeze changes how the business operates.

“One budget decision can have plenty of explanations,” she says. “Several decisions that consistently reduce staffing or hours are more meaningful.”

Another potential warning sign is a change in the company’s management structure. If there are fewer middle managers than there used to be, and larger teams are now reporting to more senior supervisors, that could suggest the company is looking to operate with fewer people, Levey says.

It’s more important than ever to have leverage

Of course, many of these signs could just mean that an individual company isn’t doing well and may not indicate a wider, looming recession. 

But in this tough economy and job market, employees should consider being more sensitive to changes at work that may hint at something bigger happening, Levey says. He adds that it’s important to maintain a sense of where you stand within the company, how secure your position is, and what the general temperature is when it comes to potential layoffs.

“You want to pay attention to messages from the CEO or upper management. Because if you think that your job is in jeopardy, then while you’re still employed there and making a wage, you’ve got to start looking elsewhere to see what your other options are,” Levey says.

The worst thing is being blindsided, so it’s always a good idea to make sure you have some leverage, he notes. “It doesn’t mean you need to microanalyze every single thing that happens with the company. But be a realist. Keep your eyes open, really understand what’s going on. That way, you’re less surprised if these things happen.”
