When aging telecommunications routers overloaded and forced the Federal Aviation Administration to issue a sudden nationwide ground stop, thousands of flights were frozen across the country. For the millions of travelers stranded in terminals, and for the air traffic controllers forced to manage congested skies with equipment built over two decades ago, the crisis was no surprise. It was the predictable result of a political system where lawmakers routinely put local earmarks and self-interest ahead of public safety.

​Across major hubs from Newark and Chicago to Atlanta, the invisible digital backbone keeping aircraft separated is running on borrowed time. Legacy copper wiring, analog voice switches, and obsolete radar systems need a complete, $50 billion overhaul. It is not just internal aviation officials sounding the alarm. Independent aviation experts, safety boards, and aerospace engineering coalitions have repeatedly cautioned that the nation’s air traffic control network is operating on a dangerous margin of error. Yet even as these warnings mount, the money required to fix the system remains trapped behind a wall of congressional gridlock.
