Emissions and SAF costs are now a standing line in European airlines’ results, arriving while fuel prices are already high. The question for 2027 is how much they can pass on to passengers.

European airlines’ climate bills are rising. Free carbon allowances under the EU’s emissions trading system ended this year, and the share of costlier sustainable aviation fuel they must use keeps climbing.

International Airlines Group, which owns British Airways and Iberia, paid €359 million ($395 million) in carbon costs in the first nine months of 2026, up 21% from a year earlier. The group said the end of free allowances in the EU and UK contributed to the increase.

Under the EU Emissions Trading System (EU ETS), airlines flying within Europe must surrender one allowance for every metric ton of CO2 or equivalent they emit. Allowances are traded on the market, but until this year airlines also received part of theirs free. That free allocation ended in 2026, so airlines must now buy every allowance they need. European airlines are now paying for every ton of carbon they emit on intra-European flights, and the cost is showing up in earnings reports. Free allowances under the EU Emissions Trading System and the UK's equivalent scheme ended in 2026, while the EU's ReFuelEU Aviation mandate requires at least 2% sustainable aviation fuel in jet fuel at EU airports, rising to 6% in 2030 and 70% by 2050. IAG, owner of British Airways and Iberia, paid €359 million ($395 million) in carbon costs in the first nine months of 2026, up 21% year over year, and said the end of free allowances in the EU and UK contributed. Air France-KLM reported €484 million ($532 million) in combined CO2 allowance and SAF costs for the nine months to September, up about 9%. Finnair's finance chief put the extra cost at roughly €10 million ($11 million) a quarter and said that level "seems to continue here." Lufthansa expects SAF mandates to cost it €200 million in 2026. These bills land alongside jet fuel at $4.02 a gallon as of Nov. 13, and a proposed EU ETS expansion would extend coverage to departing international flights of up to 5,000 km — exempting most routes to the U.S. and China.
