Jensen Huang was gone for 11 days. That was Nvidia’s succession test, and the board failed it

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Jensen Huang’s return to Nvidia is being told as a comeback. The shares jumped about 30% on the news. The founder was back at his desk. Order restored.

I read it differently. Those 11 days were the first real test of whether Nvidia could function without the man who built it. The board failed that test, and the way it failed should worry directors at every company whose CEO looks irreplaceable.

On November 12, Nvidia announced that its board had accepted Huang’s resignation, after reports about his private life and a foreign intelligence operation aimed at him. CFO Colette Kress became interim CEO. The board said it would consider internal and external candidates.

Five days later, on the earnings call, chair Harvey Jones told analysts the board did “not intend to reinstate Jensen.”

Then the alternatives fell away. The Financial Times reported that the board had approached Rene Haas, the Arm CEO who spent seven years at Nvidia. On November 23, Haas extended his contract at Arm through 2033. The same day, Nvidia’s directors voted unanimously to bring Huang back, saying they had received input from senior levels of the U.S. government. Trump told CNBC that he made a personal assurance that sealed the decision.

The board’s own independent investigation is still open. The directors reinstated their CEO before their own inquiry had finished.

That’s the detail I can’t get past. A board that says no on a Tuesday and yes six days later has run out of options.

A CEO nobody can replace is a CEO nobody can refuse.

Accountability depends on a credible alternative. When a board has a successor ready, its questions carry weight, because everyone in the room knows the company survives if the answers fall short. When it has nobody, the CEO holds the leverage and both sides know it. The duty to oversee him turns into a duty to keep him.

Huang has run Nvidia since he co-founded it in 1993. The board had three decades to prepare for the day he wasn’t there. When that day came, it had a CFO doing two jobs, one outside name, and a market that wanted him back. Yale’s Jeffrey Sonnenfeld wrote in Fortune the board had no Plan B.

Investors made the same point from the other side. Within days of the resignation, prominent shareholders were urging the board to keep the door open to Huang. Nobody outside the company had the facts. What they were pricing was a business with no one else to run it.

The board has since created a national security committee and is hiring a chief compliance officer. Those are controls on conduct. Neither one is a successor.

I coach CEOs, and I’ve watched how boards arrive here. Nobody decides to skip succession. It gets postponed, one good quarter at a time.

Over the years a founder’s identity and the company’s fuse. Raising the question of who comes next starts to feel like disloyalty, so directors leave it for another meeting. The CEO rarely pushes either. Planning your own exit means picturing a company that no longer needs you, and that is the hardest thing to ask of someone whose whole identity is the company.

So both sides stay comfortable. The cost arrives on the one day the board needs a choice and finds it doesn’t have one.

Plenty of boards are living with the same arrangement right now, and it’s invisible for as long as the results are good.

Two tests will tell a director where their board stands.

First, name the person who could run the company on Monday. One name, already inside the building, whom the top team would follow. If the honest answer is the CFO as caretaker, you have a holding pattern.

Second, recall the last time the board said no to the CEO on something he cared about. If nobody can remember one, the board has already stopped overseeing him. It just hasn’t been tested yet.

Huang may prove to be the right leader for Nvidia’s next chapter. The investigation may answer every open question. Neither would change what the 11 days showed.

The board now enters the next crisis in a weaker position than the last one. It has shown its investors, its government and its CEO that it has no alternative to him. He knows it. So does everyone who will ever need to challenge him.

Every director reading this should ask the question Nvidia’s board had to answer in public.

If our CEO were gone tomorrow, would we have a choice?
