Why a Short Week Can Feel So Long

Posted November 15, 2026 | Reviewed by Jessica Schrader

In interviews for my research on leaders, I asked executives to describe a difficult week. One leader talked me through an annual report, tax paperwork, fundraising, board work, and decisions about products and events. When I asked what was hard, she didn't single out any one task, but said, “It's not that those things are too much; it's just the changing of direction every day.”

She could handle the questions, meetings, and requests. What wore her down was having to stop all the normal stuff to deal with the annual report or tax filing, then switch to something else, and then figure out where she'd left off.

I drive my ninth-grade son to school, and yesterday he said it “felt like a long school week.” Interestingly, he had Tuesday off, an early dismissal Wednesday, and a late start Thursday… he actually spent less time at school. So, how could it feel longer!?

It reminded me of the leader story. He and I talked about routines and how little attention we pay to things when we know what comes next. Bottom line… it felt exhausting to have his normal schedule interrupted.

Let's go take a look at what research says about having to change directions so often.

What did our interrupted leader do? She tried to put some order around the week, setting aside one morning for product development and another stretch of time for board work. Then an event would need attention, or another responsibility would intrude. One day, a staff member left with little notice, and the website, online store, and event preparation were back on her desk. She could plan the week, but keeping it that way was basically impossible.

In 2001, researchers Joshua Rubinstein and his colleagues asked people to switch back and forth between simple tasks, like sorting shapes or doing math. Even small changes took extra time. Harder tasks made it worse, while a clue about what was coming next helped. This wasn't the same as someone interrupting you at work, but it shows that changing gears isn't free. My son knew that his strange school schedule would be back to normal the following week, but the executive had no idea what would interrupt her next.

But the lost time isn't the only issue. I get to interview spouses in my work. The leader mentioned above? Her husband saw what those days did to her. He told me she came home worn out with a long to-do list still on her mind. When she couldn't finish it, he said it “really puts a burden... a strain, on her.” Researcher Sophie Leroy studied something similar in 2009. In two experiments, she found that people had trouble letting go of a task they hadn't finished. Even after moving on to something else, part of their attention was still on the earlier job. She called that “attention residue.”

Think about sitting down to talk with an employee while you're still worrying about the budget you left unfinished.

Here's the most surprising finding to me around this subject: in 2008, Gloria Mark and her colleagues had people do typical office email tasks. Some were interrupted by calls or instant messages. The interrupted people actually finished their work FASTER… and their work wasn't noticeably worse (although their email replies were shorter). So why not be interrupted all day if you’re more productive? Those interrupted workers felt more stressed, frustrated, and rushed, and said the work took more effort.

The executive's week wasn't hard because of any one task. It was having to stop, switch, and figure out where she'd left off, over and over again. My son's week wasn't harder because of the assignments. It was having to think about what comes next and what comes after and hold that in the front of his mind.

Remember Sophie Leroy's finding about “attention residue”? In 2018, she and Theresa Glomb tested a way to reduce it. In their studies, people took a moment before switching tasks to think about where they'd stopped and how they'd pick up again. They called it a “ready-to-resume” plan. It helped people focus on the new task instead of worrying so much about the unfinished one.

So, here's how to fix a heavy, mixed-up week. You're halfway through a report when the phone rings. Before taking the call, jot down where you stopped and what you'll do next when you return. That's it. Now you can give the caller your attention without trying to remember every detail of the unfinished report.

My son's schedule will be back to normal next week. The executive can't count on that. But when she has to change direction, she can take a moment to write down where she left off. That won't stop the interruptions, but it will keep her from spending so much time trying to get back to work.

To lead sustainably, when you have to stop what you're doing, take a moment to plan how you'll start again.
