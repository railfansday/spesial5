The Psychology Behind “Overreacting”

Couples don’t break up because they argue. They break up because they feel alone while arguing.

If you’ve ever thought, “We just can’t communicate,” you might be asking the wrong question. The real puzzle isn’t why couples fight. It’s this: Why do people who love each other deeply end up triggering each other so consistently?

Emotionally focused therapy (EFT), an empirically-supported model of couple therapy grounded in attachment science, offers a clear answer: Love is not a negotiation problem; it's a bonding problem (Johnson, 2020). More than three decades of research have established EFT as an effective, evidence-based approach for helping couples move from distress to secure connection (Johnson, 2020). And bonding is biological.

Attachment theory, originally developed by Bowlby (1969, 1982) and extended to adult relationships (Mikulincer & Shaver, 2007), conceptualizes close relationships as emotional bonds that regulate safety, distress, and connection. In this framework, romantic partners become primary attachment figures; people we turn to for reassurance, comfort, and security in moments of stress (Johnson, 2019).

When your partner turns away mid-argument, your brain doesn’t process it as a small irritation but as a threat. From an attachment perspective, humans are wired to seek emotional closeness in moments of distress. When connection feels unavailable, the nervous system activates what researchers call protest behaviors: anger, criticism, defensiveness, or withdrawal (Johnson, 2008).These responses don't reflect character flaws; they're adaptive survival strategies. Attachment research shows that these emotional reactions are part of an evolved system designed to keep us connected (Bowlby, 1969, 1982; Johnson, 2019). What looks like “drama” is often attachment fear.

We often tell couples to improve their communication by using “I” statements, remaining calm, and compromising. This advice can help but it's not enough. Communication skills do not regulate an activated attachment system. When someone fears abandonment, logic does not soothe them. When someone fears inadequacy, confrontation feels threatening.Lasting change in relationships doesn't occur in arguments. A growing body of EFT outcome research shows that it occurs through emotional responsiveness—the experience of reaching for a partner and having them respond (Johnson, 2020).Secure love develops when the answer to the question “Are you there for me?” is consistently yes (Johnson, 2008). Without that, technique feels empty.

Attachment unfolds within social systems. Couples live inside structures shaped by race, gender, immigration status, class, sexuality, and power. These contexts shape how safe, or unsafe, connection feels.Recent developments in EFT and related relational frameworks highlight that attachment experiences are deeply influenced by sociocultural realities, including trauma, marginalization, and systemic inequality (Johnson & Campbell, 2022). When one partner has experienced chronic marginalization, their attachment system may be more easily activated. What appears as “oversensitivity” may reflect accumulated threat. Emotional safety is not only created within the relationship; it is also shaped by the world outside it. Dismissing a partner’s lived experience can represent rupture.

The turning point in attachment-based therapy occurs when partners risk vulnerability instead of protection. Instead of saying, “You never care about me," they say things like, “When you walk away, I feel scared that I don’t matter.” Instead of telling a partner they're "too sensitive," they say, “I didn’t realize you felt alone. I don’t want you to feel that way.” These are important communication—and attachment—shifts.

Process research in EFT shows that when vulnerability is met with responsiveness, the attachment bond reorganizes, creating a sense of safety and reducing emotional reactivity (Johnson, 2020). The nervous system learns that closeness is safe, and love becomes a regulator rather than a trigger.

We live in a culture that glorifies independence. We are taught that strong adults should not “need” anyone, that reliance is weakness, and that self-sufficiency is maturity. Attachment science tells a different story: Humans are wired for interdependence. Emotional closeness is not regression but regulation (Johnson, 2019). And secure relationships are built on accessibility, responsiveness, and engagement. Needing a partner is not dysfunction; it's biology.

Love falters when fear replaces responsiveness. The research is clear: Secure love isn’t built on turning toward each other when it would be easier to turn away (Johnson, 2020). In a culture that prizes independence, emotional responsiveness may be one of the most radical acts we can practice.
