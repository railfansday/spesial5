Are You Dating Someone's Potential Future Self?

Posted November 8, 2026 | Reviewed by Jessica Schrader

Here's the difference between dating and the NBA draft—besides the fact that you may not be selecting partners for their dribbling. NBA teams often draft a player based on potential, on what the player may become in a few years. That's because teams typically already have players who can take the court now while waiting for the new player to blossom. But unless you are already dating a team of people—which would be another issue—dating is about who the person is here and now.

Yet, how many times have you dated people based on whom they may become rather than whom they currently are? Like, oh, they would be so great if and when they get their act together, get a job, mature, realize their faults, become kinder and more responsible, start taking care of themselves, re-do that mullet, or whatever may or may not happen in the future. It can be easy to fall into this trap, especially if you've already invested time and effort into the relationship and view finding someone else as way too much effort.

The biggest problem, of course, is the word "if," because dating for potential is not like microwaving a sweet potato. You may hope or even believe that a change will happen. But you don't know what the outcome may be, and if it's ever going to get done.

The "when" is a problem too. Sure, dating someone's future self could make sense if you have a time machine and can see that exactly 45 days from now a major desired realization or transformation will occur. That may not be too long to wait, particularly if you can be at a spa for 44 of those days. But are you willing to wait years or even decades?

Plus, again, you could be waiting. And waiting. And waiting. And waiting for Godot—or whatever your partner's name is—to change. Meanwhile, you are losing the one thing that you can never get back—time.

Now, a potential trap might be somehow thinking that without you, the person will never change. So, you are basically the Neo of "The Matrix," you are "The One" with the responsibility for the relationship. As a result, how could you possibly leave?

Umm, [insert game-show buzzer sound for incorrect answer here.] While you can certainly provide help, that person has to already want to change. That person has to already be making the effort to change. Whether change happens will never depend solely on you. Even if you made all the effort in the world, change may never happen.

It's better, then, to ask yourself whether you would still date the person if nothing ever changed. Would you be better than OK with who the person is now? Because in life all that you have is what's here and now.

Heck, things could even get worse. That avoidant attachment style could get even more avoidant. That lack of drive could shift into overdrive—or perhaps under-drive. That constant criticism could reach a critical level. Life stressors in the future could magnify what is already present.

Even seemingly positive circumstances can make the currently bad things worse. For example, it's been said that money and power will simply amplify what's already there. So, those who start off as kind may use their newfound resources to help others. On the other hand, those who are deep-down nasty or otherwise broken people may use their newfound resources to exert power over and punish others.

Of course, this doesn't mean that you should expect anyone to be perfect already—or ever. Perfection is unachievable, with the possible exception of sushi from Kanazawa, Japan. There will always be things that you'd prefer to change. Maybe it's your partner's Crocs, velour tracksuits, or body hair. Maybe you don't want your partner dancing the robot at every social gathering. Maybe getting a finger stuck up your nose while you are sleeping is not good. Perhaps there's way too much farting on that side of the bed. But compromising is about being willing to tolerate things that aren't your non-negotiables.

The question is whether the core things that you need are already in place. This shouldn't be a list of 500 things but instead a focused list of the non-negotiables. If you feel your current significant other is missing some of these core things—especially after discussing it with them—then it looks like this person is not your future—at least in the present. So, why not make the person your past?
