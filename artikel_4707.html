Abusers Hide Behind a Mask of Sanity

Hervey Cleckley, a professor of psychiatry and neurology, wrote a seminal book The Mask of Sanity, which was published in 1941. It provided the first in-depth depiction of psychopathology, which is now integrated into the broader category of the Dark Tetrad: narcissism, Machiavellianism, psychopathy, and everyday sadism.

Cleckley’s insights are supported by advancements in psychology and neuroscience, but many are not trained to identify individuals who suffer from these traits. The key takeaway is the mask, and learning to see past it can keep individuals and organizations safer. The Dark Tetrad wear a mask of healthy and positive mental traits, while beneath it resides a harmful self.

Our tendency is to think goodness and evil, kindness and cruelty, cannot subsist in the same individual. However, the Dark Tetrad are exemplars of this blend. Our tendency is to think that medicine effectively diagnoses Dark Tetrad disorders and enables the law to protects us from them. In fact, the Dark Tetrad are adept at sliding beneath the radar.

Cleckley tackles the significant problem facing medicine and law in that those who suffer from psychopathology appear sane, competent, and rational, even though they suffer from what he diagnoses as a “severe mental disorder” making them “more dangerous to themselves and to others” than mental patients who are institutionalized.

In 2012, researchers Sun, Zhao, Yang and Fan show that positive psychological resources such as hope, efficacy, resilience, and optimism are not removed from those in the Dark Tetrad. Instead they blend in and fuel their aversive traits. The grandiosity of a narcissist, what Cleckley identifies as the trait of “pathological egocentricity,” makes them appear hopeful and optimistic.

The psychopath may appear calm and collected in crises and high-stress environments, but their resilience, as documented in the work of psychologists Babiak and Hare, and neuroscientist Baron-Cohen is grounded in a lack of emotions and empathy. Cleckley specifies psychopaths show “general poverty in major affective reactions,” and they specifically “lack remorse or shame.”

Cleckley notes that these individuals have an “absence of delusions and other signs of irrational thinking,” along with an “absence of nervousness” and “psychoneurotic manifestations.” Without identifiable markers of mental illness, it’s very difficult to assess the danger and harm of Dark Tetrad individuals.

Cleckley signals the “superficial charm” and “good intelligence” of psychopaths that further mislead us. Those with highly aversive traits—like those in the Dark Tetrad—will frequently have defenders, supporters, patrons, and even a cult-following. Despite this being well-established in history and research, we still struggle to avoid hiring and enabling disordered and dangerous individuals in workplaces.

Recent analysis of psychopaths confirm that they integrate as if mentally stable and sane into the workforce and can be found concentrated in certain industries and roles. Clive R. Boddy’s research found a greater concentration of psychopathic personalities in finance, insurance, government and education.

Even though these sectors require a great deal of trust, transparency, and fiduciary duty, they are not adept at ensuring that those prone to dishonesty, manipulation, and deception are kept at bay. Boddy found that approximately 28% of participants in the study reported “psychopathic behaviour” in governmental and educational settings.

Forensic psychologist John Clarke, author of Working with Monsters, notes that psychopathic personalities are attracted to power, prestige, and risk-taking. They seek industries with power structures (government and education), along with those offering monetary gain and risk (finance and insurance).

In The Gaslit Brain, I designed a multi-pronged strategy to better identify the Dark Tetrad at work. Each of the proven ways to see through the fictional mask of the abuser is informed by neuroscientific research.

Step 1: Gaslighting is one of the Dark Tetrad’s favourite weapons. Step one in identifying gaslighting’s harmful effects is to keep your brain healthy and fit. Your neuroplasticity – your brain’s ability to strengthen based on deliberate practice—means you can practice skepticism, practice asking questions rather than simply believing, practice focusing on acts rather than words, track record rather than promises.

Step 2: The Dark Tetrad strive to interrupt and confuse your relationship with your own inner-self. It not only makes you doubt your mind’s ability to assess reality or sort through memories, it also tries to make you misinterpret or dismiss the wealth of information you get from your instinctual systems. Pay attention to temperature, heart rate, gut distress, tension in muscles, bracing of your body. Your gut instinct is highly adept at giving you important information that can keep you safer. The goal is to develop accurate and nuanced vocabulary to interpret what it’s telling your brain.

Step 3: Manipulators who wear the mask of trustworthiness, reliability, optimism and resilience, who capitalize on their charisma and intelligence, often use your fear of shame or even disappointment that could lead you to be blocked from your community, as a way to distract you with fear. When your brain focuses on threat, it struggles to problem-solve, be productive or creative that the abuser uses to confirm their discrediting of you in the community. Learn to identify rising stress and instead of withdrawing, lean in to connections. Resist the impulse to isolate, and instead prioritize relationships. Mindfulness, exercise, being in nature are all well-backed by research to lower stress levels and return agency to you. In that more calm state, strategize.

Finally, the Dark Tetrad has no problem telling self-serving lies or using their power, prestige, and social credibility to hide the destructive person behind the mask. Their goal is to make you complicit, so do not dismiss micro-aggressions or early signs of concerning conduct; do not default to doubting those who report the abuser; do not give second chances or you run the risk of being complicit with the harm they do.
