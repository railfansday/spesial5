Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth Is in a Battle for His Own Survival at the Pentagon

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

The day after the midterm elections, President Donald Trump met with Susie Wiles in the Oval Office. He asked his chief of staff whether she believed Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth would survive the impeachment attempt that would soon be launched by a member of his own party.

Hegseth was in a precarious position. He had been among the most aggressive champions of the war with Iran, widely seen as the policy most responsible for the shellacking Republicans had taken the night before. Wiles remained officially supportive of Hegseth throughout her time in the White House, but she had harbored concerns about the secretary from the start. She did not believe he was forthright about his skeletons during a rocky confirmation process, and she considered the subsequent Signalgate scandal an amateurish disaster.

In the Wednesday meeting, according to a senior White House official, Wiles made clear that the war, the uncertainty of its next steps, and the inflation it had fueled all contributed to the midterms debacle. She offered her resignation, but Trump declined. On Thursday night, in a contentious meeting in the Oval Office, she offered to resign again. Trump announced the next day that Wiles would leave the White House for his outside political operation.

Hegseth’s fate was still uncertain. A deeply unpopular figure among the American public, presiding over an even more unpopular war, he faced an impeachment push led by Representative Thomas Massie. “He is just failing,” said Republican senator Thom Tillis of North Carolina—who voted to confirm Hegseth at the start of Trump’s term—in an interview with Vanity Fair. “In the same way that Kristi Noem failed at Homeland Security, he simply doesn’t have the instincts or the prior experience as an executive that is necessary for this particular role.”

The secretary of defense has now embarked on a campaign to save his job, and Stephen Miller’s elevation to replace Wiles as Trump’s chief of staff has helped to ensure a reprieve. Trump has so far backed Hegseth publicly, and last week, House Republicans blocked the impeachment effort. “Pete is a fighter,” the White House official explained. “Trump likes fighters.” And there are other reasons to keep him in place, the official said: Confirming a replacement will not be easy, particularly when Democrats take control of Congress, and ousting Hegseth would signal an admission that at this point, Trump’s war is a failure.

It was the president’s taste for staffers out of central casting that helped put Hegseth—a weekend morning television host with no experience in government, but with the square jaw of an Army Wives extra—in charge of the Department of Defense. He has relied on his talents before the cameras, and a team of advisers attuned to the production of his persona, to both keep his job and sell Trump on aggressive military action from the Pacific to the Persian Gulf. His survival has depended in part on his ability to project strength—not to the public, or to the rank-and-file, but to the president.

“In just under two years, Secretary Hegseth has worked side-by-side with President Trump to restore American power on the world stage, build the strongest military in generations, and instill a warrior ethos at the Pentagon,” White House spokeswoman Olivia Wales said in a statement. “President Trump and Secretary Hegseth have taken bold, necessary action to deny the Iranian regime the ability to develop a nuclear weapon, bring narco-terrorist Nicolas Maduro to American justice, and defend our homeland from dangerous terrorists and cartels—saving American lives.”

“They will stand behind him in the short term,” said a second senior administration official. And yet, “He is a cost. He’s constantly requiring people to spend political capital on him. The White House is constantly having to come in and save him. The question is, at what point is it too much of a cost to the president?”

The morning after Hegseth survived the impeachment attempt, he attended a Veterans Day ceremony at the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier. Hegseth was joined at the sacred memorial by his closest aides: Jennifer Hegseth, his third wife and a former Fox News producer who serves as an informal but influential adviser; his chief of staff Ricky Buria, a Marine Corps veteran who followed an unusual path into the top brass; and Tami Radabaugh, another former Fox News producer whom Hegseth hired as a senior adviser.

As the group posed for photos at the Tomb, they chatted and laughed loudly. A US Army sentinel guarding the memorial turned to the group and shouted out a reprimand: “All visitors will maintain an atmosphere of silence and respect at all times.” After video of the moment made for embarrassing headlines, Hegseth awarded the soldier a Joint Service Commendation Medal and posed alongside several Army officers for a photoshoot that was posted on his social media accounts. The Pentagon did not elaborate on why a soldier was awarded a medal for an act as pedestrian as scolding noisy attendees at a memorial. “Hegseth would not have given him that award had the incident not been on TV,” said a former senior defense official.

The small group of advisers beside Hegseth has helped the secretary weather a series of scandals in his two years as defense secretary using stage-managed displays of toughness. “He’s a cross between a carnival barker and a personal trainer,” said John Ullyot, who served as chief Pentagon spokesperson and senior adviser to Hegseth until he left the department last year. A loyal Trump backer, Ullyot initially supported Hegseth’s calls to restore standards and excise identity politics from the military. He has since emerged as a fierce public critic and now believes Hegseth should resign.

In his new book, The President Deserves Better, Ullyot describes a secretary of defense fixated on self-promotion and content creation. Jennifer plays an instrumental role in those efforts, directing the secretary’s social media and a small battalion of accounts that support him online. “It was as if she was producing him in his role as Secretary,” Ullyot writes. She has helped produce—and starred in—dramatic videos of Hegseth posted by the Pentagon’s official social media accounts. “Her entire job is to make his image,” one source told Vanity Fair.

House Republican Thomas Massie, who brought the articles of impeachment against Hegseth last week, argued the bombing was “the result of systemic deterioration and removal of Department of Defense infrastructure that Congress had mandated by law to limit collateral damage to civilians.”

Her role extended well beyond production, alarming senior officials in the Department of Defense. She interviewed candidates for Pentagon roles. She attended sensitive bilateral meetings with foreign officials. She was included in Signal chats that discussed the war plans for the Houthi operation in Yemen. The former defense official said they warned Hegseth about his wife’s involvement in Pentagon affairs. “She did not have a fucking clearance,” they said. “She shouldn’t be privy to this shit.”

Hegseth himself seemed too smitten to care. He covered the walls of his office, as well as other meeting rooms and corridors throughout the Pentagon, with large photos of himself and his wife. When The New York Times examined Jennifer’s influence at the department over the summer, Pentagon spokesman Sean Parnell called the former cable news producer “an accomplished leader.” He added that the Pentagon “not only welcomes her current leadership but actively plans to involve her even more deeply in the future.” Politico later reported that while advising the secretary of defense, Jennifer was being paid for consulting work by Watchtower Strategies, a Washington lobbying shop that counts Lockheed Martin as a client.

She wasn’t the only Fox News producer at the Pentagon. Hegseth also gave Radabaugh—a former producer on Fox & Friends Weekend with no government experience—broad authority over department affairs, I’m told. Radabaugh made sure that glamorous photos and videos of Hegseth were published online promptly, sometimes scolding aides for taking too long to post. In the first few months of Hegseth’s tenure, she ordered Ullyot to turn a room just off the briefing room into a makeup studio.

“This was ridiculous. I said not only no, but hell no,” Ullyot writes. He warned that news of the makeup studio would leak. “How exactly did a makeup studio fit into the warrior ethos?”

In response to a detailed list of questions, Pentagon spokesman Joel Valdez declined to answer many of them but emailed a lengthy statement criticizing Ullyot. “There is no ‘makeup studio’ in the Pentagon, and it never existed,” Valdez said, going on to call Ullyot “a skinwalker,” a “sour puss coward,” and “generally a stupid person” who left “his job in disgrace.” (According to information reviewed by Vanity Fair, Hegseth praised Ullyot after his departure from the Pentagon and offered to recommend him to Susie Wiles, then Trump’s chief of staff.)

“I volunteered to help stand up the Secretary’s press operation for a few months at the start of his term,” Ullyot told Vanity Fair in response. “I left as planned, and the Secretary thanked me for my work. My record over more than 30 years, including as a senior spokesman in the Senate and in the Trump White House, speaks for itself.”

Hegseth was bullish on the war with Iran from the start. “There weren’t any real cheerleaders for the war in the cabinet outside of Pete Hegseth,” said the second official. “It was Pete and [US Ambassador to Israel] Mike Huckabee.”

Soon after his confirmation, Hegseth moved to increase his powers and consolidate control over the Pentagon. He ousted generals and subjected senior staff to polygraph tests to weed out leakers, a crusade that resulted in the abrupt firings of top officials that demoralized staff. He tapped Tim Parlatore, his personal lawyer, as a special adviser and charged him with overseeing leak investigations. Parlatore continued to run his own private practice as a criminal defense attorney, and Politico reported this week that while serving as Hegseth’s top adviser, he is representing the son of a Libyan warlord accused of extrajudicial killings and torture.

In keeping with the president’s broad campaign for retribution, Hegseth ordered an investigation into retired Joint Chiefs Chairman General Mark Milley, who had clashed with Trump during his first term, and stripped the four-star general of his security detail. The Pentagon is now working on a new report on the 2021 Kabul Airport attack, during which 13 American soldiers and 170 Afghan civilians were killed in a suicide bombing during the withdrawal from Afghanistan. At the time, Milley served as chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff. The new assessment, one source familiar with the investigation said, is expected by the end of the month and is intended to serve as a predicate to strip Milley of his four-star rank.

Hegseth waged a parallel war on the press, starting with an attempt to ban NBC News correspondent Courtney Kube from the Pentagon. In a meeting with his advisers, Ullyot writes, Hegseth explained that he and his wife Jennifer were “angry” at Kube for how much she covered his turbulent confirmation process before Congress. “I thought this was loony tunes,” Ullyot writes. One source told me that after NBC News was kicked out of its Pentagon office, Hegseth aides took a full-length mirror from the vacated room and installed it in his makeup studio.

As Hegseth purged senior officials, he grew closer to Buria. The relationship was confounding to many inside the building: Hegseth has pushed to cleanse the Pentagon of ideological diversity, yet he elevated Buria, a holdover from the Biden administration who had previously donated to Democrats. But Buria had earned the trust of Jennifer. When explaining to advisers why he was elevated, Hegseth said he prayed about the decision with his wife. “And you know what? Jen says he’s really good with the kids,” Hegseth told them.

Buria was by Hegseth’s side throughout the Signalgate scandal, which, according to several administration officials, was a turning point for the secretary. “Every problem he is having in one way or another goes back to Signalgate,” the second Trump official said. “His extremely hostile relations with the press, his paranoia, his firing of certain people, denied promotions to generals. It was all around paranoia related to Signalgate.”

“There’s two versions of Hegseth,” said a second former defense official. “There is the pre-Signalgate version and the post-Signalgate version. It’s like Jekyll and Hyde. He was Dr. Jekyll until around Signalgate, and he’s been Mr. Hyde ever since. He’s just not making good decisions.”

It was an almost unbelievable incident: Hegseth shared war plans for a bombing campaign against the Houthis in Yemen on at least two Signal group chats, including one that inadvertently included Jeffrey Goldberg, the editor in chief of The Atlantic.

This was the first major military engagement of Hegseth’s tenure, but he chose to survey the operation with Buria from a temporary secure facility at his Fort McNair home rather than make the short trip to the Pentagon or the White House Situation Room. The decision alarmed some at the Pentagon. “It’s your first major military operation,” said the second senior Trump official. “Why would you monitor it from your shitty SCIF in your basement? Why not go 10 minutes to the Pentagon where you have the joint staff?”

“You would think with kinetic operations going on in the Middle East that could have dramatic consequences for service members, that he’d be in the office,” Tillis said.

Trump has so far backed Hegseth publicly, and last week, House Republicans blocked the impeachment effort. “Pete is a fighter,” the White House official explained. “Trump likes fighters.”

During Hegseth’s confirmation process, allegations of heavy drinking concerned Republican lawmakers he needed to vote in favor of his nomination. At the time, several Republican senators said he promised them he would stop boozing if confirmed. He affirmed this pledge publicly: “This is the biggest deployment of my life,” he told Megyn Kelly in a 2024 interview, “and there won’t be a drop of alcohol on my lips while I’m doing it.”

Whether or not Hegseth has actually quit drinking continues to be a source of palace intrigue. One official recalled watching Hegseth drink what the secretary suggested were two cocktails—though the official conceded he hadn’t seen the drinks poured, and raised a theory that Hegseth might have been testing whether he would leak the incident to the press. “This was at a time of peak paranoia,” the official said.

Buria was known to drink on official trips; the official recalled Hegseth’s aide handing out shots to passengers on a US government jet during a Pentagon junket. Two other officials said Buria would often brag about drinking with Hegseth, including on an official trip to Panama in 2025. The New York Post later reported that Buria had made up a story about him and Hegseth donning disguises to go out and drink, all in an effort to weed out leakers. One of the officials told me they believed the leak claim was Buria’s retroactive effort to cover up that he was boasting about drinking with the secretary.

“The drinking is what they’re really afraid of,” the second official said. When asked directly if Hegseth continued drinking as secretary, a Defense Department spokesperson who declined to be named said in a statement: “No, the Secretary has kept his promise that he made during his confirmation hearings and has not drank since becoming Secretary of War.”

Jennifer and Pete Hegseth have forged a close relationship with Stephen Miller and his wife Katie, their neighbors on a placid and heavily fortified army base south of the White House. Hegseth’s temporary salvation comes thanks in part to support from Miller, who values his deference to Trump. “Stephen doesn’t like all the Liberace shit, the cucaracha shit,” said the first former official. “Pete right now is fresh out of friends, and misery loves company. But don’t get it twisted. At any moment, at any second, Stephen will cut Pete’s throat.”

The secretary of defense has made few friends in Washington. One source recalled attending a holiday party stocked with members of the administration during which Hegseth spent much of his time alone in the corner of the room. “Even Kash Patel had people coming up to him to talk,” the source said. “Pete was alone and isolated. He looked depressed. And this was before the Iran war. His stock has not gone up since then.”

Hegseth has channeled much of his effort into appealing to an audience of one, telling advisers he only cares about Trump’s opinion. “The one thing he’s been successful at, and I think it’s the only thing, is flattering Donald Trump constantly,” the former official said.

The purpose of this flattery campaign extends beyond keeping his current job. In private, Hegseth has confided in several officials about his ambitions to run for president. This jockeying has exacerbated tensions with Vice President JD Vance. The relationship was already strained by Vance’s private opposition to the Iran war, and it deteriorated significantly after the vice president’s close friend, Dan Driscoll, was pushed out of his position as Army Secretary in August following months of friction with Hegseth.

When explaining to advisers why Buria was elevated, Hegseth said he prayed about the decision with his wife. “And you know what? Jen says he’s really good with the kids,” Hegseth told them.

“Secretary Hegseth and Vice President Vance share an outstanding working relationship grounded in deep mutual respect and alignment,” Parnell said in a statement to Vanity Fair.

Jennifer has worked in tandem with her husband to push allies on social media and in the press to promote the idea of a presidential run. “She wants to be first lady,” the former official said of Jennifer. “She wants to be Hillary.” After the midterms, online influencers whom Hegseth hired earlier this year to work as special government employees at the Pentagon began pitching Hegseth for president. These influencers were nominally hired to promote Hegseth’s efforts to bring war colleges in line with the “warrior ethos,” but in reality they coordinated with Jennifer and Radabaugh on “shitposting,” the second official said. Earlier this month, they rallied to Hegseth’s defense as he came under fire for the Republican ballot box-bloodbath, posting action shots of the secretary with the caption: “STAND. FIGHT. DEFEND THE REPUBLIC.”

These influencers often attack journalists who cover the Pentagon. In response to a recent story by Politico’s Daniel Lippman, an active-duty Army officer known as InfantryDort on social media—whom Hegseth hired as one of these shadow spokespeople—posted: “I expect you all to ratio the fuck out of this retard and in short order. Daniel Lippman remains absolute journalistic filth.” A Politico spokesperson responded: “Daniel is one of Washington’s best-sourced reporters, and we’re lucky to have him at POLITICO.”

When Democrats take control of Congress, they are expected to focus much of their oversight on Hegseth, including the scandals from his personal life. The subjects broached in his contentious confirmation hearings—including an affidavit signed by his former sister-in-law alleging abuse against his second wife—are likely to be relitigated. (His second wife denied that she was physically abused.)

“Is that going to be good for the president?” Tillis asked. “His entire confirmation will be re-prosecuted. That affidavit will get a lot of attention. Family members will most likely be subpoenaed. Does he really want to go through that at a personal level, and at the same time be such a massive disruption for the leader of the most lethal, complex organization in American government? Is this about Pete, or is this about America’s defense? I don’t know how any objective person can say that the value that he brings is greater than that distraction and the cost of him remaining in place.”

“If he had any dignity, he would resign and allow the president to appoint a replacement in the lame duck session,” said the first official. “But he doesn’t.”

Hegseth’s ability to project confidence is not confined to the cameras. He was bullish on the war with Iran from the start. “There weren’t any real cheerleaders for the war in the cabinet outside of Pete Hegseth,” said the second official. “It was Pete and [US Ambassador to Israel] Mike Huckabee.” In late December 2025, Hegseth met with Israeli prime minister Benjamin Netanyahu at the Four Seasons in Palm Beach shortly before Netanyahu ventured to Mar-a-Lago to make the case for fresh strikes on Iran. By February, Netanyahu was pushing Trump to join Israel in a full-blown war to topple the Iranian regime.

Hegseth was all-in, his confidence buoyed by the success of Operation Midnight Hammer—a series of strikes on Iranian nuclear facilities in June 2025—and the raid to abduct Venezuelan dictator Nicolás Maduro. His allies in the administration point to these operations, as well as a post-pandemic surge in military recruitment numbers, as his major successes in the job.

But multiple officials who spoke with Vanity Fair said they believed that when briefing Trump, Hegseth understated the risks of war with Iran from the beginning and went on to overstate its successes as the conflict dragged on. “Pete gave the rosiest picture in the world,” said the first former official. During early discussions about potentially launching the war, the official said they were struck by how quickly Hegseth started speaking about the operation as a “foregone conclusion.”

“Leading up into the war, he presented, along with [Central Command Commander Admiral] Brad Cooper, overly optimistic projections of what the air campaign could achieve,” said the second administration official. “This is how the president got this idea that it could be done in a matter of two to three weeks.”

Hegseth ran against Democrat U.S. Senator Amy Klobuchar in 2012.

Tillis, for his part, said that he did not believe Hegseth was forthright with Trump about the war. “He planned the good, bright, shiny stuff that was Epic Fury, but clearly did not brief the president on things that anybody that’s had any experience in the Middle East knows,” Tillis added. “The first thing that goes is the Strait of Hormuz.”

“Secretary Hegseth and Pentagon leadership consistently provide the President with the complete, unvarnished picture so he can make the best choices for America’s security and success,” Parnell said in a statement.

On the first day of the war, a missile struck the Minab elementary school in southern Iran, killing more than 150 people, the majority of them children. The US military has thus far refused to publicly admit responsibility for the strike, despite a preliminary military investigation concluding within days of the strike that a US missile struck the school. Before the attack, Hegseth gutted the Pentagon program aimed at reducing civilian harm during military operations, ProPublica reported in March. House Republican Thomas Massie, who brought the articles of impeachment against Hegseth last week, argued the bombing was “the result of systemic deterioration and removal of Department of Defense infrastructure that Congress had mandated by law to limit collateral damage to civilians.”

Massie cast the strike as a consequence of Hegseth’s oft-repeated mantra: “Maximum lethality, not tepid legality.”

As the war went on, officials said, Hegseth continued to trumpet its successes, showing the president what one official described as “hype videos” depicting strikes on missile launchers, Iranian ships, and old aircraft. Hegseth told Trump “that the majority of Iran’s missile capabilities and the majority of Iran’s Air Force was destroyed,” the official said, even though US intelligence “showed that wasn’t the case, and that the majority of Iran’s missiles and missile launchers by the end of the major combat phase of the war were still intact.”

“He creates an echo chamber in the press corps so that he can get only nice questions or do made-for-TV stuff,” said Senator Tillis.

Hegseth also worked behind the scenes to promote positive media coverage of the war. He briefed Fox News hosts whom he remained friendly with on the war’s progress and encouraged them to present a positive picture on air. But Hegseth himself has made little effort to explain or defend the war to Americans. Even as the war has become a political debacle for the administration, he remains bullish on escalation. “Pete is the only one who wants to restart strikes,” the first official said.

We were all sitting in our assigned seats waiting for the performance to start when a security official walked into the Pentagon briefing room to make an announcement. Hegseth had not held a Pentagon press briefing since May. Now, facing an impeachment effort from a member of his own party, he came before the cameras. “If anyone gets out of line today, you’re going to be physically removed,” the official said. “Is that clear?”

“Sense of decorum,” the official said apologetically. “Just be professional.”

The official said he was just relaying orders from Hegseth’s office. A few minutes later, Joel Valdez, the press aide, walked out and asked if anyone was confused. Everyone was. “I could tell you off the record?” he said. No, the reporters replied. “Do you want to hear it off the record?” No, they said again.

Valdez looked down at his shoes and then walked out of the room without saying another word. “In my 30 years in DC, I have never heard that,” said a cameraman. “This is crazytown.”

At 8 a.m. sharp, Hegseth walked out wearing a loud plaid suit, a striped tie, and an American flag pocket square. The timing is no coincidence; officials said Hegseth schedules briefings in the 8 a.m. hour because Trump is known to watch Fox News at that time.

The secretary of defense began reading his speech from a stack of papers on the podium. From my assigned seat in the back row, I felt as if I were watching an interpretive dance. The choreography of Hegseth’s hand movements and facial expressions was as slick as his hair, which was gelled into a stiff meringue that moved in unison with his tanned face.

Hegseth punctuated a line about President Trump’s “decisive” leadership with a clenched fist thrust downward. The word “overseeing” was expressed with a slow swipe of the hand over an imaginary stretch of purview. As Hegseth recalled the glories of the Maduro operation—and, parenthetically, his part in directing them—he dismissed criticism of the raid by leaning into the microphone, shaking his head from side to side and wiggling his eyebrows: “It wasn’t”—dramatic pause for eyebrow wiggle—“a kidnapping.”

Hegseth's presser had a pantomime quality.

The briefing itself exuded little of the confidence Hegseth sought to signal before the cameras. He took seven questions in total, sticking to friendly reporters in the front rows who had signed a pledge in September 2025 to not report “unauthorized information” from the Pentagon. One of them, a representative of Real America’s Voice, asked “what message” is sent to America’s allies when “our own politicians don’t support taking out the narco traffickers that kill their own constituents?” It wasn’t so much a question as a warm bath. Some in the room, visibly frustrated by the waste of time, rolled their eyes. Others shouted questions from the back about Iran and impeachment. Hegseth refused to look at them.

“He creates an echo chamber in the press corps so that he can get only nice questions or do made-for-TV stuff,” said Tillis, who also decried Hegseth for filming a slick video of himself outside the Department of Defense making the case for why he should not be impeached: “He demonstrated more impulsiveness by using taxpayer dollars to defend himself against impeachment.”

In his remarks before the press, Hegseth framed the effort to remove him as an attack on the troops. “If they want to come for the warriors, they’re going to have to come through me,” he said, jabbing a finger into his chest. But the impeachment targeted Hegseth, not “the warriors.” We had assembled to hear Pete make the case for Pete. Indeed, he had not held a press briefing when a Navy commander died in a helicopter crash on July 1, or when three US soldiers were killed in an Iranian strike on a base in Jordan on July 17. What brought him back before the cameras was not the ongoing war in the Middle East but his own fight to save his career here in Washington.

The performance earned a rave review from the one viewer who mattered. “Pete Hegseth gets an ‘A’ PLUS, PLUS, PLUS,” Trump wrote on Truth Social.

Early on in Hegseth’s tenure, he confided in advisers about his “biggest fear” as he took on the mighty responsibility of leading the Department of Defense, the first former official told me: “Being fired and embarrassed.” For now, Trump is standing by his secretary. He always is, until he isn’t. When asked just before the midterms whether Wiles would remain on as his chief of staff, Trump said there was “zero chance” he’d replace her. Three weeks later, she was out.
