‘Situations’ Review: Greg Vrotsos’ Filmmaking Debut Is a Winning, Relatable Throwback

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

The character actor writes, directs and stars as a fortysomething diving back into the L.A. dating pool, in a comedy-drama that owes something to the Nineties cult favorite 'Swingers.'

“Swingers” isn’t the kind of movie that people often talk about Hollywood not making anymore. But when a new film is worthy of being mentioned in the same sentence as that 1996 comedy, it’s a reminder of how rare such projects are these days. Directed and co-written by Greg Vrotsos, who also stars, “Situations” owes as much to “High Fidelity” and the “funcomfortable” storytelling of Larry David as it does to Doug Liman and Jon Favreau’s breakthrough, but the fact that it resembles a spiritual sequel to the latter — aged up but no less neurotic, with similar geographic specificity — offers a sturdy foundation for dating insights at once familiar and fresh. In his feature filmmaking debut, Vrotsos proves tough and tender — both behind and in front of the camera — while reminding audiences how vital stories about recognizable relationship dilemmas are to our moviegoing diet. Vrotsos plays Nick, a Los Angeles photographer whose girlfriend Jessica (Katie Parker) has recently broken up with him. The rift clearly makes little sense to him, but more egregious are the dollars she’s asking for, as Katie has itemized their shared expenses in a spreadsheet — one of many script details that feels too distressing not to have come from lived experience. Despite impish encouragement by his married best friend Paul (P.J. Byrne) to get back into the dating pool, Nick feels stuck and uncertain: Now in his forties, he knows what kind of partner he wants, but getting to know strangers with repetitive chitchat is a grind. His career, meanwhile, is flourishing, but turmoil over his unresolved relationship prevents him from committing to any of the attractive options presented by his manager Brianne (Melora Walters).

Nick agrees to go on a double date with his pal Mario, whose appetite for L.A. nightlife overshadows his own lack of interest in casual flings. But after Paul bullies him into accepting a blind date with florist Gabrielle (Fernanda Andrade), Nick begins to question if he’s moved on sufficiently from his previous relationship to seriously entertain a new one. In the meantime, Katie pops back up to reconnect with him, hinting at the possibility of a reconciliation — well, once he’s remunerated her for his share of their previous cohabitation.

Much of Vrotsos’ previous work is as a character actor on shows like “Parks and Recreation,” “Orange is the New Black” and the 2017 return of “Twin Peaks,” but he has a handsome leading-man quality, leveraged effectively in “Situations” to make the character’s sensitivity more charming than exasperating. It almost certainly helps that he directs himself from his own script (co-written with Daniel Hartigan), and he injects Nick with enough complexity to make him brainy but not pretentious, introspective but not self-obsessed. Yet while our sympathies are almost immediately aligned with the character, the film doesn’t over-indulge him, or fail to hold him accountable when he makes some thoughtless decisions.

We’re relieved for Nick, for example, when he finds temporary solace in the bed of a similarly disillusioned young woman (Fiona Dourif) who playfully shares her own horror stories after the two of them flee the site of his blind date. But the movie ensures that he eventually faces Gabbie, his ghosted companion, in a forum that’s suitably awkward and naturally comedic, but more modestly believable than the seemingly obligatory public third-act humiliations that occur in studio romcoms.

Because he’s a decade or more older than the lovelorn guys in “Swingers,” Nick is less thirsty and more thoughtful as he returns to singledom. The choice both grounds his journey and amplifies his epiphanies. Those include his realization that an ex-partner’s presumption that they’ll stay friends is a sign he’s being kept under glass in case of emergencies — best to move on, immediately and without prejudice.

Acting opposite Parker’s slightly entitled, cucumber-cool Jessica, Vrotsos makes a meal out of that moment, inflecting it with a bit of “if I only had the presence of mind to say that in real life” wish-fulfillment while digging deep into feelings as cathartic as they are painful. But he broadly and vividly portrays single living for a generation of people who grew up just too long ago to comfortably navigate a scene that mostly exists online now. More than that, they’re mature enough to try and avoid petty melodrama, whether or not they succeed. That Paul — played by Byrne as a mixture of Vince Vaughn and his own buffoonish “Wolf of Wall Street” character Rugrat — is not just a married man with kids, but one who can admit to his own failures and vulnerabilities, lends forther sophistication to a story that, in less capable hands, could feel formulaic.

Despite being shot on a shoestring budget (reportedly $130K), the film looks rich and lived-in, utilizing real locations in Los Angeles’ east side in much the same way that Liman did with Hollywood’s late-‘90s watering holes. Working with cinematographer Trevor Wineman, Vrotsos favors uncomplicated setups, but repeatedly places the camera in ways that make conventional interactions more interesting. Meanwhile, composer Ben Romans finds smart, subtle ways to evoke Nick’s spartan but emotionally complex world with cues that are unobtrusive, but more intense than mere vibe-setting.

After being road-showed by distributor Utopia following a 2025 festival run, the film is due on physical media next year, but its charms feel destined for future cult adoration — again, an irony given subject matter that, in any era, has plenty of mainstream appeal. Central to its charm is the relatability of getting dumped by somebody you’d like to get back together with, not just because you still have feelings for them, but because starting a new relationship is just too much trouble. 30 years after the romantic misadventures of Favreau and his pals, this next-generation follow-up reiterates the adage that there’s someone out there for everybody, but adds an important footnote born of a single fortysomething’s life experience: The first step to finding that person is often getting out of your own way. Let’s hope “Situations” is Vrotsos’ first cinematic lesson of many.
