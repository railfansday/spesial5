The Liquidity Illusion in Tokenized Funds: Bridging the Chasm Between 24/7 Trading and Real-World Redemption

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

The advent of blockchain technology has sparked a profound transformation across numerous industries, with finance standing at the forefront of this revolution. One of the most compelling promises of decentralized finance (DeFi) and distributed ledger technology (DLT) lies in its potential to enhance liquidity, transparency, and accessibility for a wide array of assets. Tokenized funds, which represent fractional ownership in real-world assets (RWAs) or traditional financial instruments on a blockchain, epitomize this promise. They offer the allure of 24/7 trading, atomic settlement, and reduced operational overhead, seemingly transcending the limitations of conventional financial markets. However, beneath this shimmering surface of perpetual trading lies a critical, often misunderstood, dynamic: the "liquidity illusion."

This illusion arises from the fundamental disconnect between the instantaneous, round-the-clock tradability of a token on a secondary blockchain market and the inherently slower, often constrained, process of redeeming the underlying real-world asset for fiat currency or other liquid instruments. While a token representing a share in a real estate portfolio or a U.S. Treasury fund can indeed be traded at any hour on a decentralized exchange (DEX) or a centralized digital asset platform, the actual conversion of that token back into its underlying value — i.e., the primary market redemption — remains tethered to the operational realities, regulatory frameworks, and inherent liquidity characteristics of the off-chain asset. This article delves into this critical gap, dissecting its mechanisms, examining real-world manifestations, and exploring the limitations that challenge the true realization of ubiquitous liquidity in tokenized funds.

Tokenized funds are digital representations of traditional investment vehicles, such as mutual funds, ETFs, private equity funds, or even individual assets like real estate or fine art, issued as security tokens on a blockchain. At their core, these tokens aim to fractionalize ownership, enhance transferability, and embed programmatic logic into financial instruments. By leveraging blockchain technology, tokenized funds seek to overcome several inefficiencies prevalent in traditional finance, including manual processes, lengthy settlement cycles (often T+2 or T+3), limited trading hours, and high minimum investment thresholds.

The operational model typically involves an issuer or fund manager creating a smart contract that defines the rules of the fund, including subscription, redemption, and dividend distribution. Each share or unit of the fund is then represented by a unique security token compliant with standards like ERC-20 (Ethereum) or similar protocols on other chains (e.g., Stellar, Polygon). These tokens are held in digital wallets, providing investors with direct, transparent ownership records.

In traditional fund structures, an investor wishing to exit their position typically submits a redemption request to the fund administrator. This request is processed based on the fund's Net Asset Value (NAV) calculated at a specific cut-off time (e.g., end of day), and the underlying assets may need to be sold to meet the redemption, with settlement occurring days later. This process is inherently designed around business hours, banking systems, and the liquidity profile of the underlying assets. Tokenized funds promise to revolutionize this by enabling investors to sell their tokens instantly on a secondary market, creating the perception of continuous, deep liquidity. However, this perception often overlooks the critical distinction between secondary market token trading and primary market asset redemption.

The fundamental distinction fueling the "liquidity illusion" lies in the operational mechanisms of secondary market token trading versus primary market fund redemption.

Secondary Market Token Trading: On a blockchain, tokenized fund shares can be traded 24 hours a day, 7 days a week, on various platforms.

Primary Market Fund Redemption: Despite the 24/7 tradability of the token, the actual process of redeeming the token for its underlying value — typically fiat currency or the underlying asset itself — remains fundamentally tied to the traditional financial infrastructure and the nature of the RWA.

The "gap" is thus the chasm between the speed of on-chain token transfer and the speed of off-chain asset value realization. While the blockchain facilitates instantaneous ownership transfer of a digital representation, it does not inherently accelerate the underlying asset's liquidity or circumvent the operational and regulatory realities of its traditional market.

Several projects have brought tokenized funds to market, each navigating the complexities of liquidity and redemption with varying degrees of success and transparency.

Ondo Finance: A prominent player in the tokenized RWA space, Ondo Finance offers several tokenized funds, primarily focused on U.S. Treasuries and money market instruments (e.g., OUSG, OMMF). These tokens can be traded on secondary markets, often paired with stablecoins like USDC. While OUSG represents shares in a BlackRock ETF that invests in short-term U.S. Treasuries, and OMMF invests in funds like the BlackRock Federal Trust Fund, the redemption process for the underlying assets still adheres to traditional financial timelines. Investors wishing to redeem their OUSG tokens for USDC directly from Ondo must go through their platform, which involves KYC/AML checks and adherence to their operational windows. While the underlying assets (U.S. Treasuries) are highly liquid, the fund's operational processes and the settlement of fiat or stablecoin payments still introduce friction and time lags that are not 24/7. The 24/7 nature primarily applies to the token's secondary market tradability, not the direct conversion of the underlying treasury into cash at any given moment.

Franklin Templeton's OnChain U.S. Government Money Market Fund (FSHYX): As one of the largest traditional asset managers to embrace tokenization, Franklin Templeton launched FSHYX on the Stellar blockchain, later expanding to Polygon. This fund tokenizes shares of a traditional money market fund that invests in government securities, offering daily liquidity. While the fund's token can be held and potentially transferred on-chain 24/7, the actual subscription and redemption processes are still tied to the fund's official NAV calculation at the end of each business day and processed through traditional channels. For instance, redemption requests must be submitted by a specific cut-off time on a business day, and the proceeds are typically settled T+1 or T+2 in fiat currency, reflecting the underlying fund's operational cycle and banking system limitations. This clearly illustrates that while the token exists on a blockchain, the fund's operations remain squarely within the TradFi paradigm for primary market interactions.

Tokenized Real Estate Platforms (e.g., RealT, Propy): Platforms like RealT and Propy have pioneered fractional ownership of real estate properties through tokens. These tokens represent equity in an LLC that owns the physical property. While these tokens can be traded on specific secondary markets (e.g., RealT's peer-to-peer exchange or through Uniswap pools for some tokens), the underlying asset (physical real estate) remains inherently illiquid. If a significant number of token holders wished to "redeem" their tokens for the actual value of the property, the fund would realistically need to sell the physical asset, a process that can take months, involves significant transaction costs, and is entirely decoupled from the instantaneous nature of token transfer. The secondary market for these tokens, while offering some liquidity, often trades at a discount to the perceived underlying value, precisely because of this primary market illiquidity. The "liquidity" here is often a thin layer for small trades, not a robust mechanism for large-scale exits.

The promise of enhanced liquidity through tokenized funds faces several critical limitations that contribute to the "liquidity illusion":

Regulatory Hurdles: The current regulatory landscape for tokenized securities is fragmented and evolving. Many jurisdictions still apply traditional securities laws, which were not designed for 24/7 blockchain-based markets. Regulators are cautious about allowing instantaneous, permissionless primary redemptions for complex funds, especially those with illiquid underlying assets. This often necessitates "off-ramps" that adhere to existing financial regulations, limiting the blockchain's full potential for speed.

Operational Complexities of On-chain/Off-chain Interoperability: Bridging the gap between the speed of blockchain settlement and the slower pace of traditional finance (TradFi) operations is a significant challenge. This includes automating KYC/AML for redemption requests, ensuring seamless fiat on/off-ramps, and managing the reconciliation between on-chain token movements and off-chain asset positions. These processes often require manual intervention or rely on centralized entities, introducing bottlenecks and negating some of the blockchain's inherent efficiencies.

Underlying Asset Illiquidity: The most fundamental limitation is that tokenization does not magically imbue an illiquid asset with liquidity. A token representing a share in a commercial building or a private equity fund will still be subject to the inherent difficulties of selling those underlying assets quickly and efficiently in the traditional market. While secondary token trading can provide some exit opportunities, it cannot guarantee deep liquidity or prevent significant discounts during periods of high selling pressure if the underlying asset cannot be readily liquidated.

Market Fragmentation and Thin Secondary Liquidity: While tokens can technically trade 24/7, the actual secondary markets for many tokenized funds are still nascent and fragmented. Liquidity pools on DEXs might be shallow, and order books on centralized platforms might be thin, especially for less common or niche tokenized assets. This means that large trades can suffer significant slippage, further eroding the perceived benefit of 24/7 trading and potentially leading to a price disconnected from the NAV.

Smart Contract and Custody Risks: While not directly related to liquidity, the reliance on smart contracts introduces new risks such as bugs, exploits, or governance vulnerabilities that could impact the integrity of the fund and, by extension, investor confidence and the token's perceived value. Furthermore, the custody of the underlying off-chain assets often remains with traditional custodians, adding another layer of counterparty risk and operational dependency.

The promise of tokenized funds to revolutionize investment by offering 24/7 trading and enhanced liquidity is undeniably compelling. However, a critical examination reveals a nuanced reality: the "liquidity illusion" where the instantaneous tradability of a token on secondary markets often masks the inherent stickiness and operational constraints of primary market redemption for the underlying real-world assets. While blockchain technology dramatically improves the transferability and fractionalization of ownership, it does not, by itself, accelerate the liquidation of illiquid assets or bypass the established regulatory and operational frameworks of traditional finance.

As demonstrated by projects like Ondo Finance, Franklin Templeton's FSHYX, and various tokenized real estate ventures, while tokens can be bought and sold around the clock, the process of converting those tokens into fiat currency or the underlying asset remains tethered to business hours, banking systems, and the fundamental liquidity profile of the off-chain investment. This creates a potential disconnect between the token's secondary market price and the fund's Net Asset Value, especially during periods of stress or high redemption demand.

As an expert in this domain, my opinion is that the future success of tokenized funds hinges on addressing this gap. This requires not only technological innovation in bridging on-chain and off-chain settlement but also significant evolution in regulatory frameworks to accommodate the unique characteristics of digital assets. Furthermore, fund structures need to be transparent about the actual primary market liquidity mechanisms, managing investor expectations regarding redemption speeds. True, ubiquitous liquidity for tokenized funds will only be realized when the entire lifecycle, from subscription to redemption, can operate seamlessly and efficiently within a legally compliant, 24/7 digital ecosystem, rather than simply tokenizing an illiquid asset and hoping the secondary market provides the magic bullet. Until then, investors must remain acutely aware of the distinction between token liquidity and underlying asset liquidity.
