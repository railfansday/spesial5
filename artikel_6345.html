Why some cities embrace new technology faster than others

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

A study of 3.2 million Americans links residents’ openness to new experiences with earlier adoption of Airbnb, Uber, and Flickr.

Why does a new app catch on quickly in one city but struggle in another? Income, internet access, and nearby universities may help explain the difference.

A study of 3.2 million Americans points to another factor: residents’ openness to new experiences.

Across 349 metropolitan areas, regions whose residents scored higher on openness took up Airbnb, Uber, and Flickr earlier.

That held even after income, education, internet access, and a long list of other differences were accounted for.

Tobias Ebert of the University of St. Gallen in Switzerland and Martin Obschonka of the University of Amsterdam led the work.

The findings show an association, but they do not establish that openness drives adoption.

“What stood out was the consistency of the pattern,” Ebert said.

“Psychological openness was the only factor reliably associated with adoption across all of the three innovations we studied.”

Psychologists sort personality into five broad traits. One of them covers curiosity, a taste for new ideas, and a willingness to try unfamiliar things, and they call it openness. It is partly shaped by genes.

The survey measures it with plain statements like “Is curious about many different things,” rated from strongly disagree to strongly agree.

Between 2004 and 2015, 3,234,899 people in the United States answered a 44-statement version of that survey online and gave their zip code.

The team averaged the scores for each of 349 metro areas in the lower 48 states.

The smallest regional sample, Victoria, Texas, had 406 people, and New York had 127,859. The co-authors include researchers at the University of Cambridge, the University of Texas at Austin, and the IT University of Copenhagen.

The averages ran from 3.48 out of 5 in Bismarck, North Dakota, to 3.93 in Santa Fe, New Mexico. That gap looks small until it is counted in people.

Counting anyone who scored 4 or higher as open, the share of open residents ranged from 21% in the least open region to 48% in the most open one.

The companies’ own early numbers are patchy, so the team turned to tax records.

The team used tax records for businesses without employees in accommodation and passenger transportation as indicators of hosting and driving activity.

The team counted new accommodation filings for the five years after 2009, when Airbnb took its current name and landed its first major funding.

Uber launched city by city and reached 142 regions in its first five years.

In each of those regions, taxi and ground transport filings were flat before Uber came and rose sharply in the five years afterward.

For Flickr, the photo-sharing site that peaked around 2008, the team sampled 80 million photos uploaded between 2005 and 2015.

It counted how many different people in each region posted during the site’s first five years.

In the five years after Airbnb launched, San Diego gained 9.5 new accommodation businesses per 100,000 residents, more than four times the 2.2 in Minneapolis.

Regions that were early on one platform tended to be early on the others, and openness tracked that general readiness. Its correlation with early adoption was about 0.4 for each platform and 0.49 for the three combined.

A region one standard deviation higher in openness gained about 2.4 more accommodation hosts per 100,000 residents and about 48 more ride-share drivers.

For a region the size of New York, the team calculated, that is roughly 450 more hosts and more than 9,000 more drivers over five years.

San Francisco, Portland, and Austin were repeatedly among the strongest adopters, and El Paso, Flint, and Jackson among the weakest.

The association weakened somewhat but remained when the team adjusted for age, gender, race, income, education, and unemployment.

It also held after adjusting for internet access, tourism, housing, transportation, politics, religiosity, social ties, and the other four personality traits.

Openness was the only variable linked to adoption of all three platforms.

All three platforms asked ordinary people to do something unfamiliar. An Airbnb host let strangers sleep in the spare room.

An Uber driver gave rides in the family car. Flickr users posted their photos in public before that was common online.

None of those services works until enough people in one place are willing to go first.

“Someone has to be willing to try a new behavior before everyone else regards it as normal,” said Obschonka, of the Amsterdam Business School.

“This can be especially important for platforms, which need enough local users, hosts, drivers or content creators to generate the network effects that make them useful and viable.”

The team calls regional openness an invisible social infrastructure. It can’t be seen the way roads and fiber lines can, but it may shape how readily a place takes to something new.

Openness does not replace those familiar factors, the authors wrote, but it may help explain why otherwise similar regions adopt new services at different rates.

The data are a snapshot, so they can’t say whether open people make a region quick to adopt, or whether something else produces both.

The team checked two alternatives: open people moving to places that already had the platforms, and the platforms raising a region’s openness.

Neither explained the pattern, and Uber’s choice of cities didn’t either, since its launch order tracked population and income, not openness.

The surveys were taken by whoever chose to answer online, a group skewed toward women and younger people, with an average age of 27.

Reweighting the sample to match the population did not change the results.

The team also treated each region’s openness as fixed across the decade, and the data could not show how the trait turns into a decision to rent out a room.

The study did not look at artificial intelligence either, and the authors cautioned against stretching the result to it.

“When a genuinely new technology arrives, its future does not depend only on the technology itself,” Obschonka said.

“It also depends on whether people are ready to experiment with it.”

The authors wrote that studies following regions over time, or treating a platform’s launch as a natural experiment, could help clarify whether openness influences adoption.

The full study was published in the journal Research Policy.
