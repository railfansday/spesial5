What Else Is True?: Lessons About Suffering and Healing

Twenty years ago, I helped establish a nonprofit organization called the Trauma Resource Institute. I could not have imagined where that decision would take me, the people I would meet, or how profoundly the journey would change my understanding of human suffering and resilience. My work has brought me into communities experiencing extraordinary adversity. I have witnessed the aftermath of natural disasters, the heartbreak of war and conflict, and the suffering of people whose lives have been disrupted by trauma, violence, and loss.

Yet, amid the suffering and the heartache, I have encountered something else. I have witnessed extraordinary courage, generosity, humor, creativity, kindness, and compassion. I have seen people reach toward one another as their own lives unraveled. I learned that suffering can profoundly alter the course of our lives, but suffering is never the only truth about who we are or what remains possible.

Throughout my work, I have returned again and again to a simple question:“What else is true?” When something terrible happens, what else is true? When we feel overwhelmed by loneliness, grief, or fear, what else is true? When the world seems divided or hopeless, what else is true? This question is not an invitation to deny suffering nor does it ask us to turn away from injustice, grief, trauma, or pain. Rather, it invites us to widen the lens through which we experience ourselves and the world.

Alongside fear, there may also be courage. Alongside grief, there may be love. Alongside helplessness, there may be one small place where choice still exists. And sometimes, amid tremendous suffering, hope begins to emerge. We can grieve and still experience gratitude. We can feel frightened and still recognize moments when we feel safer. The presence of suffering does not erase our capacity for well-being.

One of the most profound discoveries is that trauma is not only a cognitive or emotional experience. It is also physiological. Our nervous systems continually respond to what is happening around and within us. When we perceive danger, our bodies mobilize to protect us. Our hearts may race, our muscles tighten, or we may become numb or disconnected. These responses are not signs of weakness but reflect our biology working to help us survive. The body carries a wisdom that words alone cannot always express. Our nervous systems communicate distress and danger, but they can also register safety, connection, comfort, strength, and possibility.

The Trauma Resiliency Model (TRM) and Community Resiliency Model (CRM), approaches I developed, make knowledge about trauma and the nervous system accessible. Central to these models is body literacy: learning to recognize and understand the sensations through which our bodies communicate. People can begin to tell the difference between sensations of distress and well-being without judgment. Distress does not necessarily disappear, but it need not be the only experience available to us.

As my understanding of trauma deepened, I began asking another question: What if knowledge about our nervous systems did not remain only within therapy rooms? This question flooded my waking moments: What if teachers, nurses, parents, children, first responders, and entire communities could learn simple skills to restore well-being?

These questions changed the direction of my life. I have watched people adapt CRM and TRM wellness skills for schools, hospitals, shelters, recovery programs, disaster response, and communities affected by war and other disasters. I have learned that resilience grows between us. Our nervous systems respond to the presence of others. A caring voice, a compassionate expression, or the steady presence of someone we trust may help us feel safer and more connected.

I invite you to consider your own life's journey: Who or what has lifted you when you stumbled? Who or what reminded you of your worth when you doubted yourself? When loneliness or despair entered your life, who or what helped you reconnect with hope?

As I reflect on this journey, I find myself increasingly drawn to the idea of human flourishing. Healing from trauma can open possibilities beyond the reduction of distress. As people experience greater safety and physiological regulation, there may be more room for curiosity, creativity, meaningful relationships, purpose, and joy.

Flourishing does not mean living without pain, nor is it an inevitable outcome of adversity. It is the possibility of living with greater meaning, connection, and agency while acknowledging life's complexities. Communities flourish when compassion becomes action, human dignity is protected, and people recognize their shared humanity.

I no longer think of hope simply as optimism about the future. I understand it as an openness to possibility, even when we cannot predict what comes next. Hope may begin with an unexpected kindness, a moment of physiological steadiness, or discovering that we still have a choice. After twenty years, I continue returning to the question that has accompanied my journey: “What else is true?”

Sometimes the answer is someone I love. Sometimes it is the generosity of a stranger, the beauty of nature, or the quiet recognition that my body can still experience peace in the midst of chaos. And sometimes the answer is simply that I do not yet know what else is possible.

Resilience reminds us that suffering never gets to tell the whole story. Within the human spirit, the wisdom of the body, and the connections we share, another truth may be waiting to be discovered—a possibility for connection, healing, and hope.

I began this journey wanting to help people recover from trauma. Twenty years later, the people I have met have taught me something equally important: Even in a world filled with suffering, our capacity for compassion, connection, and hope remains one of the most remarkable expressions of our shared humanity, and it provides a pathway to healing. And fundamentally, the body has a wisdom that words cannot speak.
