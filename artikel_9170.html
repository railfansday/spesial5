Creatures of Habit May Have Less Pain and Depression

Creatures of habit—those who have a daily routine and stick to it—may benefit physically and psychologically from their predictable schedules, according to a new study published by researchers at the University of Missouri.

This might seem counterintuitive to people who live more unpredictable lives. Going about your day with no set schedule may seem exciting to some, but that chaos and uncertainty might be taking a hidden toll on well-being over time.

Temporarily escaping the confines of a routine during vacation is great, but living everyday life without the comfort of a set schedule creates anxiety and stress. Researchers also hypothesize that unstructured days can interfere with the body’s natural internal clock.

Circadian rhythms refer to the physical, mental, and behavioral changes we experience over a 24-hour cycle. Our body has an internal biological clock that has been honed by evolution over millions of years, largely calibrated to the lifestyle of a hunter-gatherer. Deviations from this ancient way of life often put us out of sync with our biological clock. Many psychologists believe this is an underappreciated source of unhappiness, fatigue, and illness.

When the sleep-wake cycle is disrupted, for example, it can have negative repercussions on other bodily systems. In other words, your body’s internal clock can impact your mood, hormones, energy level, cognitive abilities, metabolism, and more.

The primary driver of our sleep-wake cycle is light, but other factors influence it as well. Food, caffeine, exercise, stress, screen time, and more can potentially interfere with our natural internal clock. Scientists at the University of Missouri wanted to address whether maintaining a predictable daily routine helps keep our internal clock ticking the way it should.

The researchers recruited 67 subjects who were older adults—37 had insomnia, and 30 were good sleepers. Using surveys, the team examined whether regimented routines made a difference in health outcomes. Since the study included participants with insomnia, they could also determine whether insomnia played a role in any of the health outcomes reported.

The results revealed a striking difference between creatures of habit and those who lack structure in their daily lives. Participants who adhered to a daily routine reported less physical pain and fewer symptoms of depression. This association was maintained in those who had insomnia.

While the study does not demonstrate causality, it suggests that regular mealtimes, wake-up times, social activities, and bedtimes furnish the brain with signals that help keep the internal clock functioning properly. In turn, this likely explains the better physical and psychological outcomes seen in those who live by set schedules.

The researchers conclude that sticking to a consistent routine is “associated with more favorable health outcomes and may represent a promising target for educational and interventional strategies for older adults, irrespective of insomnia status.”

The new findings support similar studies that found structured habits to be associated with lower mortality risks in aging seniors.

It happens to all of us—life’s events often disrupt our circadian rhythms every now and then. But there are evidence-based ways to get your internal clock back on track.

But sticking to a daily routine can help.

Another important factor is light management. As mentioned, light is the primary signal our brain uses to distinguish sleeping time from awake time. Bright lights in the morning, preferably sunlight, trigger your body to produce cortisol, the wakefulness hormone.

At night, darkness stimulates the release of the sleep hormone melatonin. Our use of artificial lights or screens at night can fool our brain into thinking that it is still daytime, hindering our ability to fall asleep. It is recommended to dim lights and stop screen usage 1-2 hours before bed.

Be mindful of alcohol, caffeine, and certain medications, as these agents can keep you awake or interfere with restful sleep.

Eating late at night, napping in the afternoon, and lack of physical activity can also make falling asleep difficult. Investing in a comfortable mattress and keeping your bedroom temperature cooler can also promote quality sleep.

See your doctor if you continue to experience trouble sleeping.

If you struggle with pain or depression, try adhering to consistent times for sleeping, eating, and other activities throughout your day. Adopting a predictable schedule is a natural and easy way to alleviate anxiety. Syncing your body's internal clock to the rhythms of the day promotes better mood, sleep, and overall health.

“Behavioral-social rhythms and insomnia symptoms: associations with pain, body mass index, and depressive symptoms among older adults” by Eunjin Lee Tracy, et al. Journal of Behavioral Medicine, March 15, 2026.
