From Social Leasing to Social Fleets: A Proposed Law Lends Credibility to the Goal of One Million Affordable Electric Cars

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Social leasing could soon change in scale, name, and business model. The bill introduced by MP Christine Arrighi aims to create a public service offering one million “low-cost electric vehicles” (ALM). Scheduled for December 3rd in the National Assembly, its examination is expected to place at the heart of the debate a now crucial question: how to make social leasing sustainable and widespread?

Several presidential candidates have made reaching one million vehicles (or even more) one of the hallmarks of their campaigns¹. While this convergence is excellent news, the battle of figures says nothing about how to achieve this goal.

The current system cannot simply be scaled up twentyfold. Its cost per vehicle, intermittent financing, imperfect social targeting, lack of a guaranteed solution after three years, and the resale risks borne by dealerships are all structural limitations. The 2026 edition also revealed its dependence on Renault and Stellantis, which can at any time restrict volumes to favor more profitable traditional sales.

The proposed law changes its approach. Sponsored by MP Christine Arrighi, it draws inspiration from the proposals of T&E France and Bernard Julien, stemming from their study on the future of social leasing, published in June 2026.

In this new model, the government would directly purchase the vehicles at an average net cost of €15,000. These vehicles would be kept for twelve years in a public fleet and leased successively to one or more households for an average monthly rent of €150 before being resold. The day-to-day management of the fleet would be delegated to groups of public or private operators within the local areas.

The initial investment to build the fleet would be financed by borrowing, with a maximum outstanding amount of €7.8 billion in 2035. Lease payments and resales, supplemented by €350 million in annual public support (equivalent to the current social leasing budget), would cover service costs and repay the debt within eighteen years. By then, the system would be self-financing, and the state would own one million cars worth approximately €10 billion.

“This model is much more beneficial for public finances,” explains Diane Strauss, director of T&E France. “It requires an initial funding effort, but it eventually pays for itself. Instead of subsidizing at a loss, we build up an asset that generates its own revenue. This is what makes the move to a million vehicles financially viable.”

In addition to providing long-term protection for low-income motorists against rising and volatile fuel prices, the proposal represents a genuine industrial planning policy. The announcement of a public order for two million vehicles over twenty years (including fleet renewal) would indeed give the French automotive sector the visibility necessary to invest and prepare for the future.

“This will also allow us to direct our production towards the vehicles that households truly need: affordable, fuel-efficient, repairable electric cars, produced in France and Europe,” concludes Léo Larivière, automotive transition expert at T&E France. “This proposal must now open a substantive discussion among all stakeholders. It is high time to prepare the next steps for one of the rare examples of policies that reconcile climate, social justice, and industrial planning.”

¹ Raphaël Glucksmann and Marine Tondelier aim for one million vehicles. Jean-Luc Mélenchon proposes a public fleet of 1.4 million cars by 2032, and François Ruffin raises the target to three million. Gabriel Attal and Dominique de Villepin also advocate expanding social leasing, without specifying a target.
