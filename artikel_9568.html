70 the cost buys +6 points: what a real-robot benchmark says about capability per dollar

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

In the LLM market, price tracks capability so reliably that "expensive" has become shorthand for "better." A public-interest evaluation company called Robocurve just published a real-robot benchmark that puts both axes on the table at once — success rate and cost per attempt — and the shorthand breaks in an interesting way.

The data below is theirs, published on robocurve.org (retrieved 2026-11-24). The benchmark is RoboDojo-RC Tier 1: real robot, 120 real task attempts per configuration, LLM-based agents capped at 40 model calls, VLA models capped at 3,600 control steps. I have not run any of these systems; what follows is a reading of their published numbers plus some arithmetic anyone can check.

(Full table on their site — 16 configurations; I'm showing the ones that carry the argument, plus their Pareto-frontier members.)

1. The 70× jump buys almost nothing. Dream-1 lands 59.5% at $0.15 per attempt. The most expensive configuration tested, GPT-6.2 Astra (high, ultrafast), lands 65.7% at $10.51. That is seventy times the cost for +6.2 points. Per dollar of score, Dream-1 is roughly 63× more efficient. If you are buying robot task completion — or evaluating vendors who claim it — the middle of the LLM price ladder is dominated on both axes by two cheap points and one clever system.

2. The top scorer is not a big model. It's a system. Waddle-1 (81.4%, the best score in the table) is described as a full system: a set of LLMs controlling the robot through lower-level policies, one chat per task, a 10-minute limit, no call cap. The two cheap VLA models sit at the bottom on score. The frontier winners are architecturally diverse — a VLA at $0.05, a hosted model at $0.15, an orchestration stack at $2.46 — rather than "the biggest LLM, twice as expensive." On physical tasks, where you put the intelligence matters more than how much of it you bought.

3. Cost per attempt is a modeled number, not a receipt. The page states its assumptions, which matters: VLA entries are priced at a RunPod on-demand RTX 5090 rate ($1.19/hr); Dream-1 at $3/hr hosted inference; Waddle-1 at a planned $20/hr. Rate × wall-clock with a few knobs (queue time, minimum billable increments, how retries count) can move frontier membership. When a vendor quotes you "$X per successful task," the honest follow-up question is how that number was constructed — the same question this series keeps asking about success rates.

At 120 attempts, a binomial 95% confidence interval on a score near 60% is about ±8.8 points. That means Dream-1 (59.5%) and GPT-6.2 Astra (65.7%) are within each other's aggregate error bars — the +6.2 points separating them is not resolvable from the aggregate counts alone, so the "70× for +6" headline really reads "70× for something we can't yet distinguish from zero." (A paired analysis over the same 120 tasks can do much better than aggregate CIs — which is exactly why per-task data, not just summary tables, is what buyers should ask for.)

Meanwhile the genuinely large gap survives the arithmetic: Waddle-1's 81.4% (±7.0) against GPT-6.2 Astra's 65.7% (±8.5) barely separates at the edges, and Waddle-1 does it at a quarter of the per-attempt cost and 4.3× less wall-clock than the slowest configuration (Gemini 4 Carbon: 54.5% at 32.1 minutes per attempt — that's over 64 hours of robot-cell time per 120-attempt evaluation, a real cost that never shows up in the $ column).

There's also a time axis hiding in their table: 0.8 minutes per attempt (MolmoAct2) to 32.1 minutes (Gemini 4 Carbon). Cost per attempt and minutes per attempt are different budgets — one is money, the other is robot throughput — and they rank the field differently.

This series has been circling one claim from different directions:

Robocurve's cost frontier is the same discipline applied to money: publish the axis nobody else publishes, state your assumptions, and let readers do the arithmetic. Real-robot evaluation is expensive enough that "capability per dollar" isn't a nice-to-have — it is the number procurement will eventually ask for, and right now almost nobody reports it next to the score.

If you're building or buying robot autonomy: when someone shows you a benchmark bar chart, ask for the fourth column. Score, cost, minutes — and the confidence interval.

This is part of an ongoing series by an anonymous robotics-RL reproducibility project: we reproduce robot-learning papers with everything public (code, per-seed logs, plots), and we read other people's benchmarks the way we'd want ours read. All numbers in this post are from Robocurve's public page (robocurve.org, Nov 24, 2026) or arithmetic on those numbers; none are ours.
