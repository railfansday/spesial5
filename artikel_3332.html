Star Trek’s New Movie Reboot Officially Switching Genres Is Perfect For The Franchise’s Future

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

After what might be charitably called a rather uneven chapter for Star Trek, Paramount's next theatrical reboot of the famous sci-fi franchise can't come soon enough. We've seen a decade of false starts, abandoned scripts, and stalled announcements following 2016's underappreciated Star Trek Beyond. While the TV arm of Gene Roddenberry's franchise still has its fans, Skydance's takeover and the reshaping of studio priorities mean we have a good chance of the kind of rejuvenation that is desperately needed for the good ship Enterprise.

Via flirtations with a Quentin Tarantino Star Trek movie, fans have spent years hoping either for the long-delayed Star Trek 4 to wrap up the Kelvin timeline or for a revival that truly respects Gene Roddenberry's foundational universe while also still boldly pushing towards uncharted territory. Unfortunately, the exact balance between those two requirements has proved rather tricky, as recent small-screen creative swings have proven.

Fortunately, everything that has been revealed so far about the upcoming Star Trek reboot from writers and directors John Francis Daley and Jonathan Goldstein sounds genuinely thrilling. The decision to shift Star Trek into a bold new genre that might not be expected by fans is a particularly inspired move.

Earlier this year, as the Apple war movie Mayday was about to release, Daley and Goldstein sat down with Inverse to discuss their approach to the franchise and dropped an interesting reveal about the inspiration that was very difficult not to pay attention to.

Rather than suggesting that their take on Star Trek would be based on classic space operas or previous Trek installments, the filmmaking duo revealed the script uses two iconic Denzel Washington thrillers as spiritual touchstones: Antoine Fuqua's intense masterpiece Training Day and Tony Scott's Crimson Tide.

Interestingly, both films revolve around institutional friction and power and morality struggles, which the directors confirmed are right at the center of their Star Trek pitch. Daley revealed how the movie will focus on Starfleet:

I would say it's really kind of a look at the institution of Starfleet and any institution as a whole that has existed for a long time and what that does. I think the portrayal of Starfleet and the people that work within it have always been generally idealized versions of what the future could be. And while this [script] isn’t a cynical or nihilistic take on it, I think it does acknowledge that there are some bad apples in every institution that can have a strong voice and can really make an impact on that institution and everything it stands for.

This is a bold move, given how much that sort of interrogation of Starfleet has happened on the TV side over the past few years, and not always to fans' tastes. Daley then added that the narrative will unfold almost entirely in real time and those "bad apples" will not come from any version of either the Original Series or Next Generation's cast, but with a brand-new group of original characters in place of the legacy icons.

Turning Training Day and Crimson Tide into a Star Trek movie is as intriguing as it is alien-feeling. Even with a little creative license learned from those inspirations, imagining an idealistic new Star Trek officer assigned to a vessel under a decorated, charismatic captain (whose approach violates every directive the Federation claims to uphold) could be a great story.

The real-time structure also means there probably won't be much room for exposition, but we don't really need that in this sort of universe because we've been here so many times. That means a more urgent sense of storytelling and focus on building up pressure-cooker tension instead of some faceless alien armada threatening to blow up part of the Federation again. The primary threat being ideological and personal is a smart move for a franchise testing the waters of a new reboot and looking for something new. There is familiarity here, of course, but at least the tonal shift suggests difference, which is exactly what is needed for the new era.

It would be foolish here to ignore the potential for red flags. Whenever you throw around words like "dark," "bad apples," and "Starfleet," some people get a little nervous, after years of streaming shows leaning into grim dystopian politics and a deconstruction of Starfleet as an organization. Plenty of Trekkies are understandably protective of the franchise's core optimism, and it doesn't always need to be an exercise in recognizing fascism.

Goldstein and Daley are clearly aware of that sensitivity, however, and they have been vocal about making sure that the genre pivot will actually enhance the franchise's existing DNA rather than erasing it. In the same conversation with Inverse, Daley explicitly emphasized that their goal is to offer a fresh take on what a Star Trek movie can be without betraying the fundamentals of what it should be.

Having previously proven their knack for handling beloved IP with the brilliant Dungeons and Dragons: Honor Among Thieves and Spider-Man: Homecoming, the duo understands that subverting expectations only really works if you respect the material you're building on. Even Goldstein's comment about not relying exclusively on existing fandom, which might make for some sweaty palms, comes from a place of wanting their movie to stand on its own dramatic merits rather than trading on hollow nostalgia:

We don't want to just hope that there's enough fans of the original [TOS] or The Next Generation or whatever who will turn out for this. We want to just make a great movie that lives in the world that people are familiar with — some people are familiar with — and that others will discover. And then, with good fortune, we launch a new franchise into existence.

And listen: telling a story about somebody defying what Starfleet should be doesn't fundamentally betray the image Roddenberry built. That can still be about upholding Star Trek's vision, not about revealing the truth of it. Crucially, examining corruption and rogue officers inside Starfleet has long been a familiar trope in reinforcing Star Trek's idealism. When a captain or admiral loses their moral compass, those who stand up to them prove why the Federation's principles actually matter.

Ironically, Crimson Tide actually features explicit nods to the original Star Trek and hinges on a classic Star Trek dilemma: two honorable officers clashing over protocol and restraint when the fate of millions hangs in the balance. That is very much the kind of dilemma that either Kirk or Picard would have taken on multiple times over.

Interestingly, Daley also stressed that, with television now delivering cinematic visuals, a Star Trek movie has to justify being on the big screen through genuine spectacle. Even with what sounds like a smaller-scale story, there is clearly going to be something of significant scale at the same time, which means the filmmakers are committed to safeguarding the soul of the franchise as they attempt to give it a much-needed adrenaline shot.

You shouldn't need convincing that swapping genres can be a masterstroke for Star Trek, because you only need to look back at what happened in 1982 when Paramount decided to follow up 1979's cerebral, slow-moving Star Trek: The Motion Picture with Star Trek II: The Wrath of Khan. They gave director Nicholas Meyer the opportunity to steer the franchise away from high-minded philosophical sci-fi and basically allowed him to make a World War II submarine warfare movie.

It's easy to forget that, but Wrath of Khan was a radical departure from The Original Series and its first spinoff movie. Meyer stripped away the sterile white jumpsuits, introduced gritty naval traditions thanks to the influence of C.S. Forester's Horatio Hornblower novels, and created a claustrophobic cat-and-mouse thriller around two aging adversaries with universe-changing stakes. And it's not like the bold genre mashup of military thriller and space opera diluted Star Trek's appeal. If anything, it injected new life into the Star Trek movie series and guaranteed it wouldn't be lost to extinction, while also producing what is widely celebrated as the greatest movie in Star Trek franchise history.

Other Star Trek releases have played with genres as well, of course, with varying levels of success, but none did it quite as well as Wrath of Khan. If Daley and Goldstein refer back to Nicholas Meyer's playbook, which they seem to be doing by looking outside the sci-fi bubble to Training Day and Crimson Tide as inspiration, they too can prove that Star Trek's universe is both flexible and sturdy enough to allow any kind of great storytelling.

Shifting genres and vocally talking about such excellent inspiration points can also be a hook for general audiences who might otherwise skip a more niche space adventure that looks within, but which can also give longtime fans a smart, suspenseful theatrical event that still feels genuinely new. If the script captures even half of the tension of those two excellent Denzel Washington vehicles, Paramount's Star Trek reboot gamble will absolutely pay off.
