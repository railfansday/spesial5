Sunday was shaping up to be another rough day at airports across the country.

Airlines were racing to get operations back on track after Friday's rare nationwide ground stop. But travelers faced a third straight day of widespread flight cancellations.

By 8 a.m. EST Sunday, another 1,400 flights had been canceled, according to data from FlightAware.

1,600 additional flights had already encountered delays.

It's the continued fallout from Friday afternoon's outage at the Federal Aviation Administration, which saw most takeoffs halted across the U.S. for several hours.

The disruption left planes, crewmembers, passengers and their baggage out of place — and fueled what one major U.S. carrier has called one of the most significant disruptions it's ever seen.

All told, more than 17,000 flights have been shuttered since Friday. And travelers weren't out of the woods on Sunday, either.

Flight cancellations appeared to slow entering Sunday, compared to the past two days.

But operations had not fully returned to normal — especially on some carriers.

As of 8 a.m. EST Sunday, per FlightAware:

"We expect disruption throughout the weekend as we work to reset the airline," American's chief customer officer Heather Garboden said in a letter to customers Saturday evening, calling the disruption "among the most significant operational impacts American has experienced."

Delta urged passengers with flexible plans to consider postponing their travel until Monday.

Carriers extended travel waivers that allowed passengers more flexibility to re-book their travel.

FAA administrator Bryan Bedford had said the disruptions were expected to begin easing by Sunday evening.

"Based on everything that we’re hearing from our airline partners, they believe that they’ll be able to have full schedule integrity by the close of business on Sunday," Bedford said at a news conference Saturday.

As of Sunday morning, fewer than 30 flights for Monday had been canceled.

The FAA ordered a nationwide ground stop late Friday afternoon after discovering a communications outage affecting air traffic control.

It happened as crews were working to upgrade critical FAA equipment, apparently overloading the system amid a switch from copper wiring to fiber at one of the agency's facilities.

“This was an FAA system that didn’t meet the moment," Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said at a Saturday press conference. "Our contractor didn’t meet the moment, or the equipment didn’t meet the moment and caused the outages.”

The disruption marked only the second time since 9/11 that a ground stop affecting the entire nation had been issued.

If your flight is affected by this weekend's disruptions, stay glued to your airline app — you may be able to search for a new flight and rebook yourself without waiting on hold or in line at the airport.

Don't see a good flight option for days? Keep checking back: with a lot of passengers shifting plans across the country, it's still possible seats will open up.

Passengers whose flights are canceled or significantly delayed are entitled to a refund if they choose not to travel or accept a rebooked flight, regardless of the reason for the disruption.

Under U.S. Department of Transportation rules, a significant delay generally means arriving at your final destination at least three hours late on a domestic itinerary or six hours late on an international itinerary. For passengers who decide to not travel on those flights, airlines must provide refunds to the original form of payment, not just travel credits.

In large-scale travel disruptions like this, rental cars and hotel rooms at or near airports can book up quickly, so it can make sense to act fast if you want to secure a backup option.

If you have airline miles you can use to book a flight for this weekend, that can also be a good self-insurance policy in case the disruption isn't resolved quickly. Just make sure you understand the cancellation and refund rules before booking a backup ticket.

Because this disruption was due to issues outside airlines' control, carriers are unlikely to reimburse stranded passengers for hotel, ground transportation and meal costs.

Some rewards credit cards do offer built-in trip delay or cancellation protection if you used them to book your original airline tickets.

However, it's not guaranteed that those benefits will cover FAA groundings of this nature. Still, save your receipts for any unexpected expenses in case you're eligible for reimbursement.
