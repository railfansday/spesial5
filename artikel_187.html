Why GTA VI’s version of Florida feels so real

There are plenty of reasons why Grand Theft Auto VI is so popular: the 13 years of anticipation, the relatable lead characters and romantic storyline, the sheer level of depth. But another major factor is the sense of place. The GTA series is unmatched when it comes to re-creating the feeling of a bustling, real-world city, and GTA VI’s fictional rendition of Florida is the studio’s most lifelike setting yet. It has functioning buses and trains, plentiful buildings you can go into and explore, residents you can chat with, and huge areas where you can interact with wildlife far away from the bright lights of the Miami-like Vice City. For Emma Fraser, an assistant professor at UC Berkeley who studies urban ruins and virtual cities, the sense of realism has created a unique sensation while playing.

“There’s something about this particular game that is really discombobulating and distracting in a sense,” she says. “Because you have these moments where you think, ‘I’m in a place, I’m experiencing a location.’ You comprehend intellectually that it’s not real or that it’s at best a representation, but you still have this feeling in your brain and you’ll still dream about being in that place.”

Fraser teaches courses on topics including urban geography and virtual worlds and has written papers exploring topics like “mapping in the digital age” and “the virtual spectacle of the end of the city.” She says she started researching video game cities in part because, despite their influence and reach, they’re an underexplored space in academia. “I’m a geographer, and geographers are obsessed with cities,” she explains. “So much of geography is thinking about: How do we imagine cities? How do we navigate cities? How do we build cities? How do we map cities? And video games have all of that, but they’re not taken that seriously as an academic interest.”

Like much of the world, she’s been looking forward to GTA VI for years, particularly because of Rockstar’s on-the-ground approach to research. Before development on the game began, developers from the studio traveled to Florida to get an accurate picture. As GTA VI producer Rob Nelson told me, the starting point for the game was the setting, rather than the story or characters. “We just went around to feel it out,” he said. “Go to the Everglades, go to the Keys, go to the city, as many places as we can, and start thinking about what aspects of the place we want to incorporate.”

While there are plenty of open-world games, many of the most successful — from Elden Ring to The Witcher — are sent in fantastical spaces. GTA stands apart in how its various settings look and feel true to the real world. And Fraser believes that this is one of the key reasons the series has such universal appeal. “You know immediately how to function in a city,” she explains. “You know there’s going to be law enforcement, there are going to be city blocks, there are going to be pedestrians, cars, and buses. It’s this really interesting environment that gives you a lot of possibilities to get a player more immersed in your world, because they already know a lot about a city as a general concept.”

“You can’t hold a whole city in your mind at one time.”

Fraser describes GTA VI’s world as having “implied expansiveness.” It’s not a one-to-one re-creation of the real location. There are plenty of things missing; you don’t see schools or daycares, and parking spaces are relatively sparse. But the game gets enough details right to be recognizable. Fraser explains that as long as certain parts of the city look correct — the lighting, the coastline, the decoration of street corners, the major arterial roads — that’s enough to convince people that they’re in something resembling Miami.

“You can’t hold a whole city in your mind at one time,” she says. “Instead it’s these highlights. What [Rockstar] do very successfully in the game is those snapshots. They’ve got far more content that is recognizable as Florida or Miami than they’ve been able to fit into previous games. So it’s a much more overwhelming sense of being in that place.” The “implied expansiveness” also comes in the form of dialogue, radio programs, and TV shows that suggest a much bigger world by discussing places and moments that exist outside of the game you can actually explore.

This level of verisimilitude — combined with the fact that players can explore the city from multiple perspectives, everything from walking to flying a plane — has led to an experience that Fraser likens to virtual tourism, which can result in all kinds of different playstyles. “Some tourists are going to blow a lot of money on taxis and get to wherever they’re going as fast as they can,” she explains, “and some people just like sitting on a bus or a train and getting to know a place.” Similarly, there are certain players who will just experience the story and play through the main missions, and others who will get lost in side activities like bird watching.

“Some people just like sitting on a bus or a train and getting to know a place”

What’s also notable about GTA VI is how its relationship-focused premise is motivating some players to experience the world in a much less violent way. And that includes how they move through the city. Some people are driving more carefully to avoid killing civilians, while others are exploring spaces while both characters hang out together. “In the first few games, you’re this empty vessel. You’re a criminal, you’re doing criminal things,” says Fraser. “Whereas with GTA VI your motivation and the way you think about your character is really important.”

Now that the game is out and millions of players are venturing through Vice City, Fraser is hoping to use it to research the ways that real and virtual worlds intersect when it comes to how we navigate cities. GTA VI offers a lot of choice in this regard; you can still steal a Lambo and speed through the streets, but you can also hop on a train, put on some headphones, and listen to a podcast while getting where you need to be. “Because this world is so fully furnished, and you have so many options, you might start to import your own real-world practices into the game,” she says.

But at this point, what has struck her most is the attention to detail that separates the universe of GTA VI from its contemporaries. “You can’t just generate a generic city and stick icons in, which is what a lot of games do,” she explains. “You aren’t going to get a sense of a real place if you do it that way.”
