Informer TV owner Vucicevic arrested: Has the reckoning with Serbia's pro-Vucic media machine begun?

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

The arrest of media owner Dragan J. Vucicevic raises questions about whether Serbia is holding the people behind the country's pro-Vucic media accountable — or whether this is another political power struggle.

"Beat them! Break their ribs," shouted the man in the studio during a live TV broadcast.

The man in question was Dragan J. Vucicevic, editor-in-chief of the channel Informer TV, and he was urging police officers to use force against demonstrators during a major student protest in June 2025.

On November 25, Vucicevic was arrested by police on suspicion of causing panic and disorder and obstructing justice. According to the authorities, his appearances "created an atmosphere of public lynching," and he labeled 97 citizens as enemies of the state.

The authorities cite instances where he described protesters as "scum," "hyenas" and "vultures," and spread claims that the students were preparing a "bloodbath" and a "civil war."

The prosecution says that between 2023 and 2026, Vucicevic systematically used Informer TV to conduct a campaign that incited hatred and targeted activists, independent journalists and political opponents of the previous government.

Vucicevic's arrest comes at a time of profound political change in Serbia.

Following the Student List's victory in the parliamentary election in late October and the end of the Serbian Progressive Party's (SNS) 14-year rule, questions have arisen about the accountability of people who were part of the previous political and institutional system.

Critics have described Vucicevic as the mouthpiece of former President Aleksandar Vucic — a man who said publicly what the president was thinking but could not say himself because of the office he held.

"He [Vucicevic] is someone who stopped being a journalist a long time ago, and I think he crossed the line of being merely a propagandist some time ago. He was, in fact, a weapon in the hands of Aleksandar Vucic and his party," said Ivana Stevanovic of the Slavko Curuvija Foundation, a Belgrade-based organization that provides media support and fights for press freedom. It is named after a Serbian journalist who was killed in 1999.

Stevanovic told DW that Vucicevic used his media platforms to "target and endanger people, spread falsehoods that threatened their safety," all as part of a campaign against political opponents, journalists and media outlets who were critical of the government's record.

"This man showed a female student's intimate photographs live on air. He stalked people. He had his notorious 'Viewer Intelligence Service,' which secretly followed people and filmed them in their everyday lives, then turned that material into disgusting reports," she said.

"We had calls for violence, threats and insults against prosecutors who were doing their jobs and judges who were issuing lawful rulings, expressed in indescribable language. We had wiretapping, recordings of conversations in restaurants presented as conspiracies to overthrow the state. Media outlets and journalists were accused of plotting to assassinate the president, while their reporting was portrayed as treason and selling out Kosovo," she told DW, adding that "the entire television channel is one big criminal offense."

The Regulatory Authority for Electronic Media (REM) has already issued several warnings and temporarily halted the broadcasting of certain programs on Informer TV.

Journalists' and media associations, civil society organizations and representatives of academia have launched an initiative to have the channel's broadcasting license revoked.

According to the Slavko Curuvija Foundation, more than 1,000 court cases concerning the protection of reputation and honor have been brought against Informer in recent years.

"Of the cases concluded over the past three years, courts upheld the claims in 92% of them," said Stevanovic. "Compensation was awarded to people whose rights had been violated. Informer even paid damages to the same individuals several times for the same lies, which it kept publishing about them. None of these financial penalties made them stop."

On one occasion, Dragan J. Vucicevic himself asked REM to impose a financial penalty on him rather than ban part of his program.

Money, it seems, was not a problem for Insajder Tim, the company behind all Informer media platforms (a daily tabloid newspaper, a television channel and an online portal).

According to an investigation by Raskrikavanje, a fact-checking website that monitors the output of Serbian media, Insajder Tim received around €273,000 ($299,000) in public funds in 2024 alone through public procurement contracts, advertising and media project funding.

Representatives of the Student List have claimed that the state-owned telecommunications company Telekom Srbija paid Informer between €500,000 and €700,000 a month.

Telekom Srbija, which also owns a cable network distributing television channels to millions of users in Serbia, cited business confidentiality when asked about the details of its cooperation with Informer.

Vucicevic and his lawyers reject the accusations.

The defense argues that the proceedings amount to punishing someone for words they have spoken and raise questions about the boundary between freedom of expression and criminal liability.

Former President Aleksandar Vucic said Vucicevic had been arrested solely because of his journalistic work. SNS lawmakers walked out of a parliamentary session following news of the arrest and gathered outside the police station where the Informer editor was being held.

Stevanovic maintains that by arresting Vucicevic, Serbia is fulfilling its international obligations, including those arising from the European Convention on Human Rights and international conventions protecting civil and political rights.

"What constitutes a call for violence and a threat to democracy, security and health must be sanctioned and removed from the public sphere," she said.

Vucicevic has been remanded in custody for 30 days.

The offence of causing panic and disorder through the media carries a prison sentence of between six months and five years.

Stevanovic points out, however, that the outcome of the case is not certain because the executive branch, the police and the Ministry of the Interior formally remain under the control of the outgoing government until such time as the Student List forms a new cabinet.

"What is happening now could be an internal power struggle within their own ranks, or an attempt by some prosecutors to clean up their records and ingratiate themselves with the new authorities, assuming what their wishes and intentions regarding Informer might be," she said. "Or it could be a serious manipulation, a staged arrest designed to portray Serbia abroad as a country carrying out revolutionary terror, which, of course, is not true."
