Criterion Channel Acquires Three Shorts By Tomisin Adepeju; Filmmaker Talks Directing & His Popular Dailies Workshop Series

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

The Criterion Channel has acquired three short films by the London-based Nigerian British filmmaker Tomisin Adepeju.

The three films, Appreciation (2019), Journey Mercies (2023), and Lift Me Up (2026), will be available to stream on Criterion from December 1.

Adepeju wrote and directed each of the shorts, which were produced in London and feature both English and Yoruba language dialogue.

“I tried to look this up, but I don’t think there are any other Yoruba films in the collection,” Adepeju told Deadline in an interview ahead of the acquisition announcement. “As a diasporic filmmaker, I hope this means there’ll be more films by British Nigerian filmmakers included. Adepeju is a popular figure across the UK industry. His shorts have screened at Sundance, the BFI London Film Festival and the London Short Film Festival. He is also known for his influential screening programme and workshop series Dailies, which runs monthly across London. Watch on Deadline

“As a filmmaker, you’re trying to make your films and get them seen. But I realised other artists like me want to learn more about the craft of filmmaking, network, and meet like-minded people,” Adepeju says about the initial idea behind Dailies. “Initially, it was just about showing films. I curated monthly screenings of shorts and features, anchored by discussions with filmmakers about their work and craft. Through that, people could meet others and be inspired to make their own work.”

Dailies has now been running for four years. Previous speakers at Dailies events include Mia Bays, former director of the BFI Filmmaking Fund, Farhana Bhula, director of Film4, and Eva Yates, director of BBC Film. Films screened at Dailies include Akinola Davies Jr.’s My Father’s Shadow, Savanah Leaf’s Earth Mama, and Boris Lojkine’s Souleymane’s Story.

Adepeju adds, “Dailies is also now informing my practice, and vice versa. Through these events, I’m meeting commissioners, casting directors, and producers. It’s been incredible, really nice to have the two work side by side.”

Adepeju says his narrative fiction work is largely shaped by questions of identity, “exploring who you are and what informs your character, your faith, and your relationships,” particularly in relation to Nigerians in the diaspora, but not exclusively.

“I was inspired by growing up watching films by Jean-Luc Godard, Bergman, and Ozu—world cinema, really. Those films were culturally specific, but they also encompassed universal themes,” he says.

“In the same way, I’m making films about Nigerians in the diaspora, particularly in South London, and the themes and issues that shape their lives: grief, love, death, identity, and all of those things. That’s what binds the three films together. They’re culturally rooted in South London, but they’re about universal issues that, regardless of where you’re from, I hope you can see the humanity in.”

Earlier this year, Adepeju completed a new short titled It’s Dancing Time for BBC Film, produced by Jack Thomas-O’Brien and Naomi Rachel Smith of Sixteen Films alongside Paul Romero Mendez. Adepeju is also in development on a feature, to be produced by Thomas-O’Brien, with the BFI on board.

“The feature explores the same themes as my other work, but it’s exciting to work on a longer-form piece. Ultimately, that’s what I’ve been working towards for the past five or six years,” Adepeju says.

“I’ve been inspired by friends of mine, like Akinola Davies Jr., who made My Father’s Shadow. I programmed his short film Lizard, and then we screened his feature early this year. I’ve screened Scrapper by Charlotte Regan and Last Swim by Sasha Nathwani. There have been so many great first features, especially by British directors. Seeing and programming all this work is inspiring, and it has definitely informed my own first feature.”

Adepeju jokes that he aims to get his feature out while there is still a significant buzz around films directed by British-Nigerians, with filmmakers like Davies Jr. and Olive Nwosu (Lady, 2026) debuting features at major festivals.

“Olive’s film was made with Film4 and the BFI. Akin’s was made with BBC Film and the BFI. These institutions are now supporting us to make our work, which is timely, urgent, important, and well-crafted,” Adepeju says.

Appreciation (2019), Journey Mercies (2023), and Lift Me Up (2026) will be available to stream on the Criterion Channel from December 1.
