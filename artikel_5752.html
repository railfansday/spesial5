Is There Still a Place for Antibiotics in Modern Hidradenitis Suppurativa Treatment?

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

— Megan Rogge, MD, discusses the shift toward shorter courses and biologic bridging

As treatment options for hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) continue to expand, one of the questions discussed at the European Academy of Dermatology and Venereology meeting in Vienna was how antibiotics can still be beneficial in disease management, particularly as biologics and other targeted therapies take on a larger role.

In this exclusive MedPage Today video, Megan Rogge, MD, of McGovern Medical School at UTHealth Houston, discusses how she thinks about antibiotic use moving forward, including the shift toward shorter courses, their role as bridge therapy, and the importance of limiting long-term exposure to reduce resistance risk.

Following is a transcript of her remarks:

I think that we are getting away from treating HS as an infection. So, of course, the microbiome is really important and definitely sometimes infections can drive flares. However, it's not very common. A lot of hidradenitis lesions are sterile, and therefore we're getting away from antimicrobial and we're really focusing more on anti-inflammatory.

So, the tetracycline-class antibiotics such as doxycycline, minocycline, these are going to be still potentially beneficial for the patient in the long term because they have these anti-inflammatory effects. But we're not using them as monotherapy as much anymore because again, as soon as we are recognizing that there is moderate disease, we are going to be really initiating more biologic therapy and potentially some of these other things as they're approved. So, the bridging aspect is absolutely true. Maybe we'll be starting with antibiotics along with a biologic and then allowing that biologic some time to take effect.

And, yes, I think we also want to consider shorter courses of antibiotics for patients. So, the combination of clindamycin [and] rifampin, for instance, has been studied and that's really just for 10 to 12 weeks. And so, it's really kind of more of a short-term treatment and not a long-term treatment option we want patients on. Similarly, ertapenem is really more of a rescue treatment. And, again, it's a short and defined course.

We don't want to put these patients at risk for multi-resistant bacteria, and therefore using antibiotics in shorter courses as bridge therapy, or primarily for other anti-inflammatory effects, are really what we are moving towards with hidradenitis.
