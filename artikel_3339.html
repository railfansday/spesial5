Cinematographer Caleb Deschanel on Finding the Right Techniques For ‘The Right Stuff’

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

“The Right Stuff” was released 43 years ago, but its spectacular aerial and outer space sequences have lost none of their power. Director Philip Kaufman’s adaptation of Tom Wolfe’s book, which traced the history of the test pilots who became the first astronauts, features immersive set pieces that are still as convincing as ever, in spite of decades of technological advancements since it was made. To revisit it via Criterion‘s new 4K UHD physical media release is to experience one of the most groundbreaking and enduring films of all time.

The new Criterion transfer was supervised by cinematographer Caleb Deschanel, who was still relatively early in his career when Kaufman tapped him to shoot “The Right Stuff.” Deschanel didn’t have many feature credits, but the ones he did have were impressive: his early work included the stunning imagery of “The Black Stallion” and two Hal Ashby movies, “Being There” and the Rolling Stones concert film “Let’s Spend the Night Together.” He would earn the first of his six Academy Award nominations for “The Right Stuff.” Related Stories Where Are Rick Dalton and Sharon Tate in ‘The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth’? ‘Bad Lieutenant: Tokyo’ Will Come to the U.S. Next March

Deschanel had met Kaufman years earlier when Deschanel was a film student at USC, and Kaufman was a young director working on the Western “The Great Northfield Minnesota Raid.” “A couple friends and I put on these rock shows at Shrine Auditorium, and he came to a couple of those,” Deschanel told IndieWire. Deschanel visited the set of “Minnesota” and stayed in touch with Kaufman, who called him around 10 years later with the offer to shoot “The Right Stuff.”

“It was a dream come true,” Deschanel said, noting that he had always been an aviation buff who loved going to air shows with his father. He also remembered that Kaufman wasn’t the only person on “The Right Stuff” who he had met years earlier. “Years before we had moved into our first house and needed some painting done, and we had a friend who was teaching in the Cal Arts theater department. He said, ‘I have this actor who’s really great who paints houses in the summer.'”

Deschanel hired the young actor and got to know him a little bit, and five years later was having lunch with his wife Mary Jo and Kaufman when Kaufman showed them a picture of the actor he had chosen to play John Glenn. “I said, ‘We know him. That’s Ed Harris. He painted our house,'” Deschanel said. “Then, coincidentally, the woman who was supposed to play Annie Glenn dropped out of and Phil cast my wife, so they ended up playing husband and wife in the movie.”

One of the great technical challenges of “The Right Stuff” was integrating archival footage of the space race with the new material being shot — one of the reasons the movie was shot in a 1.85:1 aspect ratio instead of a wider Panavision format as one might have expected for such an epic film. “We were using actual footage of NASA’s rocket launches and things like that, and they were filming in the old-fashioned 4:3 format using the full frame of a 35mm camera,” Deschanel said. “Converting that to widescreen was really impossible.”

Working in close collaboration with visual effects supervisor Gary Gutierrez (who provides a fascinating look at the film’s miniature and rear-projection work on the Criterion disc), Deschanel solved the numerous logistical problems that came with trying to make it look as though the actors were hurtling through the air at the speed of sound and beyond. Even when Gutierrez thought he had solutions that would work, they often had to be tweaked to accommodate Kaufman’s desire to make everything feel as realistic and immersive as possible.

Early attempts to use a motion-control system for the effects, for example, were discarded because the camera movement was too smooth for Kaufman’s purposes. “Phil wanted it to have some energy to it,” Deschanel said. “That changed the whole methodology.” Whenever rockets were taking off or various crafts were flying, Deschanel made sure to add some shake to the camera. “I actually gave my camera operator, Craig Denault, a black eye at one point because I was hitting the camera so hard.”

In addition to the expected challenges, there were plenty of unexpected ones as well — like the fact that Sam Shepard, who played ace pilot Chuck Yeager, wouldn’t fly. “The only time he ever went up in an airplane was when Yeager took him for a flight,” Deschanel said. “Other than that, whenever we had a mockup of an X-1 or the F-104 or anything like that, he’d make me get in and show him how it was going to twist and turn before he would get into it.” For Yeager’s scenes and many of the others, rear projection was used — meaning Deschanel had to photograph what was seen outside the pilots’ and astronauts’ windows long before the actors’ material was actually shot.

“There was a lot of complexity to all those things,” Deschanel said. “But it gave it a real energy and sense of being there. And that’s what you try to discover as you’re filming any film: what is that visual metaphor that really makes you believe you’re having the same experience the characters are having?” Creating that sense of authenticity required some unorthodox approaches, as when Deschanel had a Learjet pilot take the nose cone off of his plane and replace it with a clear dome.

“We put a camera in there with a 15mm lens, and then we were able to go up above 50,000 feet,” Deschanel said. “At that level, it’s so dark, it’s almost like you’re in outer space. Anything you could think of to find the reality of the movie, we employed.” Deschanel’s perfectionism became the stuff of legend over the years, with some people who worked on “The Right Stuff” claiming he insisted on shooting only at sunrise or sunset to get the most beautiful possible images — a myth Deschanel is quick to dispel.

“The fact is we shot all day long and had really long days,” Deschanel said. “There was one point where, just to finish up at Edwards Air Force Base, I was on for 24 hours and changed the crew in the middle. So I had a whole new crew for the second half of that long day.” That said, Deschanel acknowledges that the film’s justly famous exteriors were important to him, in part for what they represented.

“The iconic moment is when Yeager is on horseback, and he rides up and sees the X-1,” Deschanel said. “It’s like ‘The Professionals’ or ‘The Wild Bunch,’ these movies about the end of the West as new technology takes over. To me, there’s this wonderful representation of the end of an era when Yeager comes across this fighter jet. Then, of course, he flies it and breaks the sound barrier. So it’s this wonderful juxtaposition of important moments in human history, where one person does something that further advances flight to the point that makes it possible for the next generation to go into outer space. I always miss that I never got to make a Western, but in a way, ‘The Right Stuff’ is a Western.”

“The Right Stuff” special edition 4K UHD release is now available from Criterion.
