How the Ford Bronco went from compact 4x4 to full-size SUV and back

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Ford Bronco generations span six numbered versions since 1966. This guide explains each one, plus the Bronco II, Bronco Sport and Bronco Raptor, as the nameplate turns 60

Ford $F revealed the first Bronco in August 1965 as a 1966 model, which puts the nameplate's 60th anniversary in 2025. Over those six decades, the name has gone on very different vehicles. A compact off-roader, a full-size sport utility vehicle and a current family of off-road models have all carried the Bronco badge.

The story does not run in a straight line. Ford launched the Bronco as a small 4x4 built for rough terrain. Later versions shared their underpinnings with the F-Series pickup and grew much larger. Ford dropped the name after the 1996 model year and handed the full-size SUV role to the Expedition. The Bronco then stayed off the market for more than two decades. It returned for the 2021 model year as an off-road-focused SUV once more.

Tracing the Ford Bronco generations in order shows how one nameplate tracked the changes in Ford's truck and SUV lineup. The Bronco began in a small class of compact utility vehicles. It then became a large SUV tied to Ford's full-size pickup line. Its size and styling changed several times during the full-size years. The current model returned to the off-road purpose of the first one.

The history also has practical value. A Bronco from the late 1960s and one from the early 1990s share a name, but they are very different vehicles. Anyone shopping for a classic or used example needs to know which version is which. Body styles, dimensions and platforms changed from one era to the next. Related models add more room for confusion. The Bronco II and the Bronco Sport carry the name but sit outside the main line of numbered generations.

This guide covers all six numbered generations in order, from the original compact 4x4 to the current model. It also explains the related vehicles that wear the Bronco name. One section covers a 1994 news event that put a Bronco on live U.S. television. Each section can be read on its own, in any order.

The first-generation Ford $F Bronco was a compact, short-wheelbase off-roader that Ford built to compete with the International Harvester Scout and the Jeep CJ. It was sold for the 1966 through 1977 model years. Four-wheel drive came standard on every first-generation example.

Its two rivals came from different backgrounds. The Jeep CJ descended from the military Jeep of World War II. The CJ initials stood for Civilian Jeep. Ford had a direct link to that wartime vehicle. The company built Jeeps under contract during the war, alongside Willys-Overland. International Harvester, a Chicago-based maker of farm equipment and trucks, introduced the Scout in 1961. The Bronco gave Ford its own entry in that small four-wheel-drive segment.

Donald N. Frey was the product manager behind the project. The vehicle that emerged was a dedicated compact off-roader rather than a shortened version of a bigger truck. That sets the first Bronco apart from the full-size, pickup-based Broncos that came after it.

The Bronco rode on a 92-inch wheelbase. Wheelbase is the distance between the front and rear axles. A short one lets a vehicle turn more tightly. It also lowers the chance of the underbody scraping as the vehicle crests a steep rise. Both traits help on narrow, uneven trails.

Ford fitted coil springs to the front suspension and leaf springs at the rear.

Ford kept the same basic body for all 12 model years. A 1977 Bronco shares its core shape with a 1966 example. The first generation never got a full redesign during that run.

The first engine offered was a 170-cubic-inch inline-six. Ford added a V8 later in the production run. That gave buyers a choice between six and eight cylinders in the same compact body.

The second-generation Bronco, built on a different platform, replaced it for the 1978 model year.

The first Ford $F Bronco was sold in three body styles. Buyers could choose a wagon with a full roof, a roadster with no roof or a half-cab pickup with a roof over the front seats only.

All three shared the same short-wheelbase chassis and the same front end. The differences sat behind the front seats, in how much of the vehicle was covered and what the rear space was meant to carry.

The wagon was the closed version. Its roof covered both the passenger area and the cargo space behind it. That gave occupants and anything stowed in back protection from rain and cold. The top could also be removed, so wagon owners had an open-air option without buying a separate model.

The roadster went the other way. It came with no roof at all, leaving the cabin open to the weather. That made it the most stripped-down of the three and the closest in layout to the open utility vehicles of the period.

The half-cab split the difference. A short roof sheltered the driver and front passenger and ended just behind the front seats. That partial roof is where the name half-cab comes from. Behind the seats, the body stayed open as a small cargo bed. In practice it was a compact four-wheel-drive pickup built on the Bronco's short chassis.

Ford did not keep all three for long. The roadster and the half-cab were dropped after a few years. The wagon carried on through the end of the first generation in 1977. For the later years of that run, it was the only Bronco body style Ford sold.

Ford also layered trim packages on top of those body styles. The Sport and Ranger trims added extra equipment and dressier appearance details to what started as a basic utility truck. These packages changed how the Bronco was finished inside and out. The body underneath stayed the same.

Ford later reused the Ranger name for its compact pickup truck, a separate vehicle from the Bronco.

The second-generation Ford $F Bronco, sold for the 1978 and 1979 model years, moved the SUV onto the full-size F-Series pickup platform. The result was a much larger vehicle built to compete with the Chevrolet Blazer and the Dodge Ramcharger.

That change ended the Bronco's life as a compact off-roader. The original model had been sized to take on the International Harvester Scout and the Jeep CJ. Its replacement belonged to a different class of truck, with a bigger cabin and a chassis shared with Ford's full-size pickups.

Chevrolet set the template for that class. It launched the full-size K5 Blazer for the 1969 model year on its pickup truck underpinnings. Dodge followed with the Ramcharger for 1974. Both offered far more interior room than the first-generation Bronco.

Ford had planned a bigger Bronco earlier in the decade. The 1973 oil crisis and the fuel concerns that followed pushed the program back. The larger truck did not reach showrooms until the 1978 model year.

Building on the F-Series platform let Ford share much of the Bronco's front-end structure and styling with its pickups. From the front, the Bronco closely resembled an F-150 of the same years. The difference came behind the doors, where a shorter body ended in a tailgate instead of a cargo bed.

The rear section of the roof could be removed. That design kept some of the open-air appeal of the original Bronco. With the top in place, owners had a closed cabin for bad weather. The rear window in the tailgate could also be lowered into the gate.

Every second-generation Bronco used a V8 engine. A 351-cubic-inch V8 was standard, and a 400-cubic-inch V8 was the larger option. Ford offered no six-cylinder engine, a break from the first generation.

Trim levels ranged from the basic Custom to the more comfortable Ranger XLT.

The second generation lasted just two model years, the shortest run of any numbered Bronco generation. Ford replaced it for 1980 with a redesigned version that remained a full-size truck.

Ford $F made the Bronco smaller and lighter for the 1980 model year, in part to improve its fuel economy. The third-generation Bronco kept its full-size F-Series roots in a more efficient package and stayed in production through the 1986 model year.

The redesign tracked the truck underneath it. Ford introduced a new F-Series pickup for 1980, the seventh generation of that line, and the Bronco moved onto the updated platform. The SUV and the pickup shared their front-end styling. That kept the Bronco visually tied to Ford's pickups.

Fuel costs formed the backdrop. The second-generation Bronco, sold for 1978 and 1979, was a large truck sold just as fuel prices began to climb. The 1979 oil shock followed the Iranian Revolution. It pushed U.S. gasoline prices higher and brought shortages and long lines at filling stations in some areas.

Federal rules also changed. The Energy Policy and Conservation Act of 1975 created the Corporate Average Fuel Economy program, known as CAFE. Its standards for light trucks took effect with the 1979 model year. That put SUVs such as the Bronco under federal fuel economy targets for the first time.

Ford trimmed the Bronco rather than giving up its full-size format. The third generation kept the two-door body and a removable rear roof section. Buyers still had an open-air option. The cabin and cargo area remained larger than those of the original compact Bronco from the 1960s.

The styling changed during the run. For 1982, Ford moved its blue oval emblem onto the grille of its pickups and the Bronco. It replaced the block "FORD" lettering that earlier trucks carried across the front.

The full-size Bronco also gained a smaller sibling during this period. Starting with the 1984 model year, Ford sold the Ranger-based Bronco II in the same showrooms. The full-size truck kept its place in the lineup.

The third-generation design ran for seven model years, far longer than the two-year run of the model it replaced. Ford followed it for 1987 with a fourth generation that restyled this design instead of starting from scratch.

The Bronco II was a separate, smaller Ford $F SUV built on the Ranger compact pickup. Ford sold it for the 1984 through 1990 model years. It shared the Bronco name but was not a numbered generation of the full-size Bronco, which stayed in Ford showrooms alongside it the entire time.

Ford built the Bronco II to fill a gap in its own lineup. By the early 1980s, the full-size Bronco rode on F-Series underpinnings. It had grown well beyond the compact proportions of the 1966 original. Ford had no smaller utility vehicle to sell next to it.

The Ranger supplied the foundation. Ford launched that compact pickup for the 1983 model year. The Bronco II borrowed its chassis and front-end design. The result was a short, two-door utility vehicle with a fixed steel roof and a rear liftgate. It was much shorter than the full-size Bronco.

The compact SUV segment was filling up at the same time. Chevrolet introduced the S-10 Blazer for 1983, built on its own small pickup. Jeep followed with the unibody Cherokee for 1984. The Bronco II reached buyers in the same model year as that Jeep.

Power came from V6 engines. The first models used a 2.8-liter V6. Ford moved to a 2.9-liter V6 for 1986. Four-wheel-drive versions used Ford's Twin-Traction Beam front suspension, the same design found on four-wheel-drive Rangers of the period. Ford also added a two-wheel-drive Bronco II for 1986.

Ford assembled the Bronco II at its Louisville Assembly Plant in Kentucky. The model offered only two doors throughout its seven-year run. No four-door body was ever added.

Ford replaced the Bronco II after the 1990 model year with the Explorer, which arrived for 1991. The Explorer kept a Ranger-derived foundation but added a four-door body, the configuration the Bronco II never had. Ford also offered a two-door Explorer for buyers who preferred the shorter layout. The Bronco II name did not return after that changeover.

The fourth-generation Ford $F Bronco, sold for the 1987 through 1991 model years, updated the third-generation truck rather than replacing it. Ford revised its styling to match the F-Series pickups of the period.

The update kept the Bronco visually tied to the full-size pickup line it had been built from since 1978. When the look of Ford's F-Series trucks changed, the Bronco's appearance changed with them. The SUV and the pickup kept a shared family resemblance.

The underlying package carried over from the 1980–1986 Bronco. Ford had already made that truck smaller and lighter than the 1978–1979 version. The fourth generation kept that size instead of starting from a clean sheet.

That makes the boundary between the third and fourth generations mostly a matter of appearance. The truck underneath stayed in the same family. For anyone sorting through used or classic examples, the revised styling is what separates a 1987–1991 Bronco from the full-size version that came just before it.

The run lasted five model years. That was longer than the two-year second generation. It was shorter than the seven-year third generation that preceded it.

For most of those years, the full-size Bronco shared Ford showrooms with the smaller Bronco II. The two trucks carried the same badge but sat on different platforms.

The fourth generation ended after the 1991 model year. A redesigned fifth-generation Bronco replaced it for 1992.

The fifth-generation Ford $F Bronco covered the 1992 through 1996 model years, and Ford redesigned it alongside the F-Series pickups it was selling at the time. Ford retired the Bronco name after the 1996 model year.

That shared redesign kept the full-size Bronco closely tied to Ford's pickup line. From 1978 onward, every full-size Bronco had been built on F-Series foundations, and this generation followed the same approach. Its front-end styling matched the F-Series trucks of the same model years.

The basic layout did not change. The full-size Bronco remained a two-door SUV through its final model year. Ford's lineup already included a four-door option by then. The Explorer, a midsize SUV that replaced the Bronco II for the 1991 model year, was offered with four doors as well as two.

Ford sold the fifth generation in several trim levels, including XLT and Eddie Bauer. The Eddie Bauer version carried the name of the outdoor clothing brand founded in Seattle. Ford used the same tie-in on the Explorer and later the Expedition.

Ford introduced the Expedition for the 1997 model year as its new full-size SUV. It took over the role the Bronco had filled in Ford's lineup. The Expedition shared its underpinnings with the redesigned F-150 that Ford launched for the same model year.

The new SUV's body differed from the Bronco's in two ways. The Expedition had four doors and an available third row of seats. The full-size Bronco never offered either.

The Expedition also became the basis for a luxury model. Lincoln launched the Navigator for the 1998 model year on the same foundation. It was Lincoln's first SUV. The Expedition remains in Ford's lineup today.

The Bronco name then stayed out of Ford's lineup for 24 model years, from 1997 through 2020. It returned for the 2021 model year with an all-new design.

On June 17, 1994, a white 1993 Ford $F Bronco driven by A.C. Cowlings was at the center of a televised police pursuit in Los Angeles involving O.J. Simpson. Simpson rode in the vehicle as police followed it across Southern California freeways.

The pursuit came after Simpson failed to turn himself in to police that day. He had been charged with murder in the deaths of his former wife, Nicole Brown Simpson, and her friend Ronald Goldman. Both had been found dead outside her Los Angeles home earlier that month.

Cowlings was a longtime friend of Simpson. The two played football together at the University of Southern California and later with the Buffalo Bills. The Bronco in the pursuit belonged to Cowlings. Simpson owned a separate white Bronco of his own.

The chase moved at low speed. Patrol cars trailed the Bronco in a long column rather than trying to force it off the road. News helicopters tracked it from overhead. Bystanders gathered on overpasses and along the route to watch it pass.

Television networks broke into scheduled programming to carry the pursuit live. NBC was airing Game 5 of the NBA Finals between the New York Knicks and the Houston Rockets that night. The network shifted part of its coverage to the pursuit.

The pursuit ended at Simpson's home in the Brentwood neighborhood of Los Angeles. He surrendered to police there later that evening.

Simpson's criminal trial followed. Jury selection began in the fall of 1994, and the trial ran through much of 1995. A jury acquitted him of both murders on October 3, 1995.

Within the Bronco's own history, the vehicle in the pursuit was a fifth-generation model. That generation was the full-size version Ford sold for the 1992 through 1996 model years. It was also the last version of the original Bronco before Ford retired the nameplate after 1996.

Ford $F revealed the sixth-generation Bronco on July 13, 2020, and it went on sale for the 2021 model year.

The new Ford Bronco comes in two body styles, a two-door and a four-door. The two-door rides on a shorter wheelbase. That gives it proportions closer to the compact Bronco Ford launched in the 1960s. The four-door has a longer body with rear doors and more room for passengers and cargo.

Both versions have a removable roof and removable doors. With those pieces off, the Bronco can be driven with an open cabin. The doors are frameless, which means the side glass is not surrounded by a metal window frame. Ford designed the roof and doors to come off as part of the factory vehicle, not as an aftermarket conversion.

The styling draws directly on the first-generation Bronco. Round headlights, a flat hood and a boxy, upright body recall the shape of the 1966 original.

Ford builds the Bronco at its Michigan Assembly Plant in Wayne, Michigan. The SUV uses body-on-frame construction. In that traditional truck layout, the body is mounted on a separate ladder-type chassis. The layout is common among vehicles built for heavy off-road use.

The Sasquatch package is one of the Bronco's main off-road options. It is aimed at buyers who plan to take the SUV onto rough trails. It fits larger off-road tires along with other trail-focused hardware. The name refers to the large, hairy creature of North American folklore.

Ford also equips the Bronco with a terrain management system it calls G.O.A.T. Modes, short for Goes Over Any Type of Terrain. The driver selects a mode to match the surface, such as sand, mud or rock. The system then adjusts the vehicle's settings for those conditions.

The Bronco competes most directly with the Jeep Wrangler. Both are sold in two-door and four-door forms, and both offer a removable roof and doors.

The Bronco Sport arrived for the 2021 model year as a smaller, separate model sold alongside the Bronco. The Bronco Raptor, a performance off-road version of the Bronco, followed for the 2022 model year.

The two models turned one nameplate into a family of vehicles in different sizes and with different levels of capability. Ford $F introduced the Bronco Sport at the same July 2020 event where it unveiled the new two-door and four-door Bronco. The Bronco Sport reached dealers in late 2020, months before the first Bronco deliveries in 2021.

The Bronco Sport is built differently from the Bronco. It uses unibody construction, in which the body and frame form a single structure. It shares its underpinnings with the Ford Escape compact crossover. The Bronco uses a separate body-on-frame design, the layout traditionally used for pickups and dedicated off-roaders.

Production is split between two countries. Ford builds the Bronco Sport at its Hermosillo plant in Mexico. The Bronco is assembled at Michigan Assembly Plant in Wayne, Michigan.

The Bronco Sport borrows several ideas from its larger sibling. Both offer G.O.A.T. Modes, Ford's name for selectable drive settings matched to conditions such as mud, sand or snow. The letters stand for "Goes Over Any Type of Terrain." The Badlands trim is the most off-road-focused version of the Bronco Sport.

The Bronco Raptor sits at the other end of the range. Ford Performance, the automaker's performance division, developed it. It is based on the four-door Bronco and adds wider fenders and larger tires than other versions of the SUV.

The Raptor name came from the F-150 pickup. Ford introduced the F-150 SVT Raptor for the 2010 model year as a factory-built truck designed for high-speed desert driving. The Bronco Raptor carries that badge over from Ford's full-size pickup to its body-on-frame off-road SUV.

The two additions give the Bronco name a wider spread than it had in its first life. Across the original 1966 to 1996 run, the only other vehicle to carry the name was the Ranger-based Bronco II.
