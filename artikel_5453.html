From DNS to undersea fiber: 10 organizations the web depends on

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

Free daily briefing on global business news.

A selection of companies that run the internet, plus two nonprofits, behind the domain names, cables, data centers and security certificates few people ever see

Typing a web address and watching a page load feels instant. Inside that brief gap, the request passes through a chain of organizations that most people never see and rarely hear named. Their work goes unnoticed when it functions and becomes visible only when it fails.

The first step is translation. A browser has to turn a name such as qz.com into a numeric IP address that computers use to find each other. That lookup runs through the domain name system, a layered hierarchy of registries and servers coordinated worldwide. Next comes delivery. The page often arrives from a copy stored on a content delivery network server near the reader, not from one distant machine. The site itself may run on computing power rented from a cloud provider rather than hardware its owner bought. The data moves over fiber backbone networks and, between continents, through cables laid on the seabed. An encrypted HTTPS connection depends on a digital certificate issued by a certificate authority.

This list profiles internet infrastructure organizations across 10 entries. It is a selection, not a ranking or a complete roster. Most entries are companies that run the internet's core services as commercial businesses. Two are nonprofits. ICANN coordinates domain names and IP address allocation. Let's Encrypt, a free certificate authority, is run by the Internet Security Research Group.

The reason to know these names is concentration. Many websites rely on the same small group of providers for naming, delivery, hosting and security. When one provider handles a function for many sites, a fault there can reach all of them at once. A configuration error, an outage or a policy decision at a single organization can disrupt services that look unrelated to the people using them.

The slides that follow take each organization in turn. They explain what it does, where it sits on the path between a reader and a website, and what the technical terms around it mean. Some took on their role in the late 1990s, while others arrived more recently.

Cloudflare sits between a website and its visitors, handling traffic before it reaches the site's own servers. In that position, the company combines three services: a content delivery network, a domain name system (DNS) provider and protection against distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) attacks.

Sitting in front of a website has a specific technical meaning. When a site uses Cloudflare as a reverse proxy, visitors connect to Cloudflare's servers rather than directly to the site's origin server. Cloudflare then decides which requests to answer itself and which to pass along. The origin server's real address can stay out of public view.

That position at the network edge is what lets one provider do several jobs at once. Every request to a protected site already flows through its machines. The same servers that deliver content can also inspect incoming requests and filter out unwanted ones.

The content delivery side works through caching. Cloudflare stores copies of files that rarely change, such as images, scripts and style sheets. It serves those stored copies to visitors without asking the origin server for them each time. That cuts the load on the site's own hardware.

DNS is the second layer. Cloudflare can act as the authoritative DNS provider for a domain. That means its servers hold the official records saying which IP address a domain name points to. Lookups for the domain end at Cloudflare's servers, which return the address. A slow or failed DNS answer means a page never starts loading, however healthy the server behind it.

DDoS protection is the third. A DDoS attack floods a target with traffic from many machines at once, often hijacked devices, until it cannot respond to real users. Cloudflare already receives a protected site's traffic. That lets it absorb or drop attack traffic before it reaches the origin.

Combining these roles carries a trade-off. A site that routes its traffic, its DNS and its defenses through Cloudflare depends on Cloudflare staying online. A fault inside the edge network can make the sites behind it unreachable together. Their own origin servers may keep running normally the whole time.

Akamai $AKAM Technologies is one of the earliest content delivery networks, a company founded in 1998 out of research at MIT. It runs a distributed network of servers that delivers web content to users from nearby locations.

A content delivery network, or CDN, addresses two problems: distance and demand. Without one, every visitor requests files from the website's own origin server. That server may sit far from many of the people using it.

Distance adds delay. Data crossing continents passes through more networks and more handoffs before it arrives. Each handoff is another point where traffic can slow down or get congested.

Demand creates the second problem. A single origin server has limited capacity. When a large number of people request the same page at once, that server can become overloaded and stop responding.

A CDN spreads the work out. It stores copies of a site's content on servers in many locations. It then directs each user to a nearby server rather than to the origin. The origin handles fewer requests, and the user's data travels a shorter path.

The steering often happens through the domain name system. When a browser looks up a site's name, the CDN can answer with the address of a server close to that user. The user never chooses a server and usually never notices the redirection.

Spreading content across many servers also adds resilience. If one server or location fails, requests can be sent to another that holds the same files. The site stays reachable even when part of the network is down.

The arrangement changes what a website owner has to build. The owner does not need servers on every continent. The owner pays a CDN operator to provide that reach instead.

Akamai came out of university research rather than out of a telephone or cable company. Its business is the servers and software that deliver content, not the cables that carry it.

Most people who use the internet never interact with Akamai by name. Its servers work behind the brands whose pages and files they deliver. A user sees the website's address, not the CDN carrying its content.

Amazon $AMZN Web Services, known as AWS, is Amazon's cloud computing unit. It rents storage and computing power over the internet to businesses that would otherwise have to buy and run their own servers.

AWS launched publicly in 2006 with two services. S3, short for Simple Storage Service, lets customers keep files and data on Amazon's machines. EC2, short for Elastic Compute Cloud, lets customers run software on virtual servers that Amazon operates.

Those two products split the job of a traditional server room into separate pieces. Storage holds the data. Compute does the processing. A customer can add more of one without paying for more of the other.

The model behind both is renting instead of owning. A company that runs its own servers has to buy the hardware, house it, power it, cool it and replace it when it fails. It also has to guess in advance how much capacity it will need. A cloud customer skips most of that work. It requests capacity when demand rises and releases it when demand falls. The word elastic in the EC2 name refers to that ability to scale up or down.

Renting changes the economics of starting a software business. A new app no longer needs a large upfront hardware purchase before it can serve its first user. Costs track usage, such as the amount of data stored or the server time consumed, instead of arriving in one lump sum.

The arrangement is mostly invisible to the people using those apps. A shopping site, streaming service or mobile game can run on rented AWS computing with no Amazon branding on the screen. The user sees the app's name, not the infrastructure underneath it.

AWS works in that background role. Amazon's retail site carries the company's name on every page. Its cloud unit operates out of sight, supplying the storage and processing that other companies' products rely on whenever someone uses them.

Verisign operates the registries for .com and .net, the master databases behind every domain name ending in those two extensions. There are 13 root name server identities run by 12 organizations, and Verisign runs two of them, A and J.

A registry and a registrar do different jobs. A registrar is the retailer that a person or business pays to claim a domain name. The registry sits behind it. It acts as the wholesaler and official record keeper for an entire top-level domain.

When someone buys a .com address through a registrar, the registration is passed along to Verisign. Verisign adds the name to the authoritative .com database. It also publishes the records that tell the rest of the internet which name servers answer for that domain.

That second job ties the registry to everyday browsing. A resolver looking up a .com site on a browser's behalf needs to know where that site's own name servers live. The .com registry's servers supply that pointer. If they stopped answering, names ending in .com would become hard to reach, even with the websites themselves still running.

Root servers sit one level higher in the domain name system. They do not hold the address of any individual website. Their job is to point queries toward the servers responsible for each top-level domain, such as .com, .net or a country code like .uk.

Each root identity is labeled with a letter from A to M. With 13 identities split among 12 operators, Verisign is the only organization that runs more than one.

No identity is a single machine. Operators usually serve one root letter from many servers in different locations. A routing method called anycast sends each query to a nearby copy. That spreads the load and limits the damage if one site fails.

Most lookups never reach a root server. Resolvers run by internet providers and other services store recent answers and reuse them until they expire. When those stored answers run out, the resolver can return to the root and work down the hierarchy again.

ICANN, the Internet Corporation for Assigned Names and Numbers, is a nonprofit formed in 1998. It coordinates the domain name system and the allocation of IP addresses. It is not a company in the commercial sense, but much of the internet's addressing depends on its coordination.

Two kinds of identifiers fall under that role. Domain names are the readable labels people type, such as a site ending in .org or .com. IP addresses are the numeric labels that computers and routers use to locate one another.

Both systems work only if every identifier is unique. If two networks claimed the same address, traffic could not reach either one reliably. If two operators tried to run the same domain ending, lookups would return conflicting answers. ICANN coordinates at that level to prevent such collisions.

Coordination is different from operation. ICANN does not run the servers that answer most domain lookups, and it does not host websites. Separate organizations, including commercial firms, operate individual domain registries and the servers behind them. ICANN's job sits a level above. It sets the shared rules and keeps the overall map of names consistent.

The numbers side follows a similar pattern. Large blocks of IP addresses are handed down to regional internet registries, each serving a part of the world. Those registries pass smaller blocks to internet service providers and other network operators. The operators then assign addresses to customers and devices.

Address supply is part of why that coordination matters. The original IPv4 format has a fixed, limited pool of addresses. Demand has outgrown it. The newer IPv6 format offers a far larger pool. Both formats now run side by side across the internet.

ICANN has no shareholders. Its decisions about which domain endings exist and how address blocks are distributed form the base layer that registries, network operators and other providers build on.

Equinix is a data center and colocation operator, and many internet exchange points sit inside its facilities. That makes its buildings physical meeting places where separate networks connect and hand traffic to one another.

Colocation means a customer rents space inside a data center rather than building its own. The customer typically installs its own servers and networking gear in racks or cages. The operator supplies the building, electrical power, backup power, cooling and physical security.

That arrangement suits companies that need reliable infrastructure but do not want to construct and staff a facility. It also lets many different organizations share one building while keeping control of their own equipment.

An internet exchange point, often shortened to IXP, is a place where networks connect directly to swap traffic. The participants can include internet service providers, content companies, cloud platforms and enterprise networks. Each one plugs into shared switching equipment and exchanges data with the others there.

This direct exchange is called peering. Without it, two networks might have to route traffic through a third-party carrier. That paid service is known as IP transit. Peering at an exchange can shorten the route data travels between networks. A shorter route can lower delay for the end user.

Peering arrangements differ in cost. Many are settlement-free, meaning neither network pays the other to carry the exchanged traffic. Others involve payment, which can happen when the traffic flowing between the two networks is unbalanced.

Networks tend to gather where other networks already are. Once several carriers and platforms have equipment in the same building, connecting to them there is simpler than running separate links across a city or region. Inside a single facility, two customers can be linked with a physical cable known as a cross-connect.

This explains why a colocation operator's role extends beyond renting floor space. Its buildings become junctions in the internet's map, where traffic moves from one company's network to another's.

Lumen Technologies is a fiber network operator that owns the backbone once run by Level 3. The company, then named CenturyLink, acquired Level 3 in 2017. It later took the Lumen name.

A backbone is the long-distance, high-capacity layer of the internet. It links cities, regions and the networks inside them. Local broadband and mobile carriers handle the last stretch to a user's device. Backbone operators carry traffic across the much longer distances in between.

The physical medium is optical fiber. Each strand is a thread of glass that carries data as pulses of light. Fiber moves far more data over long distances than copper wire, with less signal loss. Long-haul cables often follow existing rights of way, such as rail lines and highways.

Capacity can grow without laying new cable. A technique called wavelength division multiplexing sends many signals through a single strand at once, each on a different wavelength of light. Upgrading the equipment at each end can add capacity to fiber already in the ground.

Backbone operators sell capacity to other businesses. Buyers can include internet service providers, content companies and large enterprises. One common product is IP transit. In it, a smaller network pays a larger one to carry its traffic to the rest of the internet.

Routing ties these networks together. Networks use the Border Gateway Protocol to announce which blocks of IP addresses they can reach. Other networks read those announcements to pick paths for traffic. A mistaken announcement can send traffic down the wrong path or leave blocks of addresses unreachable until it is corrected.

Most internet users never deal with Lumen directly. Their data can still cross its fiber on the way to a website, a video stream or a cloud service. Lumen's network sits between the local access networks people pay for and the content companies they connect to.

Let's Encrypt is a certificate authority that gives website operators TLS certificates at no charge. The nonprofit Internet Security Research Group runs it, and the service opened to the public in 2015.

A certificate authority vouches for a website's identity. When a browser connects to an address that begins with https, the server presents a digital certificate. That certificate ties the site's domain name to a cryptographic public key.

The browser then checks who signed the certificate. Browsers and operating systems ship with a list of authorities they already trust. If the signature traces back to one of them, the connection goes ahead. If it does not, the browser shows a warning page instead of the site.

After that check passes, the browser and the server use TLS, short for Transport Layer Security, to encrypt the traffic between them. Encryption keeps passwords, card numbers and messages unreadable to anyone else on the network path. That includes another person on a shared café Wi-Fi network. It also makes it harder for a third party to alter a page while it is in transit.

The padlock icon in a browser's address bar is the visible sign of this process. Browsers let users click through to see which organization signed the certificate behind it.

Let's Encrypt took the price out of this step. A site owner does not pay it anything to obtain a certificate. That removes cost as a barrier for small blogs, community groups, personal projects and hobby sites that want HTTPS.

Its nonprofit structure separates it from certificate authorities that sell certificates as a business. The Internet Security Research Group operates the service, not a shareholder-owned company.

Certificates do not last forever. Each one carries an expiration date. An expired certificate triggers the same kind of browser warning as an untrusted one. Site operators have to renew certificates on a schedule, whether the certificates are free or paid.

An outage or error at a certificate authority can affect every site that depends on its certificates. Most people who visit a site secured through Let's Encrypt never see the issuer's name.

DigiCert is a commercial certificate authority. It sells the digital certificates that let a browser confirm a website is the site it claims to be.

A certificate authority sits inside a chain of trust. Browsers and operating systems ship with a root store, a built-in list of authorities whose certificates they accept. Mozilla, Apple $AAPL, Microsoft $MSFT and Google $GOOGL each run their own root program to decide which authorities qualify. A certificate from an authority outside that list triggers a warning instead of a secure connection.

Commercial authorities sell certificates at different validation levels. Domain validation is the most basic and confirms only that the applicant controls the domain. Organization validation adds a check that the applicant is a real, registered legal entity. Extended validation applies the strictest vetting of the three.

The rules for publicly trusted authorities come from the CA/Browser Forum, an industry body of certificate issuers and browser makers. Its baseline requirements govern how identities are checked and how certificates are issued. A browser maker can drop an authority from its root store for breaking those rules.

Browsers such as Chrome and Safari require publicly trusted TLS certificates to appear in Certificate Transparency logs. These are public, append-only records that anyone can search. Domain owners use them to spot certificates issued for their names without permission.

The commercial certificate business extends beyond web pages. Authorities in this market also issue code signing certificates. These let software publishers show that a program has not been altered since it was signed. Some authorities also issue certificates for signing email and documents.

Each certificate carries an expiration date. After that date, browsers reject it and the site owner must replace it. An authority can also revoke a certificate before it expires. That step matters when a private key is stolen or a certificate was issued in error. Software checks revocation status through certificate revocation lists or the Online Certificate Status Protocol, known as OCSP.

SubCom and Alcatel Submarine Networks build and lay the undersea cables that carry international internet traffic between continents. Alcatel Submarine Networks is now part of Nokia, the Finnish telecom equipment group.

A submarine cable is a line of optical fiber laid along the seabed. Data travels through the glass as pulses of light. A single cable bundles several fiber pairs inside layers of protective material.

Light weakens as it travels long distances. Repeaters spaced along the cable boost the signal so it can cross an ocean. Those repeaters need electricity. It is fed through a conductor inside the cable from power equipment on shore.

Cable suppliers such as SubCom and Alcatel Submarine Networks handle more than manufacturing. Their work can span surveys of the seafloor route, production of the cable and repeaters, and installation from specialized cable-laying ships.

The supplier is usually not the owner. Many cables belong to consortiums of telecom carriers, and some are financed by large technology companies. The owners hire a supplier to deliver the finished system.

Cable design changes with water depth. In shallow coastal areas, cables carry heavier armor and are often buried in the seabed. That protects them from ship anchors and fishing gear, two common causes of damage. In the deep ocean, where those risks are lower, a lighter cable rests directly on the bottom.

Each cable comes ashore at a landing station. There it connects to land-based fiber networks, which carry traffic onward to data centers and internet exchange points.

When a cable breaks, a repair ship sails to the fault and lifts the damaged section to the surface. Crews splice in a new length of cable and lower it back to the seabed. Traffic can often be rerouted over other cables while the repair is underway.

Satellites also carry internet traffic, but most data moving between continents travels over undersea fiber. That reliance places cable builders such as SubCom and Alcatel Submarine Networks at the physical base of global connectivity.
