4 Things the Headlines Missed in Nvidia's First Post-Jensen Earnings Report

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Nvidia easily beat third-quarter expectations, yet the real story is hiding in the fine print. Interim CEO Colette Kress just dialed back the sales swagger and put customer financing on a tighter leash.

By Anders Bylund – Nov 18, 2026 at 10:47PM EST

The leather jacket is gone, and the spreadsheets are officially in charge.

Reporting its first quarterly results just days after former CEO Jensen Huang's abrupt resignation, Nvidia (NVDA -2.90%) proved that its AI dominance doesn't depend on one charismatic ringmaster. In Q3 of fiscal year 2027, Nvidia's revenue soared 103% year over year to $115.8 billion, the new Vera Rubin architecture racked up nearly $20 billion in sales in its debut quarter, and interim CEO Colette Kress even raised the company's fiscal 2028 revenue growth target from 70% to 75%.

But beneath those breathtaking headline numbers, interim CEO Colette Kress delivered the report with the measured calm you expect from a 13-year veteran CFO. Kress toned down some of her predecessor's boldest growth framing while tightening the guardrails around how Nvidia deploys its capital.

It's a serious top-level strategy shift, brought on by an unexpected scandal. You can see most of it in the earnings release and prepared CFO comments without even digging into the earnings call.

And I think I like Kress's attitude. For the first time in forever, Nvidia's business plan makes sense to me.

Back in the second quarter, Nvidia couldn't resist hyping up its $105 billion lease guarantee for OpenAI's massive Ohio AI data center campus, telling investors that each generation of chips installed there could generate $150 billion to $200 billion in Nvidia revenue.

In her debut quarter at the helm, Kress deleted that $200 billion sales pitch from the report. Instead, she offered a step-by-step breakdown of how Nvidia's credit guarantee will take effect across nine separate buildings starting in fiscal year 2029. She also reassured investors by noting that Nvidia hasn't yet paid out any of its financing guarantees. The expected growth is still record-breaking, but on a scale from 1 to 10, Kress dialed back the marketing swagger from 11 to 6.

A longtime CFO could be expected to starve Nvidia's growth engine in order to hoard cash. The supply chain numbers say that Nvidia will have plenty of growth fuel under new management.

Kress lifted Nvidia's multi-year supply and memory chip commitments from $279 billion in Q2 to $390 billion in Q3. The boost springs from incredible demand for the Vera Rubin AI accelerator.

Where did she tighten things up? That would be in the customer financing area. Reported additional commitments related to cash-burning "neocloud" start-ups fell from $56 billion to $32 billion. A new warning in the reporting boilerplate said that those commitments can be "reduced or eliminated" if the neoclouds fail to line up their own financing. And on the call, Kress noted that the underlying deal terms haven't changed; Nvidia will simply be "disciplined and selective" about these contracts. Non-investment-grade customers had better have their capital and end buyers lined up before Nvidia commits to shipping them GPUs from a scarce supply.

To gauge Nvidia's financial health, Wall Street spends hours and hours analyzing the capital spending budgets of Microsoft, Alphabet, and Meta Platforms. Hyperscalers are no longer the fastest-growing part of the business, though. And they may soon become the smaller slice, too.

Hyperscale revenue climbed 71% year over year to $60.1 billion, but Nvidia's AI Clouds, Industrial, and Enterprise segment roughly tripled. Sales in this segment jumped 195% to $47.5 billion. The roster of non-tech-giant customers is broadening fast, including sovereign AI initiatives, enterprise quantitative trading clusters, and stand-alone Vera CPU sales.

With Nvidia currently ringing up nearly $10 billion in sales every week, that extra calendar week accounts for more than 60% of the sequential revenue jump between Q3 and Q4. The underlying demand for Nvidia's AI silicon is definitely strong. Still, investors should remember that the calendar is giving Q4 a temporary tailwind this year -- and next year's 13-week fourth quarter will face the same calendar quirk in reverse.
