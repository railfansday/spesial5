Saudi Hotel Builders Keep Going as Attacks Scare Off Foreign Visitors

Saudi hotel developers are still building through Houthi attacks, and a record pipeline shows it. But their bet rests on domestic and religious travel. The international guests that Vision 2030 needs are the ones the attacks are scaring off.

A month after a Houthi missile killed 13 people at Riyadh’s main airport, Saudi Arabia’s hotel pipeline is at a record. Developers and operators say they know of no projects canceled because of the attacks. What the attacks have damaged is harder to rebuild: the kingdom’s image as a safe place to visit.

The attacks have closed airports, canceled the kingdom’s biggest events and pushed up the cost of insuring against conflict. Investors are still signing deals, betting that domestic and religious travel will carry the market until the fighting stops. How long that takes is an open question.

Saudi Arabia’s hotel pipeline has kept growing. The kingdom has a record 401 projects in development, up 15% from a year ago, according to Lodging Econometrics. Those projects will collectively add 109,320 rooms, a 16% increase. That is 52% of the Middle East’s total project count, and 58% of its rooms.

A month after a Houthi missile attack on Riyadh's King Khalid International Airport killed 13 people, Saudi Arabia's hotel pipeline is at a record 401 projects and 109,320 rooms, up 15% and 16% respectively from a year ago, according to Lodging Econometrics. Developers and operators told Skift they know of no projects canceled because of the attacks, though IHG CEO Elie Maalouf said some would probably be delayed. The damage is showing up elsewhere: 86% of scheduled Riyadh departures were canceled between October 8 and late October, according to Cirium data; the Saudi Grand Prix, the Future Investment Initiative, and the Red Sea International Film Festival were scrapped, postponed, or moved; and the U.S. Embassy now requires government employees to get special authorization to use any Saudi airport. This year's openings are concentrated on the Red Sea coast — Four Seasons, Rosewood, Six Senses, Equinox, Nammos, and Raffles — the properties most dependent on the international guests the attacks are deterring. Investors are leaning on a domestic base that accounted for 93.3 million tourists in 2025, or 76.1% of the total, plus religious travel for Hajj and Umrah. Growth had already stalled before October, with 61.2 million tourists in the first half of 2026, up about 1% year over year, against a Vision 2030 target of 150 million a year.
