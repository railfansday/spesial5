As reported by GamesRadar, former id Software developer Sandy Petersen recently took to Twitter to make an impassioned defense of Valve founder Gabe Newell. Petersen was responding to discussion of how Newell's apparent good treatment of his crew is a rarity among superyacht enthusiasts. Here's Petersen's full statement:

"I know Gabe Newell. We have chatted at some length. I know numerous men who worked for him as well. I am sure that there must be some disgruntled ex-employees out there but everyone I know who has dealt with him respects and admires him.

"And with Steam he is literally guarding the gates of Hell⁠—preserving all gamers from the horrors of what would happen if parasites like Disney, EA, or Microsoft became the providers.

I don't think I disagree with anything Petersen said here: I certainly have criticisms of Valve, but it has been a uniquely positive force in games. I don't think PC gaming would be what it is today if a company like EA or Microsoft had beat Valve to the punch on digital distribution in the late 2000s.

There's always room for improvement, but I tend to prioritize Valve's management of Steam over concerns about its internal game development culture. If I could change one thing about the company, it would be lowering the 30% cut it takes from every game sold on Steam.

Valve punches well above its approximate 340-person headcount (336 at last call, but that was five years ago and before it absorbed Risk of Rain dev Hopoo Games). It is, pound for pound, possibly one of the most profitable companies to ever exist⁠—compare its outsized role in the industry, outperforming massive companies like EA and Ubisoft, to its relatively small size. Valve employees, by all accounts, are well-renumerated for their labor.

But I always find it uncomfortable to consider Valve's historic megaprofits and Newell's personal fortune while so many studios are struggling to remain solvent. The company would likely retain its world-historic profitability with a smaller or more graduated cut of each game sale, something the Epic Store implemented long ago, and which remains a strong talking point for Tim Sweeney's Steam competitor.

This isn't just a question of pure altruism: Steam requires a healthy development ecosystem to thrive as it does. Each studio that stays open instead of closing, each bedroom programmer who can quit their day job for indie development full time, is a future moneymaker for Valve. You get leaner, but more sustainable profit.

At the same time, even Valve's relatively high cut of sales was a massive improvement from the world of physical, retail games. And whatever gripes developers have about Steam, it is the fan-favorite PC platform for a reason: It is the only online or social media platform of comparable reach and vintage that has not been thoroughly enshittified⁠—had its usability compromised in the name of short-term profits.

Even with increasing amounts of AI shovelware on the platform and frustrating, inconsistent content moderation, it always feels like Valve is operating in good faith as a steward of the hobby, and I find that extremely precious and worth celebrating on the internet of 2026.

Completely changing the subject, I would be remiss if I failed to mention the thing that Petersen is most famous for after his work on Doom and Quake: John Romero wishing him well. Former PCG editor Wes Fenlon compiled a short history of Petersen making social media statements about the history of id Software, only to have company co-founder John Romero politely correct him.
