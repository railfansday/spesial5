Best Hyperliquid trading bots: AI agents and bots compared

Hyperliquid has become one of the busiest venues for crypto perpetual futures, and a long list of tools now automate trading on it. They differ less in what they promise than in two practical questions: who decides the trades, and what the tool is allowed to do with your account. This guide compares the main Hyperliquid trading bots and AI trading agents in 2026, plus Hyperliquid’s vaults, against those questions. Each tool was compared on who decides the trades, what its key can do with your account, and what evidence of performance exists, using each provider’s own documentation in October 2026.

Most Hyperliquid tools connect through an API wallet, which Hyperliquid also calls an agent wallet. The account owner approves a separate key that can sign orders for the account but cannot withdraw or transfer funds. These approvals expire, and approving a new wallet under the same name replaces the old one. Some tools also recommend a sub-account, which keeps the bot’s balances and positions separate. The assets stay in the user’s own Hyperliquid account throughout, which is why most Hyperliquid bots can fairly be called non-custodial.

Many tools charge through a builder fee, a small fee added to each filled order. It applies only after the user approves a maximum fee from the main wallet, and Hyperliquid caps it at 0.1% on perpetuals.

Tools are grouped by who supplies the trading logic: a managed model first, then signal execution, rule-based bots and open-source software (3Commas sits with the rule-based bots). Vaults come last because they replace a bot rather than run one. Hyperliquid’s own trading and funding fees apply to every option.

AlphaNet is built for Hyperliquid and supplies the trading logic itself. Users pick one of its deep learning strategies for crypto perpetual futures and an allocation, and the model decides when to enter and exit each trade. It connects the way the exchange intends: the user approves an agent wallet that can place and cancel orders but cannot withdraw or transfer, and trades run from the user’s own account. New deployments allow leverage up to 3× account equity. On cost, AlphaNet’s Hyperliquid trading bot charges no platform fee during the current promotional period, compared with builder fees of 0.035–0.1% per fill on several bots below; Hyperliquid’s protocol fees still apply, and a dynamic trading fee applies afterwards.

A newer group of AI agent projects on Hyperliquid connect language models to an API wallet and let them place trades. They are mostly early. In the first season of Alpha Arena, a short real-money competition on Hyperliquid, four of the six language models that took part finished with losses and two with gains.

WunderTrading is built for traders whose strategy already exists as TradingView alerts or external signals. It turns those alerts into Hyperliquid orders and also offers DCA and grid bots, charging a 0.035% builder fee with no separate subscription.

goodcryptoX is a no-code app for grid, DCA, trailing, and TradingView bots on iOS, Android, and the web. Gainium covers similar bot types and adds backtesting and paper trading, with a choice between a free mode that adds a builder fee and paid plans that do not. Bitsgap brings its hosted grid and DCA bots to Hyperliquid alongside the centralised exchanges it already supports, and 3Commas, one of the most widely used bot platforms, lists Hyperliquid among its supported exchanges for spot and futures. With all of them, the bot executes your logic; the tool does not supply a trading edge.

Hummingbot has Hyperliquid spot and perpetual connectors and includes market-making scripts and arbitrage, TWAP, and grid executors. The self-run client charges no builder fee. It accepts either an API wallet or the main wallet’s private key; the second gives the software full control of the account, so the API wallet is the safer choice.

Rule-based bots have no platform-level record, because results depend on each user’s settings. Vaults are the most transparent option: every vault’s history is on-chain and visible in Hyperliquid’s app. Managed strategy platforms sit in between. AlphaNet publishes a running record of each strategy since its start date, and because trades run from users’ own accounts, each user’s fills are on-chain and can be checked against it.

Instead of connecting a tool to your own account, you deposit into a vault that a leader trades. The protocol’s own vault, HLP, provides liquidity, performs liquidations, and earns a share of fees. User-run vaults follow individual strategies. Leaders take 10% of profits and must keep at least 5% of the vault, and deposits are locked for one day (four days for HLP).

The choice comes down to the two questions this guide started with. First, decide who you want making the trades: your own rules, a signal you already trust, a trained model, or a vault leader. Then check what the tool is allowed to do with your account. On Hyperliquid, most tools need only an agent wallet that can trade but not withdraw, so there is rarely a reason to hand over a main wallet key or move funds into someone else’s control.

Two further checks are worth making before committing real capital. The first is cost per fill: builder fees on top of Hyperliquid’s own fees can add up. The second is the record: a vault’s history is on-chain, a managed strategy should publish a live one, and a rule-based bot has none until you build your own.
