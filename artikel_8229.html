Hunters Who Always Tag Giant Bucks Have One Thing in Common — and I Don’t Have It

I was driving to the airport when I got a call from my deer hunting buddy, Josh.

“I just shot him,” he said in a hushed, grave tone usually reserved for wakes.

By “him” he meant the big buck we’d been after on a property that we leased together in Wisconsin. I had been hunting this specific buck for the previous two weeks. Josh had been on him the week before that. The neighbor had been trying to get him too. The buck was probably the biggest we’d had living on the property in the eight years that we’d been hunting there. I had him on camera, during daylight, near one of our stands, but he always seemed to show up when I wasn’t there. He was a tall, clean 10-point that Josh scored in the mid-150s after he tagged him.

“Man, I kinda wish that you had shot him,” Josh said, knowing that deer would have been my biggest archery buck. He had killed the deer on his 33rd sit of the bow season. I’d been out maybe a dozen times.

“Forget that,” I replied. “I’m just glad that I got the chance to hunt him.”

And this, honestly, was how I felt. Of course I would have loved to have shot the big buck. I had spent precious time away from my family trying to get him (even when I tried racing home after hunting, I was too late to read my two little girls their bedtime stories).

“Did you get the big buck, Dadda?” my youngest daughter would ask each morning, and I would explain, apologetically, “No, not yet, honey.”

But I didn’t feel any bitterness as I boarded the plane to Saskatoon. After all, I was headed to the land of giant Northwoods deer.

On most Saskatchewan deer hunts, a cheery Canadian guide drops you off at a ground blind somewhere way back in the bush. You sit there overlooking a bait pile at the end of a short shooting lane. You wait from sun up to sun down. You never leave the blind.

During my first sit I tried to maintain focus the best I could. I’ve done plenty of all-day sits for whitetails before, but usually I’m in a tree with a view. If I get bored, I can simply look in a different direction for a little while. But not here. There is only one way to look, and the shooting lane is narrow enough that I don’t have to scan it. The bait pile of oats and alfalfa is only about 50 yards away. There is no cell phone service so there’s no chance to be distracted by scrolling or emails. But I still find myself checking my phone every half-hour or so.

Eventually deer (does and baby bucks) come to the bait with all the wariness of a Walmart shoplifter. The long day within close proximity of the deer gives me a chance to closely study them. I learn that they are highly sensitive to the sound of fabric swishing, like the sleeve of my coat rubbing against my bino harness. But they are not spooked by leaves rustling in the blind when I move my feet. They don’t like my ground scent from the trail I walked in on, but they tolerate it. They will not tolerate my scent blowing directly at them when the wind swirls. And any combination of scent and noise spooks them extraordinarily, and produces alarm blows.

During the quiet stretches I remind myself that I must stay alert for the full 9-hour watch, because at any moment the biggest buck of my life could show up — and he could be gone in seconds. But on the first day he never appears.

At camp that night one of the outfitters tells us a story about a hunter who fell asleep in his blind under the warming afternoon sun. His target buck appeared and ate at the bait pile for a few minutes, right in front of a cellular trail camera before walking off. The hunter had no idea he was there until the guides told him about it back at camp.

I’m off to a new blind on the second day to hunt a big, old 8-point. The guides we are hunting with, Crimson Ridge Outfitters, have turned up some truly giant bucks in years past (legitimate 170-class deer). But this year antler growth in the entire region is down dramatically, they say. Massive snow storms in late spring meant that bucks had to fight just to stay alive, and they simply didn’t produce the antler mass like normal. So we would be targeting age class, not inches of antler.

The bigger issue was the weather, or lack thereof. Morning temps were in the 20s, and it warmed to the low 40s by midday. A classic Saskatchewan hunt calls for temps in the teens with a good foot of snow on the ground. The cold and snow drive deer to feed during the daylight hours.

By the middle of the second day I realized that I had stopped checking my phone. I had settled into a sort of hunter’s meditation. I didn’t get hungry or cold or thirsty now. It was a peaceful, familiar feeling that is hard to recreate in our age of social media and hyper-speed communication.

Only a couple deer visited me today. But then at last light I heard the unmistakable sound of a buck chasing does toward me. They were coming through thick cover to my right and I strained trying to spot them. I could see only 40 yards into the brush. For a moment I hesitated, unsure if they would pass in front of the blind or behind it. But then I heard the bounding does and grunting buck angling behind me. I spun around in the blind and pointed my rifle out the back window just in time to catch a white tail flagging through the timber in the fading light. I never did glimpse an antler. A moment later the four wheeler hummed down the trail to pick me up. Later on, the trail camera captured a picture of my target buck, only an hour after I had left.

Back in camp it’s hamburger night and our cook, Glenna, tells a story about the burger joint she used to own. Glenna wears an apron that says “Kitchen Bitch” written in the type of cursive font typical of Airbnb home decor. Her restaurant menu had hamburger toppings listed 1 through 62. A regular at this restaurant would order all 62 fixings on his burger, perhaps as a stunt or perhaps because he had horrible taste. But one day this regular got beaten up at a biker bar and suffered a broken jaw. He came into the restaurant and still made his usual order of all 62, but had the whole thing blended up into a kind of hamburger slurry — then he drank it through a straw.

The next morning I am back in the same blind thinking about blended up cheeseburgers and all of the biggest regrets in my life when the two does at the bait pile suddenly go on alert and snap their heads to the left.

Two ravens start cawing in that direction and I slide into position behind my rifle. I once hunted with some locals in southeast Alaska who told me that ravens will alert hunters to approaching bucks. They theorized that maybe the birds knew that hunters would provide them with a fresh gut pile to feast upon.

I hear a few twigs snap. The lead doe bounds off, followed by her fawn, and I slip my thumb toward the rifle’s safety. Maybe this is the buck?

But instead of the massive 8-point I’m waiting for, out steps an overconfident spike, grunting and looking for does. I relax in my chair. The ravens had underestimated my pickiness — but not by much.

I have hunted whitetail deer for 27 seasons now, and I have never killed a truly giant buck. (Which, if pressed, I would arbitrarily define as a deer that would surpass 160 inches and be considered a ‘shooter’ anywhere that whitetails live.) This is not because I haven’t had the opportunity to hunt where big bucks exist.

The truth is, it’s because I’m almost always happy shooting non-giant bucks. I think this is partially because of my upbringing. Where I grew up hunting in north-central Wisconsin, any 6-pointer within rifle range was in extreme danger. My dad has killed a buck nearly every season over the many decades of his hunting life and has never even considered “passing” on a 3.5-year-old buck (“Because why would ya?”). I was taught by the age of 12 to take that first clear shot at a buck because it’d probably be the only one I’d get all season.

People like my dad, and me, might shoot a giant one day, but that will be by chance. This is distinctly different from the handful of diehard whitetail hunters who shoot giant bucks on purpose. They would much rather shoot no deer than put their tag on a non-giant deer.

What separates them from the rest of us, besides the fact that they hunt exclusively in big-buck country, is that they are willing to sacrifice more (whether that be time, or money, or unfilled tags).

I once hunted with a diehard big buck hunter from Illinois who told me the story of the 200-inch deer he killed years ago. He dedicated his season to pursuing this one deer. He targeted him in the early season. He sat countless days from dark to dark waiting for him in the rut. In the process he missed family birthdays, ghosted his friends, and put off work obligations. As the weeks went by the structure of his life began to erode. By the late season he became like the solitary old buck that he pursued. When he finally did kill that deer, one frigid December day, he felt no happiness. Only relief that it was over.

With wild whitetails it is harder to buy your way to a true trophy rack than it is with, say, elk or mule deer. Many outfitters all over North America promise 140-class deer and then quietly stretch the tape when their clients shoot 120s or 130s. And even when you are hunting honest 160-class-plus deer, there is no guarantee that you will shoot one, or even see one, during the five or seven days that you paid to hunt. Most paying clients end up shooting smaller bucks rather than risk going home empty handed.

Well-off property owners or lessees who carefully manage for trophy quality have the best chances consistently. But even if they pay someone to manage the habitat for them (which is an incredible financial sacrifice all on its own) they must still put in the time to actually kill the deer. And that could be days, or weeks, or months of dedicated hunting. Whereas the high-paying guided hunter in the West can wait until his guides have located the trophy animal and then fly in to shoot him. There are not many shortcuts like that with freerange trophy whitetails.

People who regularly shoot truly giant deer on public land are outliers, anomalies, wizards.

Most of the folks who consistently kill giant deer have structured their entire lives in a way that allows them to do so. It is common for a non-giant-killer, like you or me, to say, “Well, if I had that kind of time (or money) I’d kill big bucks, too.” Which is probably true, but it’s also true that we don’t have the time or money because we have spent our lives doing other things.

I do not think that the folks who target only giant deer are more enlightened human beings compared to the rest of us. They are not providing some great benefit to society by shooting a 170-class buck every season or so. But I will not pretend to understand their motives — mostly because I’ve never shot a deer like that myself.

My third all-day sit ends quietly. Back in camp that night a guide named Mark tells his best story to bring the vibes up.“So we were up at my in-law’s farm, during calving season, eh? And they brought this cow into the barn that was having trouble birthing her calf. So my father-in-law tells his wife and his daughter, which is my wife, to go back to the house cuz it’s late and it’s going to be awhile. So they go back. And after like 45 minutes I go back to the house, too.

Well it’s a really old house and there aren’t a lot of light switches, eh? So I go up stairs and into our room where my wife is sleeping and strip down because back then I used to sleep in the raw. And I slide into bed on my side and snuggle up to my wife and grab ahold of her and she asks, ‘Did the calf work out?’ But she asks it in my mother-in-law’s voice. And I say ‘Doreen!?’, all startled, and she says ‘Mark!?’ all startled back.

And I jumped out of bed and grabbed my clothes and ran out of there. They had switched rooms but nobody told me.”

Mark says that he and his wife got divorced about a year later, but apparently it had nothing to do with that incident.

I am in a new blind today positioned in a dried up old beaver pond with a clear view in three directions.

Today is my last day to hunt, even though Crimson Ridge allows five and a half days of hunting. I must leave early because tomorrow is my oldest daughter’s kindergarten musical, and I promised her that I would be there for it. A big cold front and snow storm is coming that promises to move mature bucks. A true trophy buck hunter would push his flight back and hunt through the cold front, knowing that’s his single best chance. But some promises can’t be broken.

There have been three bucks frequenting this bait site. The one I’m really after is a mature, massive bodied 8-pointer with a short and stout rack. Deer here in Saskatchewan are known to grow huge, heavy bodies. They are the largest subspecies of whitetail. They have evolved into these hulks because a large-bodied mammal has a lower surface-to-volume ratio, meaning that it will retain body heat better in the cold (this is known as Bergmann’s rule). A stud buck here can run upward of 300 pounds, which is fully twice the size of some mature bucks taken in southern whitetail country.

Shortly after sunrise I hear a deer walking and then a buck appears to my right. I can tell he’s a bigger deer, but as he passes just 30 yards from my blind I confirm that he is not the short, fat buck I’m here for. He’s a younger 8-point with a tall, symmetrical rack that extends beyond his ears.

That old Wisconsin hunter reflex screams ‘Shoot him.’ But I hold off. I watch him for a while as he makes a scrape and eats some alfalfa. Eventually he spots a doe and chases her into the timber.

But about 20 minutes later the doe bursts out of the bush with the same buck behind her. He’s all bristled up now, making him look bigger than before. I look at him through my riflescope now but he quickly chases the doe out of sight. Behind me, I hear him make a full on buck roar. The sound quickens my heart rate. I can also hear him raking a tree. I take a quick glance at the trail camera pictures saved to my phone. There’s no doubt the older buck I’m waiting for is a bigger bodied deer, but his rack isn’t all that much better. My dad’s sentiment about passing bucks rattles around in my head. Why would ya?

The doe comes out again and now I’m down on my rifle, ready. She goes to the bait as the same 8-point tromps out in front of me, only 20 yards away. The gun booms and the buck drops instantly.

I wrap my hands around his antlers and admire their dark-chocolate color. He smells like the rut, and Christmas, with fresh spruce sap smeared on his tines. I estimate that he’s 3.5 years old, but he’s big bodied and will go well over 200 pounds.

I’ve shot many bucks like him in the past, and I hope to shoot more like him in the future.

I make it back home with my meat and horns just in time for my daughter’s musical. It’s the best version of Rudolph the Red Nosed Reindeer that I’ve ever seen, and not once do I think about the Saskatchewan cold front that’s surely driving those old bucks to feed.
