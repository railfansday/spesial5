Why Language Makes Us Absolutists and Jerks

Posted November 23, 2026 | Reviewed by Michelle Quirk

My research spans the physical origins of life, the evolution of human language and what it is like to live under its influence, and the emotional psychology of absolutists and jerks. The theme throughout is to better understand what we actually are. I have been at it for 35 years. For decades, I was dogged by the question "What's wrong with me?" I simply broadened my focus to "What's up with us?" Me included.

For 30 years, I have studied and collaborated with Terrence Deacon, a UC Berkeley biologist. I met him after reading his book on the physical evolution of human language. I call his approach physical philosophy. He is a lab scientist who tackles big questions, such as what life and consciousness are, or how purpose-driven behavior emerges from nothing but purposeless chemical phenomena.

He does not take abstractions like "information" as givens. Most scientists simply assume information exists, treating DNA as an "information-bearing molecule." Deacon holds that anything can become information, but only when it is about something for some being trying to achieve something—most fundamentally, trying to stay alive. The burden is on us to explain how beings struggling for their own existence emerged before DNA, such that molecules could become information for them.

Likewise, most social scientists simply assume motivation. If someone makes an effort, they assume the person is motivated, without explaining what motivation is. That is little better than assuming life is a soul installed in matter. Deacon explains motivation from the ground up, starting with the first chemical system motivated to keep itself going.

Meanwhile, much of my work, especially around Trumpism, has been about humans driving drunk under the influence of language. Language makes us a visionary, anxious, delusional, and dangerous species. We can imagine real and unreal threats and missed opportunities, past, present, and future, that no other creature can. We trudge through a sandstorm of confidence-eroding possibilities, and we can compensate for the anxiety with language, too, buying into all sorts of delusional and dangerous nonsense.

Organisms traffic in two basic kinds of information: icons and indexes, resemblances and pointers. Your buddy today resembles your buddy yesterday. That is iconic. Hearing your buddy's voice on the phone makes you think of him. That is indexical: a trait pointing to what it belongs to, the way smoke points to fire.

Humans add a third kind of information: symbols. As a toddler, you heard your parents repeat words while pointing at things. Eventually your ability to talk achieved lift-off from things. You did not have to point at Mommy to say she was sad. You could say it even if she was not around. Symbols have no built-in significance. The word "dog" neither looks nor sounds like a dog. It is a social convention. So you can talk about dogs nowhere in sight, or about gods who do not exist.

That freedom from present reality makes us creative and visionary, but also anxious. We can imagine our own deaths and haunting memories, and then reassure ourselves with unreal, self-soothing delusions. Recognizing how much language shapes us would fill us with compassion for ourselves and others, and help us manage our anxieties and delusions more effectively.

Language also amps up our capacity to rationalize and moralize in defense of anything we have done or want to do. Though we have language, we are still driven mostly by feelings, the urgent need to feel comfortable in our own skin. The more intense the feeling, the more it seems like reality itself: I feel it, therefore it is real. Then, since it feels so real, we assume away the feelings that drove it. You hear it in absolutists who speak intensely about what they love and hate while acting like neutral judges. You hear it in their hypocrisy: If I do it, it feels good and is therefore virtuous; if my rivals do exactly the same thing, it feels bad and is therefore evil.

Almost all organisms are parasites and predators. Only humans can become absolutists or jerks, thanks to language. We can spin words to see ourselves as superheroes and our enemies as the devil's spawn. With words, humans can rationalize anything, even killing people at the command of an imaginary God who likes us best.

A jerk is a self-absolving absolutist, certain they are right about everything. "Absolute" originally meant dissolved away from any influences. Nothing and nobody can give them a reality check. They barge through life spinning everything they do as perfect and anyone in their way as evil. They are disgusting, but we should not overlook how satisfying that feeling must be. It is the obvious cure for the anxiety of living vulnerable lives.

And it is not just absolutists. It is all of us, to some degree.

First, if you can admit that you are human, prone to trafficking in language, with high anxiety and a tendency to reassure, rationalize, and moralize, you can manage that tendency more effectively. If you deny it, or assume only the other guy has the problem, you will not manage it. You cannot manage an appetite you deny having. An alcoholic cannot manage alcoholism by saying, "I'm no alcoholic. Other people are alcoholics. They disgust me, which proves I'm not one."

Second, watch out for how feelings make ideas feel true and make us forget they were driven by feelings. Whenever you or others speak with passionate insistence on rational moral arguments, you are likely rationalizing and moralizing, spinning convincing feelings into words that make them feel absolutely true.
