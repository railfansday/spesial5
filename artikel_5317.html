Gen Zers are more likely to own crypto than an employer-sponsored retirement plan—and a fifth see it as a path to generational wealth

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Gen Zers are bucking the norms of wealth-building set by generations before them and turning to digital investments over long-vetted ways of saving. Now, more of the young investors are betting their fortunes on cryptocurrency over traditional retirement accounts.

Around 21% of Gen Z currently own cryptocurrency, according to a recent SoFi survey in partnership with YouGov, while only 19% have an employer-sponsored retirement plan like a 401(k).

It’s a huge leap from how predecessors think about saving for the future; around 34% of millennials invest in their company’s retirement savings plan, and only 20% own crypto. And Gen Zers are in stark contrast to their parents’ generations, owning crypto at seven times the rate of baby boomers and over twice the rate of Gen Xers.

“Unlike other investment assets, crypto is accessible to Gen Z,” the SoFi report notes. “With inflation outpacing wage increases and mortgages still hovering at the highest rates they’ve been in decades, they view cryptocurrency as an asset they can access now to build generational wealth down the line.”

For the young digital natives, crypto isn’t a “speculative gamble,” the report explains: it’s part of a deliberate strategy to diversify their investment portfolios.

Gen Zers are almost three times more likely (21%) to say that crypto is one of the best ways to build future wealth compared to the average population (8%). SoFi says that Gen Z being early adopters could be the “tipping point” in mainstreaming digital currency; and as they’re projected to amass $36 trillion in wealth by 2030, according to a 2025 Bank of America report, they’ll continue to invest in the digital assets they have confidence in.

From memecoins to Ethereum, cryptocurrencies have become a staple in both investing and popular culture. There have been questions if Gen Z’s fascination with digital currencies is clouded by hype.

SoFi’s report found that 34% of overall crypto investors admit they bought in simply because it “sounded interesting,” with 20% of Gen Zers reporting they felt this way. However, experts have told Fortune that cryptocurrency is still in its infancy, and young investors may not know exactly what they’re buying into.

Much of crypto’s popularity has circulated around internet hype, which has been rife with controversy. Elon Musk’s Dogecoin famously rocked the internet, and celebrities like Reese Witherspoon and Paris Hilton have publicly boasted about their investments.

Several celebrities have been accused of running “pump-and-dump” scams on their followers: raving about a stock online to attract investors and inflate the price, then turning around quickly and selling their shares, tanking the value. Caitlyn Jenner’s $jenner, Iggy Azalea’s $mother, and Jason Derulo’s $Jason were all wrapped up in that accusation. And Kim Kardashian and Floyd Mayweather were both named in a lawsuit for promoting EthereumMax on social media in 2021.

There are several reasons the crypto world is both mysterious and exciting, experts have told Fortune. And it may be that exact intrigue that pulls Gen Z to the investment, despite how risky it can be. Without employer-backed savings plans and level-headed investments, young people may be looking at financial regrets in the future.

“It’s never a bad thing for people in any generation to take interest in their personal finances,” Mark Smrecek, financial well-being market leader at Willis Towers Watson (WTW), told Fortune last year. “I think as long as they’re looking at risk and reward based on what their goals are, it’s generally fine. But I do get concerned when I see over-indexing toward risky assets.”

Beyond their favorite online personalities promoting the products, experts point out that crypto is attractive for being liquid and immediate. Gen Z workers in the early stages of their careers may not be prioritizing long-term investments like 401(k)s. Crypto coins can be sold anytime and have the potential to be lucrative in the short term.

“Oftentimes getting involved in employer-provided retirement plans seems like a long, far-off way to save,” Smrecek said. “So they look for something that’s a little bit more liquid that, in case of an emergency, they can access.”
