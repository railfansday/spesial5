PNC Bank, T-Mobile, and HPE: 3 large employers that list pensions as a perk

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Free daily briefing on global business news.

PNC Bank, T-Mobile, and Hewlett Packard Enterprise appear among 2,492 companies that list a pension as a workplace perk.

Picture a job offer sitting in your inbox, with the salary settled and only the benefits page left to read. Dental coverage, commuter stipends, and wellness credits blur together on a first pass. Then one word stands out: pension. The term suggests income that keeps arriving after the last paycheck, and it carries weight for anyone choosing between two large employers. A higher starting wage shows up on payday. A retirement benefit pays off later, across a future a worker has not seen. Weighing the two forces a job seeker to picture life at 40, 55, and beyond. A single line on a perks page can tip that choice.

A benefits tag leaves out most of what an applicant needs to judge a pension. The tag tells a candidate that some plan exists on paper. It says nothing about what the plan pays, which employees qualify, or how many years someone must stay before the benefit vests. Plan terms decide whether a listed pension delivers real security or amounts to a footnote in an offer letter. A candidate has to put those questions to the employer. The window to ask closes once competing offers expire or a start date arrives. Company listings narrow the search by flagging which employers mention a pension at all. No listing can close the gap between a posted benefit and a guaranteed one. Readers should treat every entry as a starting point for questions.

Built In maintains a directory of 2,492 companies that list a pension among their perks. Each profile card shows the employer's industry tags, its number of offices and employees, how many benefits it carries, and its open positions, along with the four departments hiring most. The page sets out no criteria for inclusion and no logic behind the order of its cards. It carries no date and gives no pension terms, plan types, or eligibility rules for any company. This article looks at three employers from the directory whose profiles each report tens of thousands of workers. All three sit among the largest employers shown on the page, but they are not the only ones at that scale. Ericsson, for one, lists 88,000 employees and falls outside this selection. The choice of these three makes no claim about which companies provide the strongest retirement benefits.

PNC Bank lists a pension among the perks it offers a workforce of 55,000 employees, who work out of six offices. The bank's profile counts 55 benefits in all, which sets the pension inside a broad package of workplace extras. By its own account, PNC belongs to one of the largest diversified financial services institutions in the U.S., with branches stretching from one coast to the other.

Six offices and a coast-to-coast branch network make an uneven pair on the page. The card does not say which sites its office count covers. A job seeker cannot tell from the listing where most of the bank's staff report for work.

Technology dominates the bank's industry labels. PNC's card pairs Financial Services with Machine Learning, Payments, Security, and Software. Four of its five tags point to the digital systems that move and protect money. The mix reads closer to a software firm's than a neighborhood lender's.

Recruiting tilts the same way. PNC shows 76 open positions, and its four busiest departments are Product Management with 24, Engineering with 20, AI & Machine Learning with 11, and Cybersecurity with eight. AI & Machine Learning and Cybersecurity combine for 19 roles, which points to demand for specialists in automated systems and digital defense. The four groups together account for 63 openings, or about 83% of the total. The remaining 13 fall in departments the listing does not break out. None of the leading four names teller, lending, or branch work.

PNC carries about one vacancy for every 724 employees, the thinnest hiring ratio of the three companies in this article. Product Management and Engineering supply 44 of the bank's posted openings, more than half its total. A "See Our Teams" link lets visitors browse individual groups, and the bank's recruiting tagline invites applicants to build a brilliant career there. The most direct path to the pension perk at PNC, on this evidence, runs through building and securing digital products.

T-Mobile $TMUS reports 89,016 employees on a profile that counts a pension among its perks, the largest stated workforce of the three companies here. The firm spreads its staff across four offices, an average of 22,254 people per location. Its card tallies 43 benefits, the shortest list among the three despite the biggest headcount. A pension, by this measure, is one item in a package of more than 40 offerings.

The company identifies itself as T-Mobile U.S. Inc., which trades on the Nasdaq $NDAQ under the ticker TMUS. It brands itself America's Un-carrier and says it provides a 4G LTE network alongside a nationwide 5G network. The directory files T-Mobile under two industry tags, Other and Utilities. Neither label names wireless service, the business at the center of the carrier's own pitch. Both other employers in this group carry technology tags, such as software at PNC Bank and cloud at Hewlett Packard Enterprise. T-Mobile alone among the three sits outside a technology category.

Sales drives the company's hiring, with 153 of its 436 open positions, or about 35% of the total. Product management follows with 25 openings, engineering with 22, and finance with 14. The three departments account for 61 roles. Sales postings outnumber that group by a ratio of 2.5-to-1. Engineering trails sales by about 7-to-1 in openings, a mix that tilts toward customer-facing jobs. The four biggest teams claim 214 openings between them, which leaves 222 positions in departments left unnamed. Combined, the remaining departments hold about 51% of all openings.

Vacancies look modest against the company's size. The 436 listings amount to less than 1% of T-Mobile's roster, or one for every 204 people on its payroll. Sales postings alone work out to one for every 582 people. Candidates outside sales compete for the other 283 slots, a narrow door into a pension-listing employer of this scale.

Hewlett Packard Enterprise advertises 2,773 open positions, and a pension appears among the 45 benefits on its profile. The company reports 85,422 employees across 25 offices. Hiring at this volume works out to about one vacancy for every 31 people on the payroll, or close to 111 openings per office. The ratios point to an employer recruiting in bulk across its entire footprint.

Engineering claims the largest share of those listings, with 897 posts, and sales follows at 804. Together, the two groups hold 1,701 jobs, or about 61% of the total. Program & Project Management, at 149, and Operations & Support, at 141, trail by a wide margin. The narrow 93-position gap between engineers and sellers suggests the company wants people who build its products about as much as people who sell them.

The four named departments account for 1,991 roles between them, and the top pair outnumbers the next two by more than five to one. Another 782 positions fall outside those four categories. More than a quarter of the demand sits with smaller teams the profile does not break out. The smallest of the named groups caps any unlisted team at 141 slots.

The company's industry tags cover artificial intelligence, cloud, information technology, and consulting. The engineering-heavy demand matches a tag list built around technology. Its own account of its history starts in 1939, with Bill Hewlett and Dave Packard setting up shop in a rented Palo Alto garage. Audio oscillators were their first products. The company says those devices laid the foundation for its growth into what it calls a global leader in enterprise technology.

Candidates drawn to the company by its listed pension have the best odds of entering through engineering or sales, the two teams with the most doors open. Outside those fields, prospects thin out. Program & Project Management, the next-largest group, offers about one-sixth as many roles as engineering.
