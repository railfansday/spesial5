A turnkey rental, a house a company has already renovated and rented, comes at a premium, and the seller often stays on as its property manager. Before you buy one, check the seller’s price, rent, and renovation against your own appraisal, local rent listings, and your own inspection.

For an investor who has decided to become a landlord, the premium pays for convenience. You get a house that’s fixed up and rented in a Midwestern or Southern market cheaper than your own, financed like any other rental.

Every number in the listing comes from the seller, including the rent paid by a tenant it chose. It often manages the house afterward too, so the same company that did the renovation tells you what needs fixing. A projected return that counts no empty months, no repairs, and nothing for a new roof or furnace overstates what you’d keep.

Then redo the projection with the rent you found, one empty month a year, and repairs and replacements at 3% of the house’s value a year, within Fannie Mae’s rule of thumb of 1% to 4%. Say the seller projected 9%, $14,400 a year after costs that left out empty months and repairs, on a $160,000 house renting for $1,400 a month. Your appraisal says $140,000, and similar homes rent for $1,250. The house nets $7,150 a year, a 4.5% return on the price and half the 9% projected.

Before signing the seller’s management contract, read how to end it, since a long notice period or a cancellation fee can keep you as its client.

Make your offer contingent on the inspection and appraisal, so a bad inspection or a low appraisal lets you renegotiate the price or back out without losing your deposit.
