How to finance nature

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

From debt-for-nature swaps to wildlife bonds, new financial deals are bringing private investors into conservation

More deals making time was set up for the summit. The COP organizers are putting on a Finance Day that will cover how to make nature projects something investors can actually buy and a Nordic development fund has planned a session where climate funds would pitch investors directly.

There are several financial instruments for financing nature. The oldest and best known is the debt-for-nature swap. A country with heavy debt gets some of it bought at a discount, then renegotiated on better terms in exchange for spending part of the savings on conservation. Bolivia struck the first in 1987, which was worth $650,000.

The deals have grown since. Ecuador’s 2023 swap for the Galapagos Islands bought roughly $1.6 billion of its bonds for $656 million and freed about $450 million for conservation. Many of the biggest swaps relied on a safeguard. Guarantees from the U.S. government promised to repay investors if political turmoil interrupted payments, which made the new debt safe enough to buy.

The market went quiet for a while, with only about $6 billion in swaps with private capital being done from 2021 to 2024. Renewed interest returned in February, when Britain’s biggest asset manager, Legal & General, committed up to $1 billion to anchor a new generation of deals.

Bonds are another route, and come in a variety of setups. The World Bank’s “rhino bond” was one. In 2022 it raised $150 million from investors who gave up their interest payments, about $9 million in all, so the money could go to protecting black rhinos in two South African parks. Investors were due their principal back in March 2027, plus a bonus of up to $13.8 million if the rhino population grew.

Others looked more like ordinary business. Funds like the one with King Charles III put money into companies and expected a commercial return. Payments for ecosystem services, usually run by governments, rewarded the conservation of a forest or wetland, and environmental credits, which works like carbon offsets, sold verified conservation as a tradable certificate.

It's still early to judge how well many of these instruments work. Some of the biggest deals are only a few years old, and their conservation commitments will take much longer to measure.

But there's pressure to scale them: the market is being built against an enormous funding gap. Private finance classified as nature-related has grown from $9.4 billion in 2020 to $102 billion in 2024, but even that jump left a vast amount of conservation unfunded, with BloombergNEF estimating the debt swap market could be as large as $1 trillion.

The deals themselves have drawn criticism. Debt Justice, a British campaign group, found that four swaps cut national debt by an average of 3%, while ordinary restructurings cut it about seven times more.

A French advocacy group calculated that Belize put $84 million of a $553 million deal toward nature. In Gabon the figure was $67.5 million, with the rest going to repay debt and pay fees.

Then there is Washington. Political risk guarantees from the U.S. government underpinned swaps in Ecuador, Belize, Gabon and El Salvador, and that went away after Donald Trump returned to office.

That leaves private investors to carry more of the load. But getting them to invest means finding conservation projects that can deliver a return, and there aren't enough of those yet. McKinsey cited fragmented data and ecosystem outcomes that are hard to price as two reasons why.

Even if private investment grows, the hardest conservation work may remain beyond its reach. Markets can channel money toward nature where they can extract a return. They are less equipped to pay for protection whose benefits are diffuse, long-term or impossible to monetize.
