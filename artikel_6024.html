Hail Mary effort on college sports bill could boost power conferences

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Sen. Ted Cruz, R-Texas, pictured talking with reporters Nov. 17, has expressed confidence the Protect College Sports Act can clear Congress before the end of session. (Tom Williams/CQ Roll Call)

President Donald Trump and GOP leaders are mounting a late push for a sweeping college sports bill that has stalled in the House, with new revisions that appear designed to appeal to several large state delegations by making it easier for high-profile schools to jump to the most lucrative conferences.

The Senate’s version of the bill known as the Protect College Sports Act, which passed in September, would cap collegiate athletic conference sizes at 20 schools. But a revised House version released Friday evening would raise that conference roster cap to 22 institutions after several Atlantic Coast Conference schools went public with their collective desire for the change.

The change could clear the way for another round of conference realignment, including the Southeastern Conference and Big Ten expanding again after both did so in 2024.

College sports insiders have reported for some time that SEC and Big Ten officials have been eying a number of ACC schools to join their ranks — a list that includes the University of Florida, Florida State, the University of North Carolina and South Carolina’s Clemson University. All four recently sent letters to House lawmakers requesting the conference size change, according to the Charlotte Business Journal.

The SEC and Big Ten currently have 16 members for revenue-generating sports.

To reach the revised House bill’s 22-team ceiling, there has been speculation about other ACC schools also jumping ship, including North Carolina State University, Duke University, Wake Forest University, the University of Virginia and Virginia Tech. Because the bill, even with changes made by House Republican leaders, faces an uphill battle in the chamber, the 22-team cap change could assist its proponents.

That’s because of factions that have opposed college sports legislation. The nearly 60-member Congressional Black Caucus still opposes the measure over GOP efforts to terminate majority-minority House districts and an assessment it would not do enough to help historically Black colleges and universities. And some House conservatives have raised concerns that it might hurt colleges and conferences by allowing student athletes to do things like unionize.

The Sunshine, Tar Heel and Old Dominion states have 53 total House seats, making the conference cap change a potentially tactical weapon, should the amended measure hit the House floor. The House Rules Committee, in a Friday evening announcement, signaled the revised bill could get floor votes next week, after the chamber returns from a Thanksgiving recess.

The same Rules announcement informed members they have until midday Wednesday to submit further amendments ahead of a likely committee session on the legislation next week. If the panel signs off on a rule for the revised measure and some amendments, the next stop would be the chamber floor.

The bill got a big boost from Trump during a Friday press conference at the White House.

“If we don't pass that bill, you're not only going to not have sports, but you're going to have every college in the country go bankrupt,” Trump said.

A White House official signaled the president was not concerned about the proposed House changes. “The president wants NIL reform to get done and for Congress to send him a bill that can reach his desk quickly,” the official said in an email.

House Republicans did not make any changes to the Senate-passed measure’s provisions meant to help safeguard women’s sports.

House GOP leaders have proposed dropping the Senate’s threshold for when any conference merger would trigger antitrust violations. The House is proposing a breach of those laws if a conference that makes $600 million attempts to merge with or acquire another conference, down from the Senate’s $700 million level.

Along with the proposed conference size and antitrust changes, the chamber’s revised bill would make a handful of targeted changes, including new rules for how colleges and universities count name, image and likeness (NIL) agreements against a revenue-share cap.

It also proposes changing schools’ disclosure requirements for student athletic fees and a lower threshold for certain conference mergers. And it proposes changes to what would be a new congressional commission to oversee the college athletics landscape.

The House has proposed altering the Senate-passed bill’s NIL accounting language with a provision stating that any institution with annual average media rights revenue of at least $50 million could no longer use student fees to help finance their sports teams. The change appears geared toward lawmakers hailing from districts with HBCUs, smaller “Group of Six” FBS conference members and schools that compete in Football Championship Subdivision.

The revised measure would stipulate that all NIL agreements between a student athlete and an institution, an institution employee or volunteer, a conference, a conference employee or an associated entity would count against that institution’s revenue-share cap and player-retention funds.

In another proposed alteration, institutions of higher learning would be required to report certain student athlete compensation data “individually by program and in the aggregate.” The Senate-approved bill would have required reporting “for personnel of each intercollegiate sports program.”

Instead of allowing “a person” to bring a civil action, as would the Senate-approved language, the rewritten House provision would grant such rights only to a “current or former student athlete.”

The House version also would make several changes to the Senate’s proposed 24-member Congressional Commission on the Future of College Athletics by shaking up which congressional committees would get to select its members. The changes also include adding representation from midsized conferences and would give one seat to “a student athlete or former student athlete.”

In a brief interview last week, Senate Commerce Committee Chair Ted Cruz said he was confident the House would pass the bill “the week after Thanksgiving.”

“There’s plenty of time,” the Texas Republican, a co-author of the Senate bill, said when asked if the lame-duck session’s remaining planned three weeks would allow the House to finish its work on the measure and then give the Senate time to vote on an amended version. The House is slated to leave for a holiday season recess on Dec. 17; the Senate is scheduled to exit the next day.

“I believe the House is going to take it up and pass the bill,” Cruz said, adding that he had talked to Speaker Mike Johnson, R-La., about his plans. “I believe we will see in the House what we saw in the Senate: an overwhelming bipartisan vote.”
