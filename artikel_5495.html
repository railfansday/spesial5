Ballot roulette: Florida voters pick property tax break over local services

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

Florida voters approved a homestead exemption expansion with 62% support, with increases to $150,000 in 2027 and $250,000 in 2028. Opponents warned of public safety and service impacts, and state analysts projected nearly $5 billion in lost local revenue in 2027-28.  AI Summary

With the cost of living on the ballot, Florida voters approved a major tax break for homeowners.

The amendment to increase the Sunshine State’s homestead exemption won the 60% it needed on election night. Voters weren’t swayed by warnings that the higher exemption would devastate municipal coffers and services.

The ballot measure was given little chance of winning. But current economic conditions proved an insurmountable obstacle for the argument against the measure.

Unofficial returns showed it finishing with 62% of the vote. The changes take effect in phases starting in 2027.

“An incredible victory for Florida homeowners!” the Florida Republican Party wrote on social media. “More money stays in your pocket.”

Neil Schiller, managing partner at Government Law Group, told HousingWire TBD the result will change how cities weigh development.

“Projects that generate jobs, economic activity, and alternative tax revenues may become more attractive, while developments that require significant public services and infrastructure will face greater financial scrutiny,” Schiller said. “Amendment 3 may be remembered as the moment Florida cities had to fundamentally reinvent how they do business.”

The vote shows how important affordability has become, Andrew Hellinger, co-principal at Urban-X Group, developer of River Landing Shops & Residences in Miami, said in an e-mail to HousingWire TBD.

Hellinger added, “However, people still need roads, public transportation and essential community services, and those have to be paid for.”

The amendment replaces the $50,000 homestead exemption with a phased increase. It rises to $150,000 in 2027 and $250,000 in 2028. Only primary residences qualify, so second-home and investment-property owners get nothing.

Homeowners must establish Florida residency by Dec. 31, 2026, to get the full benefit. Later arrivals get the $50,000 exemption for four years before qualifying. The school board levy is excluded.

The measure also cuts the annual assessment cap on non-homestead commercial property from 10% to 5%. That change starts Jan. 1, 2027. Renters, who occupy about a third of Florida’s housing units, get no relief.

Gov. Ron DeSantis called a special session to put the amendment on the ballot. Lawmakers approved it with changes protecting school funding. DeSantis had sought a broader exemption.

DeSantis cast the measure as a fix for tax bills inflated by pandemic-era migration. Newcomers, led by New Yorkers, overwhelmed the housing supply and lifted values and tax burdens for longtime residents. Florida home prices rose more than 50% between 2020 and 2022.

Prices have since stalled, and the average home value fell 3.7% over the past year. Assessments haven’t followed: under the Save Our Homes recapture rule, they keep rising until they meet market value. The annual increase is capped at 3% or inflation, whichever is less.

Most counties held millage rates steady or cut them, yet bills still climbed. City tax bills statewide rose about 50% on average over several years.

The Florida Sheriffs Association said in August it was “significantly concerned.” By mid-September, it was running ads featuring sheriffs from four counties. One said, “We want a tax cut too, but Amendment 3 is the wrong way to do it.”

Association President Billy Woods warned of fewer deputies, slower 911 response and more crime. Opponents also included the Florida State Fraternal Order of Police, Florida Professional Firefighters and Florida Fire Chiefs Association. The Florida League of Cities and Florida Association of Counties opposed it, citing too much uncertainty.

According to the legislature’s economic research office, estimated local governments would lose nearly $5 billion in 2027-28.

The Florida Policy Institute, a progressive think tank, created Floridians for Shared Prosperity, which sponsored a campaign urging a no vote.

“Amendment 3 will leave working families and seniors with a lose-lose choice: pay more through new fees and higher taxes or face major cuts to the services you depend on,” the campaign’s website stated.

Voters approved the amendment anyway, with encouragement from the Florida Realtors and others in real estate.

“This is your one shot at real property tax relief,” apartment owner and investor Grant Washington wrote on social media Monday. “Vote Yes on Amendment 3 and put your money back where it belongs. In your pocket!”
