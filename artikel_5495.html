10 ways to train indoors this winter, and what changes calorie burn in each

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Free daily briefing on global business news.

These indoor winter workouts, from rowing to jump rope, keep you training through the cold, with a clear look at what drives calorie burn for each one

No workout has a single true calorie count. Two people can finish the same 30-minute session on a rowing machine and burn very different amounts of energy. The number on a cardio machine's display is an estimate, built on assumptions about the person using it.

That matters in winter, when more training moves indoors. Short days, icy pavements and cold wind make outdoor runs and rides harder to schedule and riskier to attempt. Gyms, pools and living rooms fill the gap. The choice of what to do inside often comes down to a practical question: how much work does this session do?

A standard reference for answering it is the Compendium of Physical Activities. It assigns each activity a MET value, short for metabolic equivalent of task. One MET represents the energy a person uses while sitting quietly at rest. An activity with a higher MET value demands proportionally more energy than rest.

To turn that into an estimate, multiply the MET value by body weight in kilograms, then by the number of hours spent on the activity. The result is an approximate calorie figure. It is a starting point, not a measurement.

Four variables shape the real result. Body weight comes first: a heavier person burns more calories than a lighter person doing the same activity at the same intensity. Intensity is the second. It is the one a person controls most directly, through pace, resistance, load or rest. Duration is the third. Fitness level is the fourth. A trained athlete can often hold a higher intensity for longer than a beginner, which changes how much work a session does.

The 10 indoor winter workouts below are a selection, not a ranking. They run in alphabetical order, and none is presented as the top calorie burner. Each slide explains what the workout involves, which muscles it uses and what equipment it needs. Each also covers which adjustments raise or lower its calorie burn, and why it suits a season that keeps training inside.

The list spans machines found in most gyms, classes offered at studios and routines that need nothing more than floor space. That range lets a reader match a workout to the room, budget and joints they have.

A bodyweight circuit strings together push-ups, squats, lunges, burpees and planks with short rests, using the body as the only resistance. It needs nothing more than a patch of floor. That makes it a practical way to keep training at home when winter weather rules out exercising outside.

The structure is simple. Each exercise runs for a set number of repetitions or a fixed stretch of time, and the next one follows straight away. Rest comes after a full round of every move rather than between individual exercises. A common format is three to five rounds, adjusted for fitness level and the time available.

Each move covers a different part of the body. Push-ups load the chest, shoulders and triceps, while the core holds the torso in a straight line. Squats work the quadriceps, glutes and hamstrings. Lunges train the same muscles one leg at a time, which adds a balance challenge. Burpees combine a squat, a plank and a jump into a single movement. Planks build endurance in the abdominal and lower-back muscles that stabilize the spine.

The order of exercises shapes how the circuit flows. Alternating upper-body and lower-body moves lets one muscle group recover while another works. That keeps the session moving without forcing long pauses.

Progression comes from changing leverage and speed instead of adding weight. Incline push-ups, with the hands on a kitchen counter or sturdy chair, reduce the load for beginners. Decline push-ups, with the feet raised on a step or couch, shift more of the work onto the shoulders and upper chest. Jump squats add an explosive phase to a standard squat. A side plank moves the demand onto the obliques.

Rest length is a major lever on calorie burn in this workout. Shorter breaks keep the heart rate elevated across the session. As a result, the circuit works as cardio as well as strength training. Adding rounds raises the total. Swapping in explosive variations such as jump squats or full burpees raises it as well.

The format suits small winter living spaces. A floor area about the size of a yoga mat covers every move in the circuit. In an apartment, jump squats and burpees can be loud for neighbors below. Step-back burpees, which replace the hop with a step, keep the noise down. Standard squats in place of jump squats do the same. A few minutes of marching in place and arm circles before the first round warm up the joints. A non-slip surface keeps hands and feet from sliding during push-ups and lunges.

Dance cardio turns continuous, choreographed movement set to music into an aerobic workout. Its calorie burn depends less on any single step than on how fast the routine moves, how big the movements are and how long the dancer keeps going without stopping.

Zumba is one of the most widely recognized versions of the format. Alberto "Beto" Perez, a dancer and fitness instructor, created it in Colombia in the 1990s. Classes draw on Latin rhythms such as salsa, merengue, cumbia and reggaeton, layered over simple aerobic patterns. Instructors tend to lead by demonstration and hand signals rather than long verbal breakdowns. Participants follow along in real time.

Zumba is one option among many. Jazzercise, founded by Judi Sheppard Missett in 1969, blends jazz dance with aerobic exercise. Studios and streaming platforms offer hip-hop cardio, Bollywood-style sessions and other music-led routines. Ubisoft's Just Dance video game series scores players on how closely they match the choreography on screen.

Tempo is the first lever on calorie burn. A faster song demands quicker footwork and more steps per minute. Range of motion is the second. Deep knee bends, high knees, full arm sweeps and small jumps move the body through more distance than a shuffle in place. The third lever is rest. A dancer who stops to drink water or wait between every track lets the heart rate settle. Moving straight from one song into the next keeps it elevated.

The format also trains coordination. Routines pair footwork with arm patterns, turns and changes of direction. That challenges balance and rhythm alongside the cardiovascular system. Beginners can learn the footwork first. The arm patterns can be layered on once the steps feel familiar.

Group classes add a social structure. A live session supplies a fixed schedule and an instructor who sets the pace. A solo workout at home has neither unless the dancer builds them in.

At home, dance cardio needs very little: a cleared patch of floor, a screen and supportive shoes. Footwear deserves attention. Running shoes with deep tread can grip the floor during pivots and strain the knees. Cross-trainers or dance-fitness sneakers with smoother soles turn more easily. People in apartments can swap jumps for low-impact steps to limit noise for downstairs neighbors.

That flexibility suits winter. A session can start the moment the music does, with no drive to a gym and no warm-up in freezing air. It fits into a dark evening after work.

High-intensity interval training, or HIIT, alternates short bursts of near-maximal effort with brief recovery periods. It is defined by the timed pattern of work and rest, not by any single exercise.

The best-known format is the Tabata protocol. It comes from research led by Izumi Tabata at Japan's National Institute of Fitness and Sports, published in 1996. The protocol calls for 20 seconds of maximal-intensity work followed by 10 seconds of rest, repeated for eight rounds. The full set lasts four minutes. The original study used a stationary cycle ergometer. The effort was meant to be close to exhausting by the final rounds.

Most gym and home HIIT sessions are less extreme than the original Tabata format. A common structure pairs 30 seconds of hard work with 30 seconds of easy movement. Another uses 40 seconds on and 20 seconds off. Beginners often flip that pattern, working for 20 seconds and resting for 40.

The work-to-rest ratio is the main dial on intensity. Shorter rest leaves less recovery before the next burst. Heart rate then stays higher across the session, and that sustained effort drives calorie burn in an interval workout. Very short rests at a truly maximal effort usually lead to a drop in output in the later rounds. Slightly longer work periods at a hard but repeatable pace can keep the total workload high.

Interval timing works with almost any movement. Sprints on a spin bike, hard pulls on a rowing machine and fast treadmill efforts all fit the format. So do floor moves such as mountain climbers or high knees.

The efforts are demanding, so HIIT is usually spaced through the week rather than done daily. Training plans commonly place an easier day or a strength session between interval days to allow recovery.

Beginners can scale the format in three ways. They can lengthen the rest periods or cut the number of rounds. They can also swap jumping moves for low-impact ones, such as step-backs in place of burpees. Several minutes of easy movement before the first interval prepares the muscles and joints for the hard efforts.

For winter training, the appeal is time. A complete interval session, including a warm-up and cool-down, can fit into 20 to 30 minutes. That suits short days and dark evenings, when a long outdoor run is harder to schedule.

Indoor cycling is low-impact cardio that works mainly the quadriceps, hamstrings, glutes and calves. Its two main controls, resistance and cadence, let a rider set the effort precisely without leaving the house.

Cadence $CDNS is pedal speed, measured in revolutions per minute, or RPM. Resistance is how hard the pedals push back. On most spin bikes, a knob or lever sets it. Raising either one, while holding the other steady, increases calorie burn. A rider spinning fast against almost no resistance does less work than the speed suggests. Pushing heavy resistance at low cadence builds leg strength, but it can be hard to sustain. Structured sessions often move between the two, pairing faster seated spins with slower, heavier climbs.

Equipment shapes the experience. A spin bike has a heavy flywheel at the front and a riding position close to that of a road bike. An upright stationary bike seats the rider more vertically. A recumbent bike adds a backrest and places the pedals forward. That layout supports the lower back. Outdoor cyclists can also mount their own bike on a smart trainer and ride virtual courses in apps such as Zwift.

Bike setup affects both comfort and efficiency. A common starting point is to set the saddle level with the hip bone while standing beside the bike. At the bottom of the pedal stroke, the knee should stay slightly bent rather than locked straight. A saddle set too low pushes stress onto the front of the knee. A saddle set too high makes the hips rock from side to side.

Studio classes and home riding suit different needs. Instructor-led classes add music, coaching cues and a group pace that can push effort higher. SoulCycle built its studio business on that format. Peloton streams live and on-demand classes to its connected bikes at home. Riding solo offers more control over timing and session length.

The low impact sets indoor cycling apart from running. The feet never leave the pedals. That spares the knees, hips and ankles the landing forces of each running stride. It also makes cycling a common option for people easing back from injury. For road cyclists, an indoor bike is a way to hold on to fitness through months of ice, snow and early darkness.

Jumping rope is a compact, low-cost cardio workout that builds footwork, coordination and calf endurance. It needs little more than a rope, a patch of sturdy floor and enough headroom for the rope to pass overhead.

Rope length shapes the whole session. A common check is to stand on the middle of the rope with one foot and pull the handles up along the body. For beginners, handle tips that reach about armpit height are a typical starting point. Experienced jumpers often shorten the rope toward chest height to speed up each turn. A rope that is too long slaps the floor ahead and slows the rhythm. One that is too short catches the toes.

Space matters in three directions: above, in front and behind. The rope sweeps a full arc over the head on every turn. Ceiling height, hanging lights and ceiling fans count as much as floor area. A garage, basement or wide hallway often works better than a furnished living room.

The type of rope changes how the workout feels. Beaded and PVC ropes turn more slowly and are easier to time. That makes them a common pick for beginners. Speed ropes made of thin coated steel cable spin faster and are the usual choice for advanced footwork. Weighted ropes put more load on the shoulders and forearms, adding upper-body effort to the session.

Good form keeps each jump low, just high enough to clear the rope. Landings come on the balls of the feet with the knees slightly soft. The elbows stay close to the ribs, and the wrists do the turning rather than the whole arm. Common progressions run from the basic two-foot bounce to the alternate-foot step, high knees and side-to-side skier.

Calorie burn depends mostly on skip speed and how long a person keeps the rope moving without a miss. Frequent trips break the rhythm and lower the effort. Double-unders, where the rope passes under the feet twice in a single jump, demand a higher jump and quicker wrists. That raises the work involved in each repetition.

Every session involves a long run of small landings, which load the ankles, calves and Achilles tendons. A wooden floor or rubber gym mat absorbs more of that impact than bare concrete or tile. Cushioned cross-training shoes help too. In an apartment, a mat also cuts the noise that reaches neighbors below.

Boxers have long used the rope for footwork and conditioning, often skipping in timed rounds that mirror a fight. Beginners can borrow that structure with short rounds of skipping separated by rest. Each round can grow longer as misses become rarer. For winter, the rope folds into a bag and needs no power, machine or gym membership.

The kettlebell swing is a hip-hinge movement that trains the glutes, hamstrings and core while driving the heart rate up. Paired with a few other kettlebell lifts in a circuit, a single bell can serve as both a strength session and a cardio workout.

The swing starts with the bell on the floor between the feet. The lifter pushes the hips back, keeps the spine neutral and hikes the bell back between the legs. A sharp drive of the hips forward then sends it out in front of the body. The arms act as connectors. The power comes from the hips, not the shoulders.

Two versions are common in gyms. The Russian swing stops the bell at about chest height, with the arms roughly parallel to the floor. The American swing carries it all the way overhead. That overhead finish lengthens the path of the bell. It also demands more shoulder mobility and makes it harder to keep the lower back neutral at the top of each rep.

A circuit adds movements the swing leaves out. The goblet squat, with the bell held by its horns at chest height, trains the quads and an upright torso. The clean brings the bell from the floor to the rack position, resting against the forearm and the front of the shoulder. The Turkish get-up takes a lifter from lying on the floor to standing with the bell locked out overhead, then back down again. It is slow and deliberate, and it tests shoulder stability and coordination more than conditioning.

Bell weight and pace set the calorie burn. A heavier bell moved for the same number of reps asks more of the working muscles. A faster pace with shorter breaks keeps the heart rate elevated across the whole session. Long pauses between rounds pull the workload down.

Lower-back safety comes down to technique. The swing should be a hinge rather than a squat, with the shins staying close to vertical. Each rep should finish with the lifter standing tall and the glutes contracted, not leaning backward. Rounding the spine to chase the bell on the backswing is a common error, and it shifts strain onto the lower back.

For winter training at home, a single kettlebell fits in a closet corner. Bells were traditionally sized in a Russian unit called the pood, equal to about 16 kilograms. Modern bells come in a range of lighter and heavier sizes, sold in kilograms or pounds.

A rowing machine works the legs, core and upper body in one linked stroke. Calorie burn on an indoor erg follows the power a rower puts into each drive. That power appears directly on the machine's monitor.

Erg is short for ergometer, a device that measures work done. Competitive crews have long used ergs to keep training through winter, when rivers and lakes are too cold or frozen to row on. Home and gym models come in air, magnetic, water and hydraulic designs. Concept2's air-resistance rowers are a common sight in gyms and at indoor rowing competitions. The rower stays seated throughout. That keeps impact on the knees and ankles low.

Each stroke follows a fixed order. The drive starts at the catch, with shins close to vertical and arms straight. The legs push first. The torso then swings back from the hips, and the arms finish by drawing the handle toward the lower ribs. The recovery reverses that sequence: arms extend, the body tips forward and the knees bend to slide the seat back to the catch. Pulling with the arms before the legs have finished is a common error. It shifts work away from the large leg muscles and onto the smaller muscles of the arms and shoulders.

The damper lever on an air rower is often mistaken for a resistance dial. On a Concept2 machine, it runs from one to 10 and controls how much air flows into the flywheel housing. A higher setting makes each stroke feel heavier and slower to accelerate, much like rowing a larger, slower boat. It does not by itself make the workout harder. Resistance on an air rower comes from how hard the rower pulls. A faster-spinning flywheel pushes back with more force.

Stroke rate and power are separate measures. Stroke rate counts strokes per minute. Power reflects how hard and how fast each drive moves the handle. A high rate with weak strokes can produce less work than a slower rate with strong leg drive.

The monitor reports that work as watts, as a pace per 500 meters or as a running calorie estimate. Raising the wattage, holding a faster split or extending the session all increase the energy a workout demands.

Stair climbing lifts your full body weight upward with every step. That puts sustained work on the glutes, quadriceps and calves. It can be done on a gym stepmill or in an ordinary building stairwell. Both options keep training going when sidewalks are icy.

Each step works like a small single-leg press. One leg drives the body up while the other swings through to the next stair. The hamstrings help extend the hip, and the core holds the torso upright. The body works against gravity the whole time. Heart rate climbs quickly as a result, even at a modest pace.

Gym machines come in two main styles. A stepmill, such as the StairMaster StepMill, has a revolving set of stairs that rolls downward like a short escalator. The user keeps climbing to stay in place. A stepper uses two pedals that move up and down without a full step cycle. The stepmill more closely copies the motion of a real staircase. Both let the user set a step speed and hold it for an entire session.

A building stairwell costs nothing and is common in apartment blocks and office towers. It offers real stairs with landings, which break up the climb with brief turns. It has no speed setting, so the climber controls pace alone. Check building rules first. Some stairwell doors lock from the stairwell side, and some are fitted with alarms.

Posture has a direct effect on calorie burn. Leaning on the side rails of a machine shifts part of body weight onto the arms. The legs then lift less, and the workout gets easier. Standing tall with only a light touch on the rails for balance keeps the full load on the legs.

Pace and step pattern are the other levers. A faster step rate raises effort. Taking two stairs at a time lengthens each stride and works the glutes and hips through a deeper range of motion. Alternating single and double steps adds variety within one session.

The way down needs attention in a real stairwell. Descending puts braking force through the knees and the front of the thigh, which can strain sensitive joints. Riding the elevator down and then repeating the climb spares the knees that load. A stepmill avoids the descent entirely.

Lap swimming in an indoor pool is a full-body, low-impact workout. Water supports most of a swimmer's body weight. The arms, legs, back and core can work hard without the pounding of running or jumping.

That buoyancy makes swimming an option for people with sore knees, hips or ankles. Each stroke pulls against the resistance of the water rather than against gravity. The shoulders, lats and chest drive the pull. The hips, glutes and legs supply the kick. The core holds the body in a long, flat line, which reduces drag as the swimmer moves through the water.

Lap swimmers usually rotate among four strokes. Freestyle, also called front crawl, is the fastest and the one most people use for continuous laps. Backstroke uses a similar alternating arm pattern on the back. It keeps the face out of the water and makes breathing simple. Breaststroke combines a simultaneous arm sweep with a frog-style kick, and it is the slowest of the four in competition. Butterfly moves both arms together over the water with an undulating dolphin kick. It is the most technically and physically demanding of the four. Most recreational swimmers use it only in short bursts.

Stroke choice is one of three main levers on calorie burn. The other two are pace and the time spent resting at the wall. A swimmer who glides through easy breaststroke and pauses after every length uses far less energy than one holding a steady freestyle pace. Shortening rest between lengths keeps effort high across the session. Swimming sets on a fixed send-off time, where each repeat starts at a set point on the clock, does the same. Technique also matters. A swimmer with inefficient form can tire quickly while covering little distance.

Two common pieces of pool equipment let swimmers target one part of the body. A kickboard is held out in front of the swimmer. The arms stay still and the legs do the work. A pull buoy is a foam float squeezed between the thighs. It keeps the legs afloat without kicking and shifts the load to the arms, shoulders and back. Fins and hand paddles add speed or resistance. They also change stroke mechanics, and swimmers tend to use them in limited doses.

Pool length shapes how a workout is counted. Many U.S. pools are 25 yards long, while long-course competition pools measure 50 meters. Swimmers often plan sets by distance rather than minutes. A pace clock on the wall helps them track intervals and rest.

The winter case for swimming rests on heated indoor pools at gyms, YMCAs, community recreation centers and universities. The water temperature stays steady regardless of snow or ice outside. The main seasonal adjustment comes after the swim. Wet hair and damp skin lose heat quickly in cold air. Drying off fully and covering the head before leaving the building limits that heat loss on the way home.

A treadmill lets runners and walkers set an exact speed and incline. Those two settings are the main ways to change how many calories a session burns.

The machine moves a motorized belt beneath the user at a chosen pace. In the U.S., consoles usually show speed in miles per hour, while most other markets use kilometers per hour. Incline is shown as a percentage grade, and raising it tilts the front of the deck upward. With no wind, ice, traffic lights or uneven pavement, a runner can hold a target pace for an entire session. That precision suits training plans built around specific paces.

Speed is the first lever. A faster pace covers more ground each minute, and heart rate and breathing climb to match. The jump from walking to running also changes the movement itself. Running includes a moment when both feet leave the belt. That raises both the effort and the impact on each landing.

Incline is the second lever. Each step on a raised deck lifts the body upward as well as forward. That shifts more of the work onto the glutes, hamstrings and calves than flat walking does. Heart rate can climb to a demanding level even at a walking pace. For people who want to avoid the pounding of running, incline walking offers a lower-impact way to raise the intensity.

How a person holds on matters too. Gripping the handrails, especially on a steep incline, lets the arms carry part of the body's weight and often shortens the stride. The legs do less work, and the calorie burn drops. The console calculates its calorie readout from settings such as speed, incline and any body weight entered. It has no way to detect someone leaning on the rails. A light touch for balance keeps most of the effort on the legs.

Safety starts before the belt moves. Most treadmills have a safety key that clips to clothing. If the user drifts too far back or falls, the key pulls free and the belt stops. A safe start means standing on the side rails, starting the belt at a slow speed and then stepping on. Clear space behind the machine reduces the risk if someone slips off the back.

In winter, a treadmill at home or in a gym supports steady runs, interval sessions and long incline walks after dark or during a snowstorm.
