Vendor Lock-In Is Creating a Market for Escape

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Co-founder of Gale Strategies, a marketing and public relations firm serving technology companies and tech investors.

Co-founder of Gale Strategies, a marketing and public relations firm serving technology companies and tech investors.

Co-founder of Gale Strategies, a marketing and public relations firm serving technology companies and tech investors.

Jeff Bezos famously said, “Your margin is my opportunity.” Where incumbents carried generous margins and sought rents, a hungry challenger saw room to win by serving customers better and charging less. Today, the line could be, “Your switching costs are my opportunity.” A new wave of enterprises is building a business out of jailbreaking customers from platforms.

And “platform” used to be a positive word. Investors liked platforms. Customers liked them too. And the latter dependably picked a platform over an agglomeration of best-in-class solutions. Our own little outfit is no exception. Maybe you weren’t excited about the platform over the best in class alternative. But the platform application fit with every other platform application better, and the combined cost and security were and remain seductive.

Well, the security may remain seductive. But the pricing is changing.

We have lived this at our own firm. ActiveCampaign notified us that our monthly plan would move from $295.29 to $399.46 on December 19th, an increase of about 35%, with the new rate applying unless we completed cancellation before that date. ActiveCampaign's price increases stem from a multi-year shift from simple email marketing to an all-in-one, AI-powered automation platform.

But putting that aside, its last raise was in 2021, the peak of the zero-interest-rate tech bubble. Venture capital and growth-equity funds like those that put their then-cheap money into the company typically look for an exit within five to seven years. So it’s time to monetize the switching costs developed over that period. Essentially firms like ours whose contacts and CRM intelligence are in the platform. But to the extent that money is being put into improving the platform, it’s not easy to see.

The market is also saturated, and smaller firms like ours require proportionately more handing than large enterprises for less revenue, so we’re no longer welcome unless we’re willing to pay closer to what big companies are seeking to pay. We’re not going to get better service out of this price increase, but large enterprises might.

Anyone who has read about the history of redlining in the U.S. and how places like Harlem paid for America’s suburbs will start to get aspects of the picture, though in an exponentially more insidious and morally corrupt form. Not to pick on ActiveCampaign, because despite their attempting to mollify us by inviting us to pay a little bit less in absolute terms by paying it all in advance, they’ve been accommodating enough about letting us get off here, and us letting them try to focus on the large enterprises.

With Cision - our reporter database and wire service platform - we had stayed through earlier price increases even though the service wasn’t delivering anything new or better that we wanted. We did this because we assumed that in exchange for our loyalty they would help us out if we needed it.

As we’ve pursued a pivot, we asked for help with the cost. They couldn’t give us any, and we gave notice of non-renewal. We then learned that services couldn’t be removed mid-contract and they were bent on holding us down. While previously it was just the reporter database we wanted to address, now we’ve realized that while we saw them as a partner who would reward loyalty, the company is actually a risk and can amplify and compound problems if and when they occur. So we are now moving off both of their services. We’re also looking more carefully at the ownership structure and financing of the tech solutions and platforms we use, because we don’t want our data and workflows in the hands of an outfit that must monetize its switching costs on that same five-to-seven-year clock.

Neither vendor lost us over a feature or poor service. The products were great, exactly what we needed. Though interestingly in both cases once we left the platform we realized we had been missing superior services from competitors or we could just build what we needed internally. It wasn’t a priority to find alternatives, but both companies made it one for us, and we have benefited, as have our own clients.

This is the problem with switching costs as a competitive advantage.

The more a company raises prices, no matter how much it claims the increases fund improvements the customer doesn’t recall asking for, the more the switching costs become a bitter experience. As one operations leader said within earshot of me recently, “I hate platforms, I don’t want us to be on a platform.” And as the cheap money becomes scarce, and U.S. policymaking does that it has been doing in very recent years, that sentiment is likely to increase.

Vertice, a software-procurement firm that tracks what its customers pay, reports preliminary SaaS inflation of 14.3 percent in September 2026, after a record 16.4% in June. Over the same twelve months, US consumer prices rose 3.7 percent according to the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, CPI release of October 14th. Vertice’s figure is a vendor-published average drawn from its own customer base, not an official statistic. But Microsoft’s own pricing announcement shows the pattern at the largest platform, effective July 1st Microsoft 365 Business Basic rose from $6 to $7 per user per month. That’s 16 percent. Business Standard went from $12.50 to $14 So 12 percent. And Microsoft 365 E3 has gone from $36 to $39 for 8 percent. Existing customers move to the new prices at renewal.

When a software company loses confidence that it can win customers by building better products, it stops trying to keep customers because they love the product. It tries to keep them because they can’t leave.

Challenger, Gray & Christmas counted 171,501 announced US technology job cuts through October 2026. That is up 21 percent from a year earlier and already more than the 154,445 for all of 2025. Layoffs.fyi lists about 149,000 tech employees laid off at 366 companies worldwide so far this year. Companies increasingly say AI is the reason. The evidence for that is thin.

Challenger has tracked AI as the most-cited reason for layoffs for several months running in 2026, but laid-off workers describe something else. In a February 2026 Gallup survey of more than 23,000 US workers, only about 1 percent of those laid off said AI directly caused their job loss. Most cited restructuring, cost-cutting and economic conditions, and that includes a shift away from customer-centrism to rent-raising. A study reported by Fortune in August 2026 foud some of the companies studied cut staff before investing much in AI, allegedly to free up money for it. Revelio Labs found that companies announcing AI-driven layoffs had actually been adding AI talent more slowly than their industry peers, at a median of about 11 percent over two years compared with more than 13 percent. Forrester estimates that only 6 percent of US jobs will be automated by 2030. If AI were doing the work of the people being let go, these numbers would look different.

The layoffs line up better with a change in where companies want their people. Support and product engineering are shrinking. Sales, account management and migration programs are protected or growing. And prices are rising on customers who find it hard to leave.

Start with Broadcom and VMware. VMware had more than 38,000 employees at the start of 2023 and about 16,000 two years later, according to Business Insider. Smaller customers lost direct support and were routed to a distributor, with response times stretching to a week or more. European cloud providers documented price increases of 800 percent to 1,500 percent. Even so, a January 2026 CloudBolt survey of 302 IT decision-makers found that only 4 percent had fully replaced VMware. Untangling the dependencies typically takes 18 to 24 months. The study concluded that Broadcom is deliberately accepting customer churn while maximizing margins on the customers who remain. Support quality was a top concern for 78 percent of respondents, and 85 percent expect further price increases. None of this depends on AI.

Broadcom ran the same playbook after buying CA Technologies in 2018, and Constellation Research analyst Ray Wang said when the VMware layoffs began that this is how Broadcom makes money from its acquisitions.

At Salesforce, CEO Marc Benioff claimed in September 2025 that AI agents had allowed the company to cut its customer support workforce from about 9,000 to 5,000. He says he has held engineering headcount flat at roughly 15,000 for two years. In January 2026 he said the company was hiring 20 percent more account executives. In August 2025, Salesforce raised prices on Enterprise and Unlimited editions of Sales Cloud, Service Cloud and other products by an average of 6 percent, after a 9 percent increase in 2023. AI is harder to accept as the explanation, given that Business Insider reported Agentforce usage remained relatively low and the product hadn’t matched the company’s demos. In early 2026, Salesforce cut roles on the Agentforce team itself.

In May 2026 Cloudflare eliminated more than 1,100 positions, about 20 percent of its workforce, in the same period it reported record quarterly revenue of $639.8 million, up 34 percent. Its executives said AI use had increased more than 600 percent in three months and that employees were running thousands of AI-agent sessions a day. If AI had been driving the decisions, you would expect the heaviest AI users to be protected, but according to reporting on the earnings call, the company protected salespeople carrying revenue quotas instead, and the cuts reached across every other team and geography.

In January 2026 Amazon announced 16,000 corporate job cuts, following 14,000 in October 2025. Washington state filings show the largest category was software development engineers, along with security, data and quality-assurance engineers, applied scientists, UX designers and product managers. Amazon described the cuts as removing layers and bureaucracy. One of those let go was N. Lee Plumb, his team’s head of AI enablement, whom Amazon had flagged as one of the top users of its AI coding tool, Kiro, by his account, only five people in the company used it more. A company replacing people with AI would have little reason to let him go.

SAP’s 2024 restructuring affected about 10,000 roles and cost €3.1 billion. Its CFO said the goal was to decouple expenses from revenue growth. The same year SAP created a board-level unit dedicated to moving customers to the cloud. Mainstream support for its ECC system ends in 2027, and SAP has said future innovation, including AI, will be available only in the cloud. Senior executives surveyed by Constellation Research described its RISE migration program as financial engineering more than a benefit to customers.

Regulators have documented how hard it is for customers to leave. In November 2024 the US Government Accountability Office found that five of six federal agencies it interviewed had run into restrictive vendor licensing practices. Three were required to buy again licenses they already owned in order to run that software in the cloud. One had to pay to regain ownership of its own data when a contract ended. In July 2025 the UK Competition and Markets Authority concluded that switching between cloud providers was extremely rare and that egress fees and licensing practices were hurting competition. It estimated that prices just 5 percent above competitive levels would cost UK customers about £500 million a year in a market of roughly £10.5 billion.

AI gives these companies a forward-looking explanation for cuts that investors reward. The pattern underneath appears more prosaic. Vendors may be cutting the engineers and support staff who understand the customer’s problems. They are putting their headcount and money into the sales teams, migration programs onto platforms with switching costs and contract structures that move customers onto their platforms and keep them there. And they appear to be raising prices on customers whose cost of leaving keeps rising. Customers get less help, pay more and have fewer ways out in this formulation.

Enterprise software used to win by building a better product, solving harder problems and giving customers a reason to renew. Today a vendor can do well by building an ecosystem customers can’t easily leave, cutting the cost of serving them and charging the installed base more. Engineering then gets judged by how efficiently the vendor makes money from the customer’s dependence.

CIOs and CFOs evaluating a cloud migration or AI rollout may want to look past this year’s budget savings and ask whether the vendor’s product, service and flexibility are improving, or whether their company is just becoming more dependent on a vendor that profits from engineered retention more than satisfaction. A vendor that cuts the engineers who understand its users while protecting the people who lock those users in has stopped competing on product, and its customers usually find that out at renewal.

To execute a pivot toward raw infrastructure rent-seeking, corporate leadership almost always chooses a specific archetype of manager we feel, the platform generalist. These leaders’ core competencies are systems integration (without passing the benefit on to customers), horizontal scaling, and cross-application alliances. They don’t see software as a solution that delivers greater benefits to users, they see it as a distribution channel to force consumption of underlying cloud servers.

And if some feature or pricing model will lure the unsophisticated into the trap, this manager is willing to be rewarded for it, trading integrity for short-term margin and a hoped-for getaway when the market catches on.

Under this framework, specialized, domain-rich product teams are systematically trimmed or ignored to fund massive infrastructure buildouts. This creates an organizational environment of intellectual debt, the company stops asking how to innovate to save its users time, and starts asking what the bare minimum is to prevent a total customer revolt.

When a company reaches this stage, it stops creating value and starts extracting rent, it weaponizes its legal departments, licensing audits, and predatory contract bundles to squeeze extra margin out of a captive customer base, and product excellence gives way to operational coercion.

The fatal flaw in the rent-seeking playbook is the assumption that your moats are made of stone, when they are actually made of code and human expertise.

When an organization defunds its application layer to buy database chips, for instance, it inevitably triggers a catastrophic brain drain. The elite domain specialists, the institutional anchors who spent decades perfecting niche compliance features, complex calculation logic, and granular user workflows, realize there is no longer a seat for them at the table. They leave. Sometimes it’s worse: bad strategy leads companies to escort their best people out the door as they pivot away from the user and toward servicing their debt covenants.

Losing a superstar domain specialist is not just an HR headache; it is a structural breach in the corporate moat. Even if the individual is deep inside the org and you think the customers can’t see them, other team members can, and they get the message: caring about the user and customers is not rewarded here, and it’s time to get out while you still can. These specialists you’re losing possess a rare dual capability: they understand the hyper-complex, real-world problems of the industry, and they know exactly where the legacy software’s seams are weak. When an executive lets an elite product leader and engineer walk out the door, they lose their translation shield, and with it the internal credibility required to keep customers from rebelling against an unusable interface.

More importantly, they give the open market a weapon. In the modern cloud landscape, enterprise lock-in is ultimately just a data mapping problem. The moment an elite architect leaves and teaches rival platforms or cloud-flexible competitors how to untangle, translate, and automate the migration of your proprietary code, the “divorce-proof” illusion evaporates.

This is where Hamilton Helmer’s book 7 Powers is instructive. He names switching costs as one of the seven sources of durable competitive advantage, and the most deceptive of them. Switching costs are the one ring that rules them all, they make you feel powerful because people are forced to stay with you, even against their will.

But like the ring, the power corrupts the one who wields it. Once you lose your customers’ will, and they must stay with you without choosing you, you have already lost. You are no longer earning loyalty; you are guarding prisoners. And a new wave of enterprises is attacking exactly that vulnerability.

The customer’s side of the trap looks a lot like asking the Mafia for a favor. The first offer is generous: a bundled discount, free migration credits, a deal that solves today’s problem. It feels like a friendship. Only later do you learn the favor was never a transaction; it was a debt, and the debt has no payoff date. Every renewal comes with new terms, and every attempt to leave is met with a reminder of what you owe. You didn’t buy a product; you accepted a relationship you can’t exit.

The more a business or investor falls into its own trap, using switching costs to save itself and squeeze customers and clients, the more hated it becomes. Even those who stay are like the subjects of a Soviet regime, compliant on the surface, waiting for their chance to escape or overthrow it. When that chance arrives, they take it.

This is why switching costs are a toxic, addictive, and self-destructive power to lean into. The more you use it for short-term returns, the more you sacrifice the trust and authenticity on which long-term persistence is best built.

The tragedy of the platform generalist is that financial metrics often shield them from reality until it is too late. Because enterprise data gravity is heavy and moving off a core utility layer is a multi-year, multi-million-dollar risk, revenue may continue to climb for years on forced renewals alone.

Coming back to VMware under Broadcom returns to a test case. Broadcom’s infrastructure software segment, which includes VMware, reported $6.9 billion in revenue in the fourth quarter of fiscal 2025, up 19 percent from a year earlier. AT&T alleged in a 2024 lawsuit that Broadcom proposed a 1,050 percent increase to its annual VMware bill. The case was settled out of court.

Customers have been slow to leave, however. The CloudBolt survey cited earlier, of 302 IT decision-makers at North American companies with at least 1,000 employees, found that 86 percent were actively reducing their VMware footprint even though only 4% had fully migrated off it. That is the illusion in numbers, revenue climbs on forced renewals while customers plan their exit. CloudBolt sells hybrid cloud management software, so treat its survey as indicative rather than definitive.

But a company that takes an anti-customer approach and sells to anti-customer gatekeepers has written its own obituary. It becomes an empire built on quicksand. Modern organizations, the startups, high-growth scale-ups, and developers of the next generation, will avoid these legacy utilities like the plague. They build on open, flexible, product-led architectures designed to remain rent-proof from day one.

The lesson for modern tech leaders may be absolute, infrastructure means nothing if the software on top of it is unusable or beige as one revenue leader in professional services and tech said recently.

If you strip your company of its product-deep soul to fund a commoditized infrastructure floor, you are not building a permanent monopoly. You are handing agile, user-first challengers a highly profitable opening to deliver a clean-break data blueprint and hollow out your captive customer base from the outside. You might win the budget war today, but you have guaranteed your long-term irrelevance.

A simple test, can you say upfront that what you are selling is handcuffs? If, instead, you are selling how cool-looking and comfortable the handcuffs are, you are losing. Your switching costs are someone else’s opportunity, and they are coming for it.

Platform lock-in is now big enough to support its own escape industry.

Nutanix added 3,000 new customers in fiscal 2026, its third straight year at that pace, and much of that growth came from customers leaving VMware. Red Hat stripped the container features out of OpenShift to give VMware users a product that runs only virtual machines. It says the number of VMs running on that platform grew 417 percent in a year and that its services team has assessed more than 1.46 million VMs for migration. HPE became, in Forrester’s words, the first hardware vendor to build its own virtualization software in response to Broadcom’s takeover of VMware.

Many of the people building this escape industry learned the trade inside the platforms customers are now trying to leave. VergeIO, which sells a VMware replacement and recently sped up its migration tools, put former VMware CTO Kit Colbert on its board and hired Parag Patel, a former VMware sales vice president, as CEO. About 19,000 people have left VMware since Broadcom took over. KORE1 says the largest share is landing in the migration practices of system integrators such as Accenture, Deloitte, Kyndryl and HCLTech. A single exit can absorb a team. KORE1 estimates Western Union’s migration of 900 to 1,200 applications needs 30 to 50 engineers. The pattern reaches past VMware. Rimini Street says its primary support engineers average more than 20 years of experience. Dreamteam, founded by former Freshworks executives, has raised $53 million for an AI-native CRM and has already replaced Salesforce at DMS Health.

At the end of the day, where do you invest into your own tech capabilities, both as an individual advancing your career, or as an enterprise leader or investor? The Jailbreak economy is looking promising.
