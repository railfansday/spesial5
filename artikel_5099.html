Three Central Banks Test a Bridge Between Traditional FX and Tokenized Settlement

The Bank of England, Monetary Authority of Singapore and Bank of Thailand have completed a cross-border foreign-exchange settlement experiment designed to connect existing payment infrastructure with tokenised systems. The project might sound technical, but it addresses a familiar financial problem: two parties need to exchange different currencies without either being exposed to the risk that one side pays and the other does not.

The trial offers evidence that traditional central-bank settlement systems and distributed ledgers can be coordinated in a controlled setting. It does not mean a new public FX network has launched or that ordinary customers can use the prototype today.

In their joint announcement, the three central banks described work under the Meridian FX APAC initiative. The experiment tested how payment-versus-payment settlement could function across different systems, including a distributed-ledger environment and conventional real-time gross settlement arrangements. Payment-versus-payment means one currency leg is settled only if the other can be settled too.

That principle is not new. What is new here is the attempt to apply it when separate technology stacks are involved. As the industry experiments with tokenised deposits and other digital representations of money, mismatches between systems can create delays or counterparty risks. A synchronised approach seeks to reduce that gap without requiring every participant to migrate onto the same ledger.

The trial also explored multilateral netting. Rather than having every participant settle each obligation individually, a system can calculate offsetting positions so that fewer funds need to move. In principle, this reduces liquidity demands and can make cross-border payment arrangements more efficient. In practice, netting depends on legal finality, settlement windows and clear responsibilities if a participant fails.

Partior developed the prototype used in the trial, according to reporting on the project. This was a test environment, not a new production service from the central banks. Each jurisdiction's live payment systems and regulatory frameworks would still need to support any eventual deployment, which makes the operational path more complicated than a successful demonstration might suggest.

Much of the industry's attention has been directed at tokenising bonds, shares or funds. Yet creating a digital security does not solve the equally important problem of settling the cash side of a transaction. If a tokenised asset settles instantly but payment remains trapped in an overnight banking process, a large part of the efficiency claim disappears.

That is why central-bank experiments deserve attention even from readers who do not trade foreign exchange. European work on tokenisation standards covered by NewsBTC reflects a related concern: interoperable rules and infrastructure are needed if digital securities are to operate beyond isolated pilots.

The Bank of England has discussed a synchronisation interface with a target timeline in 2028. That is a useful reminder of the gap between a promising proof of concept and regulated, always-available payment infrastructure. Security controls, access criteria and recovery procedures are as important as transaction speed.

For businesses, the near-term value is the direction of travel rather than an immediately available product. Stablecoin payment networks are already being used in commercial contexts, but central-bank settlement operates with distinct legal and systemic requirements. Meridian FX APAC shows institutions are working on the bridge between those worlds — carefully, and not yet at full scale.
