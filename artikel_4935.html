Functional Core Workouts Are the Key to Moving Better in and Out of the Gym

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

Add these 15 expert-approved exercises into your weekly routine.

I don’t know about you, but doing core exercises is my least favorite part of workouts. Unfortunately, though, neglecting functional core training can make a big difference in your total-body strength, and how you move day to day.

Functional training focuses on compound movements that engage multiple muscle groups simultaneously and mimic the way our bodies move during everyday activities. Functionally training the core, specifically, involves strengthening the abdominal muscles that attach to the pelvis or spine.

For instance, a farmer’s carry mirrors the movement of carrying grocery bags. “This is an example of ‘bracing and carrying,’ where you need to keep a tight, braced core to carry the heavy weight,” says Corrine Carnation, CPT, a personal and group trainer at Equinox and creator of the Better You, Better Living program. Plus, a weak core can lead to poor posture and an increased risk of injury.

But the benefits extend way beyond preparing your body for real-life activities and protecting against pain or injury—keep reading for all the reasons you should do functional core movements.

Meet the experts: Corrine Carnation, CPT, is a personal and group trainer at Equinox. Cori Ritchey, CSCS, is the fitness editor at Women's Health, as well as a certified strength and conditioning coach and group fitness instructor in New York City.

Time: 30 minutes | Equipment (optional): kettlebell, dumbbells, slam ball, resistance band, or Pilates ring | Good for: core, hips, back

Instructions: Choose 5 to 6 exercises from the list below. Complete 2 to 3 sets of the indicated number of reps for each move. Aim to integrate functional core exercises into your training routine once or twice a week.

As these exercises become easier with practice, challenge yourself by increasing resistance while slightly decreasing the number of reps. For example, if 3 sets of 10 reps using bodyweight alone feels easy, try adding a weight or resistance band for 2 or 3 sets of 8 reps. By the last rep of each set, you should feel ready to stop but not completely at failure. When in doubt, start with bodyweight only, then add resistance until you feel the exercise is challenging, but doable.

Pro tip: Exercises aren’t one-size-fits-all, so it’s important to recognize when an exercise is either too challenging, too easy, or not comfortable for you. Keep in mind that every exercise can be modified if needed!

Why it rocks: The suitcase carry prepares the core for real-life activities such as carrying heavy things by your sides. It strengthens the core by making you resist the urge to bend, improving stability and balance—the added grip demand is a fun challenge, too.

1. Holding a weight in one hand, stand up tall and pull your shoulders back and down to prevent them from rolling forward. Keep your core tight and spine neutral.

2. Walk forward for 30 to 60 seconds while maintaining a strong grip and upright posture.

4. Turn around and pick the weight up again, this time with your left hand. Complete 4 reps on each side.

Why it rocks: The halo exercise improves shoulder mobility and stability while strengthening your core. Moving the weight around your head causes a perturbation (or distracting force) to your body, forcing you to stabilize using your core. This particular halo variation has the added benefit of enhancing upper-body strength, targeting the shoulders, back, and arms.

1. Stand with feet shoulder-width apart, holding one dumbbell in front of your chest with both hands and elbows bent at 90 degrees.

2. Slowly circle the dumbbell around your head, moving it clockwise from ear to ear, elbows hugging in.

3. Return to start and repeat the movement counterclockwise. That's 1 rep.

Why it rocks: Have you ever worried about straining your back when picking something up or twisting? This move strengthens the core, improves stability, and builds rotational strength to protect your body during these movements.

1. Sit on the floor with your knees bent and upper body leaned back until abs are engaged (aim for a 45-degree angle relative to the floor). Hold a slam ball or dumbbell close to your chest with your elbows bent.

2. Keeping your arms slightly bent, twist your torso to the left and slam the ball on the floor next to your left hip.

3. Twist your torso through center to the right and slam the ball on the floor near your right hip. That's 1 rep.

4. Continue alternating for a total of 10 to 12 reps.

Why it rocks: The overhead slam exercise requires you to stabilize your body as you lift and forcefully throw the ball down to the floor. It also strengthens your shoulders, arms, and upper back while improving explosive power and coordination.

1. Stand with your feet shoulder-width apart, a soft bend in knees, and core engaged, while holding a slam ball at your chest with both hands.

2. Rise up tall on your toes and extend your arms overhead holding the slam ball.

3. Bend at your hips (hinging forward slightly) and slam the ball on the floor directly in front of you. Exhale as you throw the ball down toward the floor with force. Be careful not to arch your back.

4. Catch the ball on the bounce while lowering back into the squat position. That's 1 rep.

Why it rocks: The wood chop exercise helps strengthen your core by working your abs and obliques, which can boost your twisting power and stability, building strength and coordination.

1. Start in a half-kneeling position with your left leg forward and your right leg back, both bent at 90 degrees, holding a dumbbell in front of your chest.

2. With your arms straight, torso upright, and hips facing forward, swiftly lower the weight diagonally across your body to the right hip while twisting to the right. Then, raise the weight to upper left diagonal while twisting to the left. That's 1 rep.

3. Complete 10 reps, then repeat on the other side.

Why it rocks: Keeping your lower back and head flush with the floor while your limbs shift weight will engage your deep core stabilizers. Being able to lift your legs and arms from this position is essential for a healthy spine.

1. Lie faceup holding one dumbbell horizontally in front of your chest with both hands. Lift legs to a tabletop position with your knees over hips, your lower back glued to the floor, and your core engaged.

2. Keeping one leg at a 90-degree angle, lower the other to tap your heel to the floor. As your leg lowers, reach your arms above your head to lower the weight toward the floor behind your head.

3. Engage your core to lift both your arms and leg back to start, then repeat on the opposite side. That's 1 rep.

Why it rocks: This exercise recruits your abdominal muscles to maintain balance and stability. A resistance band offers an added challenge for the core and lower body.

1. Stand with your feet shoulder-width apart and your hands on your hips or relaxed at your sides. (Optional: Place a resistance band around the arches of both feet.)

2. Balancing on one leg, draw one knee up toward your chest, hold for 1 second, then return it to the floor.

3. Repeat on the opposite side, alternating for 30 seconds. That’s 1 rep.

Why it rocks: Planks help you build the strength to push yourself up from the floor, which is important for many day-to-day activities. Adding the knee drive challenges your upper body and core to stabilize, improving your balance and coordination with uneven movements.

1. Start in a high plank position with your right foot lifted slightly up off the floor.

2. Exhale and pull your right knee toward your chest while rounding the spine to bring your nose toward your knee.

3. With control, reverse movement to return to plank position with right foot lifted. That's 1 rep.

Why it rocks: This exercise challenges you to sit up and lie down without using your hands, which requires strong core muscles. You can do it with a Pilates ring, a ball, or no equipment at all.

1. Lie faceup with your legs slightly bent, heels on floor, and arms extended straight above your head. (Optional: Place a mini resistance band around your wrists for an added challenge.)

2. Engage your core and draw your belly button in as you slowly begin to curl your spine off the mat one vertebra at a time. Roll up to an upright seated position while keeping biceps by the ears and arms reaching to the ceiling.

3. Once you reach the upright position, gradually reverse the movement by slowly rolling back down to the lying position. That's 1 rep.

Why it rocks: This exercise targets the glutes and core, the muscles needed to lift your hips. It's great for anyone who sits a lot, as it both strengthens and mobilizes the hips.

1. Lie faceup with knees bent and feet planted shoulder-width apart on the floor. Place your arms on the floor by your sides or for an added challenge, extend arms above with your hands directly above your shoulders and your palms facing in.

2. Engage your core as you press up into a glute bridge with your knees, hips, and shoulders in alignment, and hold this position.

3. From here, raise your right leg to a 90-degree angle, keeping the stationary leg firmly on the floor and pelvis stable. Return right foot to meet left foot in starting position. That’s 1 rep.

4. Continue alternating legs with your butt lifted and complete 10 to 12 reps on each side.

Why it rocks: The classic side plank targets the obliques, which are essential for stability and rotation in daily activities. It also works your shoulders and hips, promoting good posture and alignment.

1. Lie on your right side with your legs bent, your right elbow directly under your shoulder, and your left hand palm down.

2. Lift your hips off the mat, extending your legs to form a straight line from your head to heels.

3. Hold the position for 30 seconds, then repeat on the opposite side. That’s 1 rep.

Why it rocks: It's similar to the suitcase carry, but levels up the move a bit by testing your stabilization, as you have to stay upright even though the weight is trying to pull you down. Plus, it boosts your grip strength.

1. Stand up straight with feet shoulder-width apart and dumbbell in left hand.

2. Extend your right arm out to the side at a 45-degree angle from your body.

3. Slowly lift your left leg up until knee is at hip height.

4. Then, with control, lower your leg down to the floor. Repeat with right leg. That's 1 rep.

5. Complete 10 to 12 reps, then switch the dumbbell to your right hand.

Why it rocks: It targets your obliques, but also challenges your other core muscles in a more uncommon position. Additionally, it works your spinal mobility.

1. Kneel on left knee with toes pointed to the right, and right foot planted firmly to the side of body, holding a kettlebell in right hand.

2. Rack the kettlebell at chest, then lift it overhead, keeping eyes on the weight.

3. Slowly lower body down until left hand reaches the floor, then continue lowering until elbow and forearm reach the floor.

4. Reverse the movement with control and return to start. That's 1 rep.

Why it rocks: It's important to train rotation because we're constantly rotating in real life. Plank hip dips strengthen the obliques and stabilize the spine.

1. Start in a forearm plank position with elbows under shoulders and knees, hips, and torso in a straight line.

2. Drop right hip toward the floor, then return to center.

3. Immediately drop the left hip toward the floor.

Why it rocks: This move is one of the best for training functional power, and it demands stabilization from your core.

1. Stand with feet slightly wider than hip-width apart. Hold a kettlebell in front of your body with both hands, arms straight.

2. With a slight bend in the knees and a flat back, hinge at the hips and swing the kettlebell back through the legs. Use that momentum to stand and swing the kettlebell out in front of your body, up to shoulder height.

3. Thrust the hips forward and engage the glutes and core as you stand up straight. When the kettlebell hits shoulder height, the knees should be straight and glutes contracted in a full hip extension.

4. Allow the kettlebell to swing back down through the legs, while hinging at the hips. That's 1 rep.

The best functional core workouts include bodyweight and resistance exercises that challenge the core in different positions and directions. Your core is not one-dimensional, so it’s important to strengthen your body through movements that involve twisting, bending, and stabilizing in all directions. For example, a traditional situp only engages the core in a single plane of motion, but a workout with a Russian twist (for rotational movement), lateral side bends (for side-to-side movement), and plank variations (for multidirectional stability) ensures you are hitting your core muscles from all angles.

Carnation notes the importance of first activating your core before jumping right into a workout. To do so, she recommends the following: “Act as if someone is about to throw a basketball at your stomach.” It might sound odd, but this cue can be really effective in helping you tighten and brace your core to get ready for impact. It’s best to practice this abdominal bracing technique before going into more advanced exercises in order to ensure your core is engaged and ready for action, Carnation says.
