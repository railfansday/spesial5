When Saying ‘No’ to an Aging Parent Is Complicated

Let’s start with an example: Mira*, a 78-year-old mother of two adult children, has begun to experience significant change in her physical and cognitive abilities, causing her to need more support from her oldest adult daughter, Jackie.

When Jackie was a child, her mother experienced periods of severe depression, isolating from Jackie and her younger sister, Karen, and leaving Jackie to do things like make dinner and help Karen with her homework. Their father, Stan, was often traveling for work, and Jackie felt guilty at the thought of telling him when he returned about how their mother had essentially ‘checked out’ of parenting.

Now as adults, Karen lives across the country, and Jackie, being the closest and oldest sibling, has been tasked with taking their mother to doctors’ appointments, cooking and cleaning for her, and keeping her company when her mother calls her crying. Exhausted and resentful, Jackie begins therapy to figure out how to address her need for freedom from her mother’s demands while also supporting both of her aging parents.

Parents have an uncanny ability to move us straight back into our childhood selves. Like a game of Chutes and Ladders, one look, comment, or criticism can send us sliding back down into our most painful core beliefs about ourselves, an internal drumbeat of fears that may include, Am I enough? Am I too much? Am I lovable? As parents age, they have more needs, and what was developmentally inappropriate for someone to handle in childhood becomes a more age-appropriate task of middle adulthood: Attending to the growing needs of aging parents. But behavior that was problematic for a parent when their children were young, if unaddressed, only becomes magnified as their bodies and minds begin to slow down, illnesses take over, and independence wanes. The parentified child, perhaps doing the painful and arduous therapeutic work of self-discovery over decades, is newly practiced and armed with coping tools, one of which is learning how to say no.

How do parentified adult children navigate this juxtaposition of caring for aging parents who parentified them and still maintain clarity and the ability to set much needed boundaries?

In the United States, women are the primary caregivers for an aging society, taking on the low-paying or no-paying job of care work, both formally in the healing professions and informally within their families. As demonstrated by a recent and all-too-accurate skit on SNL, the majority of caregiving falls to women, and in the case of aging parents, often the oldest (or most available) daughter. Health, education, and literacy occupations (also known as HEAL) have long been viewed as ‘women’s work,’ and broad societal attitudes such as these have trickled down and influenced our social norms, creating the expectation for women to be more flexible, more generous with their time, and more understanding of the hardship that caregiving can create.

The weight of ingrained societal and familial expectation, the feelings of guilt for having needs and setting boundaries, and the effort to resist fixing and taking responsibility can be heavy and overwhelming. Developing a plan to manage your feelings in the day-to-day interactions with an aging parent is helpful. The next time you find yourself caught in the cycle of blame, shame, guilt, and resentment related to caregiver duties, think about the following:

The ‘no’ is complicated, especially for women. It comes with the weight of societal expectations, cultural considerations, and years of history between family members. Given this, I want to encourage setting the boundary that feels right for you at any given time. One day, it might look like not picking up the phone or returning a call until you feel ready. Another day, it might involve being proactive and stopping by before a crisis occurs. Whatever it is, know that if you attune to your own feelings and needs first, your inner compass will guide you to your best solution in that moment.

* Disclaimer: This is a composite example that captures the spirit of the many iterations of similar situations that I have seen over the years in my practice. It does not refer to any one specific situation or person/people.
