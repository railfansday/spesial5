Scientists have been using the size and orientation of these dunes for decades to act like a weather vane, figuring out which way the wind blows on these other worlds. Unfortunately, weather vanes arenât good at telling the full windy story - they canât tell you how fast the wind is going. However, according to a new paper published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences by researchers at Stanford, sand dunes can.

Each is made up of different materials, but all share a crucial characteristic - their âsandâ consists of small grains of similar particles, which could be blown around by wind.

Understanding why this is important requires looking at how wind actually moves sand in the first place. When air is moving across a dune, individual grains stay stationary until the wind itself reaches a critical speed known as the transport threshold. Once it rises above that, the grains begin bouncing across the ground in a movement known as saltation. Each time a grain crashes back to the surface, it kicks up other surrounding grains into the air, creating a self-sustaining cascade of moving sand.

What was unclear to scientists was whether these motions were primarily driven by violent dust storms that were relatively rare, or moderate winds which are much more common on the other worlds where dunes have been seen. Colin Marvin, a PhD student in the Department of Earth and Planetary Sciences at Stanford, collected wind speed data and detailed grain-size measurements from twenty-four different dune fields spread all over the world. From the Coral Pink Sand Dunes State Park in Utah to the ancient, hundred-meter-tall dunes in the Namib Sand Sea in Namibia, the researchers found a remarkable similarity.

Across every terrestrial site, the most sand was moved when the wind was slightly above the speed required to start moving it. And, crucially, the same relationship also held on a Maritan sand dune. The process didnât require the odd violent gale to get sand moving, just every day wind speeds common throughout the globe. With that data set in hand, the researchers then turned to simulations. Using a tool called ReSCAL, they grew virtual dunes from flat sand beds using different wind speeds. As part of that process, the team confirmed that, as long as a world has plenty of loosely bound grains of sand and winds that grow rarer as they grow stronger, dunes will largely be formed by the wind speeds that correspond directly to the grain sizes that compose them.

Despite the challenges of finding individual sand grain size from orbit, this technique is useful on more than just Earth. Curiosity has already drilled into the Stimson formation, a layer of sandstone that used to be a windblown dune billions of years ago. Combining the grain size measurements from these missions with the math laid out in the new paper will provide scientists with a rough indication of the history of wind speed in the area. There will also undoubtedly be other missions to other dune-ridden planets - Venus comes to mind as a potential target. And from now on, scientists will be able to read a major part of a worldâs geological history directly written into its sand.
