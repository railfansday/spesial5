The Next Great Infrastructure Reset

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Rajjie Sarmey is Founder and Chief Visionary Officer of FutureProof CXO, advancing governed AI, enterprise strategy and durable value.

The next great enterprise infrastructure reset may not begin inside a data center. It could begin beneath an ocean, at an electrical substation, along a telecommunications corridor or within an orbital network. Its consequences could cascade all four domains.

For decades, enterprise technology strategy centered around computing capacity, consolidation, connectivity, virtualization and cloud migration. We optimized utilization, negotiated consumption economics, reduced technical debt and pursued geographic scale and redundancy. Those disciplines remain essential today but are now insufficient.

As we navigate the remainder of 2026 and prepare for 2030, the strategic question shifts from where enterprises compute most efficiently to where they can operate securely, safely, reliably, lawfully and economically when interconnected infrastructure assumptions fail.

I define this emerging discipline as Infrastructure-Consequence Economics—a fundamental shift in how boards, CXOs, investors and architects evaluate digital infrastructure.

Three documented indicators reveal this transition.

1. Electricity is becoming a strategic computing constraint. The International Energy Agency’s 2025 “Energy and AI” report projected global data center electricity consumption rising from approximately 415 TWh in 2024 to 945 TWh by 2030 under its base-case scenario, acknowledging significant uncertainty in the future.​

2. Infrastructure is entering an extraordinary capital cycle. McKinsey estimated in April 2025 that data centers would require approximately $6.7 trillion in cumulative worldwide capital by 2030, encompassing AI and conventional computing workloads.​

3. Digital globalization depends on concentrated physical connectivity. A 2025 International Telecommunication Union report highlighted that submarine cables carried more than 99% of international data flows.​

These projections aren’t guarantees, but they signal increasing interdependencies. Converging capital intensity, energy availability, physical connectivity and geopolitical risk are now reshaping enterprise computing economics.

Submarine cables connect geographies, financial markets, cloud environments and global commerce. A multiregional cloud architecture may still depend on shared cable corridors, telecom providers and/or landing stations. Geographic separation doesn’t guarantee dependency independence.

AI demands dependable electricity, transmission capacity, cooling, water where applicable, network connectivity, physical security and suitable locations. Available compute without sufficient power delivery isn’t deployable capacity.

As we race toward 2030, infrastructure readiness and energy availability may influence workload placement as significantly as processor performance or cloud pricing.

Aviation communications, wireless networks and airborne platforms extend operations beyond terrestrial infrastructure. Across aviation, distributed industries, logistics and emergency response aspects, resilience demands managing spectrum, coverage, backhaul and continuity risks.

Mission-critical connectivity depends on coordinated terrestrial, airborne and satellite capabilities and not isolated networks or assumed independence.

Satellite communications and positioning, combined with navigation and timing services, strengthen operational continuity when terrestrial systems fail but introduce distinct risks that involves interference, cybersecurity, terminal dependencies, service concentration and regulatory constraints. Resilience architecture must extend into the physical and jurisdictional systems supporting them.

Decades of leading transformations across mission-critical regulated environments reinforces one principle: Redundancy isn’t resilience when independent systems share common failure points. Multiple cloud providers, facilities and backup ecosystems may share critical dependencies across identity, security, power, telecom and operations.

Resilience is measured not by provider count but by the ability to sustain or restore critical business services within defined risk and recovery tolerances. It mandates mapped dependencies, independent recovery paths, tested failovers, well-defined decision authority and verifiable recoverability. In high-risk environments, resilience must be demonstrated, not merely documented.

Traditionally, infrastructure economics focus on acquisition costs, capacity, consumption, utilization and operating efficiency. These measures remain necessary but rarely capture the full economic impact of infrastructure failure, prolonged disruption, geographic concentration or sovereign restrictions.

I propose evaluating infrastructure decisions through five interconnected dimensions:

1. Economic Sustainability: Full life cycle costs across capital, compute, energy, networking, cooling and operations.

2. Dependency Resilience: Exposure to correlated failures across facilities, providers, utilities and networks.

3. Jurisdictional Assurance: Data residency, contractual enforceability, regulatory obligations and operational control.

4. Recoverability: Demonstrated restoration capabilities, RTOs and RPOs, and service continuity.

5. Trusted Business Outcomes: Measurable value within defined security, reliability, safety and risk tolerances.

The governing principle is to optimize not for the lowest infrastructure unit price but for risk-adjusted cost per trusted business outcomes. Every material infrastructure investment should be evaluated against its life cycle economics, correlated failure risks, tested recoverability, jurisdictional obligations and measurable business consequences.

For boards, the question is whether executive leadership understands the infrastructure dependencies, capital exposures and failure scenarios that could compromise enterprise investments. For CXOs, architecture must connect business criticality, recovery engineering, workload positioning, cybersecurity and economic accountability.

For investors, infrastructure valuations demand scrutiny beyond announced compute capacity, meaning power availability, utilization, customer commitments, financing costs and monetization assumptions. For founders, the strategic optionality can outweigh capital-intensive infrastructure ownership.

Ownership doesn’t equal control. Multicloud doesn’t equal resilience. Sovereignty doesn’t equal security. Capability doesn’t equal returns. Competitive advantage requires mastering these distinctions.

By 2030, which enterprises will outperform?

1. Enterprises owning the most infrastructure?

2. Enterprises purchasing compute at the lowest price?

3. Enterprises architecting the freedom to operate across changing technological, economic and geopolitical situations?

My conviction favors the third scenario, provided freedom is measurable, tested and economically sustainable.

The next phase of infrastructure reset isn’t simply about where intelligence runs. It’s about whether enterprises retain their authority, resilience and economic capacity to act decisively when the operating conditions change.

Infrastructure will increasingly determine whether enterprise strategy creates sustainable business value. Will competitive advantage come from infrastructure ownership and lowest cost compute, or will it come from proven resilience, risk-adjusted enterprise economics and strategic freedom?​
