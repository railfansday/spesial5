Bread as a Sponge for Oil Is the Best Kitchen Hack I Learned All Year

Oily pans, stews and sauces are no match for the bread sponge trick.

If you want to degrease a pan or the top layer of an oily sauce without ruining a sponge, a single slice of bread could be your new favorite cleaning sidekick.

While we have vastly improved how we dispose of most things, excess cooking grease still presents a problem. Down the sink? Not advisable. Neither is the dishwasher—even the garbage bin can become a gnarly affair.

Getting the bulk of the grease out of a pan after cooking a batch of bacon or burgers is a good first step, but that greasy pan will either take down a sponge or half a roll of paper towels. What if a humble slice of past-its-prime sandwich bread was the all-natural solution for greasy pans you’ve been looking for?

It’s cheap and easy, and you can toss the used-up bread into a compost bin or countertop food waste processor without disrupting its yuck-free operation.

So, is it ever OK to pour excess grease or used cooking oil down the sink? Even if you run the hot water tap at the same time? Short answer: no. Long answer? Here’s an expert for the “no.” “When grease cools, it solidifies, coating the interior of the pipe,” says Jacylyn Basilone, chief operating officer of Brooks Plumbing. Even if you’re running hot water, the grease will eventually cool further down the line and cause future problems. “Over time, the solidified grease in the pipes can catch debris, thus making a partial clog grow larger and larger,” she says, reducing the pipe’s circumference little by little if you do this repeatedly.

Unlike a clogged shower drain, which you can usually clear easily, “a normal sewer machine or ‘snake’ can’t alleviate this type of stoppage,” says Basilone. “While grease solidifies, it can’t be captured by the claw head. Grease removal is best remediated with a hydrojetter, which tends to be more expensive than the traditional drain-cleaning services.”

Bread is the real answer, and I can’t believe I haven’t been doing this all along. (*Hangs head in shame, fully guilty of relying on the obviously very sketchy hot water method.*)

Bread has always been a superb grease-mopping tool; we usually think of it in a culinary context rather than for waste removal. Just as you’d drag a crusty baguette or hunk of ciabatta across the drippings on your plate, bread is an excellent sponge for all manner of oil, whether or not it is appealing to consume. Using bread to soak up excess oil essentially turns liquid food waste into a solid, making disposal much easier.

If it’s not obvious, this is also a great way to use bread that might otherwise go to waste: the ends of the loaf you can’t bring yourself to eat, or bread that’s gone stale or is starting to show the first mold spores. If you’re going to discard the bread anyway, at least put it to good use.

The bread trick comes in handy in almost endless ways. If there’s a pool of oil, it’s the mop for the job. I initially tried it after cooking bacon. A single, small slice of bread was enough to absorb what was easily four to six ounces of liquid grease left in the pan. I dragged the slice around the pan like a mop, but even after letting it sit a few extra moments, it kept working and eventually soaked up all the liquid grease. (If you’re feeling indulgent and used a fresh bread slice for this purpose, the temptation to make pretty epic croutons here is quite strong — another upside.)

If your air fryer has space between the drawer and the basket, you can place the bread there to soak up grease while cooking. Even for oily, slow-cooked proteins like pork shoulder, you can press the bread onto the meat to soak up grease before serving.

Once the bread has absorbed the oil and needs discarding, responsible food-waste methods are much easier to manage. Depending on how much oil the bread has absorbed, you can safely put the soaked bread in the compost bin or countertop composter without risking a major oil spill and a mess to clean up later. Oil can slow decomposition, so composting large amounts isn’t recommended. Small amounts, such as a few tablespoons at a time, are safe.

Even if you have no other option but to throw it away — and if that is the case, please consider getting a small composting appliance — at the very least, you’re not involving an easily recyclable glass container.
