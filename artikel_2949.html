Breakfast News: 3 Options Moves for a Jumpy Market

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

Selling puts, covered calls, and protective puts, explained in plain English.

Ten companies make up about 39% of the S&P 500, and most of them are spending heavily on artificial intelligence (AI). When investors started doubting whether those AI investments would pay off this fall, nearly every portfolio took a hit.

Options can help you ride out swings like that, but the jargon scares plenty of investors away. So today, we’ll walk you through three common options moves in plain English.

As JP Bennett, who leads Motley Fool Pro, once told a member, options "are just a vehicle" for good habits like keeping cash on hand and not letting one stock get too big.

An option is a contract covering 100 shares of a stock. There are two kinds. A call gives its buyer the right to buy those shares at a set price, called the strike, until a set date. A put gives its buyer the right to sell them at the strike price. Either way, the buyer pays the seller up front. That payment is the premium, and the seller keeps it no matter what.

Option prices usually rise when markets get jumpy, as they have this fall. That’s good news if you’re selling options and bad news if you’re buying them, which matters for the three moves below.

Say a hypothetical stock trades at $50, and you’d happily buy it at $40. You sell a put with a $40 strike that expires in a few months, and another investor pays you $150 for that promise. You set aside $4,000 in case you end up buying the shares.

If the stock stays above $40, you keep the $150. If it drops below $40, you buy 100 shares at $40, and the $150 brings your cost down to $38.50 a share.

If it keeps falling to $30, you still pay $40, so only sell puts on companies you’d be glad to own at that price.

Picture a hypothetical stock trading at $300 that now makes up 20% of your portfolio, well past your "sleep number." That’s TMF co-founder David Gardner’s term for the largest share you’d let one holding reach and still sleep soundly. You own 300 shares and would sell 100 of them at $330, so you sell one call with a $330 strike. Another investor pays you $900.

If the stock stays below $330, you keep your shares and the $900. If it rises above $330, those 100 shares are sold at $330, or effectively $339 a share, counting the payment.

You give up gains above $330, and the $900 won’t go far in a steep drop.

Say you own 100 shares of a hypothetical stock at $200 and worry it could fall hard. You buy a put with a $180 strike for $800, which gives you the right to sell your shares at $180 for about six months.

If the stock drops to $120, you can still sell at $180, so your loss is capped at $2,800 instead of $8,000. If the stock holds up, the put expires, and the $800 was the price of peace of mind.

At 4% of the position for six months, that protection is best saved for a specific worry.

Before you sell any option, ask whether you’d be happy if the other investor took you up on it. If not, skip the trade, no matter how good the premium looks.

Opening a trade is only part of the job. The decisions you make as expiration nears are where most of the skill in options lives.

Options involve risk and aren’t suitable for all investors. Hypothetical examples ignore commissions and taxes.
