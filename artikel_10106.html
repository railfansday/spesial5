When Everything Has Changed, What Do We Do Next?

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

It’s been a… year, huh? I think the most common emoji I’ve seen this year is “Face with Bags Under Eyes” (🫩) and that says a lot. One thing is for sure: the industry we make a living in is in flux, regardless of the outcomes of the AI bubble. Let’s take a look at how we can try and navigate that situation as best we can. But first…

Technology hyperscalers like Microsoft and Meta, along with AI startups like Open AI and Anthropic have transformed everything about tech and the job we do. This is true regardless of if they fail or somehow succeed in their quest to have everyone paying a subscription fee for their Large Language Model (LLM) technology, which is marketed to us as AI. I’ll refer to this technology as AI for the rest of this article for simplicity.

It’s not just tech jobs that have transformed either. Everywhere you go, you will see some slop, somewhere. Be it a flyer, website, a village poster or a food truck menu. Heck, people are even using LLMs to work out what’s special about their own children. The effects of this technology are far-reaching, way beyond our own industry.

The technology has hit us all in tech especially hard though. Mid-last year, I did some investigating because I suspected that so-called AI mandates were becoming increasingly popular and what I learned from conversations then feels like some sort of utopia compared to now. In the space between that piece being published and now, we went from token maxxing to companies realizing that actually, this technology is extremely expensive and not returning the promised outcomes.

The mandates have cooled, sure, but the demands to use AI to produce more volume, regardless of whether that volume is actually needed or not are still very much there. I’m in a fortunate position to have friendships with people all across the industry, from tech hyperscalers, to tiny little studios, and the stories I have heard from their work life are nothing short of horrifying. No one really seems to be happy with their work either.

This is such a long way away from even a handful of years ago where yes, there was a lot of problematic culture and outcomes from the tech industry, but people were generally quite happy with the work they were doing at least.

The sudden urge to sprint towards AI technologies over the last few years has had a devastating effect in so many places. The technology has encouraged suicide, devastated communities with the data center build-out and of course, displaced thousands of people’s careers. It’s worth noting that I’ve mentioned only a tiny sample of the harms there. If I listed them all, this article would be unbearably long and depressing.

Some harms I do want to touch on though are the effects on our communities in tech. The community spirit of open source and open knowledge was the foundation of the industry’s — especially the web industry’s — rapid success. At the root of everything there was people happy to share knowledge and build everyone up with them. Those people still do exist — myself included — but being one of those people is increasingly difficult.

As the numbers go up in the AI world, the numbers very much go down for independent educators, publishers and community conferences. There’s a developing and worrying culture of “I don’t need to skill up now because I can use AI for work”, so allow me to break down why that reductive view of your work and your career is harmful in the long term.

And don’t ever forget that! Let’s have a think for a second about what actually makes you a good developer, because typing characters in a text editor certainly isn’t it. What makes us good is experience, knowledge, nuance and above all else, creativity.

Look, I get it, AI really can feel like it’s a miracle machine, but it’s important to take a wider view. Along with the published studies on cognitive decline and the widening skills gap, it’s more important than ever to invest in your brain to keep your skills sharp because you’re not getting that repetitive reinforcement that writing code every day gives you anymore if AI tools do everything for you. There’s a reason that I often build most of my teaching around a linear build-along because the tangible nature of repeating patterns you’ve learned really helps that knowledge to sink in.

As always, this article is addressed to everyone, but I want to talk directly to founders (like me), decision makers, and managers for a second. A big part of our job, in fact, the most important part, is to ensure that our workers are looked after. I’m not just talking about compensation and perks here. I’m talking about our responsibility to ensure that our workers’ skills are kept sharp, their professional curiosities are satisfied and above all else that they are happy in the work they are doing. Our workers are much more than “number go up” vehicles.

You should be giving your developers more time to learn, rather than chasing endless volume. If AI enthusiasts are to be believed, there’s hours upon hours of time efficiencies created by this technology. Don’t fill that time with more volume. Instead, invest that time in developing your worker’s skills. It’s going to avoid burnout which yep, daily LLM use will likely cause in the long term.

One thing that is increasingly clear is that the health of the AI industry is not good. The bubble has already effectively burst in China and more worryingly for economy enjoyers, the “picks and shovels” — AKA the chips industry in Korea — has seen dramatic downturns. This is not how an “inevitable” outcome is supposed to happen!

The U.S. AI industry is on very shaky ground too. Circular financing, unbelievable levels of debt and a data center build out that simply cannot keep up with the required power levels to turn profits for hyperscalers and startups, mixed with a genuine hatred of the technology by the general public, creates enough risk in itself. Recent events, such as the Democrats dominating the midterms causing AI stocks to drop and as well as Nvidia’s stock because of its CEO being caught up in a spying scandal shine a light on the fragility of the industry. A healthy industry doesn’t work like this.

With all that in mind, what would be a really silly thing to do? Yeh, you guessed it: put AI at the heart of everything in your business. Sure, there’s open source models — which I think is the logical destination of the technology — but how many companies are going to be willing to maintain their infrastructure needs? Not many. The low barrier of entry of commercial AI technology is the selling point, let’s be honest.

Above everything, people need to be invested in, not pushed aside by technology like AI. As I mentioned earlier, it’s experience, knowledge, nuance, and creativity that sets humans far apart from AI. That technology does indeed keep evolving, but even the most ardent of AI supporters will concede that the rate of frontier model improvement is very much levelling out.

There’s never been a more important time to keep human skills sharp, so here’s some ideas on how to do that.

This is not just directed at individuals but also at founders, decision makers and managers. Our industry has been historically excellent at investing in skilling people up and we need to bring that back.

Give staff the time and budget to learn, to develop their skills and to engage with the community at conferences. You already know the positive outcomes that brings, so bring it back to your organization. At the absolute worst case, it’ll make the usage of AI in your organization more efficient and when the token subsidies inevitably end, more cost effective.

Again, token subsidies will inevitably end. So, getting thousands of dollars of worth from a $200 a month subscription will be something you’ll look fondly back on. When hallucinations from AI tools are suddenly costing thousands, does it make sense to force your staff to produce as much volume as possible? Of course not.

My advice is to re-evaluate those prior decisions and invest saved costs back into developing your workers instead, while there’s still a hope of them not burning out completely. Make sure you include your workers in these decisions and discuss the impacts of mandates on them. It’s important for you to understand the impact of these sort of decisions.

This advice is more aimed at developers, but stay paying attention, managers. As I said earlier, repetition (especially typing) reinforces new learning and existing knowledge. The slower pace also allows your brain to think, which from a quality of output perspective, is imperative. Doing none of that atrophies skills over time.

I recently read Jenn Schiffer’s great post. She did the Weird Web October challenge and wrote this:

[…] i encourage anyone who uses ai and/or vibe codes to consider doing an exercise like this without those tools as a way to challenge your brain and you can continue doing your regular work the way you do your regular work

I think that’s a very good idea, even if it’s messing around with a little side project — if you’ve got the energy from Producing Value all day, of course. Guess what bosses: this is another way you can make your staff happier, by giving them time to do this on the clock.

At the end of the day, you’ll miss the soul when it’s gone. It’s not gone yet though. Far from it. Independents like CSS-Tricks, my publication, Piccalilli, and Smashing Magazine are trying hard to keep the soul of our industry alive by sharing high quality educational content with the community.

Most of this content is free at the point of entry, but it certainly isn’t free to produce. We all pay writers fairly to share their knowledge with the community and we are all tiny organizations. We’re certainly not in this game to make huge profits, that’s for sure.

All of us could do more though, but funding is the ultimate barrier. For both companies and individuals, purchase courses, join the membership programs, and purchase ad space. With the right financial support, publications like ours can be a real force in pushing the good stuff back into our industry and bringing everyone up with us. That’s what we do it for.

It is a tough state of affairs, but if nothing else, at the end of the day we’ve got one of the best and most resilient communities around. Front-end is built on knowledge sharing, and as long as we hold each other up and strive for the future we want to see, we’re in it with a fighting chance. It has been hard, and it’ll continue to be hard, but the challenge is all the more reason to rise to the occasion and persevere.

I seem to be one of those ‘unique’ developers who has managed to avoid using AI entirely so far. I work for a conservation not-for-profit as the only IT guy (there’s only 5 of us in total). We regularly work with legally sensitive information (protected species records) and early on I had a long discussion with my manager about the dangers to information security of using AI, as well as the various ethical and environmental costs of the technology. My viewpoint is that AI is a tool and, like any other, the decision to use it needs to be made after carefully considering the pros and cons. At the moment, the cons seem to massively outweigh the pros and I see no sign of that changing in the near future; though I may well be wrong – I’m not an expert on machine learning after all.

Despite being ‘lucky’ in regards to management supporting my decision to abstain from AI use, I’m still not immune to the depression and burnout that this new technology has brought. I spend 50% more of my time patching websites than I did 2 years ago, and trying to find packages to use in projects is far more time consuming that it used to be due to all of the noise created by slop. Frankly, it’s sucked all the joy out of my work, and I’m just on the periphery.
