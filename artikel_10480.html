How to clock out psychologically—and leave the bad vibes at work

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

It’s hard to leave work stress where it belongs: at work. 

Stressful days spent navigating tense conversations with coworkers or clients, or staring down an endless to-do list or email queue, can cause rumination, anxiety, and even after-hours work. Your job anxiety can easily, and frequently, spill into your personal life.

To break the cycle, experts suggest creating a wind-down routine.

Understanding Emotional Residue and Rumination

Our brains are hardwired to replay unfinished business, which is why you can’t just turn off your thoughts after a tough day at work. Forcing yourself to stop thinking about a stressful situation usually backfires, and this can exacerbate your worry. 

“Trying not to think about it, generally does not work, as our brains will latch harder onto anything we’re trying to push out,” notes psychologist Meera Khan, clinical director at the mental health treatment company LifeStance Health in Bensalem, Pennsylvania.

Without a solid routine to wrap up your workday, spillover stress easily creeps into dinner time and ruins the rest of your night, according to the Journal of Occupational Health Psychology.

“When something upsetting happens at work, the stress doesn’t automatically disappear just because the workday is over,” explains neuropsychologist Sanam Hafeez, director of the neuropsychological assessment practice Comprehend the Mind in New York. “Your mind will oftentimes replay the moment over and over, trying to make sense of what happened,” she says. 

To break free from that mental loop, research published in the journal Acta Psychologica highlights a few simple strategies: First, acknowledge how you are feeling. Then, write it down or talk it through with someone. Finally, give yourself permission to step away and leave whatever is unfinished for tomorrow.

Simple Routines to Help You Unwind After Work

Having a noticeable routine at the end of the day signals your brain that work is over. Heading home used to give us a natural break, but working from home doesn’t give the same type of break. 

“Commuting is stressful, and it often means packed buses and standing in traffic,” points out Manuela Collis, assistant professor of organization and strategy at Carnegie Mellon University’s Heinz College in Pittsburgh. “But the commute also did something useful for us: It created a temporal and physical boundary between what happens at work and life outside of it,” she says.

If there’s no commute, create new ways to wind down on your next WFH day. Stroll outside for a few minutes, walk your dog, or listen to an audiobook or podcast.

And then, after you log off, instead of dwelling on work issues all night, set a firm cutoff point and shift your focus to your personal life. “I recommend taking a few minutes at the end of each workday to write down anything that’s bothering you—that way, you have a clear plan for what to tackle the next day,” suggests Hafeez, who has a PsyD degree in school-community psychology.

Another tactic is to create a buffer. Before signing off, take two minutes to write down any worrisome tasks or stressors so they don’t linger in your head all night; putting something on paper can help transition it out of your mind, at least for now. Here are a few other simple ways to bridge the gap between work and your personal time.

  • Change your outfit: Take off your work clothes and put on comfy clothing. 
  • Take a 10-minute walk: Unplug, step away from your screen, and get some fresh air. 
  • Don’t feel compelled to check email: This one can be hard, given our 24/7 availability and smartphone culture—but the messages will be there tomorrow. So step away for tonight. 

The science backs this up. Per a study published in Frontiers in Psychology, participants who use such “sensory transition rituals” reduce work-life conflict by nearly half compared with those who are constantly on their devices. 

And if work stress still manages to sneak back in, it could be helpful to give yourself a finite time to worry.

“If you find yourself replaying work stress after hours, ask yourself if the issue is something you can solve right now,” advises Khan, who has a PsyD degree in clinical psychology. “I recommend a simple five-to-10-minute rule: If you can address it in five to 10 minutes, go ahead and do it. If not, let it wait,” she says.

Setting Boundaries and Normalizing Recovery Culture

As you make the shifts necessary to preserve your health and mental wellness, don’t feel guilty about it. This is time to reclaim your evenings and set boundaries. And when you work remotely, these blurred lines can make it a wee more challenging—especially given our culture of workaholism—but not impossible. 

“Working from home can make it feel like you’re expected to be available all the time,” Hafeez says. “But being a good employee doesn’t mean you have to be available 24/7.” Physical boundaries matter just as much as mental ones. “If you have the space or privilege to set up a dedicated workspace, try to keep all work-related activities contained to that area,” Khan suggests.

Leadership plays a key role, too. Managers need to practice what they preach and show their teams that staying online around the clock isn’t a badge of honor. 

“To help teams truly disconnect, managers can avoid scheduling late-day meetings, queue emails to send during regular working hours, and be crystal clear about expectations—especially what isn’t expected,” Collis notes.

The Bottom Line

The bad vibes shouldn’t follow you home; it’s okay to want to have calm evenings. Everyone is telling us to be more mindful and to preserve our headspace, but it isn’t an easy proposition. Clocking out from your work isn’t just about closing your laptop; it’s about creating meaningful and impactful ways to disconnect from work and keep tomorrow out of tonight.
