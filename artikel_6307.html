Japan is encouraging people to use AI, but small businesses are falling behind

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

Japan is promoting AI to ease labor shortages, but a new review highlights a wide gap between experimentation and everyday business use.

When governments set out to govern artificial intelligence, most of them reach for rules. Japan reached for a law whose central word is promotion.

Passed in May 2025, Japan’s AI Promotion Act carries no penalties, no licensing and no registration requirements.

It exists because too few people in Japan were using AI.

Two researchers have reviewed Japan’s approach as AI use has grown sharply. In 2023, 9.1% of people in Japan said they had used generative AI. By 2025, the figure was 58.8%.

A large gap remained between large and small companies in putting AI to work.

The review comes from Hiromi M. Yokoyama of the Kavli Institute for the Physics and Mathematics of the Universe at the University of Tokyo and Shuo Wang of Tsinghua University.

“The instruments a government chooses reveal its assumptions about the nature of the problem,” Yokoyama said.

“The urgency of Japan’s governance problem comes not from the risks of AI being used, but from the economic and social cost of AI going unused.”

In the same 2023 government survey, 46.3% of people in the United States had used generative AI. In Germany the figure was 34.6%.

The shortfall came in a country short of workers. As of July 2025, 29.3% of Japan’s population was 65 or older. Farming, nursing, construction, retail and logistics all report labor shortages.

Japan’s industrial computer systems were aging too. The Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry warned of a “2025 Digital Cliff,” a large stock of custom-built legacy systems whose maintenance engineers were nearing retirement.

The law promotes AI as one way to ease Japan’s labor shortages.

In cross-national surveys run by the authors’ own team, Japanese respondents scored among the highest on objective tests of AI knowledge.

They ranked lowest in confidence about their own understanding.

A separate 47-country study found 28% of Japanese respondents willing to trust AI, below the global figure. Acceptance of AI was 60%, so the hesitation is about trust.

Yokoyama and Wang point to a Japanese idea called anshin-kan, an expectation that things will behave predictably.

A society that values predictability may hold back from a technology it can’t anticipate, so clear rules work as encouragement.

Elsewhere, people have been found to place too much trust in AI.

The AI Promotion Act creates no AI-specific duties that carry a fine. It asks users to make an effort to use AI properly, a duty recognized in Japanese law that carries no sanction.

The government can publish the names of companies that ignore its guidance.

Agencies steer private companies through non-binding recommendations, and a long record of cooperation between ministries and organized business lets voluntary guidelines act as real constraints.

The law’s main organizational tool is an AI Strategy Headquarters inside the Cabinet Office, set up on September 1, 2025, with the prime minister in charge.

Its job is to overcome the divisions between ministries that have slowed Japanese policy in other fields.

It commits the government to revising its AI plan every year, and that schedule held even as Japan changed prime ministers in 2026.

The EU AI Act sets penalties of up to 7% of a company’s global turnover. A 2023 Cabinet Office paper judged that burden too heavy for firms already struggling with digital change.

Personal use of generative AI, the tools that write text and generate images, posted the largest rise among the four countries surveyed.

Among companies, 86.4% of Japanese firms reported trying generative AI in at least one business function, close to the United States at 90.9%.

Trying is not the same as using. In an Information-technology Promotion Agency survey of 1,799 firms, companies with more than 1,000 employees had deployed AI in their operations at a rate of around 80%.

Among firms with 101 employees or fewer, the figure was 16.6%.

The deployment gap by company size runs to more than 60 percentage points.

“Adoption is not a single quantity,” Wang said. “Trial and organizational integration respond to different instruments. Promotional instruments lower the cost of trying.”

“They do not supply the capital, technical personnel or managerial authority that system replacement and process redesign require – and these are precisely what smaller firms lack,” he added.

The government appears to have read the data the same way. Its second AI Basic Plan, adopted on July 14, 2026, sets its goal at changing how decisions are made and how work is organized.

Whether humans and AI work better together is now a question for every small firm in Japan.

A year and a half is a short record, Yokoyama and Wang wrote. Some of Japan’s climb may reflect AI spreading everywhere at once.

The researchers identify three areas where Japan’s approach remains untested. First, voluntary compliance may prove less effective against rare but severe events.

These could include a major data breach, an AI failure in critical infrastructure, or AI-generated disinformation spread during a crisis.

The alignment between government and business has not been tested on hard questions like liability, data sharing or job losses.

And the AI Strategy Headquarters has produced two plans on schedule but has not yet had to settle a conflict between ministries.

“A governance model may succeed on its own terms while the underlying problem persists in a form its instruments cannot readily reach,” Yokoyama said.

“Japan’s case shows that where adoption is low, the governance problem has a different structure from the innovation-versus-risk balance that dominates comparative debate, and it calls for different instruments.”

Whether small Japanese companies can close the deployment gap remains an open question.

Future plans and surveys may help reveal whether efforts to reorganize work can address barriers that promotion alone has not overcome.

The full study was published in the journal Risk Sciences.
