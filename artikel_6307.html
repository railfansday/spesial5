In 1621, a letter described the first Thanksgiving: 90 men, three days, five deer, but not a word about turkey

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

During what is know as the "first Thanksgiving," four colonists went fowling and killed enough birds for almost a week. Records show wild turkeys were plentiful that fall.

The best-known description of the harvest celebration that Americans now call the first Thanksgiving comes from a few lines in one letter. It was written at Plymouth on December 11, 1621, and signed only “E. W.”

The writer was Edward Winslow, one of the English colonists at Plymouth, according to the historian who edited his letter in 1865. He was writing to a friend who was planning to join the colony, and the celebration takes up just a handful of sentences.

Those sentences mention a hunt for birds, a visit by Massasoit and “some ninetie men,” three days of feasting and five deer. They do not mention turkey, and they do not use the word “thanksgiving.”

Winslow started with the farm report. The colonists had planted “some twentie Acres of Indian Corne” in the spring and sowed “some six Acres of Barly & Pease.”

Following “the manner of the Indians,” he wrote, they fertilized the ground with fish, which were plentiful and easy to catch “at our doores.”

The corn, a crop that Native farmers in the Americas had grown for thousands of years and that was domesticated more than once in different regions, did well.

The barley was “indifferent good.” The peas were “not worth the gathering,” because the sun had parched them in the blossom.

By then, the colonists had built seven dwelling houses and four buildings for the use of the settlement.

“[O]ur harvest being gotten in, our Governour sent foure men on fowling, that so we might after a more speciall manner reioyce together,” Winslow wrote, in the spelling of the time.

Those four men, he wrote, “in one day killed as much fowle, as with a little helpe beside, served the Company almost a weeke.”

During that time, the colonists “exercised our Armes,” and many Native people came to the settlement. Among them was Massasoit, whom Winslow called “their greatest King,” with “some ninetie men, whom for three dayes we entertained and feasted.”

Massasoit’s men also went hunting. “[T]hey went out and killed fiue Deere,” Winslow wrote, “which they brought to the Plantation and bestowed on our Governour, and vpon the Captaine, and others.”

Winslow wrote “fowle,” a general word for birds. He did not say which kinds the four men shot.

Turkey could have been among them. Governor William Bradford, writing his history of the colony years later, described that same fall.

“And now begane to come in store of foule, as winter aproached,” Bradford wrote. “And besids water foule, ther was great store of wild Turkies, of which they tooke many, besids venison, &c.”

So turkeys were plentiful around Plymouth that autumn, and the colonists hunted them. But Winslow’s account of the celebration never names them.

Winslow’s letter was published in London in 1622, in a short book about the Plymouth colony now known as Mourt’s Relation.

The letter also spoke to people thinking of making the voyage. “[A]lthough it be not alwayes so plentifull, as it was at this time with vs,” Winslow wrote, “yet by the goodnesse of God, we are so farre from want, that we often wish you partakers of our plentie.”

He ended with practical advice for his friend’s journey. Among other things, he told him to bring paper and linseed oil for the windows, and he advised, “let your shott be most for bigge Fowles.”

Bradford later wrote that the plenty of that fall “made many afterwards write so largly of their plenty hear to their freinds in England, which were not fained, but true reports.”

Winslow’s passage about the celebration does not call it a thanksgiving or present it as the start of a tradition.

Paintings like the one by Jean Leon Gerome Ferris, made around 1912 to 1915, show tables, costumes and feathered headdresses that appear nowhere in Winslow’s few lines.

What the letter does preserve is simpler: a good corn harvest, a bird hunt, a three-day visit, and five deer brought in by Massasoit’s men. Turkeys have a much longer history in North America than that meal. Scientists have found that turkeys were domesticated at least 1,500 years ago.

The information was obtained from Edward Winslow’s letter of December 11, 1621, published in A Relation or Iournall of the beginning and proceedings of the English Plantation setled at Plimoth in New England (London, 1622; Boston Public Library copy), and from William Bradford’s History “Of Plimoth Plantation”, printed by order of the General Court of Massachusetts in 1899.
