From backyard chicks to hedgehogs: 10 animals that can pass on salmonella

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Pets that can carry salmonella include backyard chickens, turtles, bearded dragons, hedgehogs and dogs, and this guide explains how the germs spread and how to lower the risk

A pet can look perfectly healthy and still carry salmonella. Chicks, turtles, lizards, hamsters and even the family dog can harbor the bacteria in their intestines without showing any sign of illness. That makes appearance a poor guide to risk. The germs leave an animal in its droppings. From there, they spread to its fur, feathers or scales, and to its bedding, water, food and enclosure. People pick them up by touching the animal or anything it has touched, then touching their own mouths.

Food is the route most people associate with salmonella. Animal contact is a second, less obvious one. Salmonella is a group of bacteria, and the same germs that contaminate food can live in an animal's gut. Pet food can also bring the bacteria into a home, even before an animal touches it.

The illness usually brings diarrhea, fever and stomach cramps. Symptoms typically start between six hours and six days after exposure. Most healthy people recover in about four to seven days without treatment. Some people are more likely to become seriously ill, including young children, older adults and anyone with a weakened immune system.

Much of the exposure comes down to where an animal lives and what it touches. A backyard flock spreads germs through its coop and the shoes of whoever tends it. A turtle or frog sheds them into tank water. A hamster or guinea pig leaves them in its bedding. A dog or cat can pass them along through its food bowl.

This guide covers pets that can carry salmonella in 10 common categories, from chicks and ducklings to bearded dragons, snakes, hedgehogs, dogs and cats. It is a selection of familiar household and backyard animals rather than a complete list or a ranking. Each slide explains how the bacteria move from that particular animal to people and what precautions fit its habitat. Knowing where the germs collect makes it easier to keep them away from hands, kitchens and the people most vulnerable to them.

Backyard chickens and chicks can carry salmonella bacteria in their intestines while looking clean and healthy. A bird that seems well can still pass the germs to people who handle it or spend time around its living space.

The bacteria leave a bird's body in its droppings. From there, they reach the places and things the flock touches every day. Feathers, feet and beaks can all pick up contamination. So can the coop floor, nesting boxes, cages, feeders and water containers.

Salmonella generally does not make chickens visibly sick. An owner cannot sort safe birds from risky ones by looking at them. Every bird in a flock is best treated as a possible carrier.

Eggs can carry the germs too. Shells can become contaminated when hens lay in soiled nesting material. Salmonella can also be present inside an egg before the shell forms. Collecting eggs often, throwing away cracked ones, refrigerating the rest and cooking them until yolks and whites are firm lowers the chance of illness.

The germs travel on people as well as birds. Shoes worn in a run or coop carry droppings and bedding back toward the house. Clothing brushed against birds or dusty coop walls does the same. A separate pair of boots that stays outside the house limits how far contamination spreads.

The CDC advises keeping live poultry outside the home. That guidance covers young chicks, which are often raised indoors under a heat lamp during their first weeks. Housing a brooder in an outbuilding instead keeps feathers, dust and droppings away from living spaces.

Cleaning supplies belong outdoors too. Feeders, waterers and coop tools are safest scrubbed outside, away from household surfaces. Washing hands with soap and water right after collecting eggs, refilling feed or mucking out the coop closes a direct route from the flock to the mouth.

Ducklings, goslings and turkey poults can carry salmonella in their intestines while looking healthy. Holding, cuddling and kissing these young birds brings the bacteria close to a person's hands and mouth.

Young ducks, geese and turkeys all count as live poultry. They carry the same salmonella risk as baby chickens, and the same precautions apply to them.

Their soft down and small size invite close handling. A bird cupped in the hands or held against a cheek sits within inches of a person's face. Its feathers, feet and vent come along with it.

The CDC advises people not to kiss or snuggle poultry. That guidance covers lifting a bird to the face or holding it against the chest. A young bird can look clean and alert during that contact and still pass the germs to the person holding it.

Ducklings and goslings add another factor: water. They dabble in their drinking water and splash it into the surrounding bedding. The result is damp bedding mixed with droppings, which clings to hands that reach in to tidy it.

The CDC also says children younger than five should not handle poultry. That advice applies to ducklings, goslings and turkey poults as well as chicks. In a household with a toddler, an older child or adult takes on the holding and the brooder chores.

Washing hands with soap and water lowers the risk after contact with these birds. Wash right after holding a duckling, gosling or poult. Wash again after touching its brooder, bedding, feeder or water dish, including after refilling water or changing wet litter.

Pet turtles and tortoises often carry salmonella in their digestive tracts, even when they look healthy. Since 1975, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration has banned U.S. sales of turtles with shells under four inches long, and the rule is still in force.

The size cutoff targets a specific problem. Small turtles fit easily into a child's hand. Young children are also more likely to put their fingers, or the animal itself, into their mouths. A turtle longer than four inches is harder to treat as a handheld toy.

The FDA rule covers selling and distributing small turtles as pets. It allows exceptions for scientific, educational and exhibition purposes. The rule does not mean larger turtles are free of the germs. A turtle of any size can carry salmonella.

Tortoises are land-dwelling turtles, and they are reptiles too. They can carry salmonella in the gut and pass it in their droppings. The substrate, hides and food dishes in a tortoise enclosure can become contaminated as a result.

Aquatic turtles add a second pathway: tank water. These turtles swim, eat and defecate in the same water. Germs can build up in that water and on the filter, gravel and decorations. Splashes during a water change can carry them onto counters, floors and clothing.

Tank water should never be poured down a kitchen sink. Tanks, filters and accessories should not be scrubbed there either. A sink used for dishes or produce puts the germs right next to food. Cleaning the tank outdoors keeps the mess out of the house. If a bathtub is the only option, it needs a thorough cleaning and disinfecting once the job is done.

Hands need soap and water at the end of every tank change. The same goes for after feeding and after lifting a turtle or tortoise out for a soak or a shell check. A turtle allowed to wander across the floor can leave germs behind. Keeping it in its enclosure limits the area that needs cleaning.

Buyers can check shell length before bringing a turtle home. A seller offering turtles under four inches as pets is breaking the federal rule.

A bearded dragon can carry salmonella in its gut and pass it in its droppings while appearing alert, eating well and showing no sign of illness.

Bearded dragons are lizards native to Australia. The species most often kept as a pet is the central bearded dragon, Pogona vitticeps, which comes from the country's arid interior. Its calm temperament and tolerance of handling have made it a common pet lizard in U.S. homes.

That same tolerance shapes how the germs move. Owners often hold a bearded dragon on a lap or shoulder, let it rest on furniture, or feed it greens and insects by hand. Each of those contacts involves skin, claws or a mouth that may have touched droppings.

The enclosure is the second main route. Bacteria shed in feces can spread across the substrate, rocks, branches, hides and the food and water dishes. Basking platforms the lizard climbs on and rests against belong to the same contaminated space.

The risk does not stay inside the tank. Dishes, decor pulled out for cleaning and the lizard itself can carry bacteria onto floors, rugs and countertops. A bearded dragon allowed to roam a room can leave germs on every surface it crosses.

Bathing is a common part of bearded dragon care. Owners often soak the lizard in shallow warm water, and a household bathtub used for that purpose becomes another contact surface. Cleaning and disinfecting the tub afterward, or using a dedicated plastic basin, keeps the soak separate from human bathing.

Live feeder insects add another route. Crickets and roaches released into the tank share the enclosure with the animal's waste. Their containers and any uneaten greens should be treated as part of that same space.

Washing hands with soap and water belongs at the end of every session with a bearded dragon. That means after holding it, after feeding it, after rearranging its enclosure and after removing waste.

Pet snakes can carry salmonella, and the risk comes from two places: handling the animal and cleaning its enclosure. All reptiles can carry the bacteria, according to the CDC. That includes common pet species such as corn snakes and ball pythons.

In reptiles, salmonella lives in the gut and leaves the body in droppings. Inside an enclosure, the germs can then reach the snake's skin, its water bowl, its substrate and the hides and branches it moves across.

Handling is the first route. Snakes are often held for long stretches, draped over hands, arms or shoulders. That brings the animal's skin into contact with sleeves, collars and bare arms, not only fingers. Washing hands with soap and water after returning the snake to its enclosure keeps the germs from reaching the mouth, a phone or a doorknob.

Enclosure cleaning is the second route. It carries risk even when the snake is never touched. Rearranging a hide, scooping out soiled substrate or refilling a water dish all mean contact with surfaces the animal has used.

The water bowl needs particular care. Many snakes soak in their bowls, and some defecate in them. That puts droppings directly into water the keeper later handles. Keeping the bowl and spot-cleaning tools separate from household dishes and cleaning supplies limits where the germs can travel. Handwashing after each cleaning session closes off this route.

Feeding adds a third point of contact. Many pet snakes eat whole rodents, often bought frozen and thawed before feeding, though some keepers offer live prey. Rodents can carry salmonella too. That means the bacteria can enter the home with the food itself.

Feeding tongs keep hands off the prey item. An uneaten thawed rodent goes in the trash rather than back in the freezer. Washing hands after thawing and offering a rodent covers the contact the tongs miss.

Leopard geckos and other pet lizards can carry salmonella on their skin and on the surfaces inside their enclosures. The risk matters most in homes with children younger than five, adults 65 and older, or anyone with a weakened immune system. Those groups are more likely to develop serious illness.

The leopard gecko, Eublepharis macularius, is a small ground-dwelling lizard. It is native to dry, rocky regions of Afghanistan, Pakistan, northwest India and Iran. In captivity it lives in a glass or plastic terrarium and eats live insects such as crickets and mealworms. Unlike most geckos, it has movable eyelids and cannot climb smooth glass.

None of those traits changes its germ risk. The CDC says all reptiles can carry salmonella. That applies to crested geckos, green anoles, blue-tongued skinks and green iguanas as much as to leopard geckos. A lizard that looks alert and eats well can still have the bacteria on its body.

Young children face a higher risk for two reasons. Their immune systems are still developing. That makes a serious infection more likely. They also tend to put their hands, toys and other objects in their mouths. That gives germs picked up from a lizard's skin a direct route inside.

The enclosure is a second source of exposure. Substrate, hides, water dishes and decorations can all hold germs from droppings. A child who reaches into a terrarium to touch a basking rock can pick up bacteria without ever holding the animal.

Adults 65 and older face the same exposures with a greater chance of severe illness. So do people whose immune systems are weakened by chemotherapy, HIV or drugs taken after an organ transplant. In these groups, salmonella is more likely to spread beyond the gut and into the bloodstream.

In a household with any of these higher-risk members, other adults can take on the handling and the tank maintenance. Scrubbing hands with soap and water right after touching the lizard or reaching into its tank removes germs before they reach a face, a snack or a toddler. Keeping the lizard off couches, beds and children's play areas limits how far the bacteria travel through the home.

Pet frogs can carry salmonella, and the CDC says amphibians share this risk with reptiles. For fully aquatic species, the main exposure is the tank water itself, which picks up the bacteria the frog sheds.

Amphibians are the animal group that includes frogs, toads, salamanders and newts. Many pet amphibians spend part of their lives on land. Fully aquatic frogs, such as African dwarf frogs and African clawed frogs, spend their whole lives underwater. The axolotl, a salamander kept in home aquariums, also stays in the water for life. Everything they shed ends up in the water around them.

The bacteria live in an infected animal's gut and leave the body in droppings. In an aquarium, those droppings break down in the same water that fills the tank. From there, the germs reach the glass, the gravel, any decorations and the filter.

A person does not need to touch the frog to be exposed. Reaching into the tank to move a plant, net out debris or adjust a heater puts hands in direct contact with the water. Changing the water or rinsing a filter cartridge does the same.

Splashes carry the water farther. Drips from a net, a siphon hose or a bucket can land on floors, tables and nearby surfaces. Those spots can then pass the bacteria to anyone who touches them later.

Washing hands with soap and water belongs at the end of every tank task, not only after picking up the frog. That includes feeding, topping up the water and wiping down the glass. Wiping up spilled tank water and cleaning the surfaces it touched limits how far the germs travel.

Nets, buckets, siphons and scrubbing pads stay in regular contact with the tank water. Keeping those tools for tank use only stops them from carrying the bacteria into other household chores.

Hamsters, mice and rats kept as pets can carry salmonella, and the CDC has linked outbreaks of human illness to pet rodents. A rodent that looks healthy and clean can still pass the germs to people.

Inside a cage, contaminated droppings can reach almost every surface. Bedding, food dishes, water bottles, exercise wheels, tunnels and chew toys can all carry the germs.

That makes the enclosure a source of infection in its own right. A person can pick up salmonella while changing bedding or refilling a bowl without touching the hamster, mouse or rat at all.

Regular cage cleaning is the first of two main precautions for these pets. Removing soiled bedding and wiping down cage surfaces and accessories limits the buildup of contaminated material. Wheels and tunnels have seams and corners where droppings collect, so they need scrubbing rather than a quick wipe.

Disposable gloves keep droppings off the skin during the job. They should come off carefully, because a glove's dirty outer surface can brush the skin as it is pulled away.

The location of that cleaning matters too. Doing it outdoors, or in a utility area away from shared living spaces, keeps dirty bedding and rinse water from spreading germs through the home.

The second precaution is washing hands with soap and water right after holding the animal or touching anything from its cage. Pet rodents are often carried around and passed between family members. Each of those contacts gives germs a chance to move from fur and paws to hands. When a sink is not nearby, hand sanitizer can serve until a proper wash is possible.

Many families buy a hamster, mouse or rat for a child. Adults can supervise young owners during handling and check that they scrub their hands afterward. Holding a rodent against the face gives the germs a short path to the mouth. Kissing a pet rodent carries the same risk. So does snacking while handling one.

Pet hedgehogs and guinea pigs can carry salmonella without showing any sign of illness. The bacteria pass in their droppings and settle into their bedding, where they can reach anyone who handles the animal or its cage.

The two animals are often grouped together as small pets, but they are not close relatives. Guinea pigs are rodents that were domesticated in the Andes of South America. The hedgehogs sold as pets in the U.S. are usually African pygmy hedgehogs. They belong to a separate group of insect-eating mammals. What the two share is a living arrangement: a small enclosure lined with bedding where the animal walks, sleeps and leaves its droppings.

The CDC has linked salmonella outbreaks in people to pet hedgehogs. A hedgehog's spines and skin can pick up germs from its own bedding. Letting the animal roam across a couch, bed or carpet gives those germs more surfaces to reach.

Hedgehogs are nocturnal and do much of their running at night. Their exercise wheels and litter areas often collect droppings overnight. Scrubbing a soiled wheel is a direct point of contact with fresh waste. Washing hands with soap and water right after cleaning the wheel limits the chance of carrying bacteria back into the house.

Guinea pigs bring a different pattern. They graze on hay and fresh vegetables through the day and produce a large volume of droppings. Their bedding gets soiled quickly. Hay racks, hideouts and water bottles all sit inside the same space. Bagging soiled bedding and sealing it, rather than shaking it out indoors, keeps dust and droppings contained.

Guinea pigs are social animals and are commonly kept in pairs or small groups. More animals mean more bedding to change and more handling. Children often hold guinea pigs on their laps. Washing hands right after lap time, and after refilling the hay, cuts the chance that germs from the pet reach a child's mouth.

Dogs and cats can carry salmonella without showing any signs of illness. The food and treats they eat can also bring the germs into a home. Raw pet food and some chews, including pig ear treats tied to a 2019 outbreak, have been linked to human infections.

A healthy-looking dog or cat can pass salmonella in its feces. From there, the germs can end up on fur, paws and bedding, as well as in the yard or litter box. People can pick them up by petting the animal, scooping a litter box or bagging waste on a walk.

Raw diets are one way the bacteria get in. Raw meat-based pet foods can contain salmonella, much like raw meat sold for people. Freezing or freeze-drying the food does not reliably kill the bacteria. A dog or cat eating a raw diet can shed the germs in its feces while appearing well.

Preparing a raw diet creates exposure separate from contact with the animal. Thawing raw patties on a counter, portioning food by hand or wiping up spills with a dish towel can move bacteria onto surfaces used for human meals.

Treats can carry the bacteria too. In 2019, the CDC linked a multistate salmonella outbreak to pig ear dog treats. Chews like these are handled often. They pass back and forth between a person's hand and a dog's mouth. Each handoff gives the germs another chance to reach skin and nearby surfaces.

For dog and cat owners, handwashing is tied to feeding and cleanup. Wash hands with soap and water after feeding a pet, handing out treats, cleaning bowls or picking up waste. Bowls and scoops need a wash with soap and hot water. Use a dedicated scoop for pet food rather than a cup or spoon from the family's dishes.

Pet food and bowls belong away from where people cook and eat. Store pet food separately from human food. Keep dogs and cats off kitchen counters and other food-preparation areas. Feeding pets in a mudroom or laundry room, rather than the kitchen, keeps their meals apart from the family's.
