Former Philadelphia Museum CFO Claims Severance Was Withheld as ‘Retaliation’

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

The fallout from the contentious firing of former Philadelphia Museum of Art director Sasha Suda continues to reverberate through the institution, recent litigation suggests.

Former Philadelphia Museum of Art CFO Valarie McDuffie has accused museum director and CEO Daniel H. Weiss and board chair Ellen Caplan of withholding nearly $150,000 in severance out of “spite and retaliation.” But the museum has countered with allegations of its own, claiming that McDuffie breached her severance agreement by factory-resetting her museum-issued phone and potentially destroying communications relevant to Suda’s wrongful termination lawsuit. As first reported by Urgent Matter Press, McDuffie filed her complaint in May in Philadelphia’s Court of Common Pleas, which claims that the museum withheld the $144,691.60 severance promised her. She alleges that the museum used her purported failure to “cooperate” with its internal investigation into Suda’s firing as a pretext for withholding the payment. “What she refused to do — and was never required to do — was to submit to shifting, undefined, and extra-contractual demands manufactured to create her alleged breach of the terms of the severance agreement,” the complaint states.

It contends that Weiss and Caplan can be held personally liable under Pennsylvania’s Wage Payment and Collection Law because they controlled the decision to withhold her severance. She is seeking the unpaid pay, an additional 25 percent under the wage law, reimbursement for her COBRA health insurance premiums, and attorneys’ fees.

The museum filed its counterclaim in July. It alleges that McDuffie erased the contents and settings of her museum-issued phone around the time of her final day of active employment, February 12. According to the museum, she had received explicit instructions from the museum’s general counsel to preserve any records relevant to Suda’s lawsuit. The museum says McDuffie used the phone to communicate “on a regular basis” with Suda, and that some of their conversations concerned a central issue in Suda’s dismissal: expenses and a series of raises Suda had allegedly given herself without board approval. The museum claims that McDuffie executed the factory reset of the cellphone “without any excuse or justification.”

A representative for the museum said in a statement to ARTnews: “The Philadelphia Museum of Art continues to contest the meritless lawsuit filed by Valarie McDuffie. We do not comment on pending litigations. Our filings speak for themselves.”

According to its counterclaim, the museum’s general counsel sent McDuffie a directive to preserve record linked to Suda’s notice on November 21, 2025, and McDuffie acknowledged receiving it that same night.

Its counterclaim further alleges that McDuffie breached her severance agreement and violated her duties “as a trusted executive-level employee and an officer.” It also accuses her of violating the federal Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, arguing that resetting the phone “exceeded her authorized access” and caused the museum more than $5,000 in damages.

McDuffie says she was notified of her termination around January 16. According to her account, the museum subsequently sent two more notices: one informing her that she would receive a severance package, followed by another reiterating that records related to Suda’s litigation should be preserved.

She and the museum’s general counsel, Rebecca Wui, signed the agreement (dated January 16) on February 5. It listed March 27 as McDuffie’s final day of employment. The agreement reportedly promised a severance payout of $144,691.60 within 21 days after the agreement took effect, as well as her COBRA premiums through June 30, with caveats: McDuffie would agree to waive any legal claims against the museum and cooperate with its attorneys in “any investigation or litigation” related to her tenure. She also agreed to return all museum-issued property, including cellphones. The museum claims that it was not required to pay until she fulfilled those obligations.

The agreement also stipulated that McDuffie would be paid through March 27, regardless of whether she signed it. Her complaint adds that the museum withheld wages owed to her during administrative leave. McDuffie has put the total value of her unpaid administrative leave and severance at $189,546, a figure the museum contests in its counterclaim.

She also said the agreement included a letter of reference from Weiss and commitments from nine individuals not to disparage McDuffie: Caplan, Weiss, Osagie Imasogie, Tac Justi, Ira Brind, Orlando Esposito, Kemel Dawkins, Mark Rubenstein, and David Seltzer.

In its response, the museum said that the severance did not qualify as earned wages under state law because payment was contingent on McDuffie fulfilling her contractual obligations. It maintained that it could deduct any losses she caused from money owed to her.

McDuffie has denied all allegations of wrongdoing in regard to her museum-owned property. In August, she asked US District Judge Chad F. Kenney in August to dismiss the anti-hacking and officer-duty claims. According to the docket, Kenney denied the request on September 29.

In a statement to ARTnews, McDuffie’s attorney, Stephen Goldblum, said that “The Museum got everything it wanted. McDuffie stepped down, gave up her legal rights, and stood ready to honor her obligations. Then the Museum refused to pay.” The defendants, including Weiss and Caplan, he continued, “made a conscious, coordinated decision to withhold administrative leave pay and severance compensation that had already been earned.”

“To date, the Museum has provided no evidence whatsoever to support this false allegation,” he concluded.

The Museum got everything it wanted. McDuffie stepped down, gave up her legal rights, and stood ready to honor her obligations. Then the Museum refused to pay.

The board of the Philadelphia Art Museum abruptly dismissed Sasha Suda as director and CEO in November 2025 “for cause,” three years into her five-year contract. Suda sued the museum less than a week later, saying she was accused by board members of misusing funds for personal gain. Her lawsuit described the internal investigation into her by the museum as “a sham.” She separately indicated in an interview with Philadelphia Magazine that, in the time between her hiring and the start of her tenure, the museum board had attempted to strip her of the CEO portion of her title.

Less than a month after Suda’s termination, the museum hired Weiss, the former president and CEO of New York’s Metropolitan Museum of Art, as its new leader. The museum said that Weiss would “guide the art museum through at least 2028, providing stability for staff, stakeholders, and the community.” His tenure began that December.

Her firing followed a costly and unpopular rebranding of the institution that saw its name change from the Philadelphia Museum of Art to the Philadelphia Art Museum. Detractors swiftly deemed the rebranded institution as “PhArt” rather than the intended “PhAM,” while its new logo likewise prompted unflattering comparisons to sports club merchandising. Some board members spoke publicly of being caught unaware by the major rollout.

Following a unanimous board vote, the institution restored its name to the Philadelphia Museum of Art in February 2026.
