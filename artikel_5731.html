Was Superglue to Blame for His Lung Nodule?

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

— Benign nodule discovered during CT scan for abdominal pain

A CT scan turned up an incidental nodule in the lung of a patient being evaluated for kidney stones, and clinicians' best guess is that it had something to do with superglue.

The patient, a man in his 40s, presented to an emergency department in Belgium with severe abdominal pain, and a CT scan revealed kidney stones, Pauline Vermoesen, MD, of Ghent University in Belgium, wrote in BMJ Case Reports.

However, there was also an incidental finding of an air-filled pulmonary nodule, about 16 mm in diameter, they wrote. They followed the patient via CT scan for about 9 years.

The nodule turned out to be a pneumatocele, a gas-filled, thin-walled cavity more commonly seen in children, and typically found in the context of infection, trauma, or hydrocarbon aspiration. In this case, the patient did report that a few weeks before turning up at the ED with abdominal pain, he had "accidentally inhaled superglue."

"In the absence of infection or trauma, glue aspiration is considered the most likely cause, although without histopathological confirmation a definitive causal relationship cannot be established with certainty," Vermoesen and colleagues wrote.

The patient was a non-smoker, with no history of a respiratory infection or thoracic trauma. The nodule hadn't been there on a CT scan done 7 years prior to the kidney stone finding, the authors noted.

Positron emission tomography-CT scanning showed minimal fluorodeoxyglucose uptake, suggesting it was a benign lesion. Serial CT imaging over a total of 9 years showed, "progression from a solid nodule to a thin-walled cystic lesion consistent with a pneumatocele," they wrote.

The pathophysiology of pneumatocele likely involves parenchymal necrosis and a check-valve mechanism, Vermoesen and colleagues wrote, whereby partial bronchiolar obstruction leads to air trapping and distal cystic dilatation.

This rare case in an adult demonstrates the benign and uncomplicated evolution of a pneumatocele. In most cases, pneumatoceles regress and resolve spontaneously, they wrote.

"In our patient, however, the lesion persisted throughout the 9-year observation period, a pattern also observed in a case series of two pneumatoceles arising during sorafenib therapy, which likewise failed to fully resolve during follow-up," they wrote.

The aspirated glue was identified as a neoprene-based contact adhesive containing hydrocarbon solvents.

Serial imaging allowed differentiation from other cystic lung lesions such as bullae, blebs, or true cysts, "enabling us to forgo invasive diagnostic work-up and supporting the conservative management approach adopted in this patient, given the absence of complications," the authors concluded.
