TL;DR: Onramp has launched a business Bitcoin account with named users, role permissions and withdrawal approvals. A separate Treasury tier adds multi-institution custody. The distinction matters because team account controls and the underlying signing arrangement are different parts of security.

For a company buying Bitcoin, the first operational question arrives quickly: who is allowed to move it? A purchase approved by the board still needs a process for everyday access, record keeping and changes in personnel.

Onramp’s new offering addresses that administrative layer. Its significance is practical: making a corporate Bitcoin position something a finance team can govern through defined responsibilities, rather than treating account access as one person’s private task.

The company’s November 10 announcement says Onramp Business supports up to three named users, role-based permissions, withdrawal approval policies and an audit trail. The entry account has no monthly charge and a stated 0.85% trading fee, with assets held by a qualified custodian.

Those features are intended to separate initiating a payment from authorizing it. In a well-designed company process, the person preparing a transfer need not have unilateral authority to complete it. An audit trail can then help explain who requested and approved an action if it is later questioned.

That does not remove the need for an internal policy. A system can enforce permissions only after the business decides who should have them, which transfers require review and what happens when an employee leaves.

Onramp’s current product page lists its Treasury tier from $375 a month, with a 0.65% trading fee. It includes vaults in the company’s name and a structure requiring two of three institutions to move Bitcoin. The page labels custom approval rules as forthcoming.

The basic account should therefore not be described as already including every Treasury feature. Likewise, a planned feature should not appear in a list of capabilities customers can use today.

Separating those tiers also makes pricing easier to interpret. A monthly service charge and a trading fee pay for different things; the cost comparison depends on the amount held, transaction frequency and the control arrangements a company actually needs.

Bitcoin.org’s wallet-security guidance explains that multisignature arrangements can require several independent signatures before funds move. The general idea is to make compromise of one device or authority insufficient to authorize a transaction.

Operational approvals in an application and cryptographic signatures are not automatically the same safeguard. A finance team needs to understand both: what its own users can approve, and which parties ultimately control the keys needed to spend the assets.

NewsBTC has covered related approaches through Sygnum’s shared-key Bitcoin lending arrangement and AnchorWatch’s custody and insurance offering. Those services address different use cases, but each makes the allocation of control central to the product.

The less visible test is what happens when normal operations break down. A director may leave, an authorized employee may become unavailable, or a service provider may be unable to complete its part. A company considering custody needs a documented recovery process as well as a normal withdrawal process.

Extra checks can introduce friction, so a treasury also needs to distinguish assets reserved for long-term holding from assets it may need to move quickly. Strong controls are most useful when they fit the business’s actual payment and liquidity requirements.

Onramp’s launch offers another route for putting those responsibilities into a managed service. Whether it suits a particular company depends on the contractual terms, key structure, costs and operating requirements, rather than the existence of a Bitcoin account alone.
