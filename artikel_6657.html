The Rise, Fall, and Future of Makeup Artist-Led Brands

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

When news broke in October that Isamaya Ffrench had severed ties with her namesake beauty brand, fans of the avant-garde makeup artist were saddened but not surprised. For months, there had been growing customer frustration about unfulfilled orders and unresponsive support. Buyers reported severe shipping delays, missing or damaged items, and unanswered refund requests across the brand’s Instagram and review sites like Trustpilot. By July, its social media accounts had stopped creating new posts, while inventory markdowns on its website were as high as 70%.

Ffrench responded to a customer complaint on Instagram in October by apologizing, adding that she was no longer associated with the brand. Shortly after, she thanked her followers on her personal Instagram during what she called “an incredibly difficult and emotional time”. The Isamaya Beauty brand did not respond to a request for comment.

Though Isamaya Beauty remains operational, speculation about its future has been rife. It follows the bankruptcy of fellow makeup artist Pat McGrath’s eponymous label earlier in January. Pat McGrath Labs famously hit a $1 billion valuation in 2018, a mere three years after launch, but struggled to scale and keep pace with modern beauty demands. It exited the Chapter 11 bankruptcy process in April under new majority owner GDA Luma, a Miami-based investment firm. McGrath herself stepped down as CEO and transferred her equity to the holding company while staying on as chief creative officer.

While Ffrench and McGrath remain highly respected creative forces (McGrath is the creative director of cosmetics for La Beauté Louis Vuitton, while Ffrench currently runs the interdisciplinary concept store and gallery Studio Iron), the hurdles faced by their namesake labels highlight the structural friction between avant-garde artistry and commercial scaling. Their trajectories also signal a broader reckoning for makeup artist (MUA) brands, which have become a dominant yet deeply volatile segment of the beauty market, characterized by rapid boom-and-bust cycles.

The late 2010s marked an unprecedented period of rapid industry growth. Social media, viral launches, and Sephora shelf space helped drive hype for makeup artist-led brands, several of which subsequently enjoyed massive funding and bloated valuations. Many, however, struggled with post-pandemic relevance and debt. Today, the market has become saturated, and shoppers are increasingly fatigued by frequent drops and buzzwords. Consumer preferences are also evolving, with Gen Z opting for customizable, skin-first routines while millennials desire substantive education beyond surface-level trends.

“On the back of the success of brands like Charlotte Tilbury, which grew and ultimately sold to a strategic buyer, more people became interested in putting in capital behind the next generation [of MUAs],” says Ilya Seglin, managing director of consumer, retail and e-commerce at Cascadia Capital, citing Patrick Ta, Makeup by Mario, Danessa Myricks Beauty and Westman Atelier. However, many of these brands have not been acquired. Their founders have spoken publicly about choosing to stay independent and fund long-term growth, but at the same time, beauty strategic investors have pulled back on makeup deals.

Charlotte Tilbury at the opening of her Covent Garden, London flagship in 2025.

As Seglin explains, “Strategics have no idea how to get comfortable with color [cosmetics]”, which is increasingly viewed by investors as a “fashion” category — in other words, a volatile, trend-driven sector that is high risk but high reward. Both fashion and MUA-founded labels share the challenges of maintaining quarterly growth while mitigating trend fatigue and inventory risk. Beauty trend cycles, in particular, have accelerated with the advent of online algorithms and short-form content that create rapid microtrends that can rise, peak, and fade within weeks or months, rather than years. “The perceived risk profile of color brands has increased, so strategics have become more cautious. They don’t know if this is like a fashion brand that’s going to be around for 5-10 years, or whether it’s going to be around for longer. Strategics want to acquire brands that are going to be around forever.”

Despite the risks, makeup artist brands remain attractive to customers, accounting for roughly 30% of the prestige beauty market in the US, according to Larissa Jensen, senior vice president and global beauty industry advisor at Circana. “Makeup artist brands are the largest brand type within the makeup category. Collectively, they’re growing about 7%, which is outpacing total makeup growth,” she explains. “The units are actually performing, up 5%, compared to makeup overall, which was flat. That’s an indicator of demand.”

Still, MUAs have come a long way since early pioneers Bobbi Brown (1991), François Nars (1994) and Laura Mercier (1996) disrupted traditional department-store beauty, bringing their backstage and editorial expertise to everyday consumers via wearable neutrals and face base products. The subsequent rise of Instagram and YouTube transformed makeup artists like Charlotte Tilbury into global personalities. By the late 2010s, A-list celebrities and influencers (think Kylie Cosmetics by Kylie Jenner, Fenty Beauty by Rihanna and Rare Beauty by Selena Gomez) flooded the market. Meanwhile, contract manufacturing enabled independent, founder-led brands (like Glossier and Morphe) and beauty vloggers with massive, highly trusting audiences (such as Huda Kattan and Jaclyn Hill) to launch quickly and market directly to consumers. Built-in audiences and excited VCs temporarily overshadowed traditional artist-founded lines.

Bobbi Brown applying makeup on model backstage at Tory Burch SS07.

As consumers grow weary of celebrity white-label lines, credibility is shifting back to working makeup artists. Mary Phillips (M.ph Beauty) and Mario Dedivanovic (Makeup by Mario), for instance, regularly share behind-the-scenes details, signature techniques and product breakdowns from their red carpet prep with the likes of the Kardashian-Jenners. Both artists have long been part of the family’s glam squad and turned their routines into global beauty trends and brands.

Meanwhile, some modern founders didn’t come from traditional channels like celebrity glam or print magazines, but can demonstrate why their formulas deserve permanent space in one’s bag. Jamie Genevieve, for example, built an online following after working on makeup counters in Scotland. Her beauty brand Vieve celebrates its sixth anniversary this month. Sales have grown from £6.5 million in 2024 to £9.3 million by late 2026. Meanwhile, Jordana Ticia’s eponymous label has become one of the UK’s biggest indie beauty success stories in recent years, marked by a 239% increase in TikTok Shop GMV in 2024 (two years after joining the platform) and a May 2026 launch into Space NK where first-week sales beat forecasts, according to the Manchester-based founder.

Many of these modern MUA brands are actively pushing back against traditional “haul” culture by highlighting durability, multi-season versatility and cost-per-wear. Rather than lean heavily on novelty collaborations or viral packaging, the new generation is more focused on approachable, everyday beauty staples that can become high-repeat categories.

Violette, founded in 2021 by the Parisian makeup artist Violette Serrat, has leaned into French attitudes to beauty: to age with ease and feel comfortable in one’s own skin, and to treat skincare and fragrance like food or wine — something to savor and enjoy. Instead of seeking to mask or fix perceived flaws, Serrat — who has amassed over a million followers collectively across her personal Instagram and YouTube accounts — encourages natural-looking, lived-in looks. Her routines often show her applying products with her fingers rather than complex tools.

Violette is inspired by makeup artist Violette Serrat's love for fine art and French beauty philosophy.

Violette’s go-to market strategy was not to launch in Sephora or an equivalent retailer. Instead, the brand launched in locations like Oh My Cream, a “clean beauty” concept store, and French pharmacies, which in her view offered a real “stamp of approval” given their penchant for products that are simple, consistent and credible. Violette’s top sellers include the Bisou Balm (a lipstick-balm hybrid that creates a soft, blurred look) and Boum Boum Milk (a 3-in-1 skincare spray combining toner, serum and moisturizer).

Serrat’s belief is that while brands don’t have to speak to everyone, their products need to be universally understood. “I think it’s ok to be a little bit more niche. But my mission as a makeup artist is to simplify my technique,” explains Serrat, whose makeup has appeared editorially in American and French Vogue, and on the red carpet via figures such as Léa Seydoux and Natalia Vodianova. “If you look at my work, it’s very complex makeup. But for my brand, I want to make sure my products are understood on an instinctual level. You just put your finger in and do it.”

The British makeup artist Katie Jane Hughes, who counts a total of over two million followers across TikTok and Instagram, also bridges playful artistry with accessible, skin-first education. Rather than pushing rigid beauty rules or complicated tutorials, Hughes offers practical breakdowns and techniques that let natural skin texture show through. After years of working with high-profile clients such as Emmy Rossum, Kerry Washington, and Dua Lipa, among others, Hughes — who is based in New York — founded her beauty brand KJH in 2023, selling a tight lineup of accessibly priced items including the Precision Sculpture Stylo (a sculpting liner for the lips or eyes) and Microscara (a mascara with a petite brush built for better control). Sales have grown 250% year on year.

Core to KJH is its ability to reach a wide demographic of people. A poll conducted independently by KJH found that only 10% of the brand’s customers are makeup artists or fellow creatives. “Most of the people are not professionals. So I wanted to create products that allow them to feel like they’re more of an expert with what’s in their makeup bag,” says Hughes. As of August, the brand has expanded from DTC into Sephora in the US and Canada. Asked whether it’s a pivot in strategy, Hughes explains that cosmetics are deeply sensory products that rely on in-person testing and trust. “I like playing with a product in person before buying it. I think that’s really important.”

British makeup artist Katie Jane Hughes has won over a loyal audience for her approachable tutorials, signature glowing aesthetic and rule-breaking attitude toward beauty.

Built on technical skill and real-world experience, makeup artist-founded brands continue to stand out from celebrity or creator cash-grab lines. More are also expected to come to market, with the next highly anticipated launch to potentially come from celebrity makeup artist Nina Park, if early trademark filings are any indication. However, as Circana’s Jensen warns, “how many of [those MUA labels] really blow up and become huge or mainstays remains to be seen.”

Indeed, Violette, which is not yet profitable, raised $5 million from three existing investors in May, extending the Series B round that originally closed in 2024. It’s significantly smaller than the earlier sky-high multiples some MUA labels have previously commanded. Nonetheless, the recent funding indicates investor optimism and wider appetite for Violette, which Serrat is confident can reach profitability in the short term. The brand has entered new doors, including Sephora in the US as of 2025 and the UK this November. According to Serrat, sales at Sephora have doubled year over year, and the brand has since been “fast-tracked” to roll out in 800 doors, including in Canada, up from 80.

Taking on investment means toeing the line between creativity and commerciality. “If you’re niche, you cannot have investors,” she says, matter-of-factly. “You can do a super niche and creative brand, but you have to be self-funded. For me, raising money made me anxious. I needed to make sure I was able to have a very healthy business. You can create a brand that is inspiring, but at the same time, you need to think of the bottom line. It’s my obsession. I want to be profitable as soon as I can.”

It’s for these reasons KJH is primarily self-funded and has operated without traditional outside venture capital or institutional funding rounds since its launch. Hughes stressed the need to work at her own pace, despite ongoing interest from incubators and VCs alike. “I didn’t want to be tied to anybody. I didn’t want to feel like I had this big company above me telling me what I should do next,” she explains. “Now, we have more of a steady flow of sales, allowing us to have more launches throughout the year. We’ll probably release two to three products a year versus just one. But it’s because I want to do it. I want to do what’s right for me and my community.”

Hughes's beauty brand KJH focuses on multi-use formulas and practical makeup education.

However, experts also say that more than ever, consumers are looking to credible industry leaders and specialized experts to cut through the noise and AI-driven information overload. “For makeup artists, it’s really about bringing forth their expertise. There’s an authenticity that comes with their brands,” says Jensen. “Within the hair category, salon brands are the largest. Within skincare, it’s clinical brands founded by doctors or cosmetic chemists. What that collectively says about the consumer is that they are looking for expertise in their categories.”

Seglin adds that investor sentiment — like consumer trends — is cyclical. And the current funding constraints may, in fact, be beneficial for both the market and MUAs. “It forces them to actually be good. They will probably be better businesses than the brands that were flooded with investor cash before.” Those eager for a payout need to “be realistic about market dynamics”, he says. “If your expectation is to raise $10 million to launch a brand and in three years you’re going to sell to L’Oréal, I would temper those aspirations.”
