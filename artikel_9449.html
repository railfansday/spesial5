A Parachute for Your ADHD Medication Crash

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

Most people taking ADHD medication experience an afternoon crash. Here, ADDitude readers reveal effective strategies for a softer landing.

It’s 4 p.m. and your brain is suddenly caught in a riptide. You feel like a swimmer fighting a strong current. The focus, priorities, and momentum that have propelled you forward until now are submerged under a layer of brain fog that rolls in around the same time each day.

This phenomenon is called the ADHD medication crash, and it happens to two-thirds of ADDitude readers who take ADHD medication. When medication levels drop rapidly in the bloodstream, this can result in fatigue, slowed processing speed, and inertia.

According to 3,500 ADDitude survey responses, 57 to 78 percent of stimulant medication users experience a regular crash, with dextroamphetamine and Ritalin LA users reporting the highest prevalence. Even people taking the most popular stimulant medications reported crash prevalence rates of near or above 60 percent. The lowest crash prevalence rates were reported by people taking the non-stimulant medications Atomoxetine/Strattera (40%) and guanfacine (52.9%), suggesting that medication crashes are less common with non-stimulant ADHD treatments.

The odds of a medication crash increase when an individual is experiencing stress, inadequate sleep, poor diet, and lack of exercise, according to survey respondents. Almost two-thirds of women who experience crashes said their menstrual cycle impacts the crash symptom severity. Readers did not cite medication interactions or caffeine as significant contributors.

The ability to manage household responsibilities is “often” or “always” impaired by medication crashes for 70 percent of survey respondents. Half of crashers say work responsibilities “often” or “always” suffer, and 43 percent say social and family interactions suffer.
