How One Remote Reboot Cost Me Three Days of Work

I write software for a living, mostly small mobile games. Last week I was also setting up a Windows machine at a site I can only reach over the network. The site is a three-day trip away for me, so everything I do there happens through a remote session.

On Saturday morning I was in the middle of that setup, around ten o'clock. One of the checks I had written down was simple: reboot the machine and confirm that the app comes back up on its own. It is a reasonable test. Anything that is supposed to run unattended has to survive a restart.

The test went out. The machine went down and did not come back.

The Windows on that machine does not live on its internal disk. It boots from an external drive that I had attached earlier. Before the reboot, I had not asked a basic question: if the machine restarts, what makes it boot from that drive again? I assumed it would. I had not worked out how that boot would be restored after a restart, and I could not check it from where I was sitting.

So the reboot was a one-way door. If it worked, I would learn that the app survives a restart. If it did not, I would be locked out of a computer I could not touch.

I told my partner what had happened. The reply was short and angry, and it was fair: do not reboot; I am three days away from getting there.

The reboot test itself was cheap. Losing the ability to run anything else was the expensive part.

I treated "restart" as a harmless action because on my own desk it is. On my desk, if something goes wrong, I press a key during startup or plug in a monitor. On a remote machine, the same action has no undo.

The question I should have asked takes under a minute: if this goes wrong, what is the way back, and can I walk it from here? If the answer is "someone has to go there", the action does not belong in a routine checklist. It belongs in a separate, deliberate step, with a person on site or with a second way into the machine, like a network power switch or a remote management port.
