Can German auto manufacturing recover after Mercedes and VW cuts?

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

German automakers are pulling back on investments at home, sparking fresh anxiety about the auto sector's future. Some industry watchers think there is still a narrow window to stem the decline. But how?

Mercedes-Benz's announcement last week that it would halt further investments at its factories in Germany has added to the sense that the country's best automaking days are behind it.

In a written interview for staff, production chief Michael Schiebe noted how the running costs of German plants are far too high.

Schiebe added that the automaker is "now forced to carefully examine which German sites we would have to close if we cannot achieve a change in the cost structure."

The decision came one day after Volkswagen (VW), Europe's largest carmaker, said it was shelving plans to modernize its massive Wolfsburg plant.

Germany's auto sector is in a deep crisis, amid fierce rivalry from ambitious Chinese automakers. VW alone is looking to cut 100,000 jobs globally by 2030, while four of its German factories are earmarked for possible closure.

Yet, many auto analysts doubt this really marks the beginning of the end for car making in the country.

"We have to admit that a third of our capacity [in German plants] is overcapacity and that needs to be cut," Thomas Puls, senior economist for transport and infrastructure at the Cologne Institute for Economic Research (IW Köln), told DW. "This will hurt a lot."

A report last month by Munich-based consultancy Roland Bergeragreed that domestic automaking needs an urgent "reset," but predicted that Germany could still "preserve a sustainably profitable core of automotive value creation."

In other words, car making in Germany may shrink, but what remains could be leaner, more specialized and more profitable.

Many doomsayers overlook the fact that Mercedes' investment halt is tied to tough negotiations with unions over cutting labor costs at its German plants. This includes scrapping the 35-hour workweek. The investment freeze could be lifted if a deal is reached.

Even so, Achim Kampker, an electric mobility engineering professor at RWTH Aachen University, sees the investment cuts as more than a negotiating tactic.

Kampker compared China's lightning-fast industrial strategy with Germany's, which he said was "too slow," as excessive bureaucracy and complex business structures prevent value creation.

"We need to become faster, more innovative and significantly more productive," Kampker told DW.

China's EV-makers can bring a new model to market in around 20 months, versus 48 to 60 months for Germany, according to Roland Berger. Plus, the Chinese have a 30% cost advantage.

Production costs at German plants are also much higher — between €3,500 ($3,840) and €5,000 per vehicle — than in Eastern Europe, where they range from €2,000 to €2,500.

A separate report by consultancy PwC in September noted that despite German carmakers being well managed, well capitalized and knowing exactly what is coming, they may have a "false sense of security."

PwC said Germany needs to focus on four areas to ensure its auto industry is "future-fit" to compete with China.

These include splitting its internal combustion engine (ICE) business from the electric vehicle (EV) business, investing bumper ICE profits into those new technologies, redesigning workplace culture and training, and leveraging existing partnerships with Chinese automakers and US tech firms.

"The future viability of the German automotive industry depends significantly on decisions made in the next 12 to 18 months," wrote Harald Wimmer, global and German automotive leader at PwC Germany.

With the transition to EVs, some predict that automaking will become part of a much larger global electronics sector, which Germany must exploit.

Wimmer noted how top Chinese automakers were already benchmarking themselves against consumer electronics giants. These include smartphone maker Xiaomi, which now also produces around 400,000 EVs per year.

"Anyone who continues to base their competitive framework solely on their own industry is making a mistake that cannot be corrected once it becomes apparent," he warned.

Despite all the doom and gloom, car production within Germany is holding up pretty well.

Domestic manufacturing sits around 12% lower than pre-pandemic levels. This year, however, the auto sector is on course to meet a December estimate of 4.11 million vehicles, just 1% lower than last year, according to the German Association of the Automotive Industry (VDA).

IW Köln's Puls thinks German automakers must now avoid a trap Italy fell into in the early 2000s when its national auto champion, Fiat, struggled with profitability.

In Italy, "the politics and the unions blocked any attempt by Fiat to cut overcapacity," Puls told DW. "What's the result? They have a capacity of 1.1 to 1.2 million cars and utilization of about 250,000."

Last year, those same Italian plants built just 214,000 passenger cars, according to the FIM Cisl trade union, the fewest since 1954.

For Germany, the issue isn't a lack of expertise or ambition; it's that reforms to huge industries often get weighed down by caution and red tape.

As the country's auto giants try to push through painful cuts, RWTH Aachen University's Kampker warned that if they fail to address their shortcomings, "we will see more production sites closing and a substantial shift of industrial investment and employment away from Germany."

"There is still an opportunity to reverse this trend, but the window of opportunity is narrowing," Kampker concluded.
