Google declined to take down Russell Brands not guilty prison message. Now its investigating.

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

This month, on Nov. 15, six days after Brand was sentenced to 10 years and six months in prison for rape and sexual assault, Gallacher posted a video to his channel titled "The Good Work Continues" in which she read a message from him denying he had committed the crimes. The hour-long video drew more than 1 million views and prompted a takedown request from the Prison Service of England and Wales, which said the video could cause distress to Brand's victims.

"So Russell has asked me to read something to you today," she said. "Beloved brothers and sisters in Christ, God bless you for your support. As you know, I am not guilty for the offenses I have been jailed for, so something strange is happening."

"I don't know which other men here are not guilty, and the ones that are, they are people that Christ died to save, like you and me," he added, according to his wife.

Google, which owns YouTube, initially turned down the request. On Nov. 18, the BBC reported that Google had declined because the video hadn't technically broken YouTube's rules. But by Nov. 19, Google had told the BBC it was looking into the video. "We’re aware of the new content and our teams are investigating," a spokesperson said.

The company also pointed to its 2023 decision to suspend Brand's channel from the YouTube Partner Program, adding that the channel had been unable to earn money through YouTube since then. Google's statement gave no timeline for the review or details on what its teams were looking at, and the video was still up at time of publish (Nov. 21, 2 p.m. ET). There's another wrinkle to this story. The Ministry of Justice is investigating whether Brand broke prison rules by having his message made public. In England and Wales, prison rules generally restrict prisoners from discussing their own crimes in correspondence likely to be published or broadcast, with an exception for "serious representations about conviction or sentence."

Justice Secretary Alex Norris called the broadcast "completely unacceptable" and said he expected action if Brand was found to have broken the rules.

Prison service guidance says officials deciding whether to allow a broadcast should consider whether it could cause "serious distress" to victims, and The Guardian reported that the inquiry is weighing Brand's right to assert his innocence alongside questions about whether he sought permission and how soon the statement followed the verdict.

Mia Latter, who has publicly accused Brand of raping her at 16 and was not part of the trial, told the BBC that watching the video made her think about the women who testified. "I just again felt for those women that stood trial and everything they went through," she said.

British officials are still asking YouTube to take the video down. The Ministry of Justice still believes "YouTube should remove this content," but housing minister Matthew Pennycook told BBC Breakfast that the decision is "up to Google." Prime Minister Andy Burnham urged social media companies to "show some decency" and take the video down themselves, though saying he himself "wouldn't leap" to changing the law.

Labour MP and former safeguarding minister Jess Phillips, on the other hand, sees a case for a second Online Safety Act because, as she told BBC Radio 4, "we haven’t had prisoners who had massive YouTube followings." She cites how the current law requires platforms to tackle illegal content and protect children from harmful material; but, it doesn't specifically address what happens when someone outside prison shares a prisoner's message on social media.

Not everyone thinks the video should be taken down, though. Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch added that she would first look at whether a law had been broken before pressing a private platform to remove the video. U.S. broadcaster Megyn Kelly went further, calling the reported push to deactivate Brand's channel one of Britain's "draconian free speech crackdowns."

If the U.K. indeed writes a law in response to Brand's video, it’ll have to answer the more difficult question of how to decipher when a platform should step in to protect victims, or when there's a public interest in hearing what a prisoner has to say.
