Why I Am Hiking The AT and PCT Back-to-back

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

I remember exactly when I first met a thru-hiker. I was attending a summer camp in Maine and was on an overnight backpacking trip somewhere in the White Mountains of New Hampshire. The counselor, Jesse, led a small group of campers deemed experienced enough across the ridgeline of the Presidential Range. Under a late afternoon sun, we came to a water source where there was a hiker and a beagle, each with their own backpack. I asked the man for permission to pet his companion, which he obliged. While the dog and I were having a wonderful time making each other’s acquaintance, both of our caretakers filtered water. They began chatting and Jesse asked the hiker where he had started, to which he casually responded: “Georgia.”

Jesse nodded knowingly and they chatted about our group’s itinerary (which I have long since forgotten), but I was stuck on that one incredible word. In a lull in the conversation, I barged in and incredulously asked for clarification on whether this little beagle had truly walked from Georgia. The hiker grumbled a bit about some national park that didn’t allow dogs, but confirmed he otherwise had. He further explained that both he and his dog had gotten giardia in Massachusetts so had to take a week off, so they were still trying to catch up to the group they were hiking with. Later at a nearby campsite, Jesse shared some food (a meal replete with freshly-cooked fajita veggies) with the thru-hiker, who gleefully accepted it. This experience impressed upon me a deep sense for the wondrous possibility of trail — and the importance of filtering water.

Despite remembering when it was, I cannot be sure where I was when the world of thru-hiking opened up before me. In attempting to rediscover the exact spot of this encounter, I have wandered through hazy memories filled with the small things that stuck out to me: climbing directly up a scree field, the oddly-named Daniel Webster (Scout) Trail and the smell of fresh blueberry pancakes wafting from Mizpah Springs Hut. Based on these details, I believe it was at a stream at the junction of the Osgood Trail. This means the first mountain we climbed the next morning was likely Mount Madison.

It was on this summit (or perhaps another peak named after a long-dead president) where I began to comprehend the sheer scale of this footpath. I glanced at the white dashes along the rock and then south to Mount Washington. I imagined the dashes like the lines on a road, extending across the horizon and down as far south as the land. It was this exact moment when I first imagined walking the length of the AT, and every white blaze on the continent beckons me home to this mysterious summit.

Blue blazes lead us over Bearfence Mountain, Shenandoah National Park

Over the years, I learned of the other two great north-south trails in the US and both times I rapidly folded each into my vision for the future. After I did the Long Trail in 2022, I determined that I would get my triple crown by the time I was 30, preferably in one year. Since then, I have scrounged and schemed to be in a position to take a whole year to backpack. However, when it came time to actually map out my first real thru-hike, I kept running into reasons why doing all three in one year was suboptimal. I refuse to flip-flop, I refuse to stop taking extended lunches at beautiful overlooks, I have very limited experience backpacking on snowy peaks and I stand to gain several thousand dollars in tax savings if I worked for part of the year (more on that in a later blog post). As I crunched the numbers, two options emerged: 1) Attempt a calendar-year triple crown, rush the entire time and be scared of snowfall in the Colorado Rockies or 2) Do the AT NOBO and PCT SOBO, then spend the remaining two months of the year visiting my friends in Hawaiʻi. As someone who gets cold very easily, it was not hard to pick between Coloradan blizzards and Hawaiian beaches.

In order to make sure I get through the Sierra Nevada before the first week of October (the advice given by the PCTA), I have structured my thru-hike with a series of checkpoints. For the AT, I intend to start on the spring equinox, see sunrise on my birthday at McAfee Knob, get to Carlisle, PA in time to get down to AT Trail Days, summit Mount Washington on the summer solstice, celebrate July 4th in Monson, ME and meet friends in Bar Harbor. After a few days off, I intend to start the PCT and get to Cascades Lock, OR in time for PCT Trail Days, rip through Oregon to meet my brother at Lake Tahoe for the fall equinox, get to Kennedy Meadows South by Indigenous Peoples Day, creep into Idyllwild by Halloween and leisurely finish out the remainder of the trail.

Throughout the year, I plan to post on this blog at every town I get to (around every 5 days), so the reader can follow along to see just how much I fall short of these goals. In the meantime, I will be updating on my preparations monthly on the 15th. At time of writing, my soybeans are dehydrating, my finances are set, my resignation letter is submitted and my flights are booked. I am giddy with excitement — I have been looking forward to getting giardia for a decade.
