Paris Hilton, Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez press House to pass Defiance Act to fight AI deepfake pornography

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Artificial intelligence can turn an ordinary photo or video into a sexually explicit AI deepfake, with consequences that can follow victims into their classrooms and workplaces. Getting those images removed is one challenge. Holding the people who create or share them accountable is another. Policymakers, advocates and victims say additional federal legislation is needed, but a bill that would give victims new legal remedies has yet to receive a vote in the House after two years of efforts.

On Tuesday, Paris Hilton, one of the bill's most prominent advocates, was back on the steps of the U.S. Capitol alongside Defiance Act backers including Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez (D-NY) and Rep. Laurel Lee (R-FL) to press the U.S. House of Representatives to pass the bill into law. The U.S. Senate passed its most recent version of the measure by unanimous consent in January 2026.

The proposed bipartisan bill, the Defiance Act (Disrupt Explicit Forged Images And Non-Consensual Edits Act), would close gaps in an existing federal right to sue over sexually explicit deepfakes, creating the civil right to sue for victims of deepfake pornography. If passed, survivors could sue perpetrators who create, share, publish, solicit, or threaten to distribute this content without consent. Victims could also recover legal costs and seek court orders requiring the content to be removed.

"This work is very personal to me and it's just so terrifying now if AI gets into the wrong hands, what's possible," Hilton said during a new episode of the "CNBC Changemakers" podcast with Julia Boorstin.

When Hilton was 19, she was pressured into allowing an intimate recording featuring her and her then-boyfriend, Rick Salomon. She expected the recording to stay private and later found it was released without her consent.

AI has created an infinitely scalable platform for the online trauma she suffered.

"Somebody can literally just take an innocent photo and turn it into some explicit photo or video, and this is happening to millions of women and children all around the world, and they don't have anyone to protect them because the laws have just not caught up with this technology yet," Hilton said.

"There's girls who are teenagers in school. This is happening to them, and some of them are taking their own lives because it's just completely humiliated them, and destroyed their confidence ... being able to even walk into school," Hilton said.

Hilton is currently serving as chief advocacy officer at digital identity protection firm Loti AI, which helps people find unauthorized uses of their likeness or voice online and file takedown requests. She said the company has detected more than 100,000 explicit deepfakes depicting her and helped remove more than 60,000.

"I'm very fortunate that I have a team of lawyers. I have people helping me. But a lot of these young girls and women don't have that support," she said. "People need to know that there's going to be consequences for their actions," she added.

The Take It Down Act, which was signed by President Trump in May 2025, made certain disclosures of nonconsensual intimate images, including AI-generated deepfakes, a federal crime. Covered platforms must remove the material and any known identical copies within 48 hours of receiving a valid request.

Texas Sen. Ted Cruz introduced the bill in June 2024 after a case in Aledo, Texas, when an Instagram image of 14-year-old Elliston Berry was edited to make her appear nude and the altered image circulated on Snapchat. Berry told CNN that she woke up to calls and texts about the images. "It was out of my control. And they were spreading," she said. She also said it was difficult to tell her mother, even though she knew the images were fake.

As a mother, Hilton says she worries about what happens when these images circulate among classmates. "Even though the image is not real, the pain and suffering that comes along with it is very real," she said.

Before the Take It Down Act, state laws offered varying protections, and many did not explicitly cover synthetic images. That left victims with different options depending on where they lived, and, in some cases, reliant on platforms to act voluntarily. But Hilton says that current federal law sometimes "doesn't work," leaving victims in need of another way to hold perpetrators accountable. Other critics of the Take It Down Act's shortcomings have pointed to the reliance on federal prosecutors and lack of separate civil remedies for victims to pursue action.

The Sexual Violence Prevention Association, which helped author the Defiance Act, delivered letters to House Speaker Mike Johnson and House Majority Leader Steve Scalise on Tuesday, urging them to bring the bill to the House floor.

"I wrote the Defiance Act because survivors came to me with nowhere to go. Then, I was deepfaked by someone attempting to silence my advocacy for the Defiance Act. I was deepfaked while fighting for the very bill that would prevent it. Suddenly, I too had nowhere to go," said Omny Miranda Martone, founder and CEO of the SVPA, in a statement on Tuesday. "Now it's time for House leadership to say yes."

"The Defiance Act was designed to close those gaps. Once passed, it will help prevent digital sexual violence by giving survivors a way to hold perpetrators accountable and by ending the lack of consequences that emboldens them," Martone stated.

According to the SVPA, the Defiance Act has received 117 endorsements, including Microsoft and Snap, along with advocacy organizations such as National Organization for Women, Center for Democracy and Technology, and Center for AI Policy. In June 2026, OpenAI became the first AI company to endorse it, with Chris Lehane, the company's chief global affairs officer, calling it "smart legislation," and pushing for the House to pass it.

The Senate has passed versions of the bill twice, in 2024 and again in January 2026. Sens. Dick Durbin, the now-deceased Sen. Lindsey Graham (R-SC), Sen. Amy Klobuchar (D-Minn.) and Sen. Josh Hawley (R-MO) were among the original backers. Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez introduced the bipartisan House companion in May 2025, when it was referred to the House Judiciary Committee. It has never received a vote in the House.

In September, House Speaker Mike Johnson told reporters the bill was "good policy" but said the House had yet to build consensus around it. Some Republicans were reportedly reluctant to advance the measure this year because passage would hand Ocasio-Cortez a legislative win ahead of the midterm elections, according to a Politico report from July. Specific objections from House members have not been publicly shared.

The bill currently has 70 House cosponsors, evenly split between 35 Democrats and 35 Republicans.

If the House doesn't act before the current Congress ends, supporters would have to reintroduce the bill in the next Congress. Its path in January will depend on the new makeup of the House and whether its Democratic leaders make the measure a priority. The Democrats are projected to hold a majority in the new Congress, though its exact size is not yet known.
