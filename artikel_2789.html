'Genius' batted ball on kick return helps Seahawks burn clock, sparks officiating confusion in win over Raiders

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

A bizarre play that the CBS broadcast hailed as "genius" and drew a pat on the back from head coach Mike Macdonald helped the Seattle Seahawks run out the clock on their 21-13 win over the Las Vegas Raiders on Sunday.

It also sparked officiating confusion that remained unsettled postgame.

The play in question took place on a Las Vegas kickoff with 2:04 remaining and Seattle holding a 21-13 lead. Matt Gay's kick appeared destined to go out of the end zone for a touchback with no time taken off the clock that would have given Seattle the ball at the 35-yard line.

But Tory Horton had different ideas. The Seahawks return man leapt and batted the ball toward the field of play before it could sail out of bounds. He then instructed Rashid Shaheed to pick it up and return it. Shaheed obliged, and when he was tackled at the 19-yard line, the game clock had run down to 1:59, inside the 2-minute warning.

When the play was done, Macdonald greeted Horton with a smile, a high-five and a pat on the back.

Horton's decision to keep the ball in play cost Seattle 16 yards. More importantly, it eliminated the 2-minute warning from Seattle's ensuing offensive possession. It essentially robbed the Raiders of a valuable de facto timeout.

The Seahawks went on to get a first down and kneel out the clock on the win in victory formation. Even if they hadn't managed a first down, Las Vegas, which had two timeouts remaining, wouldn't have been able to get the ball back with any substantial time on the clock thanks to Horton's play. His decision and Shaheed's execution essentially eliminated 40 seconds of game clock.

After the game, Horton discussed his decision with reporters and what was discussed with coaches prior to to the kick.

"All he said was keep the ball in play, field every return, just don't let it go out," Horton said, apparently referring to special teams coach Jay Harbaugh. "Make sure we don't kneel it in the end zone."

From there, the situational awareness and decision-making was all on Horton.

"I took into consideration, I'm gonna try by any means keep this ball in play so we can get a return started. … I made sure I knew where I was on the field before I leaped up, made sure I was in bounds taking off.

"It's just the situational awareness of like knowing the distance and the time and the situation of the game of knowing, all right, this is a must return. .. I just wanted to make sure I made the smartest play for us."

The legality of the play wasn't immediately clear. Officials conferred at the play's conclusion and held a lengthy discussion before addressing the crowd.

CBS rules analyst Gene Steratore was left perplexed after watching the ordeal and didn't agree that a penalty had actually occurred.

"It sounds to me like Bill is saying that there was maybe a foul," Steratore said. "I don't think it's a foul. It's a kick that never hits the ground. I believe you can bat a kick in the air, provided it has never hit the ground yet."

Per the game's official play-by-play, the play was, indeed, ruled an "illegal bat." And the Raiders declined the penalty, since accepting it wouldn't have put time back on the clock.

Here's the definition of an illegal bat, per section 12-5-1 of the NFL rulebook:

any player bats or punches a loose ball in the field of play toward his opponent's goal line;

any player bats or punches a loose ball (that has touched the ground) in any direction, if it is in either end zone;

an offensive player bats a backward pass in flight toward his opponent's goal line

a forward pass that is controlled by a player prior to completing the catch is thrown forward (the ball remains alive if caught by a teammate or intercepted).

Exception: A forward pass in flight may be tipped, batted, or deflected in any direction by any eligible player at any time.

Is the ball in the "field of play" when it's sailing out of bound without having touched the ground on a kickoff? If so, the bat would appear to be illegal per subsection 1 above. If not, officials got it wrong.

Steratore appeared to have referenced subsection 2, which declares the bat of a ball "in either end zone" illegal when the ball "has touched the ground." The ball hadn't touched the ground, so this would not apply to Horton's play in determining that the bat was illegal.

The rulebook defines the "field of play" as "the area bounded by goal lines and sidelines." It goes on to define the end zones separately as "the areas bounded by goal lines, end lines, and sidelines." This appears to add up to Horton's play having legally taken place outside the "field of play" and officials getting it wrong.

Even if the Raiders had accepted the penalty, time wasn't going to be put back on the clock. The goal of pushing the clock under the 2-minute warning was accomplished, and it turned out to be a heads-up play by Horton that few if anybody watching Sunday had seen before — legal or not.
