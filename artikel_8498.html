Online Prices Can Be Set Just for You – Compare a Logged-Out View First

Online stores reprice items as demand, stock, and rivals’ prices change, and some also use your location or browsing history to set the price you see. Before you buy, check the item at another store and in a logged-out private window, then set a price alert instead of guessing when the price will drop.

From one screen, you can’t tell whether a price jumped for every shopper or just for you. Both happen, and the second has been documented for over a decade.

In 2012, The Wall Street Journal found Staples’ website showing one shopper a stapler for $15.79 and another, a few miles away, the same stapler for $14.29. The lower price usually went to shoppers the site judged to be within about 20 miles of an OfficeMax or Office Depot store. In January 2025, the Federal Trade Commission published first findings from its study of the firms retailers hire to set prices. Details like your precise location or browsing history can often be used to give you a different price than other shoppers get for the same goods, the agency found.

In New York, the Algorithmic Pricing Disclosure Act requires a store that sets a price from your personal data to say so with the notice “This price was set by an algorithm using your personal data.” Since Oct. 1, 2026, a Maryland law has barred large grocers and grocery delivery services from using personal data to charge shoppers more for groceries.

A price alert beats refreshing the page, since you can’t predict when software working from data you never see will cut the price. Google Shopping’s price tracking emails you when the price drops below a target you choose, so base that target on the item’s price history.
