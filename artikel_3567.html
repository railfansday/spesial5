Hasan Piker Joins VF’s Fair Game: “I Am Not a Terrorist”

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

One of the most prominent figures of the so-called Bluenami was not a candidate on the ballot, but rather a Twitch streamer and political commentator: Hasan Piker.

This year Hasan was involved in dozens of races across the country, campaigning for progressive candidates like Michigan senator-elect Abdul El-Sayed. But you’re probably more likely to associate Hasan’s name with what other people have had to say about him—from Fox News commentators to more established members of the Democratic Party.

Hasan regularly courts controversy on both his hours-long Twitch streams, where he’s said things like “America deserved 9/11,” and social media, where he’s written that Hamas “is a thousand times better than” Israel. He claims he’s often taken out of context, and that his audience knows what he really means—and where he stands. In his mind, he’s a leftist provocateur, maybe, but one who understands the importance of working-class values and electoral strategy.

While Hasan has walked back some of his more inflammatory language, his rhetoric raises the question: Is he a liability to the cause he champions? Or is he successfully reaching the critical audience—namely, young men—that helped elect the wave of progressives poised to take office in January?

With 2028 on the horizon, I wanted to know how Hasan thinks about his growing platform, and if he sees his role shifting in the media landscape. What responsibility does he have to his viewers and to the candidates he supports? And does he regret any of those past statements?

We also got into the DSA’s more extreme positions and how to make sense of them; why he thinks the Republicans put his thirst trap on the jumbotron at their convention; and how he, a self-proclaimed socialist, thinks about his own money.

Mark Guiducci: Hasan Piker, welcome to Fair Game.

Thanks for coming. Hasan, you are more online than just about anyone. You spend at least seven hours a day streaming. You reach tens of thousands of people at a time on average, and it’s all in the channel that you control. I’m curious, what are you doing here with me today? Why are you showing up for a show with a legacy media publication? What’s in it for you?

Well, I saw the clips and everybody looked really good. The lighting was perfect. I saw the Dari[aliza Avila Chevalier] clips, I saw Ta-Nehisi [Coates], and I was like, I got to get in on that.

Yeah. So shouts out to the team. You guys did a phenomenal job lighting this up. It looks very cool too.

But the serious answer is, I mean, I reach an audience that obviously does not watch television, does not read legacy publications at all. And I want to show people that read Vanity Fair that I am not a terrorist.

Right. Which brings me to my next question because—

Yeah, exactly. That would be the name of the episode. You’ve been everywhere this year, and you became this kind of bogeyman for the right, who probably have never heard you, watched your stream, or heard anything you’ve said out of your mouth. They’ve just heard quotes repeated by news anchors. And not even just for the right, even for some on the left. And you played this interesting role because you were a surrogate and a media figure and then a media figure covering the media as well. I want to spend a little bit of time talking about how you see your role. And to start, do you consider yourself a journalist?

Okay. Because it’s like you present this interesting new type of figure. I was thinking about how you’ve done an interview with CNN, you’ve done interviews with MS NOW, but they don’t have you on as much as I would expect them to.

I have some thoughts, but I wonder why you think that is.

I think I’ve pissed off a bunch of executives over there, because, in my experience, there are plenty of amazing reporters and journalists and producers that I know that work at all of these newsrooms, right? But from what I have seen, the masthead has a very different editorial slant than sometimes what the reporters want to cover.

They talk about me a lot. And also I’ve never been invited on any of these outlets as a pundit either, which is also strange because, as you also pointed to, I don’t just operate as a campaignster. As a matter of fact, that’s something I did very briefly and I did not like it at all. I’m first and foremost a commentator…. I do have the capacity to do journalism at times. I’ll do interviews with candidates. I’ll go out and—

Well, candidates I like. Sometimes I do interviews with candidates I don’t like or I’m unsure about as well. The problem is most of the candidates don’t want to come on if they know I’m gonna cook ’em. Like Gavin Newsom, I have had feelers out to get him on the broadcast.

I have. Gavin, Gavin, come on the show. I am a taxpaying California resident. I’m one of your constituents. You want to be president in 2028, you’re gonna have to be able to talk to people like myself.

All right. So, Hasan, this cycle you were involved in some way in, by one count, 48 different candidates’ races, and AIPAC and pro-Israel groups spent about $101 million in those races. And on Election Day, your stream was the second most watched in the country after NBC News’s “Kornacki Cam.” With a little bit of distance, how are you thinking about what happened a couple of weeks ago, both for yourself and for the country?

I feel a lot of hope. Hopefully it’s not going to be short-lived. But I feel very excited for the first time in a very long time, after 10 years of some of the most odious politics that we’ve seen from Trumpism, from MAGA. We finally saw a real sea change through electoralism. People truly have had enough with Trumpism.

I wouldn’t be so certain because I feel like if there’s one controlled opposition party that is capable of breathing life back into MAGA, it would be the establishment Democrats. So I’m a little bit worried, but it’s very clear that people are truly fed up.

Rahm Emanuel, for instance, is touting this as a victory for moderate Democrats, saying that they ran 21 points above Kamala Harris. The DSA, meanwhile, is claiming that American voters have “emphatically endorsed socialism.” Other pundits are saying that these big gains have nothing to do with the Democrats and are actually about the war in Iran and high gas prices. I mean, I think this is not even a new debate. This factional battle has existed within the Democratic Party and perhaps—

Yeah, but the progressive wing was always really fringe.

And now we’re talking about—I don’t know if 50/50 is right. I don’t want to use numbers, but—

I think it’s a testament to our success, especially in the midterms, that there are a lot of people who are trying to protect their comfortable sinecures and are outright panicking. And all of that completely sidesteps the reality that the vulgar Bernie-ism of 2016 was echoed through every single candidacy, every single campaign all across the country, from the obvious Berniecrats like Abdul El-Sayed to even people like Roy Cooper, who in the past were very moderate, very centrist. And he still is a center-right figure, which clearly works in North Carolina. But even he was railing against data centers, billionaires, the uncertainty surrounding AI. I mean, this is left populism. And that rhetoric, one could say, was deployed in the past by even the likes of Bill Clinton and Al Gore.

Absolutely. I mean, there’s certainly local moderation on issues, right? Because James Talarico went from saying “God is nonbinary” to “we need to defend our transgender brothers and sisters, our neighbors,” which is, again, insanely woke for Texas, or just in general, right? There are far more conservative figures in the party than James Talarico’s current position on the trans issue.

Okay. But sometimes the way that you run—oftentimes the way that you run needs to be different than the way that you govern or lead once you’re there. Do you think it is productive to continue pushing some of the farther-left ideas once they get to Congress? I would say, be bold, be brave, and obviously immediately implement accountability. Investigations are an absolute must. This is what people are demanding. After the economy, the second concern, the biggest concern, is corruption. The second term with Trump has been unbelievable. I mean, look, I’m Turkish. I’ve seen corruption in the Turkish government. I’ve seen corruption and tracked it and talked about it all around the world with different governments. I’ve never seen anything like what the Trump administration has done because of how flagrant and in your face it was. And I think people responded to that, finally, in the appropriate way they should, with anger.

I don’t think anyone is above the law. We have to restore law and order. So I’m a law-and-order Democrat. And I know that sounds something very different in Washington, DC, or in general. I think when politicians say that, they mean, like, punish the poor for minor crimes. I’m talking about punishing the wealthy for their corruption, for their major crimes, because they’re the ones who are robbing people blind.

What else? What are the other two that in the first year you really want to see happen?

I think it’s virtually impossible [with] the current DOJ to—they’re not impartial, right? I don’t think that we are going to move the needle on actual criminal investigations before the Democrats win the White House in 2028. But all of this is building up for that moment. And for that reason, I think we need to talk about affordability—and not just simply talk about affordability, but also explain how we’re going to solve the affordability crisis through a comprehensive legislative agenda. And one of the major issues around affordability that is oftentimes discussed as a separate thing, but it’s not, is health care. And the Democratic Party’s approach to health care has been lackluster. Even though Americans obviously overwhelmingly trust the Democrats with fixing health care or solving the problem with health care, the reality of the matter is, while the ACA was a fantastic improvement to what existed previously, it’s not enough, and we have to move the needle in a healthier direction. We have to push for a single-payer health care solution.

I’m perfectly fine with that. I’ve joked about that for years ’cause he’s so invested. He’s such a petty despot and so narcissistic that—

Yes. I have pitched this over and over again on my show. Look, you win so big with Donald Trump if you go to Donald Trump and you say, “Look, Obamacare, not the best, right? Now you get to destroy Obamacare and you get to build something else in your name. We call it Trumpcare. It’s single-payer. It’s Medicare for All.” I think he would do it.

All right, so let’s take a step back. You were talking a second ago about how Talarico was woke. And on November 4, Twitter, formerly known as X, was amazing. Memes, jokes, you know.

I see what you did there. Yeah. We flipped it back.

Yeah, we flipped it back. With the Democrats’ victory, the idea that woke was back, do you think that’s true? Do you think that we’re going to see, culturally, the return of this idea of woke? And do you think that’s a good thing for the Democrats?

Yes. I do think that the pendulum swing was an inevitability. I talked about this quite a bit. I saw the early warning signs when [Joe] Biden won. That was one of my major warnings that young men were going to go back to the Gamergate era of very far-right politics because the Biden administration was not delivering. And what ended up happening with this anti-woke narrative, this culture war that the Republicans had seemingly won, that they presented as a mandate, right, was some of the most odious commentary, some of the most vile rhetoric that anyone had ever seen.

Definitely. The idea of woke now, though, I'm just curious—being politically correct or precise in one’s language was, for a time, an outward indication of being a progressive or maybe even being a good person in culture. Do you think that’s still the case?

I mean, I’m progressive, so I think so, but I’m a little biased. But I will say this: I think that there were certainly social excesses, which is ironic because this whole Woke 1.0, 2.0 distinction, I feel a little responsible for in the discourse because I jokingly used to say—

Yeah, now I’ve joked about that too, and now journalists are reaching out to me being like, “Do you think Woke 3.0 is real?” I’m like, “I’m joking, guys. It’s not serious.” But here’s the thing, there is a little bit of sincerity there. I think one of the things that I routinely criticized during that 2016-to-2022, 2023, era of, I guess, Woke 1.0 was the social excesses, the maximalist demands, the inability to understand that people are complex and don’t have the same lived experience that everybody else has. I always would get so frustrated because we on the left say America’s a white supremacist country, and yet we don’t ever reflect on what kind of social conditioning comes along with that. And on the other side, people will say, “Oh, that’s normalizing this kind of behavior. It’s unacceptable.” And I totally see the other side of that argument as well, but I think, especially if you’re campaigning or especially if you’re building a movement, you do have to be a little bit more understanding and not be so hardheaded and assume that everyone has to agree with you from the jump without you making a convincing case as to why you might be correct.

Okay. All right. So we’ve talked a lot about what you think about the political landscape, but I want to talk also about your role in it right now and going forward. I don’t think there is an appropriate analog because I’m everything all at once at the same time, because I work out and I do collaborations with people who are not expressly political, so that’s kind of like Joe Rogan. I go out and operate sometimes as a campaign surrogate, and I do rallies at college campuses and I deliver speeches, so that’s kind of like Charlie Kirk. But then usually my day-to-day commentary is what I like to jokingly refer to as a Rush Limbaugh for Zoomers. So I do all of those things at the same time, and that’s, I think, part of the reason why it’s so hard to make sense of what I do from the perspective of someone who just doesn’t understand Twitch or doesn’t understand exactly what I’m doing and has only seen negatively framed clips or clips out of context, that kind of thing. Having said that, on the political-ideological side of things, of course I am the furthest from all of those figures. I’m just talking about the form and function of it all.

Okay. So it seems like you’ve been recently the guy that progressives come to when they’re trying to run a primary from the left, but then when they get to the general or to office, maybe they don’t need you as much, or they have left you behind, or they distanced themselves from you. And I think about [Zohran] Mamdani in this case. I just—

Maybe because of that Rush Limbaugh–esque heightened rhetoric; do you agree with that characterization? And how does that make you feel?

Well, my feelings are immaterial in this. As long as they’re delivering, I think that’s what matters. I’ve said this before: I love Zohran and, off the rip, my attitude on this was always just put the policies in the bag. If you’re delivering for the people of New York, and if you’re delivering successfully and every success that you have as a democratic socialist mayor of New York bodes well for the rest of this movement, then I’m happy.

Yeah, but are you talking to him behind the scenes? There’s still a relationship there? Because honestly—

Yes. But the thing is, what I’m doing is too new for people to fully realize, to fully comprehend. So of course there’s going to be some growing pains as far as how to navigate through an unbelievably contentious media environment, especially if the candidate[s] themselves are Muslim. And, you know, I think that is the reason why—the two major figures that I can point to that have done a little bit of distancing, at least in rhetoric, even though they never disavow the platform, disavow the person, they just say, “Oh, I don’t agree with that statement”—those two figures are Abdul El-Sayed and Zohran Mamdani. I think that is a big part of the reason why I’ve given them a lot more bandwidth to operate in this space in whichever way they want to, because the expectation or the fears that they have are very real. As someone who has been at the center of a lot of this manufactured outrage, a lot of this controversy, being presented as an Islamist, jihadist at times, a terrorist, I know the role that the first Muslim senator has to play on the campaign trail. So on that front, of course they got to do what they got to do.

So you intellectually understand it, but do you feel used?

I would only feel that way if they did not follow through on their promises that they made on the campaign trail. If Abdul came out tomorrow and was like, “Money out of politics? Maybe money in politics. Money in your pocket? Maybe you don’t get money in your pocket. Medicare for All? Medicare for some”—if he were to just flip the script on not just me, but Michiganders and America as a whole, then I would feel very betrayed.

You’ve been outspoken about distinguishing criticism of Israel from antisemitism, but do you think that there are moments when legitimate anger at Israel can create an environment in which antisemitism becomes acceptable, even among people who would never consider themselves antisemitic?
