In February 2024, Klarna announced its AI assistant was handling two-thirds of customer chats. It was doing the work of 700 agents and cutting resolution time from 11 minutes to 2. It looked like the AI success story everyone wanted. Fifteen months later, Klarna was hiring humans again. The CEO admitted that a focus on cost had led to "lower quality" and customers still wanted a person for complex cases. The AI stayed and alongside people.

So which Klarna got it right: the one that automated 700 agents' worth of work or the one that hired people back? Picture two chats. "Where's my refund?" has one answer, sitting in a database. "Why was I charged twice?" needs someone to hear the customer out and make a call. AI can handle the first on its own. The second needs a person or at least a person checking.

Most AI roadmaps skip that distinction. They list places to add AI, not jobs to split between AI, software and people. The rest of this piece shows how to make that split: a map of where AI value sits, then three tests for any idea. Is it worth it? Is it suitable? How do we build it?

Using AI is easy. Earning from it? McKinsey's 2026 State of AI survey finds nearly 9 in 10 organisations use AI but only 37% see any EBIT impact(Earnings Before Interest and Taxes) and about 6% earn more than 5% of EBIT from it.

Alexa is the cautionary tale: used daily for timers and weather, rarely for shopping. As one former senior Amazon employee told the Wall Street Journal: "We worried we've hired 10,000 people and we've built a smart timer."

Heavy use doesn't always equal value. And unlike normal software, every AI answer costs money. Whether you charge per seat, per call, per outcome or a hybrid of these, a flat price can lose money on your heaviest users, so model them first.

A support bot handling 50,000 tickets a month, resolving 60% and saving ₹40 each, is worth about ₹9.75 lakh a month after ₹2.25 lakh of AI cost. Note that the AI cost applies to every ticket it attempts, not just the ones it solves. One more catch: if nobody leaves the team, you have freed capacity, not saved cash. Be honest about which one you're claiming.

It's a role: AI can assist (it drafts, a person checks), recommend (it scores, a person decides) or execute (it acts, within set limits). Who should approve a ₹4,500 expense claim? Not the AI. AI reads the receipt, and rules approve it within caps. The pattern that falls out of all this is worth memorising: AI for ambiguity, software for certainty, humans for judgment.
