Is Your RAID Array Doing a Job It Was Never Designed For ?

Walk into almost any home lab, creative studio, or small business IT closet and you will hear the same sound: a low, persistent hum with rhythmic disk chatter underneath. Very often it comes from a 6-, 8-, or 12-bay array spinning 24/7/365, representing hundreds or thousands of dollars in drives, a chassis, cooling, and a monthly power bill.

Now run a file audit script over that array. (There is one below.) In most setups I have seen, the result is the same: the majority of the bytes on those platters have not been read or modified in more than 90 days. Finished client projects, old photo libraries, last decade's tax records, system images of machines that still run fine. It is data that is safe but not in use.

So why are we treating cold, static data as if it were a high concurrency production database?

This article separates two goals that RAID quietly blurs together: keeping data available and keeping data preserved. They sound similar. They call for different architectures, different costs, and different failure plans. Later, I will walk through the design decisions I made while building a tiering tool around this idea, including the trade offs I accepted and the ones I am still not sure about.

RAID (Redundant Array of Independent Disks) exists to solve two problems: uptime and throughput.

The goal: keep a file system online and responsive when a physical drive dies in the middle of a workday.

The cost: every drive spins continuously. Capacity usually grows in big steps (replace a whole vdev, or rebuild with larger disks). A failed disk triggers a long, heavy rebuild.

The limit: RAID protects against hardware interruption. It does not protect against deletion, overwrites, corruption that is faithfully replicated, ransomware, a bad firmware update, a flooded closet, or the passage of time.

There is one more reason people reach for RAID, and it rarely gets said out loud: a single folder. Pooling many disks into one volume means all your files live under one path, and you never have to think about which disk holds what. That convenience is real, and it is a big part of why RAID gets chosen even for data that never needed high availability. A tiered archive can give you the same thing, as the section on stubs below explains.

You have heard it before, and it bears repeating: RAID is not a backup, and it is not an archive. It is a way to keep a live system alive.

An archive is optimized for durability, cost efficiency, and long term readability, not for millisecond access.

The goal: data written today can be read back, intact, in 5, 10, or 20 years, without burning power or demanding constant attention.

The trade off: latency. Drives spin down, tapes sit on a shelf, objects live in a bucket. Access is measured in seconds or minutes, not milliseconds.

The longer a file has sat untouched, the less likely you are to need it in the next minute. Not never: the one time you need the 2021 tax return, you need it. But the odds fall steeply, and what matters is that retrieval is predictable, not instantaneous.

Keeping all of this on an active array means paying continuous power and mechanical wear costs for data that is effectively asleep.

Ask yourself honestly: what happens if this dataset is unavailable for four hours?

If the business halts, customers bounce, or a live stream fails, you need high availability. Use RAID.

If you merely wait ten minutes for a disk to spin up or a bundle to be fetched, you do not need availability. You need durability.

Confusing durability (the file is safe) with availability (the file opens in 12 ms) is probably the single biggest cause of over engineered, power hungry storage setups.

Using one big array as your only archival repository carries risks that rarely make it into the build guides.

Long rebuild windows. When a 16–20 TB drive fails in a RAID 5 / RAIDZ1 pool, the rebuild must read the surviving drives end to end, which can take a day or more. During that window you are one more failure (or one unrecoverable read error) away from losing the pool. How much risk this adds is debated, and the scary URE calculations you see online are often overstated for modern drives. But the direction is not in dispute: bigger drives and single parity layouts mean longer exposure. Mirrors and double parity reduce it; they do not remove it.

Silent corruption. Classic RAID has no idea whether the bytes it returns are the bytes you wrote. If one copy rots, parity or mirroring may happily propagate or "repair" the wrong data. Checksumming file systems (ZFS, Btrfs) and archives with stored hashes catch this; plain mdadm or hardware RAID generally does not.

Lock in. Hardware RAID controllers can make recovery on a different machine difficult, sometimes requiring the same controller model and firmware. Software RAID is more portable but still ties your data to a specific layout and OS.

Power and wear. A spinning 3.5-inch drive typically draws roughly 5–8 W at idle. Eight drives is on the order of 40–65 W, before the chassis, fans, and CPU. At 0.15 USD/kWh that is roughly 50–85 USD per year for the disks alone, and over five years it can rival the price of a few new drives. Your local rates may be higher or lower; plug in your own.

A false sense of security. A green status light feels like safety. In practice it is the reason many people never build a true offsite or offline copy. The array is healthy right up until the day something happens that RAID does not cover.

Communities that love RAID are not wrong to push back. Here are the objections worth taking seriously.

"Drives are cheap. Just keep everything online." Drives were cheap! Getting the most life and performance out of a drives matters more than it used to. For small datasets, you can get away with an oversized system. The math changes as the pool grows, or when you realize that online storage still needs a second, independent copy somewhere else. A tiered approach lets you buy fast storage for what is hot and cheap capacity for what is not, instead of paying NVMe or always on prices for everything.

"Spinning drives down kills them." Spin up cycles do add wear, and the right answer depends on the drive and the access pattern. Spin down makes sense for data you touch rarely (days or weeks apart), not data touched every few minutes. It is one reason a cold tier should be actually cold and not just a RAID array with a short idle timer.

"I want one folder tree." This is the best objection, and often the real reason people build one big array. Archives that scatter your files across separate disks, shelves, or buckets break that, and archives you cannot browse get forgotten. The stub approach described below is one way to keep the single namespace without keeping every disk spinning.

"What if I need an old file right now?" Then retrieval needs to be fast to start and predictable. Seconds to minutes from a spun down disk or object storage is fine for most people. Tape adds a mount step. Know which you are signing up for.

"An archive with one copy is worse than RAID." Correct. If you move your only copy of something onto a single cold disk, you have traded one risk for another. The cold tier needs redundancy of its own, through multiple copies across independent media.

The practical answer is not RAID or archive. It is both, each doing the job it is good at.

Tier 1: the hot landing zone. A mirror of two SSDs, or a modest RAID, sized for what you are actively creating or consuming: current projects, active media, OS and application data, this year's documents. Small is a feature.

Tier 2: the cold vault. High-capacity HDDs that can rest, standalone or external drives, LTO tape, or object storage. Its job is to hold everything past the 90 day cliff, with integrity checks and more than one copy.

Tier 3: an independent offsite or offline copy. Whatever you do with tiers 1 and 2, you still need a real backup strategy. The classic 3-2-1 rule (three copies, two media types, one offsite) applies to your hot data too.

The diagram above does not have to mean two places to look. In a stub-based design, when a file moves to the cold vault, a small placeholder stays behind at the same path with the same name and logical size. The directory tree you browse looks the same before and after archiving. Your file manager, applications, and media server keep pointing at one location while the bytes underneath live on tape, a spun down disk, or a bucket. Opening a stub triggers a recall of the content on demand.

This is the design problem that interested me most, and it is where the engineering trade offs get real.

I ended up building an open-source tool around this architecture: Huskhoard, a tiering archive for Linux written in Rust. Rather than walk through a feature list, here are the decisions that shaped it, what each one bought me, and what each one cost. Your constraints may differ, and some of these choices deserve to be made differently.

The hard part of a stub-based design is knowing when someone opens a placeholder so the data can be recalled. The usual answer is a FUSE file system that sits in front of everything. I chose the Linux fanotify API instead, which lets the tool intercept the open and fill in the content without being in the path of every read of hot files.

What it bought: the hot tier remains an ordinary file system, with ordinary performance and ordinary failure behavior. If the tool is not running, hot data is unaffected.

What it cost: portability. fanotify support for this use needs a recent kernel (5.1 or newer), so the tool is Linux-only. WSL2 is out, and NAS platforms such as Synology and QNAP are out unless you run a custom kernel. That is a real narrowing of the audience, and I accepted it deliberately. If you need a cross-platform answer, FUSE is probably the better road.

Keeping the name, path, and logical size in place makes ls, file managers, and media servers behave as if nothing moved. That solves the "one folder tree" problem. But it also hides a cost: opening a stub can take seconds or minutes, and applications that assume instant reads may time out or appear to hang. The namespace is unified; the latency is not. Anyone designing around stubs has to decide how much of that to surface to the user, and I do not think there is a perfect answer.

The cold vault can be hard drives, LTO tape through a native SCSI driver, or S3 compatible buckets. Supporting such different media pushed me toward a sequential, append oriented write model for all of them. That model is natural for tape, and it also suits SMR drives, which perform poorly with random rewrites. The trade off is that deleting or rewriting archived data is not cheap in place. Cold data is treated as written once, read rarely, and reclaimed in bulk rather than edited.

Payloads are stored as Zstd streams and verified with BLAKE3, and a scrub command re checks a volume for bit rot. The reasoning follows the archive properties from earlier: stored checksums are only useful if you actually reverify them on a schedule. The cost is time and I/O. A scrub of a large cold volume is not free, and it means waking drives that you were trying to keep asleep. Scheduling verification is a balance between catching corruption early and honoring the "cold" in cold storage.

A single cold copy is a quiet single point of failure, so cold data can be replicated N ways across local drives, tapes, and cloud buckets at the same time. The trade ff is operational complexity: each target has its own failure modes, speeds, and costs, and keeping several of them consistent is more work than one big array. It is also worth being clear about what this is not. It is a tiering archive, not a backup tool. Moving a file to the cold tier does not make the hot tier protected, and a real 3-2-1 plan is still required.

A SQLite catalog tracks every file and where it lives on media, and it can be exported to Apache Parquet for analysis in tools like DuckDB or Python. The motivation was the "open formats" requirement: if the project disappears, Zstd streams and a Parquet export mean the data and its index remain readable with common tools. Open formats do not eliminate lock in risk completely, since recall logic and media layout still matter, but they lower it, and I think any archive tool should be judged on how painful leaving it is.

A common cold data scenario is a large media file that someone wants to play. Recalling a whole file before playback starts is a poor experience. So a small HTTP gateway (StreamGate) lets players such as Plex, Jellyfin, or VLC seek into large archived files without restoring the entire file first. The trade off is that seeking into cold media depends heavily on the medium: object storage handles it gracefully, while tape does not, so the experience varies by target.

Age, extension, and directory rules decide what gets archived, and a high water mark triggers an archive cycle when the hot tier fills past a threshold. Policy is where tiering systems tend to go wrong in practice. Too aggressive, and you are constantly recalling files you still use. Too conservative, and the hot tier never shrinks. The audit script earlier in this article exists for exactly this reason: pick thresholds from your measured access profile, not from a guess.

Before you buy your next 5-bay enclosure or expand your pool:

RAID is excellent at what it was built for: keeping live systems running through a hardware failure. But if your array is mostly serving as a digital warehouse for finished projects and old memories, it is working a job it was never designed for, and costing you power, money, and a false sense of safety to do it.

Take the cold data off the treadmill. Keep a small, fast tier for what you use, and give the rest a proper home: verified, cataloged, and copied more than once.
