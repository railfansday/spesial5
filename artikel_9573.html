Explain It Like This: How Metaphors Make Technical Ideas Clear

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

A manager once asked me why a feature would take two weeks. I started in on the migration, the indexes, the backfill, and watched his eyes glaze over by the second sentence. Then I said: "Think of it like moving house while you still live in it." He nodded. Two weeks made sense.

That is all a metaphor does. It takes something unfamiliar and ties it to something the listener already knows, so they can borrow what they know. One sentence, and the hard idea is easy.

An API is a restaurant menu. The kitchen is the server. The waiter carries your order in and brings the food back. You never see the kitchen, and you don't need to. The menu is enough.

Think of a database as a library. The data is the books, the catalogue tells you where each one lives, and the query is the librarian who fetches it. A library with no catalogue is just a room full of books you can't find. A database with no index has the same problem.

A cache is the desk drawer. You keep the tools you reach for every day in the drawer, not in the warehouse down the road. The drawer is small, but everything in it is within reach. The warehouse holds everything, but fetching from it takes time. Small and fast, or big and slow. That is the trade-off, and now it takes one sentence to explain it.

DNS is the phone book. You type a name you can remember, and it finds the number behind it. A queue is the line at a shop: first come, first served, and if the line gets too long, people wait. Version control is track changes in a document, except it remembers every version, not just the last one.

None of these is exact. That doesn't matter. The listener already understands the familiar half, so they can carry it over to the technical half.

The comparison has to be familiar to the person in front of you, or it does nothing. A manager knows budgets and schedules, so "technical debt is like a loan: it lets you move fast now, but the interest grows" lands straight away. A designer knows layers and artboards, so comparing a component system to a design system makes sense to them. A user knows their phone, so "the app is slow because it downloads everything fresh, like re-reading a whole book to find one page" makes sense to them.

There is no comparison that works for everyone. There is only the right one for the person you are talking to. Before you explain anything, think about what they deal with every day. The best comparisons usually come from ordinary life: cooking, driving, money, weather, sport. Ordinary things work because everyone has lived with them.

One hard idea, one familiar thing. That is enough. Two comparisons at once just confuse people. "Our new system is like a library and a river and a safety net" asks the listener to hold three pictures at the same time, and none of them gets to do its job. Pick one. If the system is mostly about finding things, it is a library. If it is mostly about flow, it is a river. If it is mostly about catching mistakes, it is a safety net. Choose the one that fits the main idea and let the rest go.

Across a long talk you can use more than one metaphor. Just give each idea its own.

A metaphor that reads well on the page can fall apart when you say it. So say it before you rely on it. Try it in a standup, or on one teammate, and watch what happens. If they nod, keep it. If they look confused, or you end up explaining the metaphor itself, drop it.

Listen for two things while you do. Does the person get the idea faster than they would have without it? And does the comparison still hold at the end of the sentence? "Our team is a well-oiled machine" works, until you add "and the machine has feelings", which breaks the picture. When the second half breaks the picture, fix the second half or drop the metaphor. Plain words beat a broken picture every time.

Mixed metaphors are the most common one: two comparisons fighting in a single sentence. "We need to get all our ducks in a row and hit the ground running before the ship sails." Ducks, running, ships. One picture per sentence is enough.

Then there is the stretched metaphor, a comparison pushed too far. "Our company is a garden" is fine. Add gardeners, bees, harvests, and a scarecrow, and the listener stops hearing the idea and starts watching the comparison fall apart. Use the metaphor to open the idea, then let it go and speak plainly.

Tired ones are worse than useless. "Think outside the box." "At the end of the day." "The bottom line." They were fresh once. Now they fill space instead of making a picture. If you have heard a comparison a hundred times, your listener has too. Find a fresher one, or say it plainly.

And watch the feeling, not just the facts. Comparing a hospital system to a factory might be accurate, but it feels cold, and patients don't want to feel like products on a line. Comparing a school to a garden feels warm and right. A metaphor carries an attitude along with the information. Pick it with that in mind.

The places where technical people meet non-technical people. That is where a metaphor earns its keep. In a standup, one comparison can save five minutes of background. A pull request description with a good comparison helps the reviewer understand the change faster than three paragraphs of detail. User docs stop reading like a feature list once the reader can picture what the feature does. And in an incident update, a metaphor tells a manager what broke without a lecture on the stack. It is usually the one sentence people remember afterwards.

PressivoMedia looks at metaphors this way, as a way to make a hard idea clear in one line, not decoration.

That manager and the house move. He still brings it up, months later. Not the migration, not the indexes. The house. A good comparison sticks around after the details fade, and that is the whole point of it.
