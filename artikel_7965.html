Every household generates a mass of organic kitchen waste, from morning coffee grounds to evening fish bones. Quite often, thanks to social media and online forums, we’ll hear about some novel way to use key scraps to help plants. These quirky DIY tricks can seem so compelling and easy (and also free) that it’s tempting to toss scraps straight into the potting soil around your plants.

A few kitchen scraps can help a little, and some can actually help with fertilizing houseplants, once they're prepared the right way. But that last bit is important. You need to make sure you are using the right kind of scraps, and that you have prepared them so they can be absorbed or processed well. Fail to do this, and there’s a chance that helpful impulse could result in fuzzy mold and tiny gnats.

The kitchen scraps I’ve rounded up here can help to supply trace minerals, organic matter, and subtle soil-structuring benefits to your potted greenery. These scraps all need prep first, though, whether that's drying, grinding or some time in a compost pile or worm bin. If you want to unlock their gentle benefits, this is a good time to get things moving. Houseplants slow down as the days get shorter, so they don't need much extra food until spring – giving you time to turn some trash into treasure.

When it comes to the best scraps for houseplants, preparation and application are key. Never dump wet, chunky food scraps onto the soil surface, as they can trap stagnant moisture and invite houseplant pests. Instead, use one of these preparation methods: air or oven-drying, grinding into fine powder, brewing, or indoor composting with a worm bin.

Indoor composting bins don’t require lots of knowledge or experience these days, and you can pick up units that make composting a highly beneficial background activity. If you’re interested in a compact unit for indoors, the Original VermiHut System, available from Amazon, is a neat and super-easy way to turn scraps into rich, houseplant-safe worm castings. Once your scrap of choice has been treated, you can gently work small amounts into the top inch (2.5cm) of potting mix.

Prioritize pot hygiene with any scraps you do try out. Check that every container has unobstructed drainage holes and keep a close eye on treated pots for 2-3 weeks. If you spot any white fuzz, scrape it off and allow the top layer to dry. Remember that kitchen scraps are meant to be gentle supplemental boosters, not replacements for quality potting soil and a balanced seasonal feeding routine. Here are 6 scraps you can safely try with your houseplants – plus, which scraps to leave in the bin.

The buzz around coffee grounds for houseplants is mostly about nitrogen (along with trace potassium and magnesium). That nitrogen is tied up in organic matter, though, and soil microbes will need to break it down before the roots can use it. Select plain, unflavored Arabica or Robusta grounds, and avoid flavored coffees containing synthetic oils, syrups, or spices.

Never spread damp grounds thickly over indoor soil, as they can mold and dry into a crust that repels water. Instead, spread used grounds on a baking sheet to dry, then blend a small handful (no more than 5-10% of total volume) into a fresh potting mix, or process through an indoor worm bin first. Contrary to myth, brewed grounds will not reliably acidify your potting soil, as brewing pulls a good share of the acid into your cup. For a good potting mix, buy Espoma Organic Potting Mix from Walmart.

Eggshells are mostly calcium carbonate, and plants need calcium to strengthen cell walls and support healthy new leaf development. But don’t toss large shell fragments into a houseplant pot. Calcium carbonate dissolves at a glacial pace, especially in neutral or alkaline potting mixes. Instead, rinse away the egg white, let shells air-dry for 48 hours, and pulverize them into a fine powder.

For grinding, use a dedicated grinder, a pestle and mortar, or a processor like the Cuisinart Mini Food Processor from Amazon, which is compact and super-fast. For a faster mineral release, soak the shell powder in a splash of mild lemon juice or vinegar for 24 hours. The mild acid reacts with the calcium carbonate to create plant-available calcium citrate. Just remember to dilute before applying. Work a light pinch into potting soil once or twice a year, skipping acid-loving plants like African violets, as the calcium can gradually raise soil pH.

Banana peels (along with closely related plantain peels) are rich in potassium, which regulates water uptake and boosts flowering. They also contain trace phosphorus and magnesium. However, burying chunks of raw banana or plantain peel in your houseplant’s potting soil is a really bad idea. Dense, sugary peels rot sluggishly in pots, attracting fruit flies and encouraging root-rotting pathogens.

Chop banana (or plantain) peels into small pieces and process in a compost bin or worm farm until crumbly, before blending into potting mix. Alternatively, dehydrate clean peels in a low oven until crispy, grind them into a powder, and sprinkle a spoonful into the soil. While soaking peels in water for 48 hours creates a mild banana tea, it leaches only trace nutrients. If you’re looking for targeted liquid feeds, you’re better off with a balanced fertilizer such as Espoma’s Organic Indoor 2-2-2 Plant Food, available from Walmart, and adding it during spring and summer growth.

Plain, steeped tea leaves offer gentle organic matter, trace nitrogen, and mild tannic acids that subtly condition soil structure and improve moisture retention. However, you need to stick to unflavored black, green, white, or pure herbal teas like chamomile or rooibos. And make sure they are steeped without milk, sugar, or honey. Avoid heavily spiced chai blends or artificially flavored teas, as the essential oils can irritate delicate root hairs.

Tear open used tea bags and discard the pouch, as most tea bags contain plastic fibers. Never pile wet leaves on top of the soil, as they can encourage gnats and mold. Instead, spread the loose leaves on a paper towel until dry and lightly fork a teaspoon into the topsoil, or drop them into a worm bin. Used gently once a month in spring and summer, they give moisture-loving tropicals like ferns, calatheas, and pothos a gentle lift. If you don’t have any compost going, Home Depot carries Noble Worm Organics Worm Castings, and a little of that does the same job.

Pungent alliums like onions and garlic, spicy peppers, or fibrous potato skins should never be buried straight into pots. They can ferment and summon fungus gnats. However, you can safely capture minerals by making a liquid tonic from key vegetable scraps. Save the cooled, unsalted water left over from boiling or steaming nutrient-dense vegetables like carrots, potatoes, broccoli, or leafy greens. Use this mineral-rich water at room temperature instead of watering once or twice a month, discarding any leftovers after 24 hours so bacteria cannot multiply.

Alternatively, you can route clean trimmings through an indoor worm bin. Bury solid scraps like carrot peelings, lettuce cores, and squash trimmings beneath damp bedding in a compact indoor worm bin. In 8-12 weeks, red wigglers can convert them into odorless vermicompost. You can buy Uncle Jim’s Worm Farm Live Red Wigglers from Amazon for hardworking composting worms to transform carrot peels and used greens into nutrient-rich castings. Then add to plants, applying 2 tablespoons (30 ml) per quart (0.95 L) of potting mix around the base, and water in.

Rinsing uncooked white, brown, or jasmine rice leaves behind cloudy, starch-rich water containing trace amounts of nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium (NPK) along with B vitamins. While scientific evidence shows its direct fertilizer value is modest and varies widely by grain type, those gentle starches can act as a mild prebiotic snack for beneficial soil microbes in healthy potting mixes. However, use only fresh, unsalted and unseasoned rinse water (or cooled, unsalted boiling water).

Apply it the same day in place of a standard watering once a month. Never store rice water in jars to ferment. DIY fermented rice water quickly breeds foul-smelling bacteria. Likewise, avoid salted pasta water. While plain starchy pasta water is harmless, even a pinch of cooking salt accumulates in closed pots and can quickly reach a level where it scorches roots. If you’re after a good indoor watering can, choose something with a gooseneck spout that can target the base of the plant, such as the Megawodar Copper Watering Can from Amazon.

Even with careful prep, certain kitchen leftovers need to be kept far away from your indoor garden. Meat, fish, and dairy products are the big ones. Oily and greasy foods are also non-starters. Animal proteins and fats rot rapidly in warm indoor temperatures, creating unbearable odors, attracting flies, and coating soil particles in a waterproof film that suffocates roots.

Salty and sugary leftovers are equally destructive. Salted pasta water, soup broths, and seasoned cooking liquids leave behind sodium deposits that build up in closed pots, drawing moisture out of roots and leaving foliage with crispy brown leaf tips. Meanwhile, sugary sauces, fruit juices, and citrus rinds trigger fungal growth and attract household ants.

When monitoring your houseplants in the weeks after adding your kitchen scraps, keep a close eye out for fuzzy molds and fungus gnats. Used early, you can add sticky traps like LightsMax Dual-Sided Yellow Sticky Traps from The Home Depot to manage adult gnats and other flying pests.

By drying, grinding, or brewing, or letting an indoor worm bin do the heavy lifting first, you can enrich your potting mix sustainably and give your houseplants a gentle helping hand with ingredients destined for the trash.
