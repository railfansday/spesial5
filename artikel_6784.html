<i><em>Blade Runner 2099</em></i> Is a Beautiful Slog Through a Grim Future

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

The new Prime Video series picks up way, way after Denis Villeneuve’s sequel. Not that it matters.

Crowded streets. Long, cool coats. Flying cars darting through smog and neon skyscrapers. An abundance of Japanese aesthetics that imply America’s lost superpower status. Ridley Scott’s seminal 1982 masterwork Blade Runner (itself adapted from Philip K. Dick’s Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep?) laid the foundation for almost all of cyberpunk science fiction, so much so that everything else feels undeservedly derivative. Even a new Blade Runner. The new series Blade Runner 2099 looks the part, but it lacks the soul of its predecessors, leaving its perspectives on what it means to be more human than human hollow, like a Replicant without memories of its own.

Streaming now on Prime Video (and just in time for a long holiday weekend) is Blade Runner 2099. A stand-alone continuation of both Ridley Scott’s towering original and Denis Villeneuve’s remarkable legacy sequel, the series takes place in 2099 (decades after Villeneuve’s Blade Runner 2049). An unseen revolution has elevated Replicants to the ruling class of Los Angeles, rendering flesh-and-bone meat-bag humans as the new discriminated minority.

Hunter Schafer flexes some of her budding leading-lady chops as Cora, a real human posing as a Replicant to smuggle herself and her brother out of Los Angeles. Her ruse leads her to Olwen (Oscar-winner Michelle Yeoh), a Replicant Blade Runner spending the final days of her artificial life span seeking out someone from her past. A humorless buddy-cop mystery, Blade Runner 2099 is mired in a plot-centric narrative that cares less about the why of all things—an underrated and beautiful feature of its predecessors—than the what and how.

In other words, it’s the first time a piece of Blade Runner media actually zeroes in on the plot, instead of anything else more interesting. Try not to think of this as an eight-hour Blade Runner series but rather an eight-hour series stretching at the seams to feel worthy of its branded title.

Is it because streaming TV in 2026 has become so plastic and hollow that even Blade Runner 2099 feels like an unengaging slog? Or were the direction of both Scott and Villeneuve so immaculate that we were distracted from how turgid they might have been all this time? Regardless, 2099’s lack of auteurship is its most deafening shortcoming. The series has been steered to the screen by executive producer Silka Luisa, whose warmer color palettes—based on her choice of more sunshine, which glistens off surfaces—lend 2099 some identity. Some.

But beyond even that minor distinction, Blade Runner 2099 is still just a plot-y crime drama that checks off boxes and goes through the motions of its genre, with nary a real thing to say. About us, about our fragile humanity, about the demise of all we’ve built through artificial intelligence. Blade Runner 2099 is a dystopian sci-fi in all the ways you expect it, but it’s hardly a good one. Sure, the original movies were oppressive, dreary, and morbid too. But they possessed a knowing glare into our fears about the future and our imminent collapse as a civilized species. The new series lacks this same insight, or indeed any of its own, all in the name of goosing up engagement numbers for the closest thing to a real-life Tyrell Corp. (Do shareholders dream of electric quarterly reports?)

Despite surface-level similarities to the movies, plus a moody noir-like motif with a sexy saxophone that, admittedly, completely and unapologetically rips, the plodding Blade Runner 2099 is a needless walkabout through a world that never begged for one. Needless is a good word, actually. The show’s strong visual execution is negated by its inability to rise to the occasion as a worthwhile or enlightening probe of the human experience, or even just as an entertaining cop show. It may be smart, but what good is intelligence if there isn’t an ounce of grace?
