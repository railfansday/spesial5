My Most-Worn Winter Jacket Is $250

Filson’s Insulated Canvas Outfitter is everything your vintage workwear isn’t.

Welcome to the Esquire Endorsement. Heavily researched. Thoroughly vetted. These picks are the best way to spend your hard-earned cash.

I was sitting at a dive bar in Brooklyn, looking past my wife and a friend during a lull in conversation at the two pool tables with two games going on. One guy—next team up, when I see him—was wearing the uniform: mesh-backed trucker hat, work pants with carabiner keys, and a vintage work jacket (you know the brand). But vintage gives that layer too much credit. It looked like its former owner was shot and left in a ditch—and our hero, like a member of the Joad family, needed a jacket, found the deceased, peeled this jacket off him, put it on, and kept on rolling toward the big city.

I focus back on the table. “Do y’all think we’ve run out of vintage?” I blurt out. “What?” My wife, with good reason, is confused; my buddy, silent, is equally so. “Like, vintage work jackets,” I say, pointing to this guy. “You know, America has been deindustrializing for half a century, and the whole 2020 resale boom has peaked. Maybe this guy is wearing that jacket because all the good vintage workwear is gone?” Neither of them cared enough to entertain me.

But I care to talk about it. The vintage-workwear ship has sailed, the bubble has popped—whatever. You do not need to pay $500 for some old guy’s work jacket in the name of “sustainability.” If you’re doing that at this point, you’re a victim. You’d be better off talking that $500 and splitting it in two. With the first $250, take someone out on a date. With the second $250, buy this Filson jacket—the unequivocally best barn coat, work jacket, or whatever you want to classify it as on the market. This is the only winter jacket a man needs.

The three-season version of this jacket, the regular Canvas Outfitter, is a perennial top seller for Filson. It is obviously a good barn jacket, classic in its hunting-inspired styling: flannel lining, corduroy collar, hand warmers, and big shell pockets. But if I were to get that style of old-school hunting jacket—and sorry to blaspheme against my beloved Filson—I would go to a few other brands.

Where Filson runs away with the barn-coat crown is in adding insulation; no one does it better. The original, though flannel lined, feels just a little too flimsy for me, and if it’s going to hit lower than my waist, I’d rather have full butt coverage. But with the insulation and the internal structure it provides, the jacket sits ever so slightly higher on the body. It’s not cropped, but the fit becomes more trucker or work jacket, less barn coat—hence my comparison to a Carhartt Detroit. Personally, it’s exactly what I want. Looks good no matter how you wear it; flatters any body type you can imagine.

That said, the actual benefit of that insulation is not aesthetic. It’s practical. This is one of the warmest jackets in my rotation, and it’s easily the most durable. The ten-and-a-half-ounce duck canvas never gets a nick—not on a hike, not on a day doing yard work. And while this is not at all a rain jacket, the polyester lining means it can put up with moisture infinitely better than something filled with down. If you get caught in a fall rainstorm, no worries. If you have a snow day, this is the ultimate outer layer. For that reason, it often ends up as one of my most-worn garments over the colder months.

To come back to my original point: Aren’t we all tired of the absurdity of vintage? I don’t mean to be anti-sustainability or anti–old clothes. But aren’t we tired of hype cycles around buying someone else’s clothing? Workwear is supposed to be bought cheap and worn hard. This jacket is the answer in my mind. It’s not trendy, nor is it especially expensive. You can buy one brand-new—novel, no?—for $250 (a steal these days) and know that every crease, every fade, every repaired armpit hole is yours and yours alone. Wear it for a few decades, then it’ll be your vintage. When you’re dead, some kid will sell it to a yuppie for a 200 percent markup. That’s the goal.
