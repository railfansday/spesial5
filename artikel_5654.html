Most states fail ‘community power’ test, advocacy group says

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Let Utility Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

The Institute for Local Self-Reliance issued letter grades based on the presence or absence of policies it sees as supportive of virtual power plants and local control over energy systems.

The scorecards were released shortly before the midterm elections, which saw mixed results for community power advocates and proposals.

In Georgia, Democrats now hold a majority on the Public Service Commission and are widely expected to push back on Southern Company’s plans to add some 10 GW of new generation, mostly natural gas, to meet rising data center demand.

Meanwhile, in Ann Arbor, Michigan, voters rejected a bid to separate from investor-owned DTE Energy and create a public power utility.

On the community power scorecard, the seven highest-scoring states were California, Colorado, Connecticut, Maryland, Massachusetts, New Jersey and Oregon.

The scorecard values policies that aim to make it easier to deploy and monetize distributed resources, including state community solar programs, customer-friendly net metering and interconnection frameworks, streamlined solar permitting, and virtual power plants.

Other criteria relate to what ILSR calls “utility accountability,” such as transmission right-of-first refusal, restructured energy markets, laws or regulations curtailing customer disconnections, and restrictions on utilities charging customers for new infrastructure during construction.

Though state lawmakers or regulators have significant influence over most of ILSR’s metrics, some also have a national nexus.

For example, a Federal Energy Regulatory Commission order in 2011 opened transmission-construction bidding to third parties, eroding vertically integrated utilities’ access to potentially lucrative infrastructure projects.

In April, Entergy, Xcel Energy and seven other utilities asked FERC to halt competitive bidding for transmission projects in the Midcontinent Independent System Operator and Southwest Power Pool regions, which cover parts of more than 20 states. They said the move would accelerate transmission development; competitive bidding advocates countered that it would raise project costs that ratepayers would ultimately bear.

If passed, the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act, or BAAJA, could shift those rules.

Among BAAJA’s sweeping reforms to energy project permitting and regulation are a wholesale elimination of incumbent utilities’ federal right of first refusal and stricter regional transmission planning requirements.

Even states with relatively high marks on community power have room for improvement, ILSR said.

California, for example, earned B’s for solar permitting and and virtual power plants, but a D on community solar and F grades on disconnection prevention and market structure.

Texas, with an overall F grade on community power, scored a C on net metering and A grades on third-party DER ownership, right of first refusal and municipal franchise authority, which allows cities to negotiate better contracts with electric and gas utilities.

ILSR’s virtual power plant scorecard showed a similar dynamic.

No state met all of the group’s benchmarks for a perfect score, whose requirements include variable compensation based on demand and other factors; third-party aggregator participation; increased access or benefits for low- and moderate-income customers; multiple technology types; and multiple customer classes.

Six states earned a ‘B’ grade for their VPP policies: California, Colorado, Connecticut, Hawai’i, Maryland and New York.

New York and California’s VPP programs are the country’s best, according to ILSR. New York’s only deficiency was not prohibiting “participant clawbacks and fees,” such as early exit fees or penalties for failing to meet minimum performance standards.

California’s only ding was not requiring DER compensation or access parity regardless of whether a resource is owned by the customer, a third-party aggregator or a utility.
