I am thankful that we get to watch Jeremiah Smith for one more game as a Buckeye

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

At some point on Saturday afternoon, when the clock runs out on the 122nd edition of The Game, I hope that Jeremiah Smith takes a moment to stand in the middle of Ohio Stadium, look around, and soak it all in. I also hope that the more than 100,000 people in attendance and the millions of us watching from home take a moment to do the same, because we may never again have the privilege of watching a player in scarlet and gray quite like No. 4.

Since there is about as much chance of J.J. coming back for a fourth season of college football as there is of me replacing Ryan Day as Ohio State's head coach, Saturday's rivalry game very well could be — and likely should be — his final game as a Buckeye. While I would selfishly love to see him suit up in whatever B-level bowl Ohio State ends up in, it would be difficult to blame someone who figures to be one of the first five players selected in April's NFL draft for not wanting to take that risk.

So, as we gather with our loved ones today to take stock of all that we have to be thankful for, I hope that the angst, anger, and anxiety of this season haven't obscured just how lucky we have been to witness the Jeremiah Smith era of Ohio State football.

There have been countless occasions over the past three years when I have watched Jeremiah Smith make a catch, seen the replay from multiple angles, and still had absolutely no idea how he made it look so effortless. He's made catches that would be the crowning achievement of practically any other receiver's career but barely warrant inclusion in his personal highlight reel. We have become so accustomed to seeing him do seemingly impossible things that I fear we have occasionally forgotten just how exceptional he actually is.

But as much as I have marveled at his preternatural athletic gifts, this season I have grown to appreciate the competitor and leader that he has become even more. From a relatively reserved freshman who largely let his play do the talking to a junior captain unwilling to sit quietly by as the standards of the program deteriorated around him, Smith has shown the kind of accountability and competitive fire that has been all too scarce in Columbus this fall.

For someone who has so little left to prove as an individual, I love how much Smith still cares about whether or not Ohio State wins football games. As a fan, it means something to me that a player with millions of dollars awaiting him in the NFL still cares enough to get visibly frustrated on a Saturday afternoon in the middle of a season that hasn't gone nearly as well as any of us had hoped. Over these three years, I have come to admire not only what he is capable of doing in an Ohio State jersey, but how much it seems to mean to him to wear one.

Short of something completely unforeseen, Smith should spend a significant portion of December collecting Ohio State's eighth Heisman Trophy and its third Biletnikoff Award, becoming the first Buckeye wide receiver to win the former. Smith is already unquestionably among the greatest players to ever set foot on the field at Ohio Stadium, but those honors would further cement his place in the pantheon of Buckeye football and should lead to his No. 4 being retired before he packs up his campus housing.

That he has rewritten Ohio State's and the Big Ten's receiving record books before the end of his third collegiate season, even accounting for the longer seasons of modern college football, feels both incomprehensible and the most obvious thing imaginable.

One of the great luxuries of being an Ohio State fan is that, dating back to the Second World War, we have rarely had to wait very long for the next all-time great to come along.

From Les Horvath to Bill Willis to Vic Janowicz to Hopalong Cassady to Jack Tatum to John Hicks to Archie Griffin to Cris Carter to Eddie George to Orlando Pace to Mike Doss to A.J. Hawk to Troy Smith to Braxton Miller to Ezekiel Elliott to J.T. Barrett to the Bosas and Chase Young to Justin Fields to C.J. Stroud to Jaxon Smith-Njigba to Marvin Harrison Jr. to TreVeyon Henderson, and now to Jeremiah Smith.

These names, and dozens, if not hundreds, more, serve as mile markers not only in the history of Ohio State football, but along the timelines of our own lives. The games that these superhuman athletes have won, the trophies that they have earned, and the legacies that they have created matter to everyone who loves Buckeye football, but far more important than their on-field accomplishments are the memories that they have helped us create.

Think of the unbridled joy in living rooms across Ohio when Jack Tatum decleated a receiver over the middle, when Keith Byars raced into the end zone wearing only one shoe, or when Chris Spielman recorded 29 tackles against That Team Up North. Think of the embraces when Michael Jenkins hauled in "Holy Buckeye" in West Lafayette, the collective exhale in Sun Devil Stadium two months later when Maurice Clarett ripped the ball out of Sean Taylor's hands, or the delirium that descended upon Ohio Stadium when Curtis Samuel crossed the goal line in double overtime to beat Michigan.

For the rest of my life, simply hearing the words "third-and-11" will transport me back to that night in January 2025 when a freshman wide receiver hauled in a 56-yard pass to all but secure a national championship. And I imagine that, if you're anything like me, that memory is inseparable from the people with whom you shared it, and you might be tearing up thinking about it, just as I am typing these words.

That is the enduring gift that Jeremiah Smith and generations of Buckeye legends have given us.

You will not convince me that anyone wants to win on Saturday more than J.J., who guaranteed that he would not lose to TTUN again while he was a Buckeye. And on Saturday, Ryan Day and Arthur Smith have a responsibility to not only put their team in the best possible position to beat their rival, but also to give the best player in college football every opportunity to deliver the kind of performance that Buckeye fans will be talking about for decades.

Someday, when we are telling younger generations of Buckeye fans about the greatest football players we ever saw, we will tell them about Jeremiah Smith. We will tell them about the impossible catches, the national championship, and the fire with which he played the game. But regardless of what happens on Saturday, or how many trophies he brings home in December, I hope that we spend a little less time lamenting what could have been this season or worrying about who will replace him once he's gone, and a little more time being grateful that we got to see him play at all.

What an immeasurable privilege it has been to be an Ohio State fan during the Jeremiah Smith era.
