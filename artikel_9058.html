Arpita Singh, Indian Painter Who Merged Fantasy and Gritty Realism, Dies at 89

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Arpita Singh, an Indian painter whose hallucinatory work fused allusions to real-world violence with uninterpretable dream imagery, died on Monday in New Delhi. She was 89, according to a report by the Tribune that cited confirmation from her husband, the artist Paramjit Singh.

Singh frequently switched her technique and way of working across her six-decade-long career, though she is today most well-known for hallucinatory figurative paintings that are steeped in Hindu lore and Indian history. In her most famous paintings, gods and laypeople appear to float through the air in landscapes that vaguely recall maps. Devi Pistol Wali (1990), arguably her most widely discussed painting, features a shrouded woman with many arms, one of which holds a gun to a man holding a sword. The painting has typically been described as a response to violence against women in modern-day India, but Singh discouraged these interpretations, saying that current events “come into my painting like the way light comes as colour and breeze comes as movement,” as she put it in a 2025 Guardian interview.

She also tended to rebut the repeated description of her work as dreamlike, since she could control what went into her art. “Her paintings are not dreamworlds—they are real worlds of her own creation,” wrote Meara Sharma in an Artforum essay on Devi Pistol Wali. “In the worlds of her paintings, objects do not ‘float,’ because gravity does not exist.”

Singh was one of the most high-profile Indian contemporary artists, with the artist receiving the esteemed Padma Bhushan award in 1991. The Kiran Nadar Museum of Art in New Delhi organized a retrospective of her work in 2019.

But outside India, Singh was not widely known until 2025, when the Serpentine Galleries staged a survey of her work in London. “An admired artist in India, Arpita Singh rarely shows in America and that is our loss,” wrote critic John Yau in a 2017 Hyperallergic review of an exhibition at New York’s Talwar Gallery, one of the few exhibitions of her work staged outside India that is listed on her CV. (Talwar is currently staging another Singh show, on view through February 27.)

Singh did not seem entirely fazed by the recent international reception of her work, however. In her Guardian profile, she merely called the Serpentine exhibition “a prestigious thing for me.”

Arpita Singh was born in 1937 in West Bengal, India, and moved in 1946 to New Delhi. Partition, the violent cleaving of British India into a Hindu-majority India and a Muslim-majority Pakistan, happened the following year and shaped some of Singh’s early memories. She recalled standing on her balcony and observing as a man was killed below.

She went on to attend Delhi Polytechnic’s art school, from which she graduated in 1959. Her teachers included modernists such as Biren De and Sailoz Mookherjea, and her education brought her into contact with books on European modernism. She recalled gravitating toward Der Blaue Reiter, Wassily Kandinsky, Paul Klee, and Marc Chagall.

Though Singh found success relatively early on in her career, showing with the dealer Kekoo Gendhy, who had helped make S. H. Raza and M. F. Husain famous, she felt uncertain about her figurations, which were done in a faux naive style. During the 1970s and ’80s, she started working in abstraction, producing austere, largely colorless images formed entirely from dots and dashes. By the end of the ’80s, however, she had returned to figuration.

There are signs that Singh is gradually growing in prominence. In 2021, her work appeared in a blockbuster exhibition of abstractions by women at the Centre Pompidou in Paris and that year’s edition of the Gwangju Biennale in South Korea. The next year, she showed at the Kochi-Muziris Biennale, India’s top contemporary art exhibition.

What became obvious as Singh grew more internationally famous was that few others have produced work like hers, both when she started and now. “I control the law,” Singh once said, speaking of the worlds she creates in her art. “I’m the master.”
