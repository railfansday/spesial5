Fourth Circuit deals Kalshi another loss in Maryland

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

The prediction market unsuccessfully argued federal law preempts the state from requiring it to comply with state laws on issues like deceptive advertising.Â

RICHMOND, Va. (CN) â A Fourth Circuit majority joined two other federal appellate courts Wednesday in holding that prediction markets like Kalshi are subject to state gambling laws.

“Congress knows how to displace traditional state police powers when it chooses to do so,” U.S. Circuit Judge Roger Gregory, a George W. Bush appointee, said. “Here, it has not done so with the clarity our precedents require.”

A Maryland federal judge denied Kalshi injunctive relief that would have blocked Maryland from requiring it to obtain a license to offer sports betting. Kalshi argued the Commodity Exchange Act gives the Commodity Futures Trading Commission exclusive jurisdiction over swaps â a type of derivative contract dependent on the occurrence of an event associated with a potential financial consequence.

“When betting platforms operate outside state oversight, Marylanders lose the protections that keep gambling fair and safe,” Maryland Attorney General Anthony Brown said in a statement. “Today’s ruling means Kalshi must meet the same consumer protection standards as every other sportsbook serving Maryland residents.”

Writing for the majority, Gregory ruled that the lower court properly framed the preemption question not as whether the sports event contracts offered are swaps, but whether Congress intended to preempt the state’s historic police power to regulate gambling. Gregory concluded Congress did not clearly and manifestly intend to preempt state gambling laws.

“The question is therefore not whether Congress regulates these contracts through the CEA,” the George W. Bush appointee said. “Rather, the question is whether Congress clearly intended the CEA to vest the CFTC with exclusive regulatory authority over gaming contracts, thereby withdrawing Maryland’s historic authority to regulate gambling in this area.”

U.S. Circuit Judge Stephanie Thacker joined Gregory’s opinion, differing however by arguing the preemption question turns on whether the sports event contracts are swaps. Thacker, agreeing with Gregory’s ultimate ruling, argued the sports contracts are not swaps subject to the commission’s exclusive regulatory authority.

“I reject the idea that the outcome of a sporting event is itself an event,” the Barack Obama appointee said in a concurring opinion. “Instead, I would adopt the common sense conclusion that an event, in the sports context, is the contest or match itself.”

Gregory pointed to Congress’s passage of the Dodd-Frank Act, which prevents the commission from allowing trading of unlawful events, including assassinations or terrorist attacks. The Dodd-Frank Act also lists trading on “gaming” as against the public interest and thus not authorized through the Commodity Exchange Act. According to Gregory, the Dodd-Frank Act shows that preemption is not resolved by whether event contracts are swaps.

Gregory disagreed with Kalshi’s argument that the commission’s federal authority over exchange trading displaces state laws addressing the separate states’ interests that Congress directed the commission to consider under the Dodd-Frank Act. Gregory also disagreed with Kalshi that state laws conflict with the Commodities Exchange Act.

“It does not determine whether a gaming contract may be listed or traded on a DCM â that authority remains with the CFTC,” Gregory said of Maryland’s gambling law. “Instead, it imposes generally applicable conditions on entities that choose to offer these sports event contracts within Maryland, including obligations to obtain a license and comply with applicable advertising restrictions.”

U.S. Circuit Judge DeAndrea Gist Benjamin dissented from the majority. The Joe Biden appointee ruled the contracts are swaps.

“The dissent’s view would allow Kalshi, or any person or entity for that matter, to circumvent state gambling laws by repackaging ordinary gambling as futures trading on a DCM,” Gregory said. “The statutory context of the CEA does not evince a ‘clear and manifest’ intention to preempt traditional state authority and permit this loophole.”

The Fourth Circuit joined the Sixth and Ninth Circuits in ruling that states can regulate Kalshi.
