The New Economics Of Data Center Delay

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Francesco Iorio is co-founder and CEO of Augmenta, a company automating building design for the construction industry.

Data center owners and developers are under enormous pressure to bring capacity online quickly. Every month a 60 MW facility sits idle costs its owner an estimated $14.2 million in lost revenue, financing costs and contractual penalties, according to STL Partners’ research.​

Experienced project teams already spend enormous effort identifying schedule exposure and squeezing time from procurement, sequencing, inspections, field execution and other familiar levers. Yet, some of the forces affecting energization remain difficult or impossible to control.​​​

Other forces are within an owner or developer’s control. Late design changes are a good example: Owners may deliberately change requirements to accommodate newer technology such as higher-performance GPUs with different rack, power or cooling requirements, because the economic benefits justify the disruption.​​

The challenge is therefore not always just preventing change, but building a delivery process capable of absorbing disruption.​​

That requires looking beyond the traditional places teams go to recover time. Some parts of the project workflow have historically been treated as relatively fixed because there was little practical ability to accelerate them or make them more responsive.

As technology reaches deeper into design, modeling and coordination, that assumption is beginning to change.​ Rather than replace proven schedule controls, the opportunity is to expand the field of view and to identify where technology has made previously difficult-to-influence parts of the workflow newly actionable.​​​​​​​

Design, building information modeling (BIM) and coordination are examples of areas where technology is beginning to change what is possible.

For instance, AI can now automate time-consuming modeling tasks, generate and evaluate routing options and identify potential conflicts earlier in the coordination process, reducing the manual effort traditionally required to produce and revise complex building designs.​

At Augmenta, where we’re developing spatial AI technology to automate building systems design, we’re seeing firsthand how these capabilities can give skilled practitioners more flexibility to refine designs and respond to project changes. While our work naturally informs my perspective, the broader opportunity extends well beyond any one solution.​

This is particularly relevant in data centers, where owner-driven changes to technology requirements can affect electrical, cooling and other systems. The faster teams can revisit and revise work already completed, the more room they have to accommodate those changes.​​

To identify these opportunities, a useful starting point is to talk to the people performing these workflows. Where is progress constrained by available skilled capacity? Which processes have historically been difficult to accelerate? Where does a change trigger significant downstream rework? And where has technology created options that didn’t exist a few years ago?​​

The answers may reveal constraints that teams previously had to accept but are now worth revisiting, not because they were managed poorly, but because the available tools have changed.​​

Identifying opportunities for acceleration is only part of the equation. Technology only reduces schedule risk if teams can deploy it without introducing greater disruption elsewhere.

The potential impact of any new technology must be balanced against implementation time, integrations, training and change management requirements.​​

This means prioritizing technologies that work within existing processes and give practitioners greater leverage rather than requiring wholesale workflow changes. A tool that accelerates one part of the project but creates additional work or delays elsewhere may simply relocate risk rather than reduce it.​​

To distinguish between the two, teams should start with the specific workflow constraint that a technology addresses and its potential schedule impact. They should also consider the time to value, adoption and training requirements and integration with existing workflows. Equally important is whether the technology can respond to changing project conditions and whether its benefits extend downstream.​​

The goal is to identify technologies that create meaningful flexibility within the delivery process without introducing new constraints that offset the gains.​​

One important consideration is where in the project workflow those gains occur. Not all time savings have the same effect on the overall schedule. Improvements in upstream workflows like design, modeling and coordination can create margin that carries into multiple downstream activities.​​

Accelerating these workflows can create flexibility and optionality later in the schedule, which greatly reduces risk. The value becomes especially apparent when requirements change: time saved upstream gives teams more room to revise, re-coordinate and respond without immediately affecting downstream milestones.​​

I’ve seen this shift firsthand on a large-scale data center project, where an electrical contractor used spatial AI design technology to accelerate the initial modeling process. Rather than spending weeks manually populating the BIM model, the team was able to generate an initial model much faster, freeing skilled virtual design and construction professionals to focus more of their time on reviewing the design, addressing constructibility considerations and preparing for downstream coordination.​

The takeaway goes beyond time saved to how the workflow changes when teams are no longer constrained by the same volume of repetitive manual modeling. That creates additional room to revisit design decisions, coordinate with other trades and accommodate evolving project requirements before they affect downstream activities.​

This is where “speed” becomes a risk-management tool rather than simply a productivity metric.​

Owners and developers cannot eliminate every threat to a data center schedule, nor should they abandon the proven controls they already use to manage risk. ​

But now, there is an opportunity to expand the field of view. As technology reaches deeper into design, modeling, coordination and other parts of the project lifecycle, processes that once offered little room to maneuver may become new sources of schedule margin.​​

By digging deeper into workflows that have historically been difficult to accelerate or influence, teams may find new opportunities to create flexibility before they need it and make the overall schedule more resilient when the inevitable disruption arrives.​​
