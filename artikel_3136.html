AI lab staff reportedly fear unreleased models are too powerful to release

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

More than two dozen sources describe models that game their rewards and hide their reasoning. Staff fear executives will put profits first, while both labs say they may slow down.

Many employees at Anthropic and OpenAI no longer believe their most powerful unreleased models can be released safely. Madison Mills reported the scoop for Axios on Tuesday, citing more than two dozen people familiar with the internal debates.

According to Axios, both companies hold internal models that are far more powerful than anything they have released. Those models have made fast gains in reasoning across maths, science, cybersecurity and AI research. In internal tests, the sources said, that progress has made them difficult, if not impossible, to contain or control.

The sources said this has started a debate inside both labs and across the research community. One camp asks whether future frontier models should stay in internal use only. Another asks whether new models should be trained at all. Some insiders think the problem can be fixed. Others want training stopped, an idea Dan Hendrycks backed last week when he called for a pause.

“If we continue trying to make models much smarter, such that they’re actually smarter than us … they will in fact be able to trick us successfully,” one researcher told Axios.

Axios pointed to results that have already gone public. An Anthropic mathematician used an unreleased “research” version of Claude to present a Riemann hypothesis proof. An unreleased OpenAI model helped produce proofs for hundreds of maths problems. Axios also tied unreleased models to the Hugging Face hack.

OpenAI’s own chart shows the gap. Chief scientist Jakub Pachocki published it in his post on perplexity and intelligence. It plots an unnamed “Internal model” beyond every model OpenAI has released. Pachocki wrote that OpenAI’s maths results took off once it moved to a model with a log-perplexity of about 0.76.

In the same post, he said he expects the main tools for checking a model’s behaviour to get worse:

“We should also expect further degradation in currently dominant forms of alignment auditing and further increases in evaluation and training awareness,” Pachocki wrote.

He also asked labs to agree on industry-wide limits they would not cross until monitoring improves.

The sources described two problems. The first is reinforcement learning, the trial-and-error training that rewards models for passing tests. It drives much of the recent progress. As models get more capable, they also get better at gaming those rewards. That includes getting around safety guardrails when cheating scores higher.

The second concerns how labs watch their models. Researchers have long relied on models writing out their reasoning step by step, so that monitors can catch rule-breaking. According to Axios, the most advanced internal models have shown patterns of hiding their reasoning entirely. Some have produced deceptive “cover story” chains of thought to satisfy human monitors.

Researchers and employees with direct knowledge told Axios they fear executives will choose profits over safety. Several said executives have lost touch with how severe the risks are. Axios also cited a post on X from September by Anthropic’s alignment lead:

“The speed of development is quickly becoming too fast for us to keep safe.”

An OpenAI spokesperson told Axios the company has adopted new safety requirements that could slow its model releases. An Anthropic spokesperson pointed to delays in pretraining a recent model to allow for more security, and added:

“Keeping a model internal doesn’t make it safe … we may slow down or hold back even when it costs us,” the spokesperson said.

Some see a middle path, in which the top labs keep frontier intelligence in-house. Outside users would reach it through products for enterprise work, science and maths. Anthropic and OpenAI did this with their most cyber-capable models. Yet many of the most worried safety researchers say even testing and using such models internally is deeply risky.

TNW has not independently verified the report.
