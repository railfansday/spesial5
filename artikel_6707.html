Rashida Jones and Will McCormack on Telling Sex Jokes and Building <i><em>The Invite </em></i>Around Its “Prodigiously Gifted” Cast

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

The two screenwriters adapted Cesc Gay’s The People Upstairs (2020) for a modern American audience

This summer, The Invite dropped audiences into a turbulent middle-aged marriage where only bitterness simmers beneath a thin, leftover layer of love and any sexual desire vanished long ago. The couple at the center of the story, Joe (Seth Rogen) and Angela (Olivia Wilde, who also directed the film), live at each other’s throats, feigning a relationship for their daughter’s sake. This brittle romance is put to the test when a neighboring couple (Ed Norton and Penélope Cruz) come over for dinner and propose, well, group sex. Bottled-up emotions and memories fill the night as four dinner guests banter and battle, delivering the best sex comedy in years, elevated by witty, cathartic dialogue from screenwriters Rashida Jones and Will McCormack.

A great sex comedy relies on a great premise, whether it’s designed to shock or move its audience. Jones and McCormack reworked Spanish filmmaker Cesc Gay’s The People Upstairs (also known as Sentimental), crafting a script that balances both, exploring how romantic turmoil emerges from a culture of emotional suppression and what happens when this lifestyle rubs up against a more radically open approach to feelings and sex. Much of the realism in The Invite comes from Jones and McCormack closely studying their own relationships with love and loss.

“We had all these big dreams when we were young,” Jones says. “It’s a real embarrassment of riches. It’s when you get to 50, and you’ve had a lot of your dreams come true, and you have all the things that you dreamed about when you were 20, 30, and even 40. Here we are, and what now? The ‘what now’ is infused in the movie.”

Decades of friendship have informed Jones and McCormack’s writing. They first met on a blind date in 1999, but the romantic relationship did not stick; in its place, a creative partnership blossomed. In 2012, they released their first full-length feature, Celeste and Jesse Forever, a bittersweet examination of divorce and friendship starring Jones and Andy Samberg.

“When we were young, we were writing about the heartbreak of love, and now we’re writing about the heartbreak of life,” McCormack says. “Marriage is not this triumphant experience. It’s a broken hallelujah; it’s one day at a time. That’s the beauty of it: the endurance of it and the longevity of it.”

Bazaar sat down and spoke with the pair about tailoring their script around a quartet of legendary actors, the “accordion effect” of Olivia Wilde’s direction, and how the two screenwriters have bounced ideas (and sex jokes) off each other for three decades.

Rashida Jones: In 2022, we were approached by David Permit, one of our wonderful producers, and we were hired to write a script for a whole different version of this movie for different directors. It was probably the most fun I’ve ever had, writing that first draft with Will. We just had a great time because we saw the movie and it totally works and it’s culturally specific. We watched it at first, and we thought, “Wow, it’s perfectly executed. What can we contribute?” Then you watch it again and again and again, and you start having conversations about your own life and your own relationships and people that are on your mind. We started to feel like we might have something to say in this world and that could add value and be more culturally specific to being American. How we would have this conversation—this evening, in our country. It felt like the perfect combination of source material and then a place to grow.

RJ: I’ll take the easy part. We’re just immature, and we like to make sex jokes to each other because we’ve been best friends for 30 years, so that was easy.

Will McCormack: There’s no DP. We brought all the DP.

WM: We were having fun with that stuff. Cesc Gay’s movie is so brilliant. When we watched it, you think, “Oh my God, this is so beautiful.” But then, as Rashida said, you start to think about it more, and you say, “Wouldn’t it be fun to do this?” And then you get the job, and you actually get to do that. It’s so rare. When we watched the original one, I remember halfway through, we said to each other, he’s going to go in the other room at the end and play the piano, and it’s going to wreck us. And he didn’t. I liked their ending, but we thought, this is going to be so fun to dive into: [Seth Rogen’s character] pulling up the piano lid, sitting at the keys, and there’s this Graduate-style ending where she’s going to walk in and the camera is going to end on her face, and [we’re left thinking], is she in or is she out? That was our entry point to this version. It’s how we got the job, because we opened the conversation with the ending.

RJ: Rhythm is so important to us. The truth is, when you infuse a role with an actor, rhythms change because there are things that make sense coming out of people’s mouths and things that don’t. For us, this period before shooting was our rewrite period, which was insane because we were doing eight-hour days with the actors and getting a download of their ideas and concerns, then rewriting simultaneously. That period was really when the movie was the version of the movie on the screen. It was designed for these particular actors on the screen. Their cadences entered our heads, so we needed to rewrite with them in mind. We were trying our best Penélope Cruz and Seth Rogen impressions.

WC: These actors are all prodigiously gifted and giants. They all brought their own pathos and idiosyncrasies. Penélope brought this incredible mystery and mystique and aura. Edward brought this radical honesty. Olivia brought a woman on the verge of a nervous breakdown at all times. Seth brought this profound simplicity. So it was such a gift to work with them and help tailor these parts to their voices.

RJ: Penélope had a really strong instinct. She’s one of our best. What can I possibly say about her? She really wanted to talk about perimenopause, which I too have that cross to bear at the moment. She wanted to get into it and was very conscious that her character has depth. She didn’t want her to have this wise, objective point of view. She wanted to have her own life and flaws. All that stuff comes out at the end, where she bites back at Hawk and tells him to shut the fuck up and then apologizes quickly. You get a window into their relationship. That really came out of conversations of “How can we show how imperfect their very new relationship is? How can we show that everybody’s struggling with their version of an issue?” It’s not all perfect over there just because they have figured out a way to live in an open relationship.

WC: The magic of movie-making is that there are a million different versions of The Invite. This version could’ve only happened at this moment in time, in this city, with this director, with these four people. It’s magic. I was most surprised by Penélope, who was so smart and didn’t want to feel like this wise woman on the hilltop delivering sage advice to these people in the room. Because life is not like that. It’s messy and complicated, and she has her own problems. When actors are that good, they just surprise you over and over again. We’ve said it before, but the best element of storytelling and screenwriting is surprise—when you don’t know what’s going to happen next and you’re on the edge of your seat.

WC: Edward is one of the best actors we have as well. He is so smart. Right from the very beginning of the rehearsal process, he wanted. He was adamant that we talk a lot about Hawk’s backstory and that his sexual expansiveness came from a place of real pain and trauma. We talked a lot about Hawk’s backstory. We talked about how he had massive regret and pain and that he had a moment in his life where he was going to fold or turn over a new leaf and live without regret. It was incredible to talk to him about it. We never heard the final version [of the speech until the day we filmed].

RJ: He wanted to surprise the actors. We wrote him a template, a blueprint, so he had that. Then he could do whatever he wanted because he had something in mind. The sentiment was there, and it was something we had talked about because we wanted to make sure whatever, wherever it ended up, had to be tender and raw. Coming out of that, it gave Joe another opportunity to be uncomfortable with feelings. He could just make a joke, and that would set off the next part of the interaction, so everybody could be mad at him for being immature.

WC: We have Joe’s line slotted there, “I’m sorry that your dead wife couldn’t peg you,” which then detonates the entire thing again. Olivia did such a great job of orchestrating this accordion effect of quiet to blow up to quiet.

RJ: We have our go-tos. Nora Ephron. We’re always watching James L. Brooks for rhythm, pacing, and dialogue. Of course, Who’s Afraid of Virginia Woolf? was a reference for us because it’s a movie we grew up both loving and a play we love. It has this chamber-piece tension that you need to keep. There are literal steals and direct references to that movie in our version, [such as including a character who works as] an associate professor. Movies like Bob & Carol & Ted & Alice and, in general, Paul Mazursky for the reality of being in a messy relationship.

WC: [Ruben Östlund’s] Force Majeure and always Joachim Trier for the honesty of people being really vulnerable with each other. It’s never corny, and it’s really heartbreaking and true and admirable. The end of The Invite gets very real when she says, “We were best friends, and we had sex all the time, and it was incredible.” There is a moment where it really lands.

RJ: Everybody’s so different. We talk a lot because, for us, theme is king. We want to make sure we know what we want to say. We identify how we want to feel and how we want other people to feel, and we write toward that. We’re not going to leave feeling like there’s a bad guy; we want to leave feeling like we root for them as people, separately and together. Whatever happens after this night, this was a necessary step in excavating the deepest emotions and truth that were never revealed, and they are left vulnerable with tenderness and gratitude for the relationship they did have. We talk in those terms, and then we get into structure, and then we can talk about which scenes are which and how we get there.

WC: Luckily, we’ve known each other for so long, and we’re so comfortable with each other. That’s so important to a partnership. I’m not afraid to pitch any idea to Rashida and vice versa. I’m not afraid to write poorly. I feel total freedom of expression, which is an important thing in collaboration. Our lives have orbited each other in an interesting way as friends, but also creatively. We’re both really into loss, and life is a series of losses. There’s so much heartbreak and loss, but there’s so much hope and humor. The hardest moments of our lives have been the funniest, and it’s been a gift to write it all down with her. Hopefully, we can do it again!
