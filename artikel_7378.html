Ad Tech Briefing: Google’s AI-reckoning

It’s earnings season, typically the time of year when industry observers watch agog, as Big Tech companies post earnings far greater than the average ad tech company can begin to contemplate, but Q3 this year has thrown us a curveball to the usual narrative.

The unexpected glitch in the latest round ran contrary to the expected narrative, i.e., Google’s punishment for the success of its AI, a testament to the extent of the harsh realities facing the digital media sector as a whole when it comes to sustaining revenue.

It’s also likely to serve as an important footnote in the future filings of Google’s legal department, with tuned-in observers likely to recall how it was able to cite rapid AI advancements as proof of a fiercely competitive and evolving tech landscape.

Even if Google did lose both trials (on paper, at least) last year, it can be argued the above argument was made successfully to justify it continuing without forced divestiture, and in Q3 (it would appear) this argument impacted the biggest company in advertising’s bottom line.

Since then, Google has spent much of the year telling advertisers and investors that AI will make its search business bigger, not smaller. However, last week’s earnings offered the clearest indication yet that delivering on that promise is proving complicated.

On Oct. 28, Google’s parent company Alphabet reported that its search queries were growing at their fastest annual rate in five years, with total Q3 revenues nearing $127.6 billion, up 25% year-on-year.

However, a deeper analysis reveals a more troubling trend for the hegemon of Madison Avenue: more people are searching, but Google’s AI-driven search experiences aren’t yet generating ad revenue at the same rate as conventional search.

However, revenue from Google’s “Search & other” operations increased 12% to $63.35 billion, with Google’s chief business officer Philipp Schindler acknowledging as much during the company’s third-quarter earnings call, telling analysts that AI Mode’s comparatively limited monetization had contributed to the slowdown.

The disclosure is significant because search remains Google’s biggest financial engine and supports its (increasingly expensive) AI ambitions.

Advertising revenue, i.e., search in combination with other areas of its media operations, such as “YouTube” (Q3 revenues of $11.2 billion) and “Network” (Q3 revenues of $7.45 billion) — that’s the ad tech side, which is continuing its prolonged decline in revenue — accounts for approximately 64% of Alphabet’s revenue, down from 72% a year earlier.

More queries, less revenue per opportunity

Wall Street was unimpressed. Alphabet shares fell approximately 12.6% on Oct. 29, erasing close to $500 billion in market value, as investors questioned the economics of the company’s AI transition with coverage in the immediate aftermath of the results highlighting disappointment in Google’s revenue performance.

Google maintains that AI is expanding search activity rather than merely replacing existing queries. Sundar Pichai, CEO, Alphabet, told analysts that AI Mode queries are, on average, three times longer than traditional searches, while approximately one in five involves voice, images or documents.

However, executives also acknowledged that AI Mode currently monetizes at a lower rate, and further hinted that it is now expanding advertising within AI Mode internationally and introducing multi-turn advertisements in the U.S., allowing commercial placements to appear during extended conversations.

Meanwhile, the continued struggles of “Google Network” revenues, i.e., ad tech, is likely to stimulate further grimacing from publishers, with this unit generating a modest 1.4% gain in revenues during the period, with this trend accompanied by an overall increase in traffic acquisition costs, or “TAC,” of approximately 10% during the period.

Overall, TAC hit $16.4 billion, with the figures highlighting how higher impression prices can mask shrinking volumes as publishers contend with declining search referrals and unpredictable traffic. This creates an awkward backdrop for Google’s court-ordered ad tech remedies. Prebid’s proposed auction overhaul could improve publisher control and interoperability, but neither can restore lost audiences or guarantee advertiser demand.
