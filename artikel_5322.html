FinCEN Bars US Institutions From Transmitting Funds to MEXC: What Changes and What Doesn't

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

AI-assisted summary, reviewed by NullTX editors.

FinCEN's November 6 order cuts US financial institutions off from MEXC Global, barring any transmittal of funds to or from the exchange. The order spells out what is banned, what MEXC is accused of, and how the exchange has answered.

MEXC says user assets remain secure. The FinCEN order never suggested otherwise, and the gap between the exchange's reassurance and the order's actual target is the key to this story.

On November 6, the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network issued a final order under Section 9714(a) of the Combating Russian Money Laundering Act. It names MEXC Global a financial institution of primary money laundering concern in connection with Russian illicit finance. From there, it bars "covered financial institutions," a category that includes banks, broker-dealers and money transmitters, from any transmittal of funds to or from MEXC Global. Transmittals, as defined, include convertible virtual currency.

On the MEXC side, the reach is wide. The order covers the exchange's subsidiaries, branches and offices outside the United States: MEXC Global Ltd and MX Global Ltd in Seychelles, MEXC Estonia OÜ, GIDA Technology AG in Switzerland and MXC Global Ltd in the United Kingdom. Former Singapore entities and any successors are included as well. US-based affiliates, by contrast, are expressly excluded.

The ban won't bite right away. It takes effect 30 days after publication in the Federal Register, which had not happened as of the announcement, and it carries no end date. FinCEN keeps the authority to attach conditions and grant exemptions, and willful violations can lead to civil and criminal penalties under 31 U.S.C. 5321 and 5322.

MEXC's statement is short and carefully built. The exchange says it is aware of the order and is reviewing it with its legal and compliance teams. It describes itself as operating normally, with user assets secure. It also says it takes the concerns seriously, plans to engage constructively with FinCEN and other regulators, and has started an independent review of its AML and sanctions controls.

Two points stand out. For one, the reassurance lines up with the order's text. Nothing in it freezes balances or halts trading, so "operating normally" is plausible. The threat is to MEXC's access to US financial counterparties, not to the exchange's wallets.

The other point is what the statement skips. It disputes none of the allegations, and the independent review it promises targets AML and sanctions controls, the very area where FinCEN says the exchange failed. The order also invites anyone with relevant information to submit it to FinCEN, so MEXC already has a formal channel for the constructive engagement it describes.

The charges are specific. FinCEN assesses that MEXC facilitated at least $16.7 billion in likely illicit activity between May 2021 and May 2026, a figure Treasury's announcement rounded to $16 billion. The order splits that total across several categories.

On Russia, FinCEN cites nearly $1.5 billion in direct and indirect exposure to sanctions-evasion transactions, including $232 million in direct dealings with entities sanctioned by the US, EU or UK. According to the order, MEXC processed about $129 million with Garantex and $4.1 million with its successor Grinex after both were designated, with activity continuing as recently as June 2026. It also points to at least $26 million benefiting the A7 Network and nearly $200 million tied to the ruble-backed A7A5 token. Beyond that, FinCEN says MEXC handled more than $13 billion with four UK-sanctioned exchanges between May 2018 and July 2026.

Iran features just as heavily. The order cites nearly $2 billion tied to Iranian actors or terrorist groups, including about $1.87 billion in exposure to Iran and over $1 billion in direct transactions with more than 40 Iranian exchanges. About $22 million is linked to the Central Bank of Iran and the IRGC Quds Force. For North Korea, the order describes roughly $350,000 received by a sanctioned money launderer between June and September 2025. On cybercrime, it cites $1.99 billion in flows linked to illicit cyber actors and $37.9 million in suspected ransomware payments between January 2022 and July 2024.

The order then turns to MEXC's own rulebook. Its stated KYC and AML policies prohibit accounts in certain jurisdictions, yet FinCEN says the exchange facilitated transactions involving them. The order also notes that MEXC has operated without a license in Seychelles, Hong Kong and Japan, and it points to the Seychelles Financial Services Authority's May 2026 enforcement action against MX Global Ltd. All of this reflects FinCEN's assessments, drawn from public and non-public information, and MEXC has not answered them point by point.

The order is not an OFAC sanctions designation, and the difference matters. A funds-transmittal ban limits what US-covered institutions can do with MEXC. It does not make it illegal for ordinary users anywhere in the world to hold an account there. The order also states that it leaves existing Bank Secrecy Act and OFAC obligations untouched.

Section 9714 gives Treasury two routes: the special measures of Section 311 of the USA PATRIOT Act, or a ban on certain transmittals of funds. FinCEN took the second and explained its reasoning. Recordkeeping requirements would still let transfers continue, and a correspondent-account restriction would miss funds moving outside a strict correspondent relationship. FinCEN also went with an order rather than a rulemaking, citing imminent threats and ongoing transactions.

One provision stands out for its practicality. FinCEN acknowledges that a covered institution often can't reject incoming crypto before it arrives, so the order allows discretion in cases such as funds that sat in an unhosted wallet for an extended period. The carve-out underlines how difficult enforcement will be in practice.

For most MEXC customers outside the US, nothing changes on day one. Counterparties face a tougher picture. Once the order takes effect, US banks, regulated exchanges and money transmitters will have to block transmittals to and from MEXC, and global institutions with US exposure have strong reasons to follow suit. FinCEN says plainly that the order puts US and foreign financial institutions and regulators on notice.

Before issuance, the draft went to the Justice Department, State, the Federal Reserve, the FDIC, the SEC, the CFTC, the OCC and the NCUA, and none objected. The order also fits into a wider Treasury campaign. It cites the October 1 Operation Economic Outcast action against the A7 Network, and a separate FinCEN proposal on A7 sub-agents followed days later.

Predicting how users or markets will react would be guesswork. What users can do is track the dates, know which institutions they depend on, and watch for official updates from MEXC.

FinCEN used the same Section 9714 authority against the exchange Bitzlato in January 2023. This time, though, the target is by FinCEN's own account among the 20 largest exchanges by trading volume, with roughly 9% of centralized exchange market share as of December 2025 and about $5 billion in reserve assets as of June 2026. Applying a transmittal ban to an exchange of that size signals a more aggressive posture.

Three things will show what comes next. The first is the Federal Register publication date, which starts the 30-day clock. The second is whether FinCEN grants any exemptions or conditions. The third is what MEXC's independent review turns up, and whether the exchange shares the results. For now, the statement buys MEXC time without answering the order. The real answer will come from changes to its controls, and from whether its banking and liquidity partners are satisfied with them.
