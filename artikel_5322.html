Optum’s CEO still works weekends as a pediatrician. What he sees ‘should not happen in America, but it does every single day’

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

On his last couple of weekends working as a hospital pediatrician in Boston, Dr. Patrick Conway saw three patients he can’t stop talking about.

One was a 14-year-old girl who had called more than 100 people trying to get mental health care before she ended up in his hospital with broken legs. Another was a 21-year-old with meningitis. “She wakes up. What’s the first thing she says? I’m a single mother. I can’t afford this. I got to get out of here,” Conway said. The third was a 15-year-old on Medicaid who needed complex rehab for a neurologic disorder, and the hospital was struggling to find anywhere to send her.

“That should not happen in America, but it does every single day,” he said.

During the week, Conway runs Optum, the health services arm of UnitedHealth Group, which he said employs about 300,000 of the conglomerate’s roughly 400,000 people. That makes him one of the more unusual witnesses to American health care’s failures, because his company sits on the other side of many of them. Speaking at the Fortune 500 Innovation Forum in Detroit on Monday, he told Fortune’s Diane Brady that the weekend shifts are the point. “Those stories could be anywhere in America,” he said, and Optum’s job is “to prevent those stories from occurring in the future.”

Brady put the tension more bluntly. Optum, she said, is a bit like the cable guy: people need it, and they don’t always love it. Conway didn’t argue with that. He said Optum’s business customers (health systems, payers, employers) are “generally quite positive.” On the insurance side, “people have concerns,” he said, listing access, prior authorization and whether their doctors are in network.

Conway took over Optum in 2025, a year in which UnitedHealth replaced CEO Andrew Witty with former CEO, Stephen Hemsley, and said it was cooperating with federal investigations into its Medicare business.

Conway has seen the system from most of its angles. He joined Health and Human Services as chief medical officer late in the George W. Bush administration and held several roles at Medicare and Medicaid, mostly under Barack Obama, “which is a little less common these days.” He has also run a Blues plan. His first week as an attending physician at Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia, he said, a child arrived with a 1,000-page paper chart after years of bouncing between community hospitals. On page 487, he spotted a rare mitochondrial disorder. “In today’s world, that child should be diagnosed anywhere in America … within seconds, minutes,” he said.

His case for Optum is that scale and AI can close those gaps faster than a fragmented system can. On affordability, he pointed to Optum Rx’s Savings IQ tool. He said it brought the median copay for specialty drugs down from about $88 to $5 and saved consumers more than $1 billion out of pocket over the past 12 months. One patient with cancer emailed him to say he’d been going on and off his medicine because it cost about $300, and he hadn’t told his wife. “Now it’s $5,” Conway said. “I’m on my medicine. I’m doing better.”

He also pitched OptumReal, which digitizes benefits and connects payers with health systems. Conway said it can approve a service with “99 plus percent accuracy,” so the payer, the provider and the patient all see the same number at the point of care. “That’s how healthcare should work,” he said. Asked when it would be the norm, he said it “may become faster than you imagine,” because payers and providers “know the system doesn’t work as it is today.”

Brady also asked about Mark Cuban, who has spent years arguing that pharmacy benefit managers like Optum Rx are what’s driving drug prices up. Conway noted he once debated Cuban in Washington: “I think he was a little surprised. I accepted the debate.” He called Cuban’s model “a niche solution for some number of drugs” that helps people, and said the system needs something more comprehensive.

Brady raised Pfizer CEO Albert Bourla’s argument that cancer will one day be as manageable as diabetes. Conway, who lost his father to cancer, said he hopes Bourla is right. “It may take a little longer,” he said.

He closed on time. He’s been trying to fix health care for 20 to 25 years, he said. “I don’t know if I’ve got another 20. I hope so. It’s got to go faster.”

Nick Lichtenberg is business editor and was formerly Fortune's executive editor of global news.
