Satellites watched a cluster of thunderstorms become Cyclone Xavier

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

Satellite images show how quickly Tropical Cyclone Xavier developed a distinct eye as it strengthened near the northern Cook Islands.

Today’s Image of the Day features Tropical Cyclone Xavier spinning over the South Pacific near the northern Cook Islands.

Bands of white clouds curve around the storm’s eye. In satellite images from a few hours earlier, the eye was still forming.

The MODIS instrument aboard NASA’s Aqua satellite captured this scene at 4:53 p.m. Cook Islands time on November 16, 2026.

By then, Xavier’s strongest sustained winds were estimated at about 120 miles per hour.

The clouds would tighten around a storm that was still gaining strength.

A few days earlier, the system that became Xavier was a loose cluster of thunderstorms.

In microwave satellite images from November 14, its structure was hard to make out.

By late November 16, distinct rainbands curved around a well-defined eye.

A separate sequence from the GOES-18 weather satellite shows the eye becoming easier to pick out over the course of hours.

The difference between those views comes down to what each instrument can see.

A daylight image like this one shows sunlight reflected by clouds and the ocean.

High clouds can hide the circulation beneath them, making it difficult to tell where a developing storm is centered.

Microwave observations can reveal parts of the rain structure beneath those clouds, giving forecasters another way to follow a storm as it organizes.

The eye is typically the calmest part of a mature tropical cyclone. Air sinks there, which helps keep clouds from forming.

Just outside it, warm, moist air rises in a ring of thunderstorms called the eyewall. That ring is usually where the storm’s strongest winds are found.

Farther from the center, the sweeping bands visible from space can bring bursts of heavy rain and wind, with quieter stretches in between.

Someone on an island can experience several rounds of rough weather as successive bands pass overhead, even when the eye stays out at sea.

From above, the bands look like parts of a single spiral. On the ground, they can feel like a series of separate storms.

The ocean beneath Xavier was about 84 degrees Fahrenheit, warm enough to supply plenty of heat and moisture. Winds also changed relatively little with height.

That meant there was little wind shear to tilt the storm and push its thunderstorms away from the center, allowing them to build around the same developing circulation.

Xavier also formed near the southwestern edge of a broad stretch of unusually warm water associated with El Niño.

During El Niño years, cyclones in the Southwest Pacific tend to form farther toward the central Pacific, shifting where island communities are most likely to encounter them.

Xavier developed on the eastern side of the international date line, near the northern Cook Islands.

The storm eventually reached Category 5 on the scale used in the South Pacific. As it moved west, it encountered drier air and stronger wind shear and began to weaken.

On Manihiki, one of the northern Cook Islands, the rain kept falling after the winds began to ease.

A resident told Pacific Media Network that some homes had lost sections of corrugated roofing and that electricity remained a concern.

The island was relying on solar power after losing its main diesel generator more than a year earlier, and the storm’s persistent cloud cover made it harder to keep batteries charged.

Emergency generators helped residents keep essential supplies cold. The island had also begun preparing well before Xavier appeared on satellite maps.

Each October, the island council begins removing hazardous coconut trees near homes and roads, a practice shaped by the damage Cyclone Martin inflicted on the northern Cook Islands in 1997.

Aqua passed over Xavier once that afternoon, catching a brief moment when the storm’s eye was in clear view.

On Manihiki, one of the most consequential features of the storm was the cloud cover that lingered after the worst winds had passed.
