Getting this right protects everyone involved, including the people who genuinely rely on these animals every day.

Disabilities vary widely, from physical and sensory to psychiatric, intellectual, and neurological. The needs of each person are different, so the trained tasks may look different from person to person.  The ADA cites that the service animal must be a dog or, in rare cases, a miniature horse. Federal law does not require certifications, registrations, vests, or ID cards for these animals.

Beyond the specification that the animal must be trained to perform tasks, the legal standard is limited, so it can be difficult to easily identify which animals are legitimately service dogs and which are not. This is where public confusion starts.

This is the distinction many find hardest to grasp, and it matters because different laws apply to each. Service dogs are protected under the ADA and, due to their training and the tasks they perform, they are permitted in public spaces such as restaurants, retail stores, and hotels. That could mean guiding someone who is visually impaired, alerting an owner to an oncoming seizure, or reminding them to take medication. Service animals are also protected under the Fair Housing Act and require paperwork if the disability or need for the animal is not obviously understood.

Emotional support animals (ESAs) are different. Their value comes from the comfort their presence provides. They donât necessarily have to be dogs, they arenât required to be trained to perform specific tasks, and theyâre not covered by the ADA to be allowed in public spaces like restaurants.

They are, however, protected by the Fair Housing Act. Under this, landlords are generally required to make allowances for an ESA if a tenant has appropriate documentation from a licensed healthcare provider establishing a disability-related need. Examples of disabilities warranting an ESA include, but are not limited to, anxiety disorders or autism.

ESA accommodations are supposed to come through legitimate means, typically an established relationship with a licensed medical or mental health professional who can document the need. But some people have found loopholes for this by filling out forms online or paying fees for instant letters, which only fuels public skepticism about the legitimate need for these animals. Not to mention, this abuse can make people wary of service dogs, too.

While there is no foolproof visual test, trained service animals typically display consistent, recognizable behaviors. These dogs show exemplary discipline and obedience to the commands of their handler. They have the ability to ignore external distractions from other dogs, people, food, sounds, and smells while focusing on the needs of their handler. They display a calm temperament and are not aggressive or anxious. It is also expected that these animals be quiet, unless they are tasked with alerting an owner to a medical issue, and that they are housebroken and trained to walk on a leash without unnecessary pulling or circling.

Asking beyond the two questions, even if it comes from a place of genuine curiosity or caution, can expose a business to discrimination claims.

News stories about supposed service animals harming bystanders in public continue to raise questions about the legitimacy of those animals, and businesses are often caught in the crossfire. These incidents are symptoms of a larger problem: businesses and property owners navigating without clear guidance, while people with legitimate needs are met with suspicion.

Service animals and emotional support animals play an essential role in the lives of the people who rely on them, and protecting that role starts with understanding the rules.
