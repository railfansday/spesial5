China’s Greater Bay Area was built to rival Silicon Valley. Nearly a decade on, is it finally living up to the hype?

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Watch the sunset from Macau, China’s gambling capital, and you’ll see the sun drop behind a cluster of gleaming skyscrapers on an island across a narrow strait.

As recently as 2009, Hengqin—an island in the mainland Chinese city of Zhuhai, just across from Macau—was home to oyster farmers and fishermen. Today the island, three times Macau’s size, is home to an “In-Depth Cooperation Zone,” a development initiative run by Guangdong province and the Macau Special Administrative Region to help the latter diversify its economy away from gambling by tapping mainland talent and manufacturing capacity.

But Hengqin’s multilane highways, striking architecture, and manicured parks haven’t yet brought people with them. In 2009, authorities hoped Hengqin would have 280,000 residents by 2020; by the end of 2025, it had just 50,000.

Hengqin is one test of a much larger integration project: the Guangdong–Hong Kong–Macau Greater Bay Area (GBA), which comprises nine cities around the Pearl River Delta in Guangdong province, including Shenzhen, Zhuhai, and Guangzhou, plus the Hong Kong and Macau special administrative regions. Can the GBA turn connectivity and deeper integration into growth?

The GBA spans three legal systems, currencies, and customs territories. With 88 million people, the region has a larger population than Germany or Turkey; with a GDP of $2.1 trillion, its economy approaches Canada’s in size. Connecting the region’s capital, startups, and manufacturing could create a world-class innovation ecosystem.

At least, that’s how the argument goes. Beijing has chased the idea of the GBA for around a decade, but the benefits of integration have only recently begun to emerge. The GBA is real, but it remains a Hong Kong–Shenzhen story for now, pairing the former’s financial heft with the latter’s hardware and software prowess. The next question is whether the other cities will get to join in.

The Pearl River Delta has been one of China’s core economic engines for over four decades, since the country designated Shenzhen, a border market town near the then–British colony of Hong Kong, as a business-friendly “special economic zone.”

That decision helped grow this part of southern China into an economic powerhouse: Hong Kong’s financial sector surged as China opened up; Shenzhen attracted founders from across the country; and surrounding cities like Dongguan and Foshan provided manufacturing scale to make the region the “world’s factory.”

“There are very few Chinese cities where the idea of ‘build it and they will come’ has not worked out.”

The GBA, formalized in 2017, wants to double down on those ingredients. “The original idea was to compete with the [U.S.] Bay Area. That’s exactly why the name is the Greater Bay Area,” says Alicia Garcia-Herrero, chief economist for Asia-Pacific at Natixis.

“The development of the GBA does not seek to make cities in the GBA identical, or flatten their distinctive edges, but rather aims for interoperability and complementarity across different cities and systems,” says Pamela Lam, Hong Kong’s commissioner for the development of the Greater Bay Area. The goal is to build “a complete innovation and industrial ecosystem” to enable a “high-value-adding supply chain that links the entire pipeline from lab to factory.”

Connective infrastructure is a key policy focus. Hundreds of kilometers of railway lines and projects such as the Hong Kong–Zhuhai–Macau Bridge, the world’s longest bridge-and-tunnel sea crossing costing an estimated $20 billion, have been built between cities. The goal is to create a “one-hour living circle”—bringing travel time between major GBA cities to within an hour—to foster collaboration.

“The Greater Bay Area and the U.S. Bay Area are running different operating systems,” says Mehran Gul, author of The New Geography of Innovation. “The U.S. Bay Area is more organic … It almost happened by accident.” In the GBA, he says, “the division of labor has been central to making it happen.”

This greater connectivity of capital, R&D, talent, and manufacturing may already be boosting innovation. The Global Innovation Index, published by the World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO), ranked Shenzhen–Hong Kong–Guangzhou as the world’s leading innovation cluster in both 2025 and 2026. The GBA’s nine mainland cities filed more than a third of China’s international patent applications in 2025, according to state media; the region also accounts for a fifth of the country’s unicorn startups.

“Ten years ago, you could still make the argument that the Chinese tech industry is a mirror image of the U.S. that lags by about five or six years,” Gul says. “But China’s tech industry is now driven a lot more by fundamental innovation. The fact that Chinese companies have made that transition means that those linkages have been made. The GBA was a big part of that.”

Economic data is painting a less positive picture. Guangdong has grown more slowly than the national average every year from 2021 to 2025. “Growth-wise, before COVID, the GBA was excelling compared to the rest of China. Not anymore,” Garcia-Herrero says, adding that China’s property market slump and youth unemployment weigh particularly heavily on Guangdong, the latter exacerbated by increased AI-powered industrial automation.

Growth within the region is lopsided, too. “A failing of the GBA is that the increase in productivity and value creation is not being spread out that widely,” says Jerry Patchell, associate professor emeritus at the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology and author of a book on the region. “Shenzhen and Hong Kong are pulling away from the pack. Guangzhou’s not too far behind, but a lot of the other cities are not progressing at the same rate. The absolute differences in per capita income between them are significant.” (Hong Kong’s per capita GDP is more than five times Zhaoqing’s, the poorest city in the GBA; Shenzhen’s is three times.)

Nor is Hong Kong’s financial heft favoring its neighbors. “Specific measures to treat the Greater Bay Area preferentially aren’t really there. I think the key point would be for Hong Kong to financially feed Guangdong. But the reality is that Hong Kong is feeding the whole mainland,” Garcia-Herrero adds.

Still, there are early signs that growth may be recovering. In the first three quarters of 2026, Guangdong’s economy grew 4.6%—matching the national growth rate for the first time since the third quarter of 2021, according to its provincial statistics bureau.

The growth in demand for AI infrastructure, as well as robotics, autonomous vehicles, and other applications of physical AI, is “bringing important new growth impetus” to the GBA, says Stephanie Gao, a professor of practice at City University of Hong Kong, with the region gaining an edge from its “tight integration of hardware design, component supply, and software algorithm iteration.”

Gul credits that edge to the speed with which companies iterate on the products they make, “measured in days rather than months.”

Elwin Ho, a Macau native and cofounder and CEO of Pachira, a speech-recognition AI company headquartered in Hengqin, sees the same pattern. “I just visited the [U.S.] Bay Area a couple of weeks ago. I saw a lot of companies that [build] their software and algorithms in the Bay Area, but for the hardware they go to Shenzhen.”

He is bullish on Hengqin, too: “It just needs a little bit more time; the area will have a very good future.”

Gul, too, thinks the region’s underutilized spaces will get their residents and businesses in the years to come: “There are very few Chinese cities where the idea of ‘Build it and they will come’ has not worked out.”
