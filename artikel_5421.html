2027 Predictions Fall Flat, a ‘Seller’s Market’ to Rise (Barely): Report

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Real estate typically experiences a slowdown toward the end of each year, with 2026 proving to be no exception. But the outlook for 2027 does not spark hope for many economists, according to the latest predictions.

Homes.com’s 2027 Housing Market Outlook reportedly expects national home sales to fall 5.6%, with national prices to rise just 0.04%. Essentially, a flat outlook for housing.

“Homes should sell a little faster, sellers should give up slightly less from their original asking prices and months of supply should decline,” the report states. “The result is a quieter market that tilts slightly toward sellers even though national prices barely move.”

Yet, the national outlook is the collective null of several local variations, says Homes.com Chief Residential Economist Brad Case. By looking at 40 of the largest markets, Case expects home prices to fall in 24 of them while rising in the other 16.

Midwestern real estate agents may experience a vastly different housing market to their Southern counterparts, as the report highlights that price gains will likely be concentrated in the Midwest and Northeast. Specifically, across dense metros like Pittsburgh, Chicago, New York and Minneapolis.

“We’ve continually seen reasonably strong demand for houses in many of those Midwestern markets,” Case continues. “If you look at Minneapolis, that’s the market where we expect to see the strongest price performance in 2027. But other ones where we expect to see prices increasing, most of them are in the Midwest too, such as Cleveland, St. Louis, Chicago and Kansas City.”

Home prices in Minneapolis are predicted to grow 3.8%, although sales are expected to fall 7.6%, a worse decline than national expectations.

Although Minneapolis is considered the “price-growth leader” for the 40 largest markets, Case emphasizes that Seattle may be the one to watch out for, as the Emerald City is the only market where supply rises (4.9%) while prices fall (2.4%) by more than 2%.

“Seattle has some exposure to the tech industry the way San Francisco does, but a little bit of a different piece of the tech industry,” Case adds. “People in Seattle think that there’s a lot of softness to come, and our outlook reflects that. We expect the number of sales to be down by 7.7%. That’s not much worse than normal among these markets, but it’s a little bit worse.”

Inversely, Case predicts Salt Lake City to have the largest decline in sales, due to the city experiencing a uniquely strong spring in 2026. He argues that the drop in home sales activity is the city going back to normal levels, not so much a “softening” of the market.

“Part of what made the spring in Salt Lake City so strong was spillover from what’s happening in the AI industry. There is some sensitivity in a place like Salt Lake City—even though it’s quite separated from Silicon Valley—it doesn’t mean it’s separated entirely because people who live on the coast like the idea of being up in the mountains,” Case claims.

While the Homes.com report predicts a flat, national housing market with local variations, it also highlights certain factors that could alter expectations.

Mortgage rates are highly monitored by economists and agents, as “faster declines would bring buyers back and could coax rate-locked owners to list,” the report states.

Last week, the average 30-year fixed-rate mortgage was estimated at 7.28% by Freddie Mac, while Mortgage News Daily reported the average at 7.34% at press time. Considering the volatility of rates lately, Case uses previous predictions for 2026 as a basis for how expectations can change dramatically.

“2026 looked like it would be a pretty good year because mortgage rates had come down pretty sharply,” he comments. “Then—starting in early March—mortgage rates went up again sharply. Mainly because of the attack on Iran and what that did to petroleum product prices and inflation more generally. It looked as though it would ruin the housing market for the year, it didn’t.”

Ahead of the Federal Open Market Committee’s final meeting in December, Case—alongside other economists—predicts one last rate hike before the new year.

“I will not be surprised if they hike rates by 50 basis points instead of 25 basis points,” he continues. “Inflation being a problem is not just about the attacks on Iran and what it’s done to the petroleum supply situation, but it’s also about very, very strong demand for capital from AI, from both the intellectual infrastructure and the physical infrastructure.”

Case and Realtor.com® Senior Economist Hannah Jones predict one more rate hike in December. But Fed Chairman Kevin Warsh did not allude to any increases or changes to rates during his remarks earlier this month. Rather, he emphasized the impact of “superintelligence”—an alternative term for AI preferred by President Donald Trump—on the economy at large.

In terms of housing, the report notes that certain cities may feel the ripples of AI’s involvement in the economy.

“Continued hiring and wealth creation could lift demand for high-income housing,” the report states. “Slower funding, hiring or valuations would weaken demand in technology-heavy markets. San Francisco and San Jose are the most exposed, but other markets could be affected by direct spillover from a softening AI industry (Austin, Seattle) or by a broader stock market downturn triggered by a decline in AI stocks.”

Case points out the decision-making process when considering AI and mortgage rates.

“Part of the reason that mortgage rates are high is expectations and uncertainty about inflation over the next five to 10 years,” he says. “If the Fed raises its policy rates, then that could cause investors to think, ‘I don’t need to be so concerned about inflation because the Fed is on top of it.’ If the Fed succeeds in convincing investors that they are not going to let inflation get out of control, then investors are going to be more willing to put money into long-term bonds, including mortgage bonds and mortgage lenders.”

As local real estate markets can provide a vastly different experience from what’s conveyed by national data, Case again emphasizes that housing is not particularly sensitive to economic changes, and any effects might be seen later on.

“Some effects of AI or Fed decisions will be noticed by some people, but won’t be earth-shattering,” he concludes. “If mortgage rates came down and started those conversations, then you would have more home buying activity, agents would notice that. But buyers and sellers might not notice it because it might not affect prices, or the buyer-seller negotiating leverage.”
