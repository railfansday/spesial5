8 Florida artificial reef dives, from the SS United States to the Keys

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Free daily briefing on global business news.

These eight Florida artificial reef dives range from sunken Navy ships and Coast Guard cutters to a memorial reef and the planned SS United States reef off Destin

The ocean liner that set a transatlantic speed record on its 1952 maiden voyage is slated to spend its next chapter underwater off Florida's Panhandle. The SS United States, a 990-foot ship, left passenger service in 1969. It then spent decades tied up at a pier in Philadelphia while preservation groups searched for a way to save it. In 2024, Okaloosa County, Florida, agreed to acquire the vessel and sink it as an artificial reef off the Destin–Fort Walton Beach area.

The ship left Philadelphia under tow in February 2025, bound for Mobile, Alabama. Crews there were to clean it and prepare it for sinking. The county intends the liner to serve as habitat for fish and coral and as a draw for visiting divers.

Florida has used retired ships this way for decades. The state's artificial reef program is run by the Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission in partnership with coastal counties and cities. It places steel, concrete and decommissioned vessels on the seafloor. Hard structure gives corals, sponges and algae a surface to grow on. That growth draws small fish, which in turn attract larger predators. Before any ship goes down, it must be stripped of fuel, oils and other contaminants so it does not pollute the surrounding water.

For divers, the result is a set of Florida artificial reef dives that combine marine life with naval and maritime history. Former military ships, Coast Guard cutters and a cargo freighter now rest off Pensacola, Key Largo, Islamorada, Key West and Southwest Florida. One reef off Key Biscayne was built as an underwater memorial rather than from a ship. Some sites sit within recreational diving limits. Others reach depths suited to divers with advanced training.

This list covers eight of those sites. It opens with the SS United States, a planned reef that has not had decades to collect marine growth the way the older wrecks have. The rest are established sites. They include the aircraft carrier USS Oriskany off Pensacola and the USS Spiegel Grove off Key Largo. The Coast Guard cutters Duane and Bibb sit nearby, also off Key Largo. Farther south are the freighter Eagle off Islamorada and the USNS General Hoyt S. Vandenberg off Key West. The list also includes the USCGC Mohawk in Southwest Florida's Gulf waters and the Neptune Memorial Reef off Key Biscayne.

The SS United States, a 990-foot ocean liner built in the early 1950s, is slated to become an artificial reef in the Gulf off Florida's Panhandle, near Destin and Fort Walton Beach. Okaloosa County plans to sink the hull as a dive site and a habitat for marine life.

The liner was built at Newport News Shipbuilding in Virginia to a design by naval architect William Francis Gibbs. It entered service in 1952. On its maiden voyage, it took the Blue Riband for the fastest Atlantic crossing from the Cunard liner Queen Mary. The eastbound run lasted three days, 10 hours and 42 minutes, at an average speed of 35.59 knots.

Cold War priorities shaped the design. The U.S. Navy helped fund construction on the condition that the liner could be converted into a troop transport if needed. Gibbs also kept wood aboard to a minimum as a fire-safety measure. Much of the furniture and fittings were made of metal instead.

Jet airliners took over most transatlantic passenger travel during the 1960s, and the liner's commercial career ended before the decade was out. It then passed through several owners over the following decades. Its interiors were stripped, and a series of redevelopment plans failed to return it to sea.

From 1996, the ship sat at Pier 82 on the Delaware River in Philadelphia. The SS United States Conservancy, a nonprofit, bought it in 2011 and searched for a new use. A rent dispute with the pier's owner ended in a 2024 federal court ruling that the ship had to leave its berth. The Okaloosa County agreement followed later that year.

Cleanup in Mobile, Alabama, follows national best-management-practices guidance issued in 2006 by the Environmental Protection Agency and the Maritime Administration. The document applies to vessels sunk as reefs anywhere in U.S. waters, not only in Florida.

The Associated Press has reported on the ship's conversion into a reef. Divers cannot visit the wreck until it reaches the seafloor. The final depth, the site's distance from shore and the deployment date are details to confirm with Okaloosa County before planning a trip.

The USS Oriskany is a retired U.S. Navy aircraft carrier that was sunk as an artificial reef in the Gulf, about 24 miles southeast of Pensacola, in May 2006. Divers call it the Great Carrier Reef. It is the largest ship ever deliberately sunk to create a reef.

The Oriskany began as an Essex-class carrier at the New York Naval Shipyard, where construction started in 1944. World War II ended before the ship was finished, and work on it was suspended in 1947. The Navy later completed it to an updated design and commissioned it in 1950. It never saw action in the war it was built for.

Its combat record came later. The carrier launched aircraft during the Korean War and again during the Vietnam War. In October 1966, a fire broke out aboard while the ship operated in the Gulf of Tonkin. The fire killed 44 crew members. A year later, Navy pilot John McCain was flying from the Oriskany when he was shot down over Hanoi. He was taken prisoner. The Navy decommissioned the ship in 1976.

The carrier spent the following decades out of service before it was chosen for reef duty. On the day of the sinking, explosive charges sent it to the bottom in about 37 minutes.

The Oriskany came to rest upright on a sandy bottom in roughly 212 feet of water. The top of its island, the command tower that rises above the flight deck, sits about 80 feet below the surface. The flight deck lies near 135 feet.

That vertical profile shapes who can dive it. Divers with advanced recreational certification generally stay on the upper island structure. Reaching the flight deck and the hull below it calls for technical dive training and planned decompression stops.

The wreck sits in open water well offshore. Current and visibility can change from one day to the next, and trips depend on sea conditions.

The steel hull now supports fish common to the northern Gulf, including red snapper, greater amberjack, gag grouper and barracuda. Sponges and corals have also settled on the superstructure since the sinking. The wreck adds tall structure to a stretch of seafloor that is mostly flat sand.

The USS Spiegel Grove is a former U.S. Navy dock landing ship that was sunk off Key Largo in 2002 to create an artificial reef. It came to rest on its side after a troubled sinking. Hurricane Dennis pushed the hull upright in 2005, leaving it in the position divers explore today.

The ship was built for amphibious operations. Dock landing ships carry landing craft in a floodable well deck at the stern. That design lets troops and vehicles move from ship to shore. The Spiegel Grove served the Navy from the mid-1950s until it was decommissioned in 1989. Its name comes from Spiegel Grove, the Fremont, Ohio, estate of President Rutherford B. Hayes.

The vessel is 510 feet long. Its sinking did not go to plan. The hull flooded earlier than expected and settled upside down, with its bow still above the surface. Salvage crews then worked the wreck down to the seafloor, where it came to rest on its starboard side.

That position held for about three years. During that period, divers swam along decks that ran vertically and through passageways turned on their edge. When Hurricane Dennis passed through the region, the storm's surge and currents shifted the hull until it sat upright on the sand.

The righting changed the dive. The decks, superstructure and cranes now sit as the shipbuilders intended. That orientation makes it simpler for divers to track where they are on a structure of this size.

The wreck lies within the Florida Keys National Marine Sanctuary, the federal marine protected area that covers the waters around the island chain. Dive boats tie into mooring buoys attached to the wreck rather than dropping anchor on it.

The Spiegel Grove is a deep dive by recreational standards, and currents across the site can run strong. Divers need to plan their air supply and bottom time around both. Entering the interior is a separate skill that calls for wreck penetration training and suitable equipment.

After more than two decades on the bottom, the hull carries a growth of sponges and corals. Schools of fish gather around the superstructure. Goliath grouper and barracuda are among the larger species seen at the site.

The USNS General Hoyt S. Vandenberg is a retired military tracking ship that was scuttled about seven miles south of Key West on May 27, 2009. At more than 500 feet long, it gives divers in the lower Florida Keys a large steel wreck with depths suited to several levels of training.

The vessel began as something else. It was built during World War II as a troop transport and served under the name General Harry Taylor. The Navy commissioned it in 1944. Its job then was moving service members.

Its second career began in the early 1960s, when the U.S. Air Force took it over. The ship was renamed for Gen. Hoyt S. Vandenberg, the second chief of staff of the U.S. Air Force. Crews fitted it with tracking equipment to follow missile and spacecraft launches from Cape Canaveral. That work tied the ship to the early U.S. space program.

After its military service ended, the ship sat for years in the reserve fleet on Virginia's James River. It also appeared on screen. The 1999 science fiction film Virus used it as a setting, with the hull repainted under a fictional Russian name.

The wreck now sits upright on a sandy bottom in about 140 feet of water. Its upper decks and superstructure rise well above the seafloor. That layout lets recreational divers explore the top of the ship without going to full depth. The lower hull and any interior penetration call for advanced or technical training.

The site lies within the Florida Keys National Marine Sanctuary, which covers the waters around the island chain. Mooring buoys over the wreck let boats tie up without dropping anchor on the structure. Currents can run across the site, and divers commonly descend and ascend on lines tied to the ship. Barracuda, grouper and other fish have moved onto the hull since the sinking.

The Duane and the Bibb are two retired U.S. Coast Guard cutters that were sunk as artificial reefs off Key Largo in November 1987. They lie near Molasses Reef in deep water with strong currents. Depth and current make both of them dives for advanced, experienced divers.

The two ships are sisters from the Coast Guard's 327-foot Treasury class. The class was named for former secretaries of the Treasury. The Duane entered service as the William J. Duane, and the Bibb as the George M. Bibb. Both names were later shortened.

Their wartime records overlap. Both cutters escorted Allied convoys across the North Atlantic during World War II. In 1943, the Bibb rescued survivors of the torpedoed troop transport Henry R. Mallory. In 1944, the Duane served as a flagship during Operation Dragoon, the Allied landings in southern France.

The Coast Guard decommissioned the pair in 1985. Two years later, they were scuttled a short distance apart. The site now falls within the Florida Keys National Marine Sanctuary.

The two dives differ mainly in how each hull landed. The Duane settled upright on a sand bottom at about 120 feet. That posture lets divers follow the deck, the bridge and the mast in a layout that still resembles a working ship.

The Bibb came to rest on its starboard side in about 130 feet. Its exposed port hull and sideways deck give it a different profile from its sister. Divers breathing standard air get limited bottom time at those depths. Many visitors see only part of either wreck on a single descent.

Currents are the other defining factor. Boats typically tie into mooring buoys. Divers then descend and ascend along the line rather than swimming freely in open water. Entering the interior of either cutter requires specialized wreck-penetration training.

Decades underwater have coated both hulls in corals and sponges. Barracuda, schools of jacks and large groupers are common sights around the superstructures.

The USCGC Mohawk is a World War II Coast Guard cutter resting on the Gulf floor off Lee County in Southwest Florida. Lee County sank it in 2012 as an artificial reef and dedicated the site to the veterans of that war.

The cutter was commissioned in 1935. It belonged to the Algonquin class, a group of six cutters the Coast Guard built in the mid-1930s. Each measured 165 feet, far shorter than the aircraft carrier and ocean liner behind Florida's largest reef projects.

During World War II, the Mohawk served on the Greenland Patrol. The patrol guarded Greenland, which the U.S. agreed to defend in 1941 while Denmark was under German occupation. The Mohawk also escorted convoys across the North Atlantic, the supply route between North America and Europe that German submarines targeted throughout the war.

That service is the basis for the reef's memorial role. A dive on the Mohawk doubles as a visit to a veterans memorial.

The Coast Guard decommissioned the cutter in 1948. It later spent years as a museum ship on the Key West waterfront. Its final assignment moved it north from the Keys to the waters off Lee County.

Lee County sits on Florida's southwest coast and includes Fort Myers Beach, Sanibel and Captiva. The Mohawk lies offshore in open Gulf water. Divers reach it by charter boat rather than from the beach.

Much of the Gulf seabed off Southwest Florida is flat sand and limestone. The Mohawk's steel hull rises from that bottom as hard vertical structure. On an otherwise open plain, it gives fish and invertebrates a fixed place to gather and settle.

Among the eight dives in this selection, the Mohawk is the only one off Florida's southwest coast. It pairs a Gulf Coast location with an Atlantic convoy history. That puts a World War II wreck within boat range of the Fort Myers area.

Neptune Memorial Reef is an underwater memorial site in the Atlantic off Key Biscayne, near Miami, where cremated remains are mixed into concrete and set into the reef's structures. Work on it began in 2007. The site works as both a burial place and an artificial reef open to recreational divers.

The reef lies about 3.25 miles east of Key Biscayne, on the seafloor roughly 40 feet down. That depth sits within the 60-foot limit that training agencies such as PADI set for divers with entry-level open water certification.

Its structures were cast and lowered to the seabed rather than sunk as a whole vessel. There are no enclosed decks, hatches or passageways to penetrate. Divers stay in open water, moving around and between the pieces.

Miami artist Kim Brandell designed the layout around the legend of Atlantis, the lost island Plato described. Columns, archways, gates and sculpted figures are arranged to suggest the ruins of a sunken city. The result looks more like an underwater sculpture garden than a shipwreck.

Each memorial placement holds the remains of a person whose ashes were combined with cement to form part of the structure. Plaques mark the individuals memorialized. Families can visit the site by boat, and those who dive can see the placements in person. The reef was designed to keep expanding as new placements are added over time.

Over time, the concrete has picked up encrusting growth. Reef fish now gather around the columns and arches.

For divers, the draw is the sculpture and the memorial plaques, along with the marine life settling on them. The shallow depth also allows longer no-decompression time than deeper wrecks do.

The site's location off Key Biscayne puts it a short boat ride from Miami. Reaching the Keys wrecks farther south means a drive down the Overseas Highway before the boat trip begins.

The Eagle is a cargo freighter that was sunk on purpose off Islamorada in 1985 to create an artificial reef in the Florida Keys. Hurricane Georges split its hull in two in 1998. Divers today visit a wreck that lies in two separate sections.

Islamorada is a village in the Upper Keys of Monroe County. It sits along the Overseas Highway between Key Largo to the north and Marathon $MPC to the south. Its offshore waters lie inside the Florida Keys National Marine Sanctuary, a federal marine protected area managed by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration. Sanctuary rules cover the wrecks within its boundaries as well as the natural coral reefs. Those rules bar divers from taking or damaging coral.

Congress designated the sanctuary in 1990, five years after the Eagle went down. The freighter was already on the bottom when those federal protections took effect.

The Eagle has been on the seafloor longer than several other Keys wrecks. It reached the bottom 17 years before the Spiegel Grove was scuttled off Key Largo. It also predates the Coast Guard cutters Duane and Bibb by two years. Among the Keys ships on this list, the Eagle has spent the most time on the bottom.

Hurricane Georges crossed the Florida Keys in late September 1998 and made landfall near Key West. The storm broke the Eagle apart 13 years after the ship was placed on the bottom. The two pieces now rest near each other. Divers who visited before 1998 saw a single intact hull. Divers today move between two sections. The break shows that a sunken ship keeps changing after it is placed. A strong hurricane can still reshape steel lying well below the surface.

The Eagle sits on the Atlantic side of the Keys, where the Florida Current runs offshore. The Florida Current is part of the Gulf Stream system that carries warm water north along the U.S. East Coast. That current can push water across deeper sites. Conditions change from day to day.
