Reducing risk by protecting trust across the real estate transaction

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

An analysis of 668 reported wire fraud events found $123.8 million in exposure and $11.5 million in losses. About 79% of events were tied to participants outside a title company’s direct control, underscoring the need for shared verification across partners.  AI Summary

A secure lender platform cannot protect a buyer who acts on fraudulent instructions received through another participant’s email. An agent’s familiar name can make a message persuasive even when the agent has no authority to move funds. For mortgage, title and real estate professionals, those connections deserve a place in the fraud prevention conversation.

An analysis of 668 reported wire fraud events spanning a decade identified approximately $123.8 million in reported financial exposure and approximately $11.5 million in documented losses. The findings point to a practical concern for everyone involved in a transaction: protecting an organization’s systems addresses only part of the risk.

We examine what those reported events suggest about trust, why participants outside the funding process matter and how organizations can strengthen verification across business relationships.

Approximately 79% of reported events, 78% of reported exposure and 80% of documented losses were associated with participants operating outside the title company’s direct control. Of the 668 events reviewed, 57 resulted in documented financial loss.

These figures describe the reported events analyzed. They should not be treated as an industry-wide loss rate or as proof that risk has shifted over time. Association with an external participant also does not, by itself, establish where an account was compromised or who was responsible for a loss.

The concentration nevertheless raises an important business question. If a fraudulent request gains credibility through a customer, agent, attorney or other participant, will the next organization in the transaction recognize and verify it before someone acts?

The traditional approach to fraud prevention focuses on authority. Organizations identify who can move money, approve payments, authorize disbursements or initiate wire transfers and then build controls around those functions.

That remains necessary. But an attacker may also seek access to someone who influences the person sending money. Transaction details, familiar correspondence and knowledge of an approaching closing can help a fraudulent message appear credible without access to the lender’s or settlement provider’s systems.

Residential real estate transactions are particularly susceptible to this type of manipulation because they depend on trust at every stage of the process. Buyers trust agents. Sellers trust agents. Consumers trust lenders. All parties trust the professionals coordinating the transaction. Most participants are communicating with several unfamiliar parties while simultaneously navigating a process that is complex, time-sensitive and financially significant.

For a consumer, a request that appears to come from a familiar professional may feel like part of that process. Fraud prevention must help customers distinguish a trusted relationship from a verified instruction.

Mortgage lenders, title companies, brokerages and independent professionals operate with different resources and security practices. A participant may use an email account without the monitoring or administrative oversight available within a larger organization. That difference creates a coordination challenge, not a reason to assign blame.

An agent may understand the buyer’s timeline and concerns better than anyone else. A loan officer may be the person the buyer calls when a request seems confusing. A settlement professional may be the first to see that expected funds have not arrived. Each has an opportunity to reinforce safe behavior at a different point.

The strongest internal controls lose some of their value when customers receive inconsistent guidance about who will provide instructions, how to verify them or where to report a suspicious message. Those expectations should be established before the closing deadline makes every request feel urgent.

Lenders, agents and settlement providers should explain their respective roles early. Identify who will provide wiring instructions and how the customer should independently confirm them. Reinforce that guidance as closing approaches rather than relying on a single warning in an email signature.

Before acting on wire instructions, confirm them directly with the appropriate party using a previously established, independently verified phone number. Do not rely on a phone number supplied in the message you are trying to verify.

Replying to the same email thread is not sufficient because an attacker may control the account. Question unexpected changes and escalate unusual requests through your organization’s established procedures before money moves.

Awareness efforts should reach loan officers, agents, transaction coordinators and customer-facing staff, as well as employees authorized to disburse funds. Training should explain how a routine message or introduction can influence a consumer’s decision to trust instructions.

Strong email security, multifactor authentication, employee training, monitoring and incident response capabilities remain essential. Broader coordination adds to those protections. It does not reduce the need to secure accounts and systems within each business.

Organizations should identify whom to contact when instructions appear suspicious or expected funds do not arrive. Staff should know how to pause their part of the transaction, involve the appropriate settlement and lending contacts and activate established fraud response procedures. Customers need a clear way to raise concerns without feeling that they are creating an inconvenience.

The difference between reported exposure and documented loss in the analysis is substantial. It is consistent with the value of verification and intervention, but the totals alone do not establish which controls prevented losses or explain every event’s outcome.

Organizations can learn more by reviewing their own near misses. Where did a questionable request enter the process? Who recognized it? Was the customer able to reach the right person? Did a partner’s instructions conflict with the organization’s guidance? Those questions turn an avoided loss into an opportunity to improve the next transaction.

Wire fraud prevention is a shared responsibility across mortgage lending, title and settlement services and real estate. A participant does not need authority over funds to influence where those funds go.

The practical response is to make verification part of the customer experience from the beginning. Clear roles, consistent guidance and accessible escalation help people act safely when a familiar name or urgent request makes fraud difficult to recognize.

Successful fraud prevention frequently occurs because someone slows down, asks an additional question and verifies information before money moves.
