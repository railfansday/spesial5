The Silicon-Carbon Battery Breakthrough Behind 7,000mAh Smartphones

For almost five years, phone batteries were stuck. Flagships from Apple, Samsung, and Google hovered around 4,500 to 5,000mAh, and "two-day battery life" was something only chunky gaming phones could promise. Then, in about 18 months, everything changed. The OnePlus 15 ships with 7,300mAh. Tecno has put 8,000mAh into a phone that is 7.42mm thick. Honor sells a phone with a 10,000mAh battery that is 8.3mm thick.

None of this came from making phones bigger. It came from changing one material inside the battery. Here is what silicon-carbon batteries are, why they work, what they cost you, and why the iPhone and Galaxy in your pocket probably still don't have one.

Every lithium-ion battery has two electrodes. When you charge your phone, lithium ions move from the cathode into the anode and are stored there. When you use your phone, they move back, and that flow powers the device.

For decades, the anode has been made of graphite. Graphite is cheap, stable, and predictable, but it has a hard limit: it can hold one lithium ion for every six carbon atoms. Once the industry had squeezed out almost all the efficiency it could from graphite, the only way to add capacity was to make the battery physically bigger.

Silicon changes the maths. Per gram, silicon can in theory store 4 to 10 times as much lithium as graphite, according to LG Energy Solution. Even a small amount of silicon in the anode raises how much energy fits in the same space.

That is what a "silicon-carbon" battery is: a normal lithium-ion battery whose anode mixes silicon into a carbon structure instead of using graphite alone. The cathode, the electrolyte, and the way you charge it stay basically the same.

Because silicon swells. When it absorbs lithium, it can expand to several times its original volume: by up to 300% when fully charged, according to Android Authority. A phone battery that grew like that would crack its own electrodes within a few charges, and it would push against the screen and the back of the phone.

Silicon-carbon batteries are a real improvement, but they are not free.

They may wear faster. Even with all the engineering above, silicon still expands and contracts on every charge. Over hundreds of cycles, that stress adds up. A battery engineer interviewed on the Waveform podcast said these batteries tend to lose more capacity in their first two to three years than conventional cells, according to 9to5Google. Brands make strong claims of their own: OnePlus, for example, says the OnePlus 15 battery keeps 80% of its capacity after four years, based on its own simulated tests. Independent long-term data is still limited, because most of these phones are less than two years old.

Some capacity may be held back. To slow wear, makers can limit how much of the battery is used. Nothing is a clear example: the Nothing Phone (3) has a silicon-carbon cell with a physical capacity of 5,500mAh, but outside India it is sold as a 5,150mAh phone, according to 9to5Google.

They are harder to make at scale. Silicon anodes rely on complex nanotechnology processes that currently cost more than making graphite, according to How-To Geek.

This is the part that surprises most people. The biggest obstacle is not chemistry. It is shipping rules.

International transport regulations treat lithium-ion batteries as dangerous goods. Under UN Special Provision 188, which air, sea and road regulators (including the US rules in 49 CFR) all follow, a cell of up to 20 watt-hours can ship under simplified rules. Above that, the device needs dangerous-goods handling, labelling and paperwork. At a typical 3.8 volts, 20Wh works out to roughly 5,300mAh.

Look at Western flagships with that number in mind. The Galaxy S25 Ultra's 5,000mAh battery is about 19.4Wh, and the Pixel 9 Pro's 5,060mAh battery is about 19.7Wh, according to 9to5Google. They sit just under the line.

Apple, Samsung and Google ship hundreds of millions of phones, mostly through global supply chains. For them, a battery that changes their shipping status or needs a two-cell design is a big logistics and cost decision. Samsung also has a long memory: after the Galaxy Note 7 battery recall in 2016, it has tended to adopt new battery technology slowly and carefully. Samsung is rumored to be testing a dual-cell battery design, but so far the big brands have chosen proven graphite cells and longer software support over headline capacity.

What this means if you are buying a phone

Silicon-carbon is not the end point. Battery makers are steadily increasing the share of silicon in anodes, and brands are already testing 10,000mAh cells in normal-size phones. The open questions are the same ones that held the big brands back: how these batteries age over four or five years, how they handle heat during fast charging, and whether shipping rules will change.

For now, the result is simple. If you want the longest battery life in a normal-size phone, the brands using silicon-carbon cells are ahead, and the gap is the biggest it has been in years.
