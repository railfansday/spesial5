Ford casts 5,000 ‘unicastings’ for Fathom EV truck in Tesla-style shift

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

Ford has now produced more than 5,000 large aluminum “unicastings” for the Fathom, its upcoming $28,350 midsize electric pickup, as it fine-tunes the process ahead of a 2027 launch.

Each truck gets two of them, front and rear, replacing 146 individual parts. It’s the clearest sign yet that Ford is all-in on the manufacturing approach Tesla popularized with its Giga Press six years ago.

The update comes from Vladimir Bogachuk, Ford’s chief engineer of advanced vehicle structure architecture, in a post on Ford’s From the Road blog. The castings are made at Ford’s Shelbyville Technical Center and shipped to Louisville Assembly Plant for process testing and prototype builds. Louisville is the Kentucky factory Ford is retooling for the Fathom and other vehicles on its Universal EV Platform.

Ford melts aluminum ingots at about 1,300°F (700°C) and injects the metal into either a front or rear structure mold. Once a robot pulls the part out, it goes into a quench tank, gets trimmed and machined, and then runs a gauntlet of checks: industrial X-ray scans for pores and voids, dimensional measurements in a climate-controlled room, optical surface scans, and lab tensile and chemistry tests on samples.

Nothing ships to Louisville until a batch clears every inspection. Ford says it’s also working on cutting cycle time and on designing the castings for “modular repairability.”

That last part has been the main reason why automakers have been reticent about larger cast parts. Repair cost is the biggest knock on giant castings, since a damaged one-piece structure can turn a moderate crash into a write-off. Lucid executive Cory Steuben has said insurance concerns drove the company’s decision to skip gigacasting altogether.

Tesla kicked this off in 2020 with the first IDRA Giga Press at Fremont, a machine with roughly 6,000 tons of clamping force. It cast the Model Y’s rear underbody in a single piece, replacing 70 parts, and Elon Musk said the change removed the need for about 300 robots.

Tesla wanted to go much further. It added a front casting, tied the two together with a structural battery pack, and moved to even bigger presses for the Cybertruck. But in 2024, Reuters reported that Tesla had backed away from its plan to cast nearly the entire underbody in one piece, and the automaker has reportedly since dropped the front casting on some Model Y production.

So Ford’s 146-to-2 figure looks great on paper. But it’s a front-and-rear layout, basically the same architecture Tesla launched with on the Model Y. Ford isn’t leapfrogging Tesla here. It’s catching up, just with a vehicle that will cost less than any Tesla on sale.

Others aren’t buying it. Stellantis has said it won’t invest in gigacasting, while BMW and Mercedes-Benz have stuck with smaller presses for body parts. Hyundai pushed its “Hypercasting” plan back two years.

We’ve been tracking this casting race since Tesla’s first Giga Press showed up in Fremont, and the lesson so far is simple. Fewer parts is great, but the press alone doesn’t make a car cheap. The whole factory does.

That’s why Ford’s approach is worth watching. The unicastings are one piece of a bigger overhaul at Louisville, where Ford is splitting the line into three branches (front, rear, and the battery pack with the interior floor) that meet at the end. Ford says that can be up to 40% faster than its current assembly, or about 15% net once it reinvests some of the savings into automation and insourcing. It’s very close to the logic behind Tesla’s “unboxed” process, and they are betting it’s what actually gets you to a $28,350 truck.
