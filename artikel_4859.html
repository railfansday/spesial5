What Your Team Stops Telling You When Founders Fight

Nobody said a word about last week's fight with your cofounder. They don't need to. In Monday's all-hands meeting, your team watched you interrupt him. They watched him shut down, and they saw the look on his face. And now they're censoring what they will tell you directly and what they will say behind your back.

One pair of founders I worked with agreed with each other in private. In team meetings, one of them kept undercutting the other in front of the people they'd hired. When the first founder corrected someone's technical work, his cofounder called it micromanagement. Neither of them described what that word meant to them.

The team heard it as permission to ignore the feedback. The first founder became "the difficult one," and people stopped listening to him. He told me, "I feel like my rights are taken away."

His cofounder thought he was protecting the team from harshness. The team learned that the founders weren't aligned, and one of them could be ignored.

People read body language and feel tension between others even when nobody names it.

In a meeting, they notice who interrupts whom, who questions whom and in what tone, and whether one founder has withdrawn. The more often the two of you fight, the louder those signals get. It shows up in the work. One of you hands someone a task. Then the other tells them it's the wrong priority, and the work gets redone.

Founders' moods carry extra weight. You hold status and power. People idealize you, mostly without realizing it. In my experience, many employees start to relate to a founder the way they once related to a parent or to whoever held power in their lives.

In Emotional Contagion (1994), Elaine Hatfield and colleagues described how emotions spread through groups. People pick up and mirror the feelings of those around them. Later work on teams by Thomas Sy and others (2005) found that a leader's mood spreads to the group and affects how well the group coordinates. When two founders are tense in a meeting, the room tends to stay tense after they leave.

Think about children whose parents are fighting. The kids often don't know what the fight is about, and they feel it anyway. They notice the tone at dinner and the door that shuts a little too hard. They can't fix it and they can't leave, because they depend on both parents.

So they adapt. Some drift toward anxiety and some act out. Others take a side, try to become the peacemaker, or shut down. A team is in a similar spot. Their paychecks and much of their identity are tied to the two of you. And they have far less control over the company's future than you do.

When people can't fix the tension above them, many seek connection to whichever founder keeps them safest.

Say the CEO has been hinting that the CTO is underperforming. If I'm an employee, it makes sense to get closer to the CEO, because she holds more power. I'll keep feeding her information and try to learn how she thinks. I'll keep the CTO happy on the surface, but stop giving him honest feedback.

The psychiatrist Murray Bowen, in the family systems theory he developed from the 1950s through the 1970s, called this triangulation. When tension between two people gets too high, a third person gets pulled in to absorb some of it. That takes attention off the original conflict, so the conflict between the two founders stays frozen.

I often think about the people who often get pulled in to mediate conflict between founders as parentified employees—the emotionally intelligent individuals wired for caretaking. They often end up in Head of People, HR, or Chief of Staff roles. Being the go-between can feel good at first. It tells an employee, "I'm valued here. I'm important." It also pulls their attention away from their own work, and tends to raise their anxiety.

Founders can feed this dynamic without noticing. An employee comes to you frustrated with your cofounder. You're frustrated with him too, so you sigh, agree and maybe add a story of your own. This provides temporary relief from the tension and helps you and the employee feel closer, but it prevents your cofounder from hearing the friction necessary for change. Icing him out reinforces the narrative that he is not to be respected, and accelerates the chances of his eventual departure. What would be far more helpful is coaching the employee to take the feedback to your cofounder directly.

Startup Snapshot studied startup employee wellbeing. Employees whose founder was highly stressed reported 14% higher burnout. Cofounder conflict disrupts your ability to focus and execute, and negatively impacts your employees. The longer you fight, the higher the risk of churn your company faces.

Get realigned with your cofounder before you try to fix anything on the team. With the pair above, I started by bringing attention to the word that kept causing damage. I asked them to define micromanagement together and to stop using it as a weapon in front of the team. Then, I suggested they increase their frequency of operational syncs so they could enter meetings fully aligned.

After you've made initial progress with your cofounder, show the team the culture you want. Your team copies what you do, so let them see you disagree, work it out, and commit to a decision. Because of the power gap, that alone won't give most employees the same freedom to push back. So ask for it directly, and when it comes, handle it well.

The next time someone vents to you about your cofounder, ask whether they've told your cofounder directly, and help them figure out how they could. That small shift can help contain conflict so it doesn't contaminate your execution.
