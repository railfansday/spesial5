The Powerful Impact of Social Comparisons in Everyday Life

"Did she do that? Did she do that?" said the mother of my daughter's friend, her eyes wide and her jaw dropped — her entire system in a state of shock.

She had just arrived at the daycare center to pick up her daughter. Near the entrance was a chalkboard where the kids were encouraged to draw unrestricted.

Some minutes earlier, my daughter and her daughter had both been drawing stick figures. I had arrived early, and my daughter had pleaded with me to "draw some people." However, the daycare center had a strict policy against parents helping their kids; kids should develop independence while at the center.

Gently, I reminded her that I wasn't allowed to help, but she persisted. And so after checking that no staff members were watching, I made quick drawings of a couple of kids. They were barely passable, but next to her daughter's stick figures, they were Da Vincis.

I had just returned the chalk to my daughter, who was now busily applying some final touches, when the other girl's mom showed up. The two drawings contrasted sharply, leaving a powerful impression on the mom. But what could I say? I had violated the center's rules.

I had to put the mom out of her misery. Under my breath, I said, "No, no, I drew them. She begged me to do it. Don't tell anyone."

Echoing the two masks of Greek theater, her face shifted from tragedy to a kind of joy.

Why did she feel this comparison so keenly?

A few details explain this. My daughter and her friend were the same age (4) and gender. They also had the same birthday. Similarity in related attributes increases the impact of social comparisons. If her friend were a boy and a year older, the mother might have brushed off the comparison; it lacked relevance. Parents often zero in on attributes for a sense of how a comparison should register, especially at these early ages when a year or more can be especially important in determining achievement.

I often think of this episode. To me, it captures how social comparisons affect people in everyday life.

How we stack up with others often has deciding consequences for success in important areas of life, from work to romance, and for the feelings about self that follow from these outcomes. Part of growing up and maturing as adults is about assessing strengths and weaknesses largely determined by relative standing, grappling with the emotional and practical implications of such assessments, and making life choices guided by this process.

And, of course, our kids are extensions of ourselves.

In my experience, I can think of no better example of these truths than this small but telling incident occurring in my daughter’s pre-school.
