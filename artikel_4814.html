Why Being the Mom of a Gay Son Matters

Posted November 3, 2026 | Reviewed by Gary Drevitch

I am standing at the back of a darkened room. On the screen, a documentary is being shown. And throughout the room, people in the audience are openly laughing, weeping, murmuring. I can't tell you what this moment, and all the other moments like it, mean to me.

The film being shown is the documentary I produced and directed, called Mom, I Have Something to Tell You. It shares interviews I've conducted with a plethora of gay men and their mothers, talking about their relationships, uncovering hidden thoughts and reactions, and telling a story I've been helping people tell for over 10 years.

The only thing that even equaled the feeling I had that night was when the documentary made its debut on YouTube. It has since garnered more than 35,000 views and collected comments that have made me, too, weep.

I had a feeling this topic would be meaningful and exciting for other people—and is it ever! Sharing the stories of women and their gay sons, which no one had done before, evokes powerful reactions: some chuckle, some tear up, some recite long stories about how this relationship is so powerful. I frequently forget to bring tissues with me—which is a mistake.

I knew I had to capture the stories in video form—rather than writing them up like I usually do—so others could see for themselves what happens here. I wanted to share the depths of these stories, not just describe them. Every time I filmed or shared snippets, the reactions of others—on Instagram, Facebook, Tik-Tok, and YouTube—emphasized was why this is so important.

I've never met a gay man who had an easy childhood. Gay boys are nearly universally discriminated against, ignored, bullied, or even abused. What I hear from these men is that the impact of a mother who is supportive, nurturing, and who defends her child makes a tremendous difference in their later well-being.

When one of these men is given the opportunity to tell their mothers, directly, what a difference their love and support has made in their life, something magical happens for both of them. He's able to express his gratitude and love; she's able to see what was often a thankless and difficult decision as a courageous and life-affirming act.

And audiences? We all have relationships with our parents and children that are often left unexamined, and the film becomes a prism through which everyone can find their own story.

All of this was the result of my own clinical work with gay men. I was noticing how important their relationships with their mothers were. Interested, I did a literature search on the subject, coming up with...nothing. No one was interested in exploring it. So I did.

Without literature on the subject, I started to explore the relationship myself, interviewing gay men and their mothers, sometimes alone, sometimes together, until I felt I was starting to build a real body of information and a useful resource. I founded a nonprofit called Gay Sons & Mothers, and was surprised to see how much people appreciated having someone pay attention to something that was obviously of critical significance to them.

At first, it was just me: Those early days found me lugging equipment around from interview to interview, worrying about whether the microphones were picking up our voices or whether I'd remembered to turn on the video (once or twice, I hadn't); I finally hired a videographer so I could focus on the content and the people. It was, for sure, a journey of discovery. And the result, for now, is Mom, I Have Something to Tell You.

Time after time gay men have told me how their mothers enabled them to find their voice and their place, showed them that they were not deficient, and encouraged them to be fully themselves. These are mothers who stood up to spouses, families, cultures, and communities to protect their sons. Who bought their boys dolls and told them they were perfect just as they were. Who advocated for their children, risking everything themselves to stand up for what—and who—was important.

Shouldn't that be a relationship that's celebrated?

One example is that of the "Mama Dragons," a group of women who belong to the Church of Latter-Day Saints. These women loved their gay sons with their whole hearts and found themselves needing to reconcile that love with being part of a church and community that didn't recognize the value of their children.

Eventually, as I met so many of these courageous women, I began recording a video series called Mothers Who Inspire, talking with mothers of gay sons about their challenges and joys. A particularly poignant segment was my interview with Judy Shepard, who created the Matthew Shepard Foundation in memory of her murdered son.

Here's just one example: When David first heard of Gay Sons & Mothers, it opened floodgates of emotion about his own relationship with his mother. They clearly loved each other, but he'd never dared come out to her; he assumed she inferred it, and didn't want to risk bringing it into the open. But watching the film gave him the courage he needed to talk with her about his identity for the first time in 20 years. So it's no exaggeration to say the organization and film have changed lives.

I've shared these stories with medical communities, at young people’s events, with artists’ associations, and with professional organizations and workplaces. The presentation content and length is tailored to the specific audience, with question-and-answer and discussion segments available.

One of my favorite encounters was at a mental-health conference where I offered a workshop. A 78-year-old psychiatrist had driven four hours to attend my workshop, and during the sharing time, he said, "I never imagined in my lifetime I'd say this—but I am gay." The group went wild. It was one of my first presentations and his response—and that of the group as a whole—told me I was on the right path.

The impact of the film is shown clearly through the comments. "Brings back lots of memories growing up with my loving and accepting mother," writes one viewer. "We didn't even need to talk about it. I treasure those great memories seeing this video of mothers and their gay sons."

Another viewer said, "I knew my son was gay from the age of three. He howled when I gifted my niece a Marie Antoinette satin dress for Xmas, he so desperately wanted to wear it himself. I had to promise to buy him a Barbie instead!"

Echoing what I wrote about in a recent Unwrapped essay about having "extra" mothers, one viewer said, "My grandmother was the most important person in my life (...) I knew I was gay when I was around eight and my nan was never homophobic, she would drop remarks like 'you're special' and 'we love you' (...) she was my safe space. We never had a coming-out moment, as she would ask about any boys I liked."

All this confirms the impact that others' stories have on us and how they allow us to recapture the women who have made our present lives possible.

This is just the beginning: As we expand to the next generation, it's increasingly important that mothers receive the validation and recognition they deserve in order to feel supported and to be the best they can for their kids. Go check out Mom, I Have Something to Tell You, and see if you don't agree.
