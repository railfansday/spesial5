Your real estate listing is stuck and a price drop will not fix it

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Some stalled condo listings are constrained by building level compliance and HOA financials, not pricing or marketing. Leaders can protect the seller by pausing the listing, extending the agreement, and pushing repairs to regain financing eligibility.  AI Summary

There is a category of stuck listing that is going to become more common over the next few years, and most agents have not been trained for it. It is the listing that cannot sell for a reason that has nothing to do with price, photography or effort. It is the listing blocked at the building level, and if we keep coaching agents to treat every slow listing with a price reduction, we are going to watch good professionals burn good listings for no reason.

This came up on one of our Power Agent coaching calls. An agent brought me a four-unit situation. The condo had been on the market for 86 days. She had lost two solid buyers. One was all cash, and the all-cash buyer still walked. Any time an all-cash buyer walks, leaders should pay attention, because the one thing you cannot blame is financing.

The building had a balcony and structural-compliance issue, the kind of inspection-and-repair requirement that has spread across the country since Surfside and that states like California have written into law for decks and balconies. The repairs had not been started. A new management company had taken over months earlier and was still, in their words, figuring things out.

Because the work was un-started, the building landed on the list that does not qualify for conventional Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac financing. The financed buyers were gone instantly. The all-cash buyer pulled the association financials, saw thin reserves and un-started repairs, read it correctly as a poorly run building, and decided the headache was not worth it.

Powerfact: Structural-compliance problems do not lower a home’s price. They remove a home’s buyers.

That distinction matters for anyone leading a team, because it changes the coaching. When a building cannot get conventional financing, the buyer pool does not shrink, it collapses to cash buyers willing to tolerate a troubled association. We just watched one of those rare buyers say no. Telling that agent to drop the price would have been coaching malpractice. The price was never the lever.

Here is the plan I gave her, and it is the plan I would want every leader teaching.

Take it off the market, temporarily. Days on market is a public number, and a climbing one negotiates against your seller before an offer is ever written. Pull the listing before the clock becomes the story.

Then, stay hired. This is the step that separates an advisor from an order-taker. Taking a listing off the market should not end the relationship, it should restructure it. The agent and I built a new listing agreement long enough to cover the repair timeline plus fresh marketing afterward at a likely new price.

If repairs take a couple of months and the sale takes several more, that is an eight or nine month agreement, not a renewal of the old 90 days. The property comes off the MLS and is not shown, but the agreement stays active. The agent keeps her position. The seller keeps a professional who is still working.

Then, advocate for the whole building. I told her to get in front of the management company and the association board, the president and the vice president, and push to expedite repairs that are hurting all 54 owners. This is where a top producer builds a reputation that compounds. When you are the agent pushing a stalled building back into compliance, every owner in that building knows your name, and so does every agent who tried and gave up. In a 54-unit building, that is 54 future listing conversations you just earned by being the one who showed up and did the work nobody else would touch.

We also talked through the rent-instead question, because these sellers had already relocated and were carrying the unit. Renting for a year while the building gets sorted is a legitimate option, but it has to be coached with the worst case on the table. In a tenant-friendly jurisdiction an eviction can run six to nine months. Florida’s landlord rules are workable. Other markets are not.

This property sits in California, and I told her plainly that I do not know California’s specifics well enough to promise a timeline, so she needs to find out. Because if the tenant will not leave and removal takes six months, the one-year rental really delivered about six months of clean cash flow. Leaders, this is the kind of worst-case math we owe our sellers, and it is exactly the math an anxious agent skips to avoid an awkward conversation.

The larger point for the industry is this. Compliance-driven financing blocks are a growing reality, and they reward the agent who can diagnose, not just discount. The old reflex of lowering price until something moves does not clear a building-level obstacle, and agents who only know that one move will lose listings they could have saved. The professionals who win in this environment are the ones who can look a seller in the eye and say, the problem is not your price, the problem is your building, and here is the plan to fix it.

That is the advisor our clients actually need. Serve, don’t sell. In a market with this much noise, the agent who clings to a dying listing looks desperate, and the one who calmly takes the sign out of the yard, extends the agreement, and goes to fix the real problem looks like exactly what they are. The one in control.
