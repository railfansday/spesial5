There’s no polite way to say this. It stinks to get a flat tire and stinks even more when your spare is also flat. As a veteran road warrior who drives 4,000 miles round trip each year between the northern border of Oregon and the tip of Baja in search of waves, wind, and warm weather, I know firsthand that flats happen. I’ve experienced everything from a full-on blowout at highway speed to the inevitable nail-in-the-tire slow leak. I’ve also woken up to find that inexplicable flat tire which has a way of happening when the waves are pumping and I’m jonesing to get the beach—but thankfully I own a portable air pump.

For those times when you need to get air in your tire quickly and get back on the road, a portable tire inflator is ideal to have in your vehicle’s trunk and definitely a smart addition to any car emergency care kit.

A portable tire inflator is also handy to have when it’s not a worst-case scenario. I use one of these when I deflate my tires to drive my car on dirt and sand so I can access remote Baja beaches and then inflate the tires when back on pavement. Portable tire inflators are simple devices, and there are two important interrelated, objective specs to be aware of—cfm and psi. Other subjective factors to think about are a portable air inflator’s power source and features like the air-valve connection, display type and memory settings. Here’s more detail below.

Cfm measures the volume of air that the compressor can deliver in one minute and indicates how much air a compressor can move. The higher the cfm spec, the faster a compressor can fill a tire; for portable inflators, a cfm of 1 works for most usage. However, if you are using the compressor often to fill all four tires or you drive a vehicle with large tires, then you might want a device with at least a 2 cfm spec.

Many people know the term psi from filling car and bike tires. It's a measure of the amount of pressure placed on one square inch of space. While cfm measures the amount of air a compressor can deliver in one minute, psi refers to the maximum amount of air it can deliver to an inflatable such as a tire or SUP.

While not a perfect analogy, think of cfm as relative to horsepower produced by a vehicle engine that determines the maximum amount of power it can deliver, while psi is analogous to the top speed the engine can deliver—and cfm and psi work hand in hand.

For most motorists, a portable tire inflator with a maximum capability of 120 psi is plenty. If you drive a bigger rig like my RV, you may want to consider a device with a psi of up to 150.

While cfm and psi are straightforward and easy to compare, how a portable tire inflator gets power depends on your needs, how often you’ll use the device, and if you have other portable devices that use compatible rechargeable batteries.

The portable tire inflators tested here rely on one of four power sources: a built-in rechargeable battery, 12 volts by plugging into a car’s cigarette lighter, 12.6-14.7 volts by connecting to a car’s battery via jumper cables, or 18 volts from a portable battery that’s the same used to power portable tools such as drills, saws, and sanders.

For the occasional roadside emergency, a low-cost portable tire inflator with a built-in rechargeable battery, or one that plugs into your car’s 12-volt cigarette lighter outlet, is adequate. The two Viair devices we tested that connect to a car’s battery both supplied impressive inflation power, but if you frequently air up tires—or are a gear guy or gal who owns a set of portable power tools—you may want to consider the higher power provided by a portable battery.

Keep in mind: With portable tire inflators that plug into a car’s cigarette lighter or connect directly to a car’s battery for power, you won't have to worry about not having a portable battery available or making sure a device with a built-in rechargeable battery is topped off. That is, unless you are unlucky enough to also have a flat tire and a dead battery, in which case a combo device like the Battery Tender 800 AMP Jump Starter and Tire Inflator is ideal.

While portable tire inflators are uncomplicated devices with few features, pay particular attention to the length of the power cables and inflation hose. With devices that plug into a car’s 12-volt outlet or connect to the battery, make sure the power cord is long enough to reach the rear tires.

Similar, although less critical, is the length of the air inflation hose. It can make the difference between easily filling a tire and wishing for a longer hose when dealing with a flat tire. The units we tested have hoses ranging from as short as just over 15 inches to as long as 30 feet.

Portable tire inflators also come with two types of Schrader chucks, the name for the connection that fits on the valve that’s standard on most car tires. A clamp-type (or push-on) chuck connects to a tire valve with a quick-release sleeve, and is faster and easier to use than screw-on chucks but can be less airtight.

Screw-on chucks that twist onto a tire’s valve stem provide a more secure, airtight connection that’s less prone to leaks, but take a bit longer to attach and remove. The Viair 400P-RV was an exception, with a convenient quick-disconnect Schrader chuck.

Finally, every portable tire inflator tested for this roundup has a digital display and the ability to dial in the desired tire pressure so you can set and forget it while filling a tire. All the portable tire inflators tested here can also be set for kPa and bar air-pressure measurements. In addition to a Schrader chuck, they all come with a Presta valve to fill a bike tire, an inflation needle for sports balls, and various other attachments.

Which of these is the best power source: a separate battery, a rechargeable battery, a 12-volt outlet plug, or battery clamps?

It depends how you intend to use your portable tire inflator along with other factors. For example, if you already own portable power tools that use a rechargeable battery, like the Milwaukee and DeWalt devices tested here, and/or want the maximum inflation power and use the devices frequently, that’s the way to go.

On the other hand, devices that connect to a car’s battery or 12-volt outlet for power or have an internal rechargeable battery are less powerful but also less expensive since you don’t need an extra battery. Devices like the DeWalt 20V MAX that run off a separate battery and have a 12-volt cord that plugs into a car’s cigarette lighter are the most versatile.

Do I need a separate inflator for tires and one for accessories?

Not really, since one inflator can serve dual duty for inflating a tire and, say, an air mattress. All the devices tested in this article except the DeWalt 20V MAX are designed mainly for high-pressure inflation.

They can fill things like air mattress and inflatable SUPs that require a large amount of air volume to fill quickly, but use low pressure. If you want to best of both worlds, go for a combo high-pressure/high-volume inflator like the DeWalt 20V MAX.

What is the difference between an air compressor and a tire inflator?

They both push air and can be used to fill a tire and other items. Here is the difference: An air compressor is a more powerful and expensive tool designed for tasks such as inflating large truck tires or running air tools, and usually used by tradesmen and mechanics, while a tire inflator is a smaller, portable, and less expensive device.

It is specifically for inflating car tires such as bike tires, sports equipment, and air mattress. Portable tire inflators are convenient since they are often run off a car's 12-volt power outlet, battery, or a rechargeable battery.

Depending on the quality of the device, frequency of use, and maintenance, a portable tire inflator can last anywhere from 5 to 20 years. My top pick, the Milwaukee M18 Portable Tire Inflator 2848-20, has a five year warranty.

Where is the best place to store this in my car?

I keep mine with my tool kit and emergency road kit in the cargo area of my Honda Element or with similar gear in the Thor Majestic RV. It can also easily be stored in a trunk. Just make sure that if your tire inflator has a rechargeable battery that you check in on it often.
