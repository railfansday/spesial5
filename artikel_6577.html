Naomi Watts and Maude’s Éva Goicochea on Modernizing Sexual Wellness

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

It’s 2026, and women’s sex drives are still taboo. A crop of modern brands want to change that, with better products, more education and packaging you want sitting on your bedside table.

Naomi Watts founded her brand, Stripes Beauty, after starting menopause at 36. She found her typical products no longer worked, and that she didn’t understand how to navigate this stage of life — unexpectedly early — because she never had conversations about it with her mother. Maude founder Éva Goicochea started her brand with a vision to bring more access and smarter design to products once relegated to hush-hush purchasing.

At the Vogue Business Global Summit: New York, Watts and Goicochea sat down to discuss their experiences in creating brands for themselves first, what progress has been made in opening up access to women’s sexual health, and why getting older doesn’t mean that women should have to give up on their sex lives. This interview has been edited and condensed for clarity.

Vogue: We’re here to talk about women’s sexual health. How did you both end up in this space?

Naomi Watts: I stumbled into menopause much earlier than I was prepared for. I was trying to get pregnant, and I didn’t understand why I was failing at that, and my doctor did some blood work and told me that I was close to menopause. I was 36 years old, guys. So, it filled me with panic. After that, I realized I had had so many symptoms in the lead-up to that that were just sort of brushed off as nothing. I had night sweats, I had thinning periods, I had periods that were close together. And there was no information. I called my mom and said, ‘Why didn’t you tell me more about this?’ and she said, ‘These were the conversations I never had with you because my mother never had them with me.’ And so it was this cone of silence that was just agreed upon, that women must zip it and suffer in silence.

So it took me about 10 years to get organized, but at 45, I started thinking about this product line. I was insistent at the beginning of our launch that we have two products, which are vaginal moisturizer and lube. I needed it myself, and I figured some others out there would, too.

Éva Goicochea: My mom did the opposite; she handed me Our Bodies, Ourselves when I was 5. And I was like, ‘OK’. But I’m from New Mexico, and it’s a state that really has very poor sex education. So, I was always hyper-aware. I became a legislative aide in healthcare, but I always had a heart for design. And at some point, I had that lightbulb moment, and it was: use design to destigmatize, and use design to create awareness. And that is what I did. So, I left LA, where I was living, and I moved here to start Maude, and the idea was to use design and inclusivity to break barriers. And that’s where we are.

Naomi Watts and Éva Goicochea on stage at the Vogue Business Global Summit: New York.

Vogue: Why do you think that these taboos have existed in the first place?

ÉG: It’s not that we don’t understand — maybe in some households or in some partnerships — that it’s OK to talk about this. I think we’ve just been told via product and the aisle and politics and policy that it’s not OK.

NW: I think that’s beautifully said, and I would agree with that. Women specifically have always had these health gaps and education gaps, and have been taught not to complain too much and just grin and bear it. And so much of sexuality, I think, is wrapped up in youth, and sex has been about reproductive stuff. As a woman ages, she becomes more sure of herself and more intentional about how she wants to put herself out there, be it in conversation, in the sack, whatever. I personally feel like there came a time when my hormones were not driving decisions, and I was able to authentically connect with my truth of what I felt like I wanted to do and what I didn’t. And when you get more organized with products, you don’t have to be uncomfortable. We can ride through the discomfort.

Vogue: There’s a white space because, to your point, as women get older, attention can shift away. Do you think that we’re in a more progressive place when it comes to this?

ÉG: I do. I think it is changing, despite that sometimes it feels like we’re taking a few steps back. I do think there’s a lot of progress being made. Future generations are not putting up with this.

NW: There are obviously pluses and minuses about social media, and you need to trust the sources, but we can generate information quickly. I mean, certainly for me, it helped me find a community, because the only way to create change is through the power and the strength of other voices. I do think there’s still more progress to be made, and that certainly in my industry, I want to see [more complex] women reflected on the screen. We can run households, we can raise children, we can take care of the in-laws and the parents, we can run businesses, we can do all of these things and also deal with a hormonal, massive change. So let’s see that reflected on TV and movie screens as well.

Vogue: When it comes to menopause, what brands are out there that women can turn to? What was on the market when you started your companies?

NW: Because of the loss of estrogen, suddenly all these products I’d used before were not working. So I started looking, but I didn’t like this promise that you could reverse time and look 25 again. Sure, that would be great, but it’s not going to happen. I felt like there was nothing out there that was speaking the truth. I wanted to create a brand that could deal with all of these issues, and our tagline is ‘from scalp to vag’, because each point is a pain point for hydration. It’s about just really good ingredients for certain sensitivities, and we have identified what that woman’s needs are, because I am her.

Naomi Watts, who founded Stripes after starting menopause at 36. "I am her," she says of her customer.

Vogue: Éva, who is the Maude customer? Who did you want your products to speak to?

ÉG: Everyone over 18. And I was told that can’t be done, but I really think that inclusivity is such a key piece because we’ve probably all felt very excluded from this category. It’s always been gendered, it’s been ageist, we’ve been talked at, there are rarely women executives at the table. So with Maude, it was this idea of, if we’re not seeing it, let’s go make it happen.

Vogue: I want to get your take on younger customers in this space, because we hear a lot that Gen Z is not having sex, they’re not going out. But on the runways, sex is coming back into the fashion zeitgeist. How are you reading this moment when it comes to sex itself? Is it taboo? Are we talking about it? Are we having it?

ÉG: I was just thinking about this yesterday — I understand Y2K fashion is back, but I think Gen Z is wise enough to know not to bring back the culture of that time. It was really a hard time for women. That said, I also think Gen Z is very pressured to always have technology in the middle of their relationships. That’s how they’re supposed to find people. And a lot of them live at home, so I don’t blame them for not having sex. But I don’t think that’s because they don’t want to; I think they’re more empowered, more educated, more aware of what they want more than ever. So, I see it as an amazing signal that this generation, again, is not going to put up with what we put up with.

Vogue: Maude recently made a big retail push, which signifies an expansion or acceptance of this category. What were those conversations like with retailers?

ÉG: Target Beauty Studio and Ulta came to us at the same time. Target has an audience that is couples, people in their 30s, 40s, and beyond. We’re still represented in Target Beauty Studio only as a body brand, so we’re trying to get them to think a bit differently. And then Ulta has been much more open, because intimate wellness is one of their four pillars of wellness, so we do not have to explain ourselves. And they carry some of our sexual wellness products versus just body care. But they came to us. How we navigate that and the future with them, I think, will be a bit at a different pace, but they both seem very receptive.

Products from Maude, which founder Éva Goicochea started with one idea: "use design to destigmatize." The range sits body wash next to lube.

Vogue: Naomi, how is Stripes thinking about product expansion after launching with only two sexual health products?

NW: We’ve just recently launched a gummy called Rite of Passion, and it’s backed by doctors. We developed it with Dr Cindy Meston and Dr Somi Javaid, and that was really important to me. It’s for sexual arousal, and we just launched it at Ulta, and we’re super excited. We really did it because we listened to our women, and the social listening told us that women feel like they don’t have sex in their marriages anymore, and sometimes it’s about drive, or sometimes it’s about pain. And there are things we can do for that, whether that’s lube, or estrogen, or more education. There’s no shame in it. You just need a little bit more mojo. I feel like I’m on this journey with them. The good news is that there’s really no more secrecy, there’s no more stigma, and women will readily open the conversation.

Rite of Passion, Stripes’s first product beyond its original lube and vaginal moisturiser, recently launched at Ulta.

Vogue: As a celebrity founder, how do you feel you’re setting Stripes up for success from here and for the long term?

NW: It was born out of my need, but hopefully we’ve done a good job of being authentic and inclusive, and reflecting this woman, who she is and what her needs are. So it really should be able to live with or without me. That said, I don’t plan on going anywhere. Women come up to me, and instead of a selfie, they want to tell me about how they had a great conversation with their husbands, with their wives, with their children, with their boss, because permission was given to them that the dialogue was opened, and if I’ve had any part in that, I just feel so proud. It’s become my new purpose.

Vogue: What’s the biggest thing you've learned as a founder so far?

NW: Gosh. Just to continue to be authentic and to not be afraid of failure, and go after what you believe in. I’m someone who’s experienced a lot of failure as an actor, and had extreme highs, and some ones that just landed with a thud. I’m pretty thick-skinned. It’s not failure. It’s a new type of lesson that opens a door you may not have known existed.

ÉG: I live by the mantra that there is no arrival sign. I speak to a lot of founders, and they ask, ‘When am I going to wake up and feel like I’m accomplished?’ And I’m like, ‘You might not.’ And on having incredibly thick skin, it’s really not personal. You are the shepherd of your brand; it’s not about you, and that’s actually the most powerful thing you can do is recognize: ‘I have a responsibility to my customers or to my team or to the world.’ So that’s where I try to at least think for myself. ‘It’s not about you.’

Vogue: What’s one thing that you both wish the rest of the industry would learn from women in this space, women founders? And what would you challenge the industry to do differently next year and beyond?

ÉG: I am so grateful to be in rooms that talk about beauty and wellness, but I want us all to pause and remember that sexual wellness is a human need. I want the reverence and respect to be there for this category, because it permeates every part of our lives. And it’s so important that we create gender parity in these conversations for our daughters. And I just want this to be seen as something that is so deeply, ornately part of us that we don’t think of it as a blip, as a trend. This is not a trend.

NW: For a woman who’s experiencing perimenopause and menopause, she should feel supported at every stage, and she should feel vibrant. And sexuality is something that a lot of great things can come out of. I’m lucky, I have a lovely new husband — we’ve only been married a couple of years. A woman shouldn’t feel like it’s all over. I love that positive message and just want to keep expanding that, and sex ed. We don’t learn about it. Menopause is not an end, it’s just a new chapter. Just because the hormones come to an end doesn’t mean your sex life has to.
