How a Jungle Farm in the Yucatán Is Transforming Its Maya Community

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

“The future is ancestral,” said Gonzalo Samaranch Granados. “Mayans were very good observers of the sky, but also the jungle and its complex relationship of plants and animals.” The rainy season, when farming deep in the Yucatán jungle proves more challenging than usual, had ended, and he was preparing for the next growing cycle, when the garden beds at Mestiza de Indias, a 550-acre reserve and biodiversity lab, would once again sprout heirloom tomatoes, eggplant, carrots, kale, garlic, herbs—crops popular with restaurant and chef collaborators on the Riviera Maya—as well as indigenous varieties with deep cultural significance for the local Mayan community.

Mak’ulan (pepperleaf), pepitas, and maize form the base of the kots’ob tamales prepared by market stall women in the nearby town of Espita. Cempasúchil (marigolds) decorate altars during Día de Muertos. Tomatillos milperos (purple tomatillos) introduced from Hidalgo are added to sikil p’aak and sopa de lima. Samaranch selects and saves native seeds, but he also sources from organic seed companies such as Baker’s Creek and Kitazawa. He’s currently on the hunt for wild ts’unu’un p’aak, or hummingbird tomatoes, a variety endemic to the Yucatán.

Other passion projects at Mestiza de Indias include the cultivation of rare Maya vanilla orchids and the extraction of a citrusy honey produced by endangered Melipona bees. (Ancient Mayans were avid apiarists. Ah Muzen Kab, the Maya god of bees, is featured in the 1,100-year-old Madrid Codex, which also contains detailed instructions for beekeeping.) Next to a ts’iits’il che’ tree where orchid vines twined, Samaranch walked into a screened enclosure protecting square wood boxes that serve as hives for these stingless bees. He removed the top of one to reveal treasure: Instead of combs, the bees create waxen pods, oozing amber-hued honey. It’s never a lot, so every precious taste is bittersweet. Equally important, they are critical pollinators for tropical ecosystems and go crazy for the nectar of ts’iits’il che’ blossoms, which flower after the first rainfall after the dry season. “What we do is possible because I was fascinated by traditional Mayan agriculture,” said Samaranch, a native of Barcelona who traded the art world for regenerative farming. “It’s not only making food. This system is related to culture, medicine, spirituality. It’s about living in harmony with nature, a respectful system.”

This Mesoamerican worldview is known as the milpa. In the Yucatán, it refers to the symbiotic planting of corn, beans, and squashes, often called the Three Sisters, on small-acre farms. (Other Indigenous growers consider sunflowers or bee balm the fourth sister. And when watermelon was introduced to the New World in the 16th century, it was adopted as a sister, too.) The concept of caring for the land, putting back more than you take, replenishing waterways, and reviving heritage crops manifests differently across continents and climates, but it exists wherever farming communities still observe old and slow ways.

By adapting these polyculture techniques into a regenerative project, Samaranch is transforming both the land and its people, including the way they look at the culture of food itself. And he’s not alone: In Australia’s Far East Gippsland, Bruce Pascoe founded Black Duck Foods to perpetuate Aboriginal land-management traditions. Black Duck: A Year at Yumburra is his manifesto about caring for country but also a personal reclamation after devastating bushfires and the loss of elders.At Royal Queen Farms in Acadia Parish, Louisiana, artist and land steward Michelle Joan Papillion has created a food sovereignty network for her community while protecting species native to the region. Johnny Ortiz-Concha and his wife, Maida Branch, bring people together around a communal table at /shed, a subscription supper club that consciously celebrates their ancestry and ecosystem. Menus are based on the crops and livestock they raise or forage at their ranch outside Taos Pueblo. They are custodians of a landrace variety of blue corn known as Tu-ah-tah, gifted to the couple by Ortiz-Concha’s family.

For those who work at Mestiza des Indias, change also starts close to home. The reserve lies almost four miles down a rough track outside the Mayan village of San Pedro Chenchelá, where most residents still live in primitive casa de huano, or mud-daubed wooden houses thatched with palm, and wear brightly patterned huipils, the distinctive Mayan tunic that predates Hispanic fashions. Hiking on foot to the milpa, field-workers have to beware of jaguars, rattlesnakes, and even poisonous trees.

During high season, Samaranch presently employs about 10 workers, most of them women whose husbands commute to the coast for tourism-related jobs. Others leave for bigger cities. Because family members can be away from home for extended periods, this has disrupted the ancestral rhythm of life. And when they do return to the community, it’s often with addiction problems. Even deep in the Yucatán jungle, the arrival of methamphetamine has devastated the region’s younger generation.

To counter this cultural disruption, Samaranch’s Mayan wife, Martha Elena Chan Tuz, a community project leader and former municipal tourism secretary, launched a complementary initiative, forming a cooperative of local needlework crafters who hand embroider table linens with intricate xookbil chuy, or cross-stitches. Their exquisite dinner napkins appear on tables during a communal meal in La Ceiba Garden & Kitchen at Rosewood Mayakoba. “Bien contentas,” said Chan Tuz, when asked how her neighbors have responded to the project. Samaranch explained: “It gives the women some independence. They have money and can manage their time. It’s something positive and very helpful, a job they can combine with what they do at home. Normally, they have to ask permission to work with us, but in this case, the men are grateful the women can contribute.”

Each worker gets to take home a basket of produce from the milpa as part of their wages. With only modest tiendas that stock junk food and shelf-stable canned goods, it’s generally a struggle to put fresh, healthy food on the table. (Especially when backyard milpas go untended because breadwinners are absent.) “We thought the people who grow the food should eat the food,” said Samaranch. “They get the same as the resorts we supply, so we have changed the diet.” He explained that children in San Pedro Chenchelá and the neighboring village of Kunché are now more excited about vegetables than soda or chips. “When a kid asks, ‘When will the eggplants be ready again?’ I feel we’re doing right.”
