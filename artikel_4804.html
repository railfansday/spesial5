Are AI Design Visualizers Selling Us on Slop?

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Can I interest you in an autonomous sauna rideshare? How about a conversation pit car? Or a Magic 8 Ball in the shape of Rick Rubin? How about a plate for a single almond or a toilet turntable?

Friends, do I even need to say it? These objects—or slopjects, as their creator calls them—are not real. Though they look convincing on the feed, they don’t exist in meatspace the way that you or I do. They are Dadaist fantasies, dreamed up and rendered digitally without the limitations of production budgets, market demand, or even those pesky things known as laws.

The slopject’s unsavory first cousin, the slopspace—a full-scale interior scheme, created through digital rendering, and now generative AI—is increasingly populating home-decor inspiration sites like this one, where a viewer can encounter improbable living scenarios like garage apartments with no ladders or stairs to reach the lofted bed.

With hyperrealistic renderings a mere prompt away (and disclosures of AI-generated content remaining spotty at best), it’s getting harder to tell what’s real. On platforms like Pinterest and Instagram, where homeowners often seek inspiration for their design projects, the speculative images that float through the digital ether can be particularly hard to detect. Entire subreddits are dedicated to sniffing them out.

And detection is just the beginning. Aside from struggling to detangle fact from fiction in stumbled-upon images, consumers are also using LLMs—or large language models, AI tools designed to work with human speech—to generate their own visions of their dream home, without always understanding what it takes to make those fantasies a reality. That dynamic is starting to have concrete consequences for the professionals charged with designing and building the rooms we live in, where dollars and cents, artisanal skill, and the laws of physics remain painfully real. (Just ask the guy who tried to exit the lofted bed without a ladder.)

“People are seeing or prompting more fantastical solutions that are not rooted in the reality of the built environment, and then they’re presenting those to us as something that can be executed,” says Kevin Greenberg, founder and principal of the AD PRO Directory–listed firm Space Exploration Design. “We do it diplomatically and we do it politely, but we will tell them, ‘Oh, you know, it’s really difficult to achieve a stone ceiling.’”

This change is happening fast. Stanford University’s 2026 AI Index Report notes that more than half of the population has started using generative AI in the past three years—a swiftness that outpaces both personal computers and the internet itself. When Midjourney and other AI imaging tools first hit the market, Greenberg says, clients used them to test the limits of what was aesthetically possible. “The prompt was like, ‘tropical Tudor fantasy,’ and then we were left to clean up the mess,” he recalls. “The images we were seeing were just tacky, and we all got a really big belly laugh out of it.” No one’s laughing now, he says.

These days, homeowners are feeding designer-created collages, mood boards, and sketches into Claude to see what spaces might look like once they’re built. The results tend to be uncanny: surfaces gleam like the Las Vegas strip, proportions are wonky, and there’s an unsettling feeling of dead air. Cy and Genevieve Carter, the principals of the New York City–based interiors firm Carter Design, joke that the worst dig they can make on a space is to say it feels like standing inside of a rendering. In the world of AI images, “there’s not a simplicity of space,” says Genevieve, which often means that more subtle textures like plaster don’t properly register.

Jake Shapiro, the owner of Hudson Valley–based millwork studio Slabrock, compares the images produced through AI software to the bloodless cuisine now rendered on deli signs. “It looks like scrambled eggs, but it also looks like H.P. Lovecraft, and that’s just horrific,” he says. “The same thing exists in design.”

There’s treachery in these images, designers agree, in that they portray a space nearly—but not quite—photorealistically. While that theoretically might help a homeowner understand how a designer’s vision for a space maps onto the real thing, it also bakes in a false sense of exactness that can leave room for misunderstanding or even disappointment. Organic materials like wood, for instance, can be difficult to predict with precision in AI. Every tree is different, Shapiro points out.

Catherine Campbell Pearson, a set designer and stylist based in Brooklyn, is seeing AI-fantasy expectations infiltrate the environments she designs for photoshoots too. In the past she and her clients would work from a “comp,” or a Photoshopped portrayal of the imagined end shoot. With those more lo-fi pictures, there was an understanding that the finished product would look slightly different. That’s disappearing in the age of GenAI: “It’s such a cohesive version of their idea that there’s much more attachment to the finished product looking exactly like this AI image.”

This phenomenon has real consequences for designers, whose expertise is on the line when a rendering goes awry. When clients go off on their own and submit designers’ early proposals into AI platforms, “The rendering that it produces is really unsexy and clunky and ugly,” says Greenberg. “Then they’re looking at us like, ‘Well, you’re actually not that good at your job.’”

What these examples illustrate isn’t a new problem, exactly, but it is one that’s more apparent in the age of AI. Just as Pearson has relied on low-resolution comps to create environments in the past, designers often use tools like collage, drawings, and mood boards to gesture at vibe and design intent before progressing to a more dialed-in rendering. But most homeowners don’t have the language to communicate exactly what they want to a designer—and when they do, the language may not precisely match the listener’s interpretation. (One person’s “cozy” interior is another’s clutter-filled nightmare, just as one person’s “serene” oasis is another’s sterile dystopia.)

LLMs offer clients a way to articulate their ideas in a way they may not have been able to otherwise—but in doing so, they create a highly specific rendering for a space that will require negotiation with real materials, labor, and budget—the latter being a serious constraint where AI can cause problems, says Tyler Polich, cofounder of the Los Angeles–based firm Years Studio. He shares an example of a client fiddling with an image and generating an element fully clad in copper—an option he and his partner would have never presented because of the cost. “Maybe it makes for a compelling image,” he concedes, “but we know that Gemini just blew their budget immediately.”

In certain cases, these visualizations can simplify things for designers and clients. Lucca Zeray, the Berkshires-based maker of Lucca House modular shelving, has found the addition of an AI rendering tool—a “configurator” that can depict his brand’s color-edged units in the proportions they’re seeking and provide a cost estimate—to be a game changer in the ordering process. “It’s a way that we can be like, ‘Here, you draw it,’ and close the gap,” he says. Unlike the every-detail-on-the-table possibilities of a full interior design rendering, Zeray’s configurator is only manipulating a few straightforward options, which might be part of the reason for its success.

In the reality of a built design, image making is only a small piece of the puzzle. The real work is in executing—navigating the personal relationships, sourcing fabricators, and negotiating details with the give and take of material reality. “We don’t work just in ideas,” says Genevieve Carter. “We work in stuff and things, and people, and their expertise is still a dialogue.”

Beyond the making itself, the design still needs to be good. Before AI was widely adopted, the algorithm-fueled flattening of taste was already in the cultural crosshairs. Social platforms reward likeable, heavily publicized images, like Athena Calderone’s marble-wrapped Brooklyn kitchen—photos that have been shared again and again. The problem then becomes that everyone is asking for an Athena Calederone–style kitchen. (It’s worth noting that even Calderone herself seems to have moved on from this look, by the way—her most recent home tour features a much moodier, Art Deco–inflected interior.) In today’s technological climate, this dilemma is taking a new form, as visuals take shape based on all of the environments that have been photographed and put online. But, says Polich, there’s an inherent flaw in this system: “The vast amount of visual information is relatively mediocre.” Real estate websites, DIY blogs, e-commerce images—most of the internet doesn’t look like, well, AD. It’s designed to be crowd-pleasing and just fine, not necessarily something extraordinary or innovative.

To find freedom from the tethers of the algorithm, Space Exploration Design encourages clients to go analog in seeking inspiration: “Flip through old magazines, show us an image from Automotive Design, show us a watch, show us your favorite painting—show us something else that we can build from, or that we can respond to in an oblique way,” Greenberg says.

Years Studio similarly seeks to tease out its clients’ personalities—“something that is real and human and only exists in them,” as Polich puts it. That’s a vulnerable undertaking, but also one that AI cannot replicate when its foundation is built on remixing what’s already come before. Greenberg, the Carters, and Polich all recognize that there’s some element of a trust fall required on the client’s side. By the end of a project, a designer is often familiar with the most intimate details of their client’s life—even what they keep in their nightstand. (They see those specialty chargers, and they don’t judge.)

But in earning trust, ironically, AI is also occasionally lending designers an assist. For the Carters, an exacting client was reassured in the studio’s recommendation of a particular stone after she’d exhausted all of the other options herself in her AI engine of choice. Another time, they won over a prospect by tweaking his AI rendering, and suggesting color options the client hadn’t considered. Is it possible that Claude, ChatGPT, or Gemini could remind homeowners why they hired a designer in the first place? Just ask the Rick Rubin Magic 8 ball: Signs point to yes.
