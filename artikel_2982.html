O’Reilly’s Sales to Professional Mechanics Jumped 12.7% Last Quarter. Here’s Why That’s the Clearest Sign of Its Moat.

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

If you're tired of the AI trade, it's time for a closer look at this auto parts retailer’s booming business.

By Neil Patel – Nov 26, 2026 at 4:30PM EST

10 stocks we like better than O'Reilly Automotive ›

Investors might be jaded by artificial intelligence (AI), which seems to have been an even hotter topic this week with Anthropic's (ANT +1.08%) initial public offering. But it's never a bad idea to consider expanding your horizons and figuring out which other parts of the economy might deserve your capital.

O'Reilly Automotive (ORLY +1.32%) is a sleepy business, but it continues to operate at a high level. Revenue and diluted earnings per share were up 9% and 14%, respectively, year over year in the third quarter. The highlight was how well it's doing with professional customers, with sales to this group rising 12.7% last quarter.

That last data point is a clear signal of O'Reilly's wide economic moat.

Same-store sales (SSS), which strip out new locations, rose 7.2% in Q3, after 5.6% growth a year ago. Management credited "share gains on both sides of our business."

But the professional side is the standout. Sales to repair shops and other pros jumped 12.7% to $2.6 billion, roughly double the 6.2% growth in do-it-yourself (DIY) sales. Professional transactions grew at a mid-single-digit rate. More shops are placing orders, supporting volume, and not just paying higher prices.

Time is money. A mechanic gets paid when a car leaves the lift. Every hour spent waiting on a part is lost revenue. So shops pick the supplier with comprehensive parts availability and fast delivery.

That's where O'Reilly's moat shows up. Its 6,758 stores are backed by 400 hub stores and 32 distribution centers. A regular store carries 24,000 stock-keeping units (SKUs). A hub can hold up to 115,000 SKUs and resupply nearby stores several times a day.

Online sellers can't match that. The company has an intangible asset built over decades. Scale adds a cost advantage through vendor leverage and high-margin private-label brands.

Operating margin widened to 21.4% from 20.7%, including the benefit from a one-time $39 million tariff refund. Management raised its full-year SSS outlook to 5.5% to 6.5% and plans 225 to 235 net new stores this year. Each one makes the network denser.

The biggest long-term threat is electric vehicles (EVs). They have no oil to change and far fewer moving parts. Battery EVs were just 6% of new U.S. vehicle registrations in the third quarter, so the shift is slow. But it's real.

Looking ahead, pro revenue growth is the number to watch. If it continues to outpace DIY, the moat is intact.

O'Reilly has no problem growing profits. But the leadership team is also focused on shrinking the number of shares. The diluted share count fell by 5% over the past year, following $3.5 billion in buybacks through the first nine months of 2026. This ongoing trend is why EPS rises faster than net income.

The industry backdrop helps, too. The average U.S. vehicle was 12.8 years old in 2025, according to S&P Global Mobility, a figure that has steadily increased. Older cars break down more often. That's a powerful catalyst for O'Reilly's 33 straight years (and counting) of SSS growth.

The stock, up 427% in the past decade (as of Nov. 20), is rarely cheap. It trades at a price-to-earnings ratio of 28.6, versus a 23.3 multiple for the S&P 500. For investors with a five-year (or longer) horizon, I believe that premium is fair for a company this consistent.

It also offers quality exposure outside of popular AI stocks. Its fortunes depend on aging cars and busy repair shops, not data center spending. For investors looking to diversify beyond tech, that's a valuable trait. Boring businesses can make exceptional investments.
