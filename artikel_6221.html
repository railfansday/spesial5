United Airlines CEO Says FAA Meltdown Was “Nobody’s Fault” And Wants Airlines To Help Fix The System

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

United Airlines CEO Scott Kirby thanked employees for helping passengers through the nationwide FAA disruption, singling out a crew that helped a mother with three tired children through the airport. But his message also included a striking assessment of the crisis: it was “nobody’s fault,” and the private sector needs a greater role in fixing the system. I appreciate his focus on solutions, though that does not eliminate the need for accountability.

In a video message to United employees reviewed by Live And Let’s Fly, Kirby praised the airline’s recovery from the FAA ground stop and described conversations with administration officials and congressional leaders from both parties.

He argued that fixing air traffic control requires more than money and asked for private-sector involvement in the process. But he began with the people who took care of passengers during the disruption, including the crew pictured above.

Here is the full transcript of his remarks:

Hello everyone, I wanted to come to you today before the holidays and say thank you for the amazing job that you did, taking care of each other and our customers during the FAA shut down over the past weekend. You really did do an amazing job. If I look at the numbers, our airline recovered better than any of our large competitors. If I look at the NPS scores, remarkable how positive they were because all of you were really committed to taking care of customers.

While I can look at the numbers, the anecdotes meant even more to me: the customers that I heard from, all the special things that our people, that all of you, did for customers really helped build the brand and really made all of us shine in a moment of difficulty. My favorite, perhaps, was that picture of the crew helping a mother with three young children get through the airport when they were tired and they were sleepy, and it hit two of the things that are really important to me: build an airline that is great for customers, and I think of that is make it easy for parents with young children to travel, but also an airline where our people committed to doing the right thing and taking care of customers.

So thank you for all that you did working hard over the weekend and while you were working hard taking care of customers, I was busy on the phone talking to people in the administration, the leadership on both sides of the aisle in both the House and the Senate to really try to get this fixed.

So I’m not going to play the blame game because this has been building for decades. And the truth is this is nobody’s fault, but it is everybody’s responsibility to fix it. And it’s not just about money. It’s going to be about having a real process and having a real engagement to fix it. My request, frankly, is to let us be inside the tent. Let the private sector, we know how to do some of this stuff. We care about it more than anyone, for you and for our customers. And so I am committed, and I got great commitments from people on both sides of the aisle. Everyone wants to fix this but we need to stop talking about it and finally get it over the finish line.

So thank you for all that you did to work really hard taking care of customers. Thank you for the best recovery of anyone in the industry. Thank you in advance for all you’re gonna do to take care of customers over the busy holiday travel that’s about to come up on us, so thank you. I’ll see you in the friendly skies.

The photo Kirby referenced is my favorite part of this message too.

According to his account, the crew was helping a mother traveling with three young children. You can see crew members carrying children while others pull luggage through the terminal. Here’s the original Instagram post:

A post shared by Tammy Stull (@thetammystull)

As a father who has traveled extensively with young children, I recognize how valuable an extra pair of hands can be!

After a long delay, even the walk out of the airport can feel daunting (remember this?). A child falls asleep, another needs attention, and the bags (all of them) must carried. Someone stepping in to help can change how you remember the entire day.

Kirby talks about building the United brand through those encounters. That is certainly true and I really like that he recognized this particular act of kindness in a message to the broader workforce. Employees should hear that helping a tired family is a core mission of the job and that employees are already stepping up in a beautiful way.

His claims about United having the best recovery and positive Net Promoter Scores are his assessment. That could be a classic Kirby exaggeration, but having flown last weekend myself, everyone seemed to be in a good mood despite a delayed departure.

I agree that America’s air traffic control problems have developed over decades. I also agree that another round of partisan finger-pointing will not modernize the system and in some ways is just not constructive.

But I cannot agree with the blanket assertion that this is nobody’s fault. There are two different issues here: the longstanding condition of the infrastructure and the decisions surrounding the work that preceded this particular disruption.

As I discussed yesterday, new reporting raises questions about contractor supervision and changes to the FAA’s maintenance approval process. Those questions still require answers.

I take Kirby’s point to be that everyone should stop using the crisis to attack political opponents and start working together. That is a worthwhile appeal and one that I share. But identifying responsibility is part of preventing another failure.

If an oversight process was inadequate, it needs to change. If a contractor failed to follow required procedures, that needs to be addressed, no matter how often its work went smoothly in the past. If the agency lacked sufficient staff or technical expertise, the remedy should reflect that. We cannot reliably fix a problem while declaring in advance that nobody bears responsibility for it…but I’ll stop beating a dead horse.

Kirby’s request for private-sector involvement is bold, but far too generalized to give us insight into the seriousness of that wish. Airlines have a direct interest in a functioning air traffic control system. Disruptions strand their aircraft and crews, cost them money, and leave their employees dealing with understandably frustrated passengers. Their operational experience should inform the modernization effort.

But Kirby did not describe a specific governance proposal in this message. Asking to be involved does not, by itself, amount to a detailed plan for privatization or airline control of air traffic services. I would want to see what he proposes before endorsing or rejecting it…and wonder what it is that United or other carriers can bring to the table that would actually help the FAA.

There is also an obvious complication: a private contractor was already involved in the work at the center of this disruption. Private-sector participation alone does not guarantee competent execution any more than government involvement guarantees failure.

Kirby’s observation that this is not just about money is very true. New equipment requires funding. So do the people needed to install, maintain, and supervise it. But an appropriation cannot substitute for a sound implementation and oversight process.

I also appreciate he is holding conversations with leaders from both parties. That is the work airline executives should be doing when a problem this consequential affects their customers and employees…he has to be friends with everyone in the impending era of divided government.

I made a similar point yesterday: Congress needs to investigate what happened while working with the administration on practical improvements. We do not need to choose between accountability and cooperation…let’s just get it done.

Kirby can use his access to help advance that work. I hope he does.

Kirby’s message combines well-deserved recognition for employees with an appeal to stop arguing and fix air traffic control.

The crew helping a mother with tired children deserves the praise. His call for a serious process, rather than simply another demand for money, is true, but I liken it to a political talking point and working together should begin with an honest accounting of what failed. A willingness to cooperate is valuable: so is a willingness to accept responsibility.

In the meantime, that photograph is a welcome reminder that employees can still take excellent care of people during a deeply frustrating travel day. Kudos to those employees for practically showing, “Good Leads The Way.“
