Many of us have been there: You book a flight through an airline's major hub, but your first segment is delayed and you miss your connecting flight, only to find out the next available flight with an open seat is hours away (or even the next morning). Or maybe you're trying to get home, but your flight is canceled, and you're stuck at your destination an extra night.

And when things go really wrong, it could be days before you can reach your destination.

There's no way to get that lost time back, but trip delay protection can come to the rescue if you used the right credit card to pay for your trip.

Trip delay protection reimburses reasonable out-of-pocket costs (lodging, meals, toiletries and local transportation) when a trip you paid for with an eligible card is delayed past a set threshold for a covered reason. You’ll pay these costs upfront, then file a claim for reimbursement so you're not left to cover those expenses yourself.

This coverage is secondary — meaning it only kicks in after you receive any other compensation. So if an airline provides food vouchers, you can't also request reimbursement for those meals. Even if the airline covers some expenses, you can still file a claim for any remaining expenses, like if you book a hotel at your own expense.

Importantly, a canceled flight is not automatically a covered trip delay. The cancellation generally must leave you stranded long enough to meet the policy's time requirement, result from a covered reason and involve a trip booked according to the card's payment rules.

While numerous travel rewards credit cards include trip delay protection, each policy is a little different.

The $500 headline makes these policies look more alike than they are. In practice, they differ on how soon coverage begins, whether the cap scales with each traveler or ticket and whether the way you paid actually activates the benefit.

Being reimbursed per traveler means you actually have a much higher limit to work with. For example, if you're traveling as a family of four and spend a total of $800 on two hotel rooms, meals and transportation, you'd be reimbursed for all $800 if the policy is "per traveler", assuming everyone qualifies and all expenses are eligible. But if the card is capped at $500 per trip, you'll only get a $500 payout, regardless of how many people are traveling, leaving you to foot the rest of the bill.

For your travel to be covered, you must have paid for the original trip with the credit card that provides coverage. Some cards, like those issued by Capital One or Chase, include award travel if you paid for the taxes and fees on your flight with an eligible card.

However, other issuers, including American Express, require you to pay for the full cost of the trip with the card (or with reward points from the program). Amex also requires cardholders to pay for round-trip travel with their eligible card, though it's not as limiting as it sounds. Two separate one-ways still count, as long as both were purchased with the same card.

Trip delay coverage is only set up to cover expenses from lengthy delays rather than minor inconveniences. A minimum six-hour delay is required before coverage kicks in, and some cards require longer.

Some cards also allow for reimbursement of expenses during a forced overnight stay. That overnight provision makes some 12-hour policies more useful than they first appear because a flight that was supposed to take off at 10 p.m. and is now at 6 a.m. would qualify.

Every card in our table covers bad weather and equipment failure. Beyond that, the lists differ. Capital One and Chase also cover strikes. Amex and Citi don't, but they do cover lost or stolen passports.

Traffic or arriving late at the airport will not turn a missed flight into a covered delay. A previously announced or "known" disruption may also be excluded, so don't book a flight during a hurricane that's already started and expect to be covered.

Capital One has a pretty minimal umbrella of coverage when it comes to who's eligible. Trip delay protection usually only covers you, your spouse and your dependent children under the age of 22.

Chase has a more inclusive definition of family, including children (of any age), as well as parents, siblings, grandparents and more. Covered Chase family members also do not necessarily need to be traveling with the cardholder, as long as an eligible ticket was purchased with a qualifying card.

But Amex cards with this benefit generally cover companions who are traveling with you, even if they're unrelated, as well as tickets you purchased with your card on behalf of your spouse/domestic partner or unmarried dependent children up to age 19 (or under age 26 if a full-time student at an accredited college or university) even when they aren't traveling with you.

Chase doesn't list a maximum number of claims anyone can file, but American Express restricts you to two claims per eligible card per 12-month period. That distinction matters for frequent travelers.

Two claims may sound sufficient, but a frequent traveler could use both during one rough round-trip. For someone who flies regularly, an unlimited annual claim cap can be a meaningful advantage.

Claims can take weeks to be processed and paid out, which can make other card benefits you have useful in the meantime. Airport lounge access might cover the cost of food and drinks while you're waiting out a delay rather than having to collect and submit restaurant receipts, for example.

Though it's a modest credit, it can help cover your meal or transportation in real time, no paperwork required (and doesn't preclude you from filing a trip delay claim for other expenses later).

The most convenient way to file a claim is online through your card issuer's benefits website. You'll find the link within your online account or benefits guide. You can also initiate a claim by calling the phone number on the back of your card.

Usually, you must initiate a claim within 30 to 60 days of the event. I recommend opening the claim as soon as possible, even if you don't have all the receipts and paperwork yet. You'll typically have an additional grace period to submit all documentation after the claim has been formally opened.

When deciding between earning slightly more rewards and activating stronger insurance, the insurance may be the better trade on an expensive, complicated or disruption-prone itinerary. You'll also want to double-check that your trip delay coverage rules match how you actually booked.

Before travel, save your card's current benefits guide, use the card in the required way and keep every receipt. You certainly hope to never use this benefit, but it can be extremely valuable if you do.
