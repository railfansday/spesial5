Stanford's century-long AI study admits it's already out of date

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Five-year review charts upheaval in work, science, and public life – with little consensus on who's in control

Twelve years into Stanford's 100-year study of artificial intelligence, the project's third report raises more questions than it answers.

The 2026 AI100 Study Panel report acknowledges its limitations in its twelfth takeaway: "This report will be out of date by the time you read it."

But it's nonetheless a worthwhile exercise for those who haven't been paying close attention to the development of machine learning over the past few years and for policymakers who may benefit from a review of AI's metastatic growth.

"The last five years constitute a watershed moment in the history of AI," said Professor Sheila McIlraith, chair of the AI100 Standing Committee, in a statement. "This report examines what led to the extraordinary capabilities of today's AI systems, and how they are reshaping society. As we look ahead, we anticipate continued increase in AI capabilities alongside transformative advances in science and medicine.

"Rapid change will challenge governments and industry to support and safeguard our people, our institutions, and our critical infrastructure. Both the benefits and the risks of AI technologies require the same clear-eyed scrutiny."

Established in 2014, the One Hundred Year Study on Artificial Intelligence (AI100) is overseen by a Standing Committee of AI experts and administered by Stanford's Institute for Human-Centered Artificial Intelligence (HAI). It's charged with producing reports every five years, the first of which debuted in 2016.

The 2026 report's other key points highlight the transformation brought about by increasingly capable machine learning software.

It identifies scaling as the engine of AI's advance, but also points to the growing cost and difficulty of obtaining training data as constraints on further progress.

The report likens AI's disruptive potential to that of the World Wide Web – without dwelling on the irony that the web itself is among the things being disrupted. It points to creative industries, computer programming, and mathematics as fields once deemed beyond the reach of automation that are now being upended.

Science, the report says, is being transformed by AI as it accelerates research timelines and opens up novel challenges. At the same time, the report cautions that "we will need to understand how AI-mediated information environments reshape collective cognition and moral behavior." The recent retraction of some 1,400 scholarly papers for failure to meet academic standards highlights the urgency of that message.

Then there's the AI investment boom and "fears of a financial bubble," which won't be assuaged by recent NASDAQ volatility and revelations about Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang's relationship with an alleged intelligence operative for China.

Concerns about the concentration of frontier AI development, the pressure AI puts on cybersecurity, and the active use of AI for military applications are also noted in the report.

So too are worries about what AI-generated media does to trust.

Item nine on the report's takeaway list is perhaps the most noteworthy: "AI is reshaping the lives of billions of people, yet the public has had little say in how it is developed and deployed."

The recent US midterms suggest voters are finding ways to make themselves heard about AI datacenters, even if that falls short of shaping broader AI policy.

Item ten posits that longstanding academic concerns about AI bias, ethics, and safety are beginning to converge with what until recently seemed like worries inspired by speculative fiction, such as AI agents hacking into companies without human oversight.

And item 11 frames the entire report by calling out the lack of a coherent regulatory response to AI: "AI governance is fragmented and increasingly framed in terms of national security, with little international consensus among states on its direction."

Those takeaways introduce the AI100 Study Panel's examination of 12 standing questions about what AI makes possible and how humans are handling it. Easy answers are in short supply.

But as the report's disclosure section makes clear, even the people taking the century-long view are using the technology to assess itself.

"Reflecting the progress in AI over the period this report covers, AI tools were used extensively in its production," the report explains.

"The report was written by humans, and its authors used AI primarily to transcribe and summarize meetings, support dictation, and check drafts for typos, consistency, duplication, tone, and structure. In some cases, AI suggested rewording of human-written text. AI was also used to compare this report with previous AI100 reports, identifying where expectations and reality have diverged or converged over five years. After completion, AI tools checked for inadvertent plagiarism and signs of AI-generated content." ®
