Bitwise has published a five-year framework for Solana that includes very different possible outcomes for SOL’s price. The range extends from an $80 bear case to a $385 base case and an $1,100 bullish scenario for 2031, according to reporting on its November 16 research. Those numbers are scenarios, not an announcement that the token will reach any particular level. The interesting story is the set of expectations about tokenized securities, payments and on-chain trading required to justify the more ambitious outcome.

Bitwise’s paper, “Tokenization, 24/7 Trading, and the Economics of Solana,” links possible SOL valuations to the role a fast blockchain might play in financial activity. The firm argues that tokenized assets, payments and round-the-clock trading could shift more transactions on-chain over time. Its publicly available summary identifies a $385 base-case outlook for 2031. That is a modeled result within a research framework, not a quoted future market price or a probability-weighted consensus. Investors should read the full assumptions and risk disclosures before comparing the figure with spot trading.

The $1,100 scenario discussed in The Block’s coverage assumes a much larger tokenized-equity market and unusually strong Solana participation. Among the assumptions cited are a 5% tokenization of global stocks and a large share of resulting trading volume flowing through Solana. A scenario built on that scale can help illustrate upside sensitivity, but it also magnifies execution risk. There is no guarantee that securities issuers will tokenize at the modeled pace, or that Solana rather than other blockchains and traditional infrastructure will win most of the business.

For related context, see our earlier report on Solana treasury and staking disclosures.

Even if tokenized assets and payments grow, their relationship to the native token’s value depends on fee structures, network demand, supply dynamics and competition. Low transaction fees can attract activity without making each additional transaction economically equivalent to a large increase in token demand. Layer-two systems, private networks and alternative settlement solutions may also compete for institutional transactions. The mechanism from network activity to investment return needs to be assessed, not assumed. Forecast tables tend to look precise even when the underlying business model is uncertain.

The three cases are most useful as a way to frame questions: how much tokenized volume might actually migrate, how quickly payments grow, what share Solana can secure and how much capacity it would need. The bearish case also reminds readers that technological adoption and investor returns can diverge. Bitwise itself sells crypto investment products, so its commercial perspective should be noted while examining the research. Treat each figure as conditional on the inputs rather than as investment advice or an independent valuation fact.

Scenario analysis becomes more informative when its inputs are varied, rather than when the highest output is presented as a prediction. An investor could test a slower tokenization rollout, tighter fee competition, different staking rewards or a valuation multiple below the research base case. Small changes in several assumptions can compound over five years. Bitwise's work is useful as a model of how network activity might translate into token economics, but competing networks, policy choices and unforeseen technology changes could make realised outcomes very different.

Forecasts make headlines, but assumptions are where the debate belongs. Tracking tokenized equity usage, payment volumes, transaction economics and competition over the next five years will show whether the underlying thesis is getting stronger or weaker.
