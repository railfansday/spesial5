These Remote Atolls Are Among the Last Places on Earth Humans Haven’t Touched. Here’s What Scientists Found.

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Bikar and Bokak sit so far from people that they may show us what the ocean looked like before humans.

There are few havens on Earth as pristine as the Bikar and Bokak atolls. Formed from sunken volcanoes around which coral reefs grew, these atolls in the Ratak chain of the Marshall Islands teem with life and remain uninhabited by humans. Rainbows of fish swim among corals and sea anemones, while sharks glide silently through the water in pursuit of prey. Until recently, even marine biologists had rarely explored these habitats in depth.

That began to change when Alan Friedlander set out across an impossibly blue swath of ocean and led his team into paradise. Friedlander is a marine ecologist at the University of Hawai’i who served as chief scientist for National Geographic’s Pristine Seas program on the expedition, and he takes a special interest in remote and protected regions of the ocean. The people of the Marshall Islands have known Bikar and Bokak for generations, but the atolls had never been comprehensively surveyed when Friedlander and his team arrived. For the Marshallese, the ocean is home even more than the land is. Their survival depends on the health of the fragile reef ecosystems around the country’s 1,225 islands and islets, only some of which are inhabited.

The opportunity came in 2023, when Pristine Seas was invited to join the Marshall Islands Marine Resources Authority in surveying Bikar and Bokak, which are safeguarded by the Utirik Atoll community. Friedlander and his team received a traditional blessing and farewell on Utirik before sailing north to Bikar, which lies about 72 miles (116 km) from the nearest inhabited atoll. Bokak is even more isolated, about 239 miles (385 km) away. This remoteness is part of the reason the atolls have remained relatively untouched by human influence. Friedlander wanted to carry out an ecological assessment of everything from the land habitats to the reefs and the open sea. That meant studying the structure and biodiversity of these ecosystems, documenting how well they had been preserved and how the two atolls differed, and identifying the conservation measures they would need in the future.

“The remote northern atolls of Bikar and Bokak represent some of the least disturbed coral reef ecosystems remaining in the tropical Pacific,” Friedlander and his co-authors wrote in a study recently published in Frontiers in Marine Science. “Both atolls are uninhabited and geographically isolated from major population centers and industrial development. Previous expeditions documented portions of their terrestrial habitats, seabird colonies, sea turtles, and shallow coral reefs.”

The team’s first stop was Bikar, which curves across the water like a crescent moon. There, the researchers used a variety of sampling methods, including scuba surveys, photography, underwater video, deep-sea cameras, and a submarine, to record which species were present and in what numbers. Polynesian rats, which humans introduced, were sometimes seen scurrying through the island’s old-growth Pisonia forests. Even so, native species still thrive. Seabirds such as masked boobies, great frigatebirds, black noddies, and white terns filled the skies, while shorebirds like the migratory Pacific golden plover pecked at the sand in search of small invertebrates. Bikar’s beaches are also a nesting ground for green sea turtles; the team recorded 132 nest pits there. Its reefs are full of fish, including several species of snapper and grouper. Giant clams, which host photosynthetic algae, filter the water and are vital to the health of the reef.

Farther north and even more isolated, Bokak is an oasis that has seen very little human interference. Its drier, savanna-like landscape is covered in tufts of bunchgrass and tree heliotropes, which bear clusters of small white flowers. Many of the seabirds that soar over Bikar are also found on Bokak. Bokak’s waters are even richer than Bikar’s: its reefs are dominated by coral and hold more fish by weight. Friedlander dove in to investigate. He came across a school of humphead parrotfish, a threatened species once thought to have nearly vanished from the islands. He also saw an unusual reversal of the typical food pyramid. Reef sharks and black jacks outweigh their prey in total biomass. Because these top predators feed so often, they keep energy and nutrients moving quickly through the reef, which helps the ecosystem run efficiently.

After Bokak, Friedlander’s team had one more location to survey, and it offered a sharp contrast. From 1946 to 1958, Bikini Atoll was a center of U.S. nuclear testing, starting with Operation Crossroads. When the islanders were evacuated before the first tests, they took only a few changes of clothes, not knowing that they would never be able to resettle their home for good. The series of detonations devastated the atoll. From the ocean’s glassy surface, Bikini seems to be ringed by cities of coral. But divers who swam closer found that many of these formations, which had taken thousands of years to grow, were little more than skeletons. Still, Bikini Atoll is not without hope. After searching long enough, Friedlander found patches of live coral that had settled on the dead reef and were swarming with fish. It was a sign of how resilient life can be.

Even Bikar and Bokak can’t fully escape what is happening elsewhere. Their remoteness protects them from most direct human pressures, but climate change is slowly reaching them, too. When the water gets too hot, corals expel the symbiotic algae that feed them and give them their bright colors, a process known as coral bleaching. Leftover radiation from the nuclear-testing era is still a source of contamination in the region. Meanwhile, currents carry in trash from other countries, and plastics break down into microplastics. People are responding with conservation measures. In 2025, two years after the expedition, the government of the Marshall Islands designated the waters around Bikar and Bokak, about 18,500 square miles (48,000 square km), as the country’s first national marine sanctuary, as part of the community-led Reimaanlok conservation framework. Despite the threats that come from far away, Friedlander is optimistic.

“As relatively intact tropical marine ecosystems become increasingly rare worldwide,” he and his co-authors concluded, “remote atolls such as Bikar and Bokak serve not only as regional conservation priorities but also as globally important ecological reference systems for understanding the structure and future trajectories of tropical oceanic ecosystems in the Anthropocene.”
