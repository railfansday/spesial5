NAD supplements promise to fight aging, but a new study complicates the story

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

A study of more than 1,000 adults links blood NAD levels to cardiovascular health, challenging a common claim behind anti-aging supplements.

NAD supplements are usually marketed on the idea that levels of the molecule fall with age and therefore need to be replaced. A new study of healthy adults found no evidence of such a decline in their blood.

The researchers instead found lower levels in people with heart and blood vessel disease.

People with high blood pressure showed the largest difference. This led the researchers to conclude that blood NAD is more closely tied to heart health than to age.

A team at Vilnius University in Lithuania carried out the research and tested blood from 1,068 adults. The study shows a link, not a cause. The authors make this clear.

Dr. Dalius Ratautas headed the work from the university’s Life Sciences Center.

“For years, the prevailing narrative has been that NAD declines as we age and therefore needs to be restored,” said Dr. Ratautas.

“Our findings suggest that this is not what happens in the blood of healthy adults.”

NAD is short for nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide.

Every cell in the body uses it to turn food into energy.

Studies in animals and humans have found age-related declines in NAD in some tissues, including muscle, brain and skin.

These results helped build a longevity market for pills such as NMN and nicotinamide riboside.

The body converts these compounds into NAD.

Each volunteer pricked a finger one morning before eating and left the blood drops to dry on a paper card for later laboratory analysis.

In the 707 participants who reported neither a chronic disease nor any use of NAD supplements, the team detected no consistent relationship between age and NAD among the men.

Women also showed no age-related decline. Instead, older women had slightly higher blood NAD levels.

The increase was small but statistically significant, and to the authors’ knowledge it has not been reported before.

A different picture emerged among the 243 volunteers who had a chronic illness.

Among men, median concentrations were approximately 9% lower in those with cardiovascular disease and approximately 11% lower in those with hypertension, compared with healthy controls.

For women, the comparison needed to account for age. Those with cardiovascular disease were older on average than the healthy participants, whose NAD levels tended to be slightly higher at older ages.

The team therefore compared women of similar ages.

Those with heart disease or high blood pressure had about 14% to 15% less NAD than their healthy counterparts.

The relationship between age and NAD also differed by heart health. Among men with heart disease, older participants had lower NAD levels. Healthy men showed no consistent relationship.

Women with heart disease did not show the small age-related increase seen in healthy women.

Other conditions were different. High cholesterol was not associated with different NAD levels, nor was thyroid disease in women.

“Instead, we found a clear association between lower NAD levels and cardiovascular disease. This raises a different and potentially important question: could NAD levels tell us something about cardiovascular health?” Ratautas said.

Earlier studies of NAD in blood often disagreed. The new findings may explain why.

According to estimates from the World Health Organization, about 1.4 billion adults between 30 and 79 years of age live with high blood pressure, and the condition becomes more frequent in later life.

If a study ignores heart health, its older age groups will include more people with high blood pressure.

Lower NAD in older volunteers could therefore reflect an association with high blood pressure rather than age.

On their questionnaires, 10 men and 12 women from the healthy group stated that they were already using an NAD booster. These 22 people had significantly higher concentrations than those who used none.

In six of them, the values were two to three times the normal range. A level of that kind turned up in only one person out of more than 1,000 who took nothing.

Two men who had been buying NMN on their own were monitored over 19 days, and within days of starting a daily 1,000-milligram dose, their blood NAD levels had entered the same high range.

No one knows what happens if blood NAD stays this high for years. The authors say most supplement trials last only a few weeks or months. The largest long-term trial they cite had just ten patients.

Some early trials found that NAD pills lowered blood pressure a little in people with hypertension, as an earlier analysis explained.

But the new study does not support taking supplements.

The team did not design it to test cause and effect. The authors say people should not use the results to promote NAD pills as a treatment for high blood pressure or heart disease.

Most NAD in blood is found in red blood cells, which work differently from muscle and brain cells.

In a commentary, Stephen Gardell and Joseph Baur warned that blood NAD levels may not reflect levels in other tissues.

The Vilnius team agreed but said blood tests could still be useful for doctors.

The researchers measured total NAD but could not measure hematocrit, the proportion of red blood cells in blood.

People with high blood pressure usually have more red blood cells. That should push NAD up, not down.

So the authors think the real gap may be even bigger than the one they found.

The patients’ medicines may also have affected the results. The authors cannot rule this out.

A financial interest should also be noted. Five of the authors are or were employed by SensoLife, the company that manufactures the NAD test kit used in the study.

Five others are or were employed by LifeLab1, the laboratory in which the samples were analyzed.

Scientists still do not know why NAD levels are lower in people with heart disease.

Low NAD may contribute to the disease or result from it. Another factor could also explain the link.

The main analysis used one blood sample per participant, so the researchers cannot tell which explanation is right.

The team now wants to see if blood NAD levels can help predict heart disease risk. They also plan to study how NAD and heart health are connected.

The full study was published in the journal Nature Metabolism.
