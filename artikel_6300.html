Canada’s endangered wildlife is stuck in a legal loophole

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Canada's Species at Risk Act has a stage with no deadline, and since 2017 just 6% of species found to be at risk cleared it on time.

When scientists assess a species as endangered, it is easy to assume legal protections will follow. In Canada, that process often stalls.

Since 2017, the country’s experts have recommended 288 wildlife species for listing under the Species at Risk Act.

Only 17 of them, about 6%, reached cabinet within the government’s own recommended timelines.

Peter Thompson, a postdoctoral fellow at Simon Fraser University, led the study with colleagues from the University of British Columbia and ten other institutions.

They rebuilt the paper trail for 963 species from the federal registry, about 5,700 documents in all.

“Canada boasts a wonderful array of wildlife and Canadians care about protecting our biodiversity,” Thompson said.

“When endangered species are left in endless political limbo, the chance of them going extinct increases, which is really against the best interests of those who care.”

Listing a species in Canada takes two stages. First, an independent panel called COSEWIC assesses how much trouble the species is in and recommends a status.

Then the federal government consults provinces, Indigenous rights holders, industry and the public, weighs the economic fallout, and the cabinet makes the final call.

The law sets deadlines at the start and the end.

The environment minister must respond to the panel within 90 days, and once the file reaches cabinet, a decision is due within nine months.

Both of those deadlines were met for every species in the study.

The middle step, where the minister’s office consults and decides whether to forward the file at all, has no legal deadline.

In 2017, the government published recommended timelines for it: 12 months for most species and 24 months for those needing longer consultation.

The authors call this stage the loophole, and it is where every delay in their data occurred.

Comparing eight years of assessments before 2017 with the eight years after, the team found that fewer species reached cabinet within the recommended timelines.

Before the policy, 16% of assessed species reached cabinet within the recommended timelines. After it, 6% did.

Species that did get forwarded took about 29 months on average.

As of January 2025, 181 species assessed since 2017 were still waiting. Most of those, 153, had already passed their deadline.

The degree of danger made no difference. Species assessed as endangered waited about as long as those assessed as special concern, the lowest tier.

The sharpest pattern was in the water. Of the 49 marine fish and 15 marine mammals assessed since 2017, none had been sent to cabinet by January 2025.

Among freshwater fish, 79% were still waiting, and among land mammals, 90%. Fish and mammals, far more than birds or plants, were the groups left waiting.

The waiting list includes 31 populations of Pacific salmon, every one assessed as endangered or threatened.

In the government’s 2023 report to Parliament, all 31 carried the same sentence: the collection of information needed for a decision is extensive and underway.

Aquatic species answer to a second department, Fisheries and Oceans Canada, which is charged with both the fishing economy and conservation.

The authors wrote that much of the marine delay appears tied to worries about how a listing would affect fisheries, though they could not measure that influence directly.

The 2017 policy requires the government to publish a reason whenever it misses a timeline.

The team checked the 2023 report against the 173 species then in limbo and found entries for only 110 of them. Of the 63 missing, 57 were fish.

Among the entries that did exist, nearly half cited a need for more information and a fifth gave no specific reason.

Four in ten said consultations were already finished, without explaining why the file had then stalled.

“Many of the explanations given are pretty unsatisfactory, often amounting to nothing more than ‘the analysis of information is underway,'” Thompson said.

The Saskatchewan-Nelson River population of lake sturgeon was assessed as endangered in 2017 after a 90% decline since the 1960s.

As of January 2025, it had received no decision, and the panel named dams as a key threat. In the years since, a new hydroelectric station began operating on the Nelson River.

Limber pine, a tree assessed as endangered in 2014, completed consultations in 2021 but was still awaiting a decision in January 2025.

Several caribou herds completed consultations years ago and remained unlisted, leaving them without the Act’s requirement to identify critical habitat.

The study can’t say how many of these species declined because of the wait, only that none of them had the law’s protections while they waited.

The authors propose binding deadlines for every stage, better-resourced consultations, and a specific public reason each time a file stalls, so the listing process can be checked from outside.

The proposed changes would make the process more transparent and give species awaiting decisions a clearer path toward protection.

The full study was published in the journal FACETS.
