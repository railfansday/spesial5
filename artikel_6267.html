Wildfire smoke over Borneo hints at danger beneath the forest

Tanggal: 2026-11-15

A satellite view of Borneo’s wildfires reveals how prolonged drought can put forests and carbon-rich peatlands at risk.

Today’s Image of the Day captures smoke rising from multiple wildfires across Indonesian Borneo, revealing a landscape caught in a severe fire season.

Seen from space, the plumes show how smoke can spread far beyond the fires themselves.

The Copernicus Sentinel-3 mission recorded the scene on October 14, 2026.

Released by the European Space Agency, the image shows the consequences of prolonged dry conditions that have allowed fires to spread.

ESA links the severity of this year’s fires to El Niño, the climate pattern that can reduce rainfall across Indonesia.

The smoke is the most visible part of the story.

Beneath it, damaged forests and vulnerable soils connect the immediate emergency to a longer struggle over how much carbon these ecosystems can keep out of the atmosphere.

A change in the tropical Pacific can alter the weather thousands of miles away.

During El Niño, unusually warm water in the central and eastern equatorial Pacific develops alongside changes in winds and rainfall.

The trade winds that normally push warm surface water westward weaken, and tropical rainfall shifts eastward.

For Indonesia, that shift can mean fewer clouds, less rain, and drought.

A region associated with tropical downpours can enter an extended period in which vegetation and soil steadily lose moisture.

Drought does not supply the spark for every fire. It changes what happens after a fire starts.

Land-clearing practices, forest degradation, and peatland drainage also influence Indonesia’s fire risk.

When those pressures coincide with unusually dry weather, fires have more opportunities to escape and spread.

A recent assessment of the 2026 season identified both El Niño and human-caused warming as contributors to dangerous fire weather.

In parts of Borneo, the fuel extends beneath the forest floor.

Peat forms where waterlogged conditions slow the breakdown of dead vegetation.

Over time, that plant material accumulates, creating a soil rich in carbon. Keeping it wet helps preserve that store.

Drainage changes the balance. As water levels fall, peat becomes more exposed to air and more vulnerable to burning.

Drought can deepen the problem, allowing fires to move from surface vegetation into the ground.

Once established, these underground fires can smolder persistently and prove difficult to extinguish.

NASA’s reporting on Indonesia’s 2026 season described fires burning both at the surface and below it, with dry conditions allowing flames to reach peat deposits that would normally be too wet to burn.

That background helps explain why a smoky satellite scene can represent more than the loss of standing trees.

In peatland areas, a fire may also consume part of the carbon store beneath them.

Forests absorb carbon dioxide as they grow, holding carbon in trunks, branches, roots, and other plant material.

Fire releases some of that carbon back into the atmosphere while damaging the vegetation that would otherwise continue absorbing it.

Peat adds another layer to the loss. Its carbon can remain underground for centuries or longer before drainage and burning release it.

Research in Central Kalimantan, a province in Indonesian Borneo, has documented the release of centuries- to millennium-old carbon following peatland drainage.

Subsequent fires caused further losses, showing how disturbance can dismantle a store that took far longer to build.

The consequences therefore continue beyond the disappearance of the smoke.

Protecting these landscapes means preserving both living forests and the conditions that keep their underlying carbon secure.

A satellite image brings scattered fires into one view, helping reveal an event’s wider reach.

Sentinel-3 also gathers measurements that support more detailed monitoring. Its Sea and Land Surface Temperature Radiometer includes channels designed to detect fires.

Those observations help scientists assess burned vegetation, estimate associated carbon emissions, and examine recovery.

Another instrument tracks vegetation conditions through measurements related to leaves and chlorophyll.

Combined with weather forecasts, satellite observations can help assess forest fire risk and guide prevention efforts.

Today’s image captures Borneo’s fires on October 14, but their effects will outlast the smoke, leaving damaged forests, altered soils, and less carbon stored underground.
