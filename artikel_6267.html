Live Science spoke with astrophysicist Erin Macdonald about her role as a science adviser for 'Star Trek' and other Hollywood movies.

From gravitational waves to time travel, science has always been at the heart of "Star Trek". While the franchise has never shied away from imagining the impossible, much of its appeal comes from making strange situations seem believable.

That's where the role of a science adviser comes in. It's not necessarily about making sure everything is scientifically accurate, but understanding the science well enough to know where creative liberties can be taken and how to make them come alive on screen.

Erin Macdonald knows this balance all-too-well. After studying general relativity and gravitational waves for her doctorate and spending years working at Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory (LIGO), Macdonald shifted gears and became a science advisor for six "Star Trek" series, including the "Discovery" franchise, as well as a variety of other television and film projects. Live Science spoke with Macdonald about how she brings the world of "Star Trek" to life, and her job's biggest pitfalls.

Erin Macdonald is an award-winning writer, science communicator, producer, and science advisor. She is most well-known for her advising work on 'Star Trek' and has played her own character across multiple iterations of the franchise. As a science communicator she has also appeared on NPR, BBC, CBS, and StarTalk Live.

KHC: How did you end up getting into the field of science advising?

EM: After I got my Ph.D. and did a little bit of research, I realized academia and that world just weren't really for me. So I went into engineering, but I really missed teaching and being able to engage with students. I was trying to find more opportunities to do public science communication. I'm a big sci-fi nerd, so I started going to sci-fi conventions and giving solo panels and workshops that were basically, "Here's the physics behind faster-than-light travel," and "Here's all the physics in 'Star Trek'."

This article is part of a series called "Science Behind the Camera" which focuses on the role of science advisors. From helping to create engaging stories to set design and script rewrites, these experts shape some of the most compelling narratives on screen.

Through that particular talk and just life happening, I met a lot of people on the convention circuit. I decided I wanted to go to L.A. I learned that science advising was a job, so I moved out there, still working as an engineer.

CBS and "Star Trek" got wind of my talks, and they invited me to give presentations at their official licensed "Star Trek" events. That's how I got on their radar. A couple of years later, we were in a world where there were a lot of 'Star Trek' shows being produced, and they were looking for a science adviser for "Star Trek: Discovery". [Editor's Note: "Star Trek: Discovery" ran for five seasons (65 episodes) from Sept. 2017, to May 2024 on CBS] That went well, and they brought me on as an in-house science adviser.

KHC: You've now advised on how many shows?

EM: Well, six "Star Trek" shows, but I'm starting to lose track of the films and other shows I've been called into for either one-off things or bigger jobs. It's been a great experience.

I think a lot of being successful in L.A. and Hollywood is that every job is an audition for your next job. You work on one show, everyone goes their separate ways and works on new shows, and if they know someone they might want to bring in, they'll give you a call. I've been very lucky in that regard.

KHC: What does the day-to-day look like for a science adviser?

EM: All the jobs I've done are different. When I get pulled in to talk to someone about a new project, it could be almost anything.

The biggest role, and the one I enjoy the most, is being brought in when a show is just getting off the ground and the writers' room is being established. They'll bring me in to sit in that writers' room and help contribute to the ideas and stories as they're being developed. If questions come up in real time, I can help answer them. That's the most ideal scenario for being a science adviser because you can get ahead of any issues as plots are starting to be developed.

A lot of the time, when you're doing something wild like that, I'll just say, "Great, don't explain it. Do your thing. Try not to science it."

But it can also look like being handed production scripts. Basically, they've gone through the whole process, they're nearing the point where they're going to start filming, and they're like, "Hey, can you check this for us?"

That can be a blessing and a curse because it's a little less work, but then you can't course-correct as much if there are big plot problems. You have to find a way to work with the story and try to get the science as accurate as possible. In that scenario, it can end up being, "Don't explain it. Take these lines out. You're getting yourself in trouble. Leave everything else."

The last kind of job, and one I've had a lot of colleagues do, is set design. They'll be brought in when they're building sets or doing post-production and want to make sure what's on screen looks science-y.

The best example I can give is someone writing all the equations on "The Big Bang Theory". All those whiteboards you see? That's a scientist coming in to write all of that.

Science advisors may help with set design, such as writing the equations on the whiteboards in CBS's popular show "The Big Bang Theory."

I worked on a show called "Good American Family" on Hulu. It's not a science show at all, but they had a kid who was really interested in physics. I went in and wrote all the equations and talked to the actor about how to say the things he was going to be saying.

The process can vary greatly. Even within "Star Trek", my relationship has been different from show to show. That's an exciting part of it, but I think people can see that it's a very varied position.

KHC: Out of all your 7 years of experience in science advising, is there a particular scene, moment, or script you're especially proud of?

EM: There are certainly ones that are really close to my heart. I'll give a couple of examples.

One was the season two finale of "Star Trek: Lower Decks." I wasn't brought in until season two, At the end, the showrunner was basically like, "Hey, I have this vision, but we need to build the 'Star Trek' science around it."

He just had a scene in his mind and wanted to build the story. We came up with this great story about an asteroid belt with a lot of charged particles that disrupted the [protective] shields. There was a lot of good technobabble in there, too. It was really fun. Watching that episode, it just feels like a really authentic "Star Trek" episode because of all the science in it. I'm really proud that I was able to contribute to that.

If you spend your time just criticizing the science in films and TV, this is not the job for you.

Another one that stands out to me is the musical episode of "Star Trek: Strange New Worlds". That was one of the first times the show was taking one of these big swings. The show is kind of known for doing all these big swings now, but that was one of the first. A lot of the time, when you're doing something wild like that, I'll just say, "Great, don't explain it. Do your thing. Try not to science it."

But they pushed back on me and said, "No, no, no. We need a science explanation."

We really worked together and came up with a detailed science explanation. What I'm really proud of is that they created that ready room scene [Editor's note: a ready room scene is a dramatic sequence set usually in a command office before a major battle or mission] where Spock explains everything that's happening. It's probably one of the longest ready room scenes we've had in "Star Trek". It's like, "And then this, and then this, and this is why, and this is why, and this is why, and this is what we need to do."

It's goofy, but there was a lot of science put into that episode, which was a really fun process.

KHC: I would be remiss if I didn't bring up the creation of Dr. Erin. How did that character come about, and how have you felt about the impact of the character?

EM: It's really funny. The character of Dr. Erin has now shown up in multiple series, video games, and comic books, which delights me.

Macdonald's influence as a science adviser resulted in the creation of the character Dr. Erin, based on Macdonald herself.

The origin of it started on "Star Trek: Prodigy". It was a "Star Trek" show aimed at children. They wanted me to come in not just to help make sure the science was right, but also to do more traditional science communication and help create a character who was a kid who wanted to be a scientist and talk about what that process is like. That's the character of Rok-Tahk, and she's adorable.

As they were reaching the end of season one, the kids were going to Starfleet Academy, and the showrunners were like, "She has to meet Dr. Erin. She is who she is because of Erin." That was just one of the greatest honors. It was a quick, one-line mention, not even named in the episode.

KHC: Is there anything you wish you could change from your perspective?

EM: Yeah, there are a few that haunt me a little bit.

There are some shows where I was really involved, we meshed well together, and I helped with the story. There are very few things I would change. But then there are other shows where I'm just given the script at the 11th hour. Plot-wise, would I have suggested something different? Yes. But it was too late to course-correct.

One really good example was in "Star Trek: Discovery". They had an episode where they were trying to ride gravitational waves out of a nebula, as one does. They had that ready room explainer moment, and they were showing the gravitational waves as literal sine waves, like the ocean waves you think of. I was working with the visual effects team, and I pushed back. I said, "Really, these are compression waves." You could still make an argument that they were going to ride them out, but it's more like how sound waves move. They're compression waves.

To their credit, they animated that and put it in the scene. But as people were watching it, they felt that the way we were talking about it versus what we were seeing just wasn't working. It was pulling people out of the scene, so they had to revert back.

It was funny because when that aired, I had three or four former gravitational wave colleagues who were like, "Erin, you had one job."

Gravitational waves are compression waves, like sound waves, as opposed to sine waves.

Another quick example was an episode in season one of "Strange New Worlds" where Spock says, "We're being pulled into the black hole."

That's one of those moments where it's technically not correct. You fall into black holes. It's a very public misconception that I try to fight back against a lot. Black holes aren't hoovering things up. It's just deep gravity. But in the context of the scene, Spock is being very condescending and talking down to La'an, so he's simplifying the language for the public. I can sort of get away with that.

KHC: Is there anything else you think our readers should know about science advising?

EM: I meet a lot of people who, delicately or not, say, "I could do your job. I'd be a great science adviser." I think what's made me successful in science advising is that I take a very "yes, and" attitude.

I'm a fan first and foremost. If you spend your time just criticizing the science in films and TV, this is not the job for you. You need to work with the stories you're given, and you need to understand the mission.

There's a quick anecdote. I worked on another film called "Space Cadet," which is a stoner comedy on Amazon, and they were having a really hard time finding a science adviser because everyone was trashing the script. It's ridiculous, goofy, and fun. They were like, "Look, we just want someone who makes sure the NASA people know what NASA people would say. That's it."

The job isn't for everyone. Storytelling comes first.
