Why the incoming US Congress should ban the development of self-improving AI

Alex Bores is a US politician in the New York State Assembly in New York City, New York, USA, and a co-founder of AI-safety advocacy group Who Decides.

The recent US midterm elections were a democratic test of the country’s rapid expansion of artificial-intelligence infrastructure. Data centres emerged as a key issue in several election campaigns, and prominent Democrats have outlined policies to strengthen federal regulations on AI once they take office in January.

I am a Democratic politician trained in computer science who stood in a June congressional primary election that attracted millions of dollars from groups backed by the technology industry (I narrowly lost my race). I took one central message from the midterm election results: US voters are unhappy with the industry-led roll-out of AI and want greater control over how the technology is developed and deployed.

Why AI companies can’t be trusted to self-regulate

Why AI companies can’t be trusted to self-regulate

The newly elected US Congress must respond to the popular mood. An immediate priority should be to ban AI systems that can improve themselves without human authorization or oversight. This process, known as recursive self-improvement (RSI), is thought to be close to realization at some frontier AI laboratories. Voters fear that granting such autonomy to the technology would risk surrendering human control over AI altogether.

Most of the major AI companies are already using the technology to automate parts of AI development. Anthropic reported in August that its Claude model assists with more than 90% of the firm’s AI research and development and leads 26% of that work, completing specific tasks under human supervision. OpenAI, meanwhile, notes that its researchers now use more than three days of AI-agent work for every day of human work. And the firm is open about its eventual goal of full automation, including areas in which AI systems could design and build increasingly powerful successors with little or no human oversight.

In my view, this is a dangerous path, particularly because there is enough evidence on what could go wrong when humans lose control of AI agents. In July, some 700 OpenAI agents unexpectedly coordinated to hack into the servers of Hugging Face, another AI firm. In a separate incident in October, AI agents found ways to manipulate the records of their actions, making their behaviour harder for humans to monitor. If AI systems increasingly participate in building their successors, such failures could intensify. (OpenAI has not responded to requests for comment.)

We need a new ethics for a world of AI agents

We need a new ethics for a world of AI agents

The companies are racing each other to market dominance, and against that economic pull, no voluntary norm will stand. Hence, Congress must step in. A ban on fully autonomous RSI would build on a rare area of cross-partisan agreement: individuals from independent senator Bernie Sanders to Republican political strategist Steve Bannon think that humans must remain in control of AI.

To be effective, such a ban must be specific. A person merely clicking ‘approve’ on thousands of AI-proposed experiments each day is not exercising meaningful control. The law should instead prevent AI systems from independently taking consequential steps in AI development, such as launching a training run for a new frontier model or deploying it internally for AI research. One way to do this would be to require a senior executive to certify that qualified human reviewers have examined the proposed changes and relevant safety evaluations before any development actions proceed. Executives should be personally liable for failing to perform oversight duties.
