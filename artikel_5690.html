RMI’s Hydrogen Research Is Getting Better. Its Investment Advice Still Falls Short.

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

RMI has become much more realistic about hydrogen demand and competing technologies. That makes the gaps between its findings and its recommendations harder to excuse. Its latest work acknowledges that battery trucks generally have better economics than hydrogen fuel-cell trucks, that Europe needs far less hydrogen in 2030 than it originally targeted, and that low-emissions ammonia still costs substantially more than conventional ammonia. Several recommendations still seek public money to develop the very hydrogen markets the research has found difficult to justify.

I have been challenging this tension since January 2024. RMI matters because governments, investors and industrial companies use its research when deciding where to spend money. Its hydrogen program has made genuine analytical corrections. It has not made all of the accompanying strategic decisions.

Consider RMI’s February 2026 report on hydrogen trucking in India. The baseline comparisons find that fuel-cell trucks do not reach battery-electric truck ownership-cost parity through 2040, including the long-haul use case. The report also explores a much more favourable hydrogen price in an alternative sensitivity, which brings fuel-cell trucks closer to parity. Those are different findings, and the baseline matters to investment decisions today.

The report nevertheless recommends hydrogen vehicle subsidies, support for refuelling stations, production incentives and tax relief. It treats a parallel hydrogen-truck market as something to build. The case for spending public money on that market should start with evidence that a particular freight service cannot be served as well or better by batteries. The reported cost advantage of batteries does not disappear when the policy section begins.

The problem reaches beyond India. RMI’s 2025 US hydrogen report says battery trucks have greater market readiness and lower total cost of ownership, especially in the near term, yet calls for hydrogen distribution and refuelling infrastructure along freight corridors. The independent NACFE Run on Less ownership-cost work, developed within RMI’s freight research ecosystem, projects batteries becoming the lowest-cost option across its modelled duty cycles by 2035 without incentives. Hydrogen remains the most expensive option across nearly all of them. These findings support battery infrastructure as the default and a high evidentiary bar for hydrogen exceptions.

RMI already knows how to make appropriate distinctions elsewhere. Its 2026 steel research separates hydrogen used to reduce iron ore from hydrogen burned merely to supply process heat. In the illustrative plant, electric heat requires about 850 GWh a year, compared with 1,300 GWh of electricity to produce enough additional hydrogen for heating. That is the kind of direct service comparison that ought to govern recommendations throughout the organization.

Its new Green Ammonia Buyers Alliance also has a coherent industrial purpose. Fertiliser production already requires ammonia, commonly made with fossil-derived hydrogen. Purchasing lower-emissions ammonia to replace that supply addresses an existing industrial input. It does not require inventing a new energy market for ammonia combustion.

Europe shows how improved diagnosis can coexist with excessive policy ambition. RMI’s March 2025 recalibration reduced expected mandate-driven renewable hydrogen demand in 2030 to 2.2–2.8 million tons, far below the EU’s original 20-million-ton supply-and-import ambition. Its analysis identifies €13.9 billion in public support under existing mandates, plus €31.7 billion for an accelerated scenario. The combined €45.6 billion deserves a comparison with the other ways industry could obtain the same low-carbon products and services, including direct electrification and imported green materials.

That comparison changes the question for ammonia, steel and refining. A European fertiliser or chemical manufacturer needs ammonia, not necessarily hydrogen manufactured inside Europe. A steelmaker needs iron meeting its grade and emissions requirements. A mill needs process heat at specified temperatures. Governments can pay a premium to preserve domestic capacity for security or resilience, but the benefit must be valued and the public cost made explicit.

My November 2026 European hydrogen assessment applies that approach. In its central 2050 scenario, ammonia produced in Europe with on-site green hydrogen costs approximately €1,222 per ton. Qualifying imported green ammonia costs €745–755 per ton under the model’s assumptions. These are modelled values, not supplier bids. They demonstrate why an analysis that starts with hydrogen production targets can produce the wrong infrastructure plan.

RMI’s 2026 China green ammonia and methanol report acknowledges that green production costs remain two to three times conventional costs and that demand development lags supply ambitions. The report nevertheless discusses expanding ammonia and methanol as energy carriers, including ammonia for electricity. The relevant comparison for electricity is renewable generation with storage and other flexibility, not fossil-derived ammonia alone. RMI has not demonstrated that adding an ammonia combustion pathway wins that comparison.

This is a strategy problem as much as a modelling problem. Using Richard Rumelt’s framework, RMI’s diagnosis has improved, its guiding policy remains too committed to expanding hydrogen markets, and some coherent actions are missing. An economically unfavourable comparison should be able to end an infrastructure recommendation. Instead, it too often generates proposals for subsidies that would make the hydrogen route easier to sell.

RMI has the expertise to correct this. Its hydrogen recommendations should carry explicit alternative-technology comparisons, investment thresholds and exit criteria. Cross-program review should include the researchers measuring battery costs, industrial electrification, freight operations and grid flexibility. Where hydrogen is an essential chemical input or demonstrably the best means of delivering a service, support it. Where competing technologies win on cost and deployment readiness, stop trying to create demand through policy.
