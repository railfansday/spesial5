Across the U.S., homegrown experiments aim to save kids from their phones

MILWAUKEE — Kat Zilka sat on the floor of her living room, beside a glowing Christmas tree and a pile of essays, and felt like weeping. It was late in 2022, and she had asked her 12th grade digital journalism students to reflect on their relationships to devices.

“The things they were writing were deeply disturbing,” she said.

Teenagers quietly enduring online abuse, using their phones all night without their parents’ knowledge, or trapped in a cycle of deleting and redownloading social media. Their words made real what Zilka had been observing for years — how the light in students’ eyes was dimmer, especially since Covid — and revealed the astonishing magnitude of the problem.

“It was like they were just waiting for someone to ask,” she said.

That semester would change the trajectory of Zilka’s career, and place her at the forefront of an emerging national conversation about kids’ degrading attention, and its effect on their health. At that time, there were no surgeon general advisories on the matter, nor were there dozens of state legislatures clamoring to crack down on phones. It was people like Zilka, on the ground, trying to beat back an encroaching mental health crisis with minimal guidance.

Over winter break, she sketched out an elective course called “Digital Detox.” Students loved the class, she said, in part because Digital Detox was unlike anything else they were doing in school. That began with the first 10 minutes of class, when students would just sit with their thoughts. “Halfway through the semester, they were, like, hankering for it,” Zilka said.

Despite the mostly positive feedback, she felt the course was bound by an unresolved tension: It was adding to the academic pressure, while trying to help students’ mental health.

The students didn’t want more coursework. They wanted a support group.

Zilka thought something spiritually connected to Alcoholics Anonymous, or even the church youth groups of her childhood, could fit the bill. “The opposite of addiction is connection,” she said. Elsewhere in the country, other grassroots experiments began to take shape — all molded by youth, and fueled by a sense of urgency that was absent from mainstream politics and the slow crawl of science.

“Young people are developing today. They don’t really have time for us to wait 10 years to find out how to help them,” Rachel Hanebutt, a Georgetown University researcher studying teens’ relationship to tech, said.

As regulators and researchers, already years behind on the problem, struggle to respond to ever-evolving platforms, homegrown efforts have a distinct advantage, she said: They are nimble.

Some projects teach young people to set hard boundaries with social media, and use rich experiences IRL to draw them away from digital isolation. Others are blatantly political, aiming to turn teenagers’ rebellious streaks on Big Tech itself.

Their collective mission is to give generations of American kids raised with an iPad and schooled with a Chromebook an escape hatch — out of the disorienting blue haze and into the rushing, cool air of the real world.

Zilka was so convinced by what she saw in Digital Detox that she formed a nonprofit, called dis//connect, and quit her teaching job. The first meeting took place in May 2024.

She also raised a small pot of money, around $1,200. About a year ago, Zilka brought in clinical psychologist Thekla Ross, a leader in the emerging field of “digital harm reduction,” to measure how well the program is working and find ways to expand the model.

Now, on most Monday and Thursday nights, Zilka and Ross gather with about 8 high schoolers in their respective meetings. The hourlong sessions start by logging average daily screen time by hand and slipping silenced phones into a filing box. Then they celebrate the “slow media” they’re consuming, and sit quietly for 10 minutes.

Spread across the tables are a variety of fidget toys, coloring books, and word searches so the teens have something to do with their hands. They talk about what’s on their minds: Friend breakups, frustrating teachers, and toning themselves down in public because someone might be recording (a habit Zilka and Ross dub, in a forthcoming book, “flatlining”).

Caroline, an ambitious rower with a darting gaze and mouthful of braces, is nervous about not making the team for an upcoming tournament in Boston. By Thursday night, she’d made the boat, and was troubled by other ideas.

Tonight’s reading, from Anna Lembke’s “Dopamine Nation,” is about how the author became obsessed with romance novels — a parallel to how social media hijacks the brain’s reward system. Like Lembke, Caroline has recently struggled to read anything but love stories.

“I didn’t even think about it until now,” she said, noting how short she is of her goal of 150 books for the year.

A copy of “To Kill a Mockingbird” sat on her shelf for weeks, and went back to the library untouched, as she devoured romance stories instead. The harder something is for the brain, the more delayed the reward, Zilka acknowledged.

“It can be really hard in our world to convince ourselves to do the delayed gratification thing,” she says.

It all reminds Caroline of a recent discussion in her literature class, about how books have changed as fewer people read for pleasure. She and Tala, a curly-haired friend who’s new to the group, commiserate over the lack of fellow bookworms at school.

Their male counterparts are even less likely to read for fun — or, it seems, come to groups like these. Boys at school are gripped instead by the mind-altering influence of Clavicular and online gambling, the girls say.

(Earlier in the night, a polite overachiever named Ethan said attending dis//connect eased his anxiety about feeling left out on apps like Snapchat. Now less self-conscious, he goes out of his way to wish classmates “happy birthday” to their face. Recently, a girl responded by saying he could’ve texted her instead.)

Boys and girls tend to use the internet about the same amount — four in 10 U.S. teens say they are online almost constantly, according to a 2025 Pew survey of 13-to-17-year-olds. However, boys face different pressures online, such as the meteoric rise of sports betting apps, which experts say build on the alluring design elements of other platforms.

“Convince me that Kalshi, predictive markets, FanDuel, and DraftKings are not social media companies,” said Hanebutt, the Georgetown researcher.

Betting apps could further obscure the nature of digital addiction, who is most vulnerable to harm, and why.

As it is, we don’t really know what phones are doing to us.

Type the words “phone addiction” into a search bar on social media, and you will find a long and seamless scroll of hundreds of videos on the topic.

It might be a surprise, then, to read credible researchers’ disagreements about whether the word “addiction” is the right term for what’s happening. Some, like the prominent Digital Mental Health Group at the University of Cambridge, question whether social media shares the same underlying neurocognitive mechanisms as addictive substances.

But other researchers say the “infinite scroll” and algorithmic feeds are addictive by design, and that heavy users can experience addiction-like symptoms: craving, withdrawal, quitting attempts, and relapse.

“It’s akin to each time you smoke a cigarette, the cigarette itself increasing in nicotine content to you in particular,” said Vikram Bhargava, an assistant professor at the George Washington University business school who studies the ethics of technological innovations.

(Meta, as part of its historic $17.1 billion settlement with dozens of states, agreed to interrupt endless scrolling and add time limits to teen Instagram accounts. The company has denied that its products are addictive.)

The questions don’t stop there. Over a decade after the birth of Instagram, researchers aren’t even sure about whether social media causes mental distress. It might just be feeding into vulnerable people’s underlying problems. Or, it could be doing both. Two people, given the same platform for 30 minutes, can have vastly different experiences.

“It might not be the dose of use — how many hours an adolescent uses social media — but actually the nature of that use,” Kelila Kahane, a psychiatrist and fellow in child and adolescent psychiatry at NewYork-Presbyterian, said.

This is the technological maze researchers find themselves in as they confront the fact that the kids are not alright. Kostadin Kushlev, leader of the Happy Tech Lab at Georgetown University, is one of the people grappling with these unknowns.

His lab received a grant from the National Institute of Mental Health to study how social media directly affects teens’ mental health. The study involves giving a group of 10-to-14-year-olds their first phone. For three months, half the group has as much access to social media as their parents allow, while the other half has none. All of the kids complete brief daily surveys, along with three longer surveys, over the course of six months.

The phones also track their movement, sleep, and online activity, such as most-used apps and what times of day participants are opening them.

“That sort of addresses a second critical issue in the literature: A lot of the evidence is based on these very basic, rudimentary, almost laughable measures of screen time,” Kushlev said. One common metric asks: On a spectrum of “not at all” to “several times per day,” how often did you use your phone in the past week?

Tech companies are still the keepers of the richest datasets, which could actually help researchers tease out differences between users. But in the U.S., the companies are not required to share that information with outside scientists. Most of what we know about the depths of data extraction has come from Silicon Valley whistleblowers, he said.

“It’s so stark when you think about it,” Kushlev said. Researchers spend years designing studies and applying for grants, only to spend bucketloads of money for data that’s less useful than what tech companies already have.

That lackluster data is then driving policy.

“All we have is these negative correlations, and it’s sort of like, ‘Oh, I guess it’s bad, so let’s ban it all,’” he said.

Dozens of states have enacted phone restrictions in recent years, and the Trump administration has encouraged others to pursue “bell-to-bell bans.” Congress too has floated screen time-related bills, including a recent bipartisan proposal that would limit social media use for those under 16.

Evidence is mixed on how well bans work. In Australia, where in 2024 lawmakers banned kids under 16 from making accounts on certain social media platforms, initial data suggest little changed about teens’ reported use.

In some cases, in-school restrictions have been shown to reduce phone usage in class, or marginally improve academic performance in certain subjects. But they can also lead to more disciplinary action or even declines in school performance at the start of the policies. Data are still unclear on whether they provide any long-term mental health benefits.

While it’s too soon to tell what really works, a recent review by the Cambridge group found promising evidence for things that add friction — think: a pause or prompt before opening a certain app — and programs that teach practical digital skills. Teacher- or peer-led sessions focused on attention and self-control also demonstrated some short-term benefit.

“The underlying mechanisms of change may be more important than the intervention level itself,” they wrote.

People leading attention projects intuit something else: Simply taking screens away won’t solve the nation’s problems. Teens need something new to be excited about.

Consider a bright September day on Brooklyn’s picturesque Washington Street, where tourists are weaving around each other, necks craned, snapping pictures of the Manhattan Bridge, its enormous crown poking between historic buildings.

Here, all of a sudden, are a half-dozen teenagers bisecting the cobblestone street, arms lifted, hands assuming the claw shape of a smartphone holder, but grasping only air.

For five minutes, the small band of “attention scholars” does this: Pretends to take pictures of the bridge while surrounded by people trying to take actual pictures of the bridge. After a few minutes, their presence becomes a human barricade, then an attraction — cameras click at them, people stare and giggle. Others frown.

One woman, clad in a sunhat, tries to fill the claw-shaped void with her own cellphone, wordlessly requesting that one of the teens take a picture of her against the backdrop.

Then Jahony Germosen, who has been standing nearby with a timer, rings a bell and the teens file back into the lobby of their building, where they erupt into giddy laughter.

That was a “public intervention” done by “attention activists” training at the Strother School of Radical Attention.

SORA, as insiders call it, is perhaps the best-known group in the U.S. devoted to this kind of work. Started by academics and artists in 2023, it operates as a nexus for a movement that seeks to liberate people’s minds from Big Tech.

“You can think of this as a health problem. You can think of this as a productivity problem. We’re thinking of it as a systemic problem,” Peter Schmidt, SORA’s program director, told STAT. “You need collective action.”

Its weeklong summer camp, now in its third year, introduces young people to the idea of attention as political, and something worth defending. “Our attention is the precondition for our freedom,” Schmidt said.

Some of the campers have never been on social media. They feel that gives them a clearer vision of their peers’ dependence. (STAT agreed to identify participants only by age.)

One participant, a 14-year-old science fiction lover, wasn’t allowed to have a cellphone until seventh grade — later than most American kids — and felt routinely excluded from conversations because of it. SORA camp has been refreshing, she said. “It helps that other people are noticing the same thing.”

The 17-year-old sitting beside her, wearing thick eyeliner and armfuls of beaded bracelets, recently stumbled upon the New York City Luddites, an anti-smartphone group that hosts events around the city.

She found the idea of “human fracking” — SORA’s teaching that tech companies are forcibly breaking, capturing, and selling human attention — particularly compelling, since she already disliked the fossil fuel industry.

The 17-year-old started hating how she felt when she’d mindlessly scroll TikTok and Pinterest, so she deleted the apps. In her free time, she’s learning how to hand-code a website. And she tries to talk to her parents instead of scrolling before school. Sometimes, they are the problem.

Another 14-year-old, sporty and shy in a black hoodie, was forced by his dad to come to the camp, but has found some of the discussions thought-provoking. That may be as far as it goes.

When he called a friend to share what he’d been up to at the end of August — turning off his phone, staring at a sculpture with childlike wonder, talking about extractive profit models — his friend scoffed, “What the hell is that?”

There was more to it: Together, the teens identified the New York City subway as a site of human fracking, because so many people are scrolling during their commute. The students identified “questions to live with” — What would my life be like as a fish? How long would it take to swim to Manhattan from here? — instead of consulting artificial intelligence chatbots. They’ve also delved into “Attensity!” the movement’s manifesto, published this year.

Camper Consuelo “Leo” Burnett, 17, is well acquainted with this world because her father, the Princeton history professor D. Graham Burnett, is one of SORA’s founders. Nevertheless, she has grown personally invested in the subject of attention, and acts as an idea translator for younger students. (STAT obtained permission to use Burnett’s full name.)

During the Covid pandemic, Leo Burnett lived in an extended lockdown, barely leaving home for over a year as her family tried to protect two immunocompromised members. She got her first cellphone around the same time. “The internet was my one way of feeling connected with the world,” she said, her blue eyes wide.

But she was also prone to anxiety and panic attacks throughout childhood, and in her search for queer community, joined online groups where mental illness was seen as a “badge of honor,” Burnett said. Her mental health cratered, and she developed an eating disorder.

This complicated history with social media started to make more sense once Burnett went to SORA camp. Her reliance on TikTok for bursts of dopamine no longer felt like a personal failing.

“It’s a multibillion dollar industry trying to control young people’s attention,” she said.

There is a view of the attention crisis that deems it a runaway train.

But the other take — the one adopted by many who work with youth, and who must hold onto some optimism to get through the day — is that we have more control than we think.

“Attention spans have not, in some deep, permanent way, been hampered,” Michael Vazquez, associate director of UNC Chapel Hill’s Parr Center for Ethics, said.

Vazquez and his colleagues have undertaken one of the largest academic efforts so far to test that idea. Last year, they received an almost $800,000 grant to run a three-year experiment, “Educating for the Virtues of Attention,” or EVA.

Attention is a virtue because it’s “upstream of a lot of other things we care about,” such as learning, flourishing, and building relationships, Vazquez said.

As part of EVA, every freshman at Chapel Hill is exposed to curriculum about the demands on our attention, and how those pressures shape our well-being. Some students are finding courses gone totally analog: Instead of submitting assignments on a web portal, they are told to write them in a physical journal. Or, a professor might add extra hoops to jump through before a student can use technology in class.

The idea is to give all students a taste of EVA, “and then we have deep dive opportunities for those who want to continue,” he said. That could look like an oral reading of ancient poetry at a local bookshop, or it could be a phone-free picnic in the university’s arboretum.

This fall, a cohort of over two dozen undergraduates committed to restricting their social media use to a certain number of hours per week as part of a new program called “Carolina Unplugged.” They gather weekly for dinner to reflect on their progress and plan cultural events on campus.

Each student received a Brick, a physical device that disables select apps once a person taps their phone to it. Apps remain blocked until the phone is tapped again. The freebie was what initially drew in Natalie Easley, a 20-year-old junior from Bethesda, Md. She had tried all of the built-in tools for controlling screen time, but nothing worked.

Since middle school, Easley had deleted apps and then caved, redownloading them. How many times had she gone through that cycle? “This is not even exaggerating: Probably like 50-plus,” she said.

But within days of leaving home with her phone Brick’d — and not being able to undo it until the evening — she realized how much better she felt. “I didn’t even realize how much of a compulsion it was, just to pick up my phone,” said Easley, a math and philosophy double major. Then she started to notice how many people walked around with their heads down, scrolling between classes. Her evenings spent on X were no longer satisfying.

“Twitter is fake. My phone is fake. Why is something that is not tangible ruining my day or making me pissed off?” she said.

She deleted X and Instagram, and slashed her average daily screen time by one-third, down to four hours. Easley has found that, free from social media, she can use her phone for discrete tasks — to take photos, learn about things she’s interested in, or text her friends.

She also has more time to let her mind wander. On a recent day, it summoned Aristotle’s assertion: We are what we do.

If one-third of her waking hours were spent online, Easley realized, “then maybe I am, like, my phone.”

“When you’re not distracted, you’re actually able to think about it.”
