Johnny Depp on Honoring Hunter S. Thompson’s Final Wish With a “Death Cannon” That Stood “Taller Than the Statue of Liberty”

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

He couldn’t remember if it was day or night when he found it, in the place where “one never quite knew or cared for time,” Johnny Depp wrote. He remembered the year—1997—and that he was with Hunter S. Thompson in his basement War Room at Owl Farm. It was just across the hall from Depp’s bedroom, with that old keg of dynamite serving as his bedside table.

Rummaging through beaten-up boxes that comprised Thompson’s archive, Depp unearthed something that would turn out to be equally explosive. “A battered typescript,” he called it. Abandoned among the detritus of Thompson’s renegade life, it was “bound together by rubber bands.” On its cover, “in Hunter’s own hand,” Depp noted, were the words THE RUM DIARY.

“He wrote the book in his early twenties,” Depp wrote. “And then stuffed it away.”

Side by side on the floor of the War Room, Depp and Thompson began “reading it to one another.” And when they were done, Depp had made a decision: “The Rum Diary was simply too good to remain lost.”

Thompson wasn’t able to find a publisher for The Rum Diary when it was written, but now he was, well, Hunter S. Thompson. With Depp as his advocate, The Rum Diary was published in 1998. “Almost immediately, the subject of a film was broached,” Depp wrote in a foreword to the screenplay, recalling Thompson saying, “‘Hot damn . . . yessir, let’s you and I produce this caper together, Colonel! We’ll have Hollywood groveling at our feet! They will crawl over a road of bones to get to us! Yessir!’”

A movie didn’t happen “as easily as we had hoped,” Depp continued in his foreword. “Fiery hoops of all dimensions, covert bear traps, woefully obstinate billfolds” arose in their path, “which neither Hunter or I were remotely experienced in battling . . . But we weren’t giving up.”

Hunter S. Thompson at his ranch in Colorado, 1990.

As the years wore on, Thompson grew increasingly inflamed over the development hell of Hollywood, writing producer Holly Sorensen a fiery missive in January 2001: “Dear Holly, Okay, you lazy bitch, I’m getting tired of this waterhead fuckaround that you’re doing with The Rum Diary. We are not even spinning our wheels aggressively. It’s like the whole Project got turned over to Zombies who live in cardboard boxes under the Hollywood Freeway. I seem to be the only person who’s doing anything about getting this movie Made. I have rounded up Depp, Benicio del Toro, Brad Pitt, Nick Nolte and a fine screenwriter from England named Michael Thomas.”

The eventual screenwriter for The Rum Diary turned out to be Bruce Robinson, the British director of the twisted 1987 cult classic Withnail and I, which both Depp and Thompson were “mutually obsessed with.” Depp had originally asked Robinson to direct the film adaptation of Fear and Loathing in Las Vegas—but by then, Robinson had quit directing, wrung out by the Hollywood system that had subjected him to making the Uma Thurman–led thriller Jennifer 8 for Paramount. He was content to spend the rest of his life writing screenplays and books and had vowed to never direct a movie again.

In 2005, Johnny tracked Robinson down while he was on vacation in Spain. “Have you read The Rum Diary?” Depp asked. Bruce hadn’t. The next day there was a knock on Robinson’s hotel room door, along with “the book The Rum Diary that Johnny had sent,” said Robinson.

“I wasn’t impressed by the book,” he continued. Neither were most critics: “For someone who gets to drink rum all day and have sex on the beach (the real thing, not the cocktail) all night, Kemp is pretty morose,” wrote one reviewer of the Thompson character. Despite some early incantations of the hellish voice that Thompson used with such tremendous force later in his career, it had many of the problems that plague early attempts at fiction. But because it was Johnny Depp, Robinson said he would try his hand at writing the screenplay.

Once back in Herefordshire, however, he found he could not write. Not a single word. “Nothing, nothing, nothing,” he said.

He knew the problem: abstinence. “I think it’s called alcoholism, probably,” he said of his tolerance for red wine. He had been sober for six and a half years, before which he had been “literally drinking wine all day while I worked. I’d finally gotten to the point where I was tired of doing that every day, seven days a week, so I cut the booze out of my life completely.”

Now he was stuck. Without the wine, which he said unleashed him from the gridlock of his superego, he was blocked. “You know I’m just going to have to drink some wine for this,” he finally confided to his wife. With her blessings, he opened a bottle of red, and then another and another, “one bottle of red wine a day—every day—for the three months it took me to write the story,” he said.

Then, as he was almost done with the screenplay (along with the Bordeaux), he drove into town to get the Sunday papers. The news was all over his radio: the American writer and journalist Hunter S. Thompson had killed himself.

“Oh, my God,” Robinson thought. “That’s the end of the movie.”

Hunter S. Thompson takes on his ranch in Colorado, 1976

Sunday, February 20, 2005. A heavy rainstorm lashed the streets of Los Angeles. Mist clung to the palms and a sense of gloom fell over the city, as it always does when it rains in L.A. Christi Dembrowski turned off Sunset and drove up the winding road to her brother’s home, bearing news that she knew would break Johnny’s heart: Hunter S. Thompson had died that afternoon of a self-inflicted gunshot wound to the head.

“My sister Christi turned up at the house in a pouring rainstorm,” Depp said. “She’d received a phone call two hours after it had happened and didn’t want me to see it on CNN. She told me Hunter had gone. But as much of a shock as it was, as much horror as I felt when you receive such blunt, bad news, I also knew Hunter was never going to be the guy who was going to collapse in a bowl of soup.”

After the shock—along with the eulogies for the fallen writer dominating airwaves, telephone lines, and barrooms worldwide—came what Depp called the “devastation.”

“Tragic,” he told Tom Thurman in the 2006 documentary Buy the Ticket, Take the Ride: Hunter S. Thompson on Film. “Shocked but not shocked. Angry but understanding. A zillion different emotions flying around all the time. It was like somebody had removed something from your body. It was a heavy hit.”

Mourning the loss of his brother, mentor, hero, and friend, Johnny’s mind flashed back to the 1978 BBC documentary Fear and Loathing in Gonzovision, in which Thompson described in minute detail how he would like to be sent off from this world into the next: he wanted his remains cremated and shot from a cannon on his land in Woody Creek, Colorado.

“When I got the news that Hunter had made his exit, obviously I was devastated,” Depp said. “I remember thinking almost immediately, ‘The cannon. We’ve got to do the cannon.’”

For Depp, this wasn’t a writer’s fantasy; it was Thompson’s final command. “That bastard,” Depp said. “I know somewhere in his crooked mind . . . he knew that I was the only one perhaps loyal enough, perhaps insane enough, to attempt it.”

Over the years, Thompson had spoken about what would be called “the death cannon,” Depp said. “You know, he brought it up every now and again; ‘Oh, yeah, I’ve got to have—I need the cannon, you know. . . . I may have even said, ‘I’ll send you off.’”

Thompson’s son, Juan, was determined to make it happen, too. Only Juan envisioned a twelve-foot cannon, costing a couple of hundred dollars. Johnny wanted something bigger, grander. “A few days later, Doug Brinkley, Hunter’s good friend and editor of his letters books, came over to our cabin and said Johnny Depp had called and said he wanted to pay for the construction of the cannon,” Juan wrote in his 2016 memoir Stories I Tell Myself: Growing Up with Hunter S. Thompson. “We were astonished . . . it is one thing to be a friend; it’s another thing to volunteer to build a friend’s death cannon.

An initial commemoration of Thompson’s life was held at the Hotel Jerome in Aspen on March 5, 2005. It was “essentially a party with music, photos and videos, interspersed with testimonials from friends and family,” Juan wrote. Depp flew to Aspen with Benicio del Toro and John Cusack to attend it. During the event, one of Depp’s assistants found Juan in the crowd “and asked me to come with him to Johnny’s room,” Juan wrote.

The assistant “slowly opened the door. And I entered the dark room and looked around, and in the center of the bed was a sculpture, lit from within. A giant boom box was playing Norman Greenbaum’s “Spirit in the Sky,” with its lyrics, “When I lay me down to die / Goin’ up to the spirit in the sky.”

“The sculpture was a tapering silver tower made of aluminum, about three feet high, topped by a blood-red fist,” Juan remembered. “The two-thumbed fist. The peyote button glowed from within with shifting colors. There was a small hole in the top of the fist where the projectile would exit.”

It was a magnificent miniature model that exceeded even Hunter Thompson’s wildest dreams. The full version, as envisioned by Johnny, would be “153 feet tall, 15 stories, taller even than what Hunter imagined,” wrote Juan. “Taller than the Statue of Liberty.”

The cannon would be fabricated in Los Angeles and hauled in pieces to Owl Farm on flatbed trucks. “It has to be perfect,” Depp told his long-time contractor, Steve Cohn, who coordinated the event over three months in Aspen (residing in three suites in three different Aspen hotels; at one of them, the Sky Hotel, Cohn allegedly set the record on room service booze and “incidentals,” spending more than $100,000). No expense was spared for what would become a monumental undertaking, requiring six months of massive set designing, and construction and engineering crews “numbering at least a hundred” from Hollywood and beyond, said Cohn.

On August 20, 2005, a crowd of 250, including Jack Nicholson, Sean Penn, Bill Murray, and Senators George McGovern and John Kerry, assembled on the meadow behind Thompson’s home. After the toasts and speeches (“Hunter, this is for you,” said Depp), a video clip of Thompson discussing his funeral cannon in his 1978 BBC interview was played on a giant screen. A Japanese drum ensemble began performing. Glasses of champagne were passed around. Then all eyes were on the massive cannon, its red fabric curtains unfurling to reveal the gonzo logo—the two-thumbed fist and the pulsating green peyote button—at the top of the 153-foot tower. “Spirit in the Sky” blasted from the speakers. “Then, at 8:46 p.m., more than thirty fireworks rocketed high above Owl Farm . . .” Doug Brinkley wrote in Rolling Stone. “The cannon atop the tower fired, and Hunter’s ashes fell over the assembled guests like gray snow. ‘Mr. Tambourine Man’ blaring from the sound system on cue. Hunter was literally all around us now. . . . ‘I have never seen an event like this,’ whispered the actor Harry Dean Stanton. ‘And I’m old. Very old.’”

“Everybody was speechless,” said Cohn. “Johnny and I were sitting together when it went off. He looked at me, smiled, and said, ‘That was awesome.’”

With Thompson’s ashes in the air, the party began in a massive tent decorated to look like Hunter’s kitchen and living room.

Bruce Robinson’s initial thought was that the death of Hunter Thompson would mean the death of The Rum Diary. But he was wrong about that.

“I had lost my dear friend and co-conspirator,” Depp wrote. “Sure as fuck wasn’t going to give up now.”
