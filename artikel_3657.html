Darializa Avila Chevalier Explains the DSA

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

In June, Darializa Avila Chevalier, the Democratic nominee for Congress in the 13th District of New York, burst onto the national stage when she won her primary against five-term incumbent Representative Adriano Espaillat. Along with several other candidates this cycle, Chevalier became emblematic of a new swell of progressivism in America. Almost immediately, she came under fire for inflammatory posts she had made on the internet—about defunding the police, abolishing prisons, and Israel’s actions in Gaza and the West Bank. She has what many would characterize as some pretty radical views.

Chevalier comes from the world of academia—she’s a PhD candidate, a longtime grassroots organizer, and a member of the New York chapter of the Democratic Socialists of America. But we don’t live in a theoretical world, and it seems to me that ideological purism often gets in the way of getting real things done. So, ahead of next week’s midterms, I wanted to know whether she stands by some of her most divisive statements and how she plans to govern once the race is over and she (presumably) takes office. And I really wanted to know who Chevalier is as a person. I sat down with her on October 26 for a conversation about how she views protests, being recruited to run for office, being compared to AOC, why President Donald Trump likes Mayor Zohran Mamdani, and more.

Mark Guiducci: Candidate Darializa Avila Chevalier, welcome to the show.

Darializa Avila Chevalier: Thanks so much for having me.

Thanks for being here. Darializa, you are the Democratic candidate and presumptive winner for Congress in the 13th District of New York. You’re also a member of the Democratic Socialists of America, one of many candidates this election cycle who represents a swelling progressive movement. Your national profile is growing.

You were photographed by Annie Leibovitz for our New Establishment Issue, and today I wanna talk to you about how you situate yourself in the political landscape and the leftist surge that we’re seeing right now. And I also wanna talk to you about you. So let’s start a little bit with your background.

New York is your home now, of course, but you were born in Florida and briefly lived in the Dominican Republic and in Venezuela before settling in Tallahassee, I think, as a teenager. And, you know, looking back at your childhood, I wanna know, what was the moment that you had your first political inkling?

Yeah. I mean, I moved around so much as a kid—not only around all those different places, but also within those different places, right? Because my mom was a working-class single mom. My dad wasn’t very involved, but he was also very working-class and, you know, he’s a truck driver. My mom’s a caseworker, worked multiple jobs, and it was not always easy.

And I think my first moment where I realized, Oh, we’re, like, very working-class, if not working-poor, was when I was driving to school with my mom one morning and we had to stop for gas, and my mom said to me, “Crouch down and look for coins,” because we weren’t sure whether we had enough to get gas to get to school.

And we crouched down; I think we barely got to a dollar, and I went into the, the gas station and I gave the man at the counter the dollar, and he looked at me, looked at my mom—who was waiting and looking into the gas station, watching me—and looked back down and said, “I put $5 on the pump.” And I think that was the first time, and I was small. I was still in elementary school at this point that I realized, Oh, this is not something that people normally experience, right? And we were literally relying on the kindness of a stranger that day.

When would you say that you were able to start putting language to that? You know, a five-year-old or a six-year-old probably doesn’t know that they’re working-class.

They know the situation, they know the emotions, but when were you able to start putting the language to it? ’Cause I think that’s an important piece to the politics, you know?

Yeah, I think, you know, as I got older, I was definitely aware that class was a thing.

I think I didn’t call it class, right, until I started reading more about, you know, people’s experiences. I think even in our society, we confuse what working-class means often, right? And we think about ourselves; everyone wants to be middle-class, right? And even folks who are really struggling, like they’re one paycheck away from losing everything, seeing themselves as middle-class [is] a point of dignity, right?

No one wants to see themselves as struggling so much that they could lose everything, but that is the reality for most Americans in this country. And once I was reading more, you know, just progressive, leftist, socialist literature, right, that was talking about these things in a very concrete way and giving language to it—I was reading folks like Baldwin and Du Bois and Angela Davis, right?

And they were really talking about these experiences in ways that really resonated.

Yeah, high school I was reading Baldwin; I was reading, you know, more class-conscious literature. And college, I guess, it was really where, like, things started to get a concrete definition for me, right?

I realized I just really wanted language for my experience, for the experience of my community, and, you know, I really felt really immersed in that world of literature and theory.

So you went to college at Columbia. You’re a New York transplant. How do you think your experience as a non-native New Yorker—I also didn’t grow up here—how do you think it influences your relationship to the city that you’re about to represent?

Yeah, I mean, I love the city so much because it made me the woman that I am. You know, I came and I was 18, but I also grew up coming here often because my mom’s side of the family was here, and New York always felt like the place where I could be all parts of myself. And having lived here since I was, you know, a teenager and 18 and growing into myself, I learned that I love organizing here, right?

I became politically active, and I became active in my community here. And, you know, if you ask me where my roots are, it’s here at this point, because this is where most of the people I love are.

I understand that. I moved here the day after college, and—

I get it. You were just saying your organizing started here.

And that really started when you were an undergraduate at Columbia as part of the Students for Justice in Palestine. I’m curious what you thought that movement was missing when you came across it and what you thought you could bring to it.

Yeah. Well, I actually started organizing earlier than that.

I was—well, in college, but before I joined Students for Justice in Palestine. I was organizing around ending sexual violence on campus. Emma Sulkowicz’s name is coming up a lot now because of the case at Cornell, but I was at school at the same time as her. And this was something that we, on campus, were really starting to build around and organize around to get the university to make meaningful change.

And I think, you know, unfortunately, clearly from what we’ve seen in the last few months, a lot has not changed on college campuses for a lot of people. But that was the work that we were already doing. And I was also organizing with the Black Students Organization, which takes its name because it was an organizing organization, whereas, you know, at other universities, it’s usually the Black Student Union.

We were actually invested in organizing Black students on our campus. And I joined SJP after having gone to Palestine. I’d spent a summer there in the West Bank. And having firsthand experience for, you know, just witnessing the apartheid system there, and—

That was 2014. And I was there as tensions were rising in the West Bank.

And the day after, or within a few days of getting home to the US, you know, Israel began bombing Gaza, and it was a very dark experience. I don’t have another word for it. I just felt so disempowered to be at home scrolling through the names and images of the people who had been slaughtered in Gaza, and people who look so much like the people I had just left behind in the West Bank.

And I think that was where I first started to really take an interest in the notion of power.

Because I’d never felt so powerless in my life.

You attended the pro-Palestine rally on October 8, 2023. What was your thinking in attending that event, and how do you think about it now? Do you regret that?

You know, I have always believed that protests are about power. Again, like, I think power itself is a morally neutral thing. It is about whether or not you can change the conditions around you, right? And so empowering communities to be able to change those circumstances is something I think about all the time.

Like, what are the conditions that you’re thinking, That’s the day to go?

Yes, and so, you know, as someone who’s an American citizen, as someone who pays my taxpayer money, the thing that I have power over in this cycle of violence is where my government uses my tax dollar in this cycle, and the party that we pay our tax dollars to and that we support and arm is the Israeli government, right?

And, you know, are there folks who say things you disagree with at any protest? Yes, of course. The same is true for many elected officials who attended the Israel Day Parade with Israeli officials who have openly called for the genocide of Palestinians, right? And I would hope that they have vehemently disavowed that, right?

But when it comes to advocating for the human rights and human dignity of Palestinians, there seems to be a double standard, right? Where we can’t express our outrage at our tax dollars being used in a way that denies people their human rights and their dignity, particularly when so many people in our communities are being denied those resources, right? Whether it’s in funding schools, whether it’s in addressing childhood poverty.

And I really deeply believe that we are responsible for having a power analysis when we engage in any kind of protest, right?

I think the people that you mentioned, especially public officials, should have to answer the question that you just posed to them.

But specifically on the October 8, you would do it—like, you would go back again on October 8, 2023, to that protest?

I would, because that same day, what I was thinking about was my experience in 2014, where I had sat in my bed and felt powerless, right? And knew in 2023 what was coming was Israel’s response would lead to more and more death.

And that same day, hundreds of people had already been killed, right, in Gaza. And to me, when we mourn human life, we have to mourn human life and prevent more loss of life.

I’m thinking about, on October 7, 2023, people who have done Birthright trips to Israel or have visited in some way and are scrolling through faces and names of people they knew. How do you feel your experience differs from theirs?

I think where, again, asking a question of, where are we positioned to interrupt cycles of violence? I think losing someone you care about is something that, universally, we can all empathize with, right? It is something that is tragic and heartbreaking and is hard to wrap our heads around. If in that moment I could’ve called up the president to yell at Netanyahu, to tell him to stop dropping the bombs, or if I could have, you know, called whoever at any point in the cycle of violence, of course you’d wanna do that.

That’s not a power I have. The power I have is to organize my community into creating that societal shift.

But do you see how those experiences are similar?

Again, the experience of losing somebody, I think that is a universal, like, experience of loss and trauma and pain and grief. Where I see things are different is, again, around a question of power.

Do you have the power to create a world where there is less grief, right? And I think if we look at things institutionally, our government currently is investing in continuing grief for a certain group of people.

Today you are no longer just an organizer. You are poised to be an elected official. When you were younger, did you ever think that you’d be running for office?

Can you describe the moment that you decided to run?

I was recruited to run by Justice Democrats, and I—you know, it’s an organization that takes community nominations, and they told me that someone from the community had nominated me, and that they had spoken to other members of the community and really were excited about this idea.

And I was like, All right, well, I’m just gonna take some time to think about it, ’cause this wasn’t something that I’d thought to do. And I had many conversations with other organizers, with members of my community, with folks who I was like, Oh, they’ll definitely tell me this is a bad idea. And to my surprise, everyone was like, “No, this is a great idea, but it really is up to you.”

This is the same organization that found AOC. How did they find you, and how did you start that relationship?

Yeah, I mean, I’ve been organizing for a really, really long time. And we actually had spoken with them about running somebody else, and a few months later, I get an email, and they told me that folks had reached out to them about me.

And the first question I asked was, “Why me?” And I was like, “And do you know—”

Well, I was like, “And do you know that, like, I have been very vocal,” right, “on a lot of issues?” And they said that’s why. Because they really just wanted someone who felt firm in their convictions and on their view of what this country can and should be for working people across this country.

And I had to really sit with that because it was not an easy decision to make, and so that’s why it took me so long to give them a yes.

How did you win? Because beyond grassroots organizing, moving out an incumbent like Adriano Espaillat in Harlem is no mean feat.

In the clearest possible terms, how do you think you did it?

And it was bringing groups together and building a coalition that knocked over 155,000 doors, that talked to their neighbors even when it was not an easy conversation. And it was, you know, organizing all of the members of our coalition into this larger vision, because not everyone was sold at first.

You have been using this word organizing. What is organizing?

So organizing is coming together with others in your community or who share your same understanding of what the problem is, right? And coming together to address that issue. It is about fundamentally shifting power away from the concentrated few toward the many, right?

So when we think about, for example, housing organizing or tenant organizing, right?

It is about tenants coming together, right, to build power among themselves to change the conditions in their building, whether that is because the tiles on the floor are actually dangerous for the grandma who might trip on them, right?

Or the leaks that are making it so that you are having to endure black mold and just deeply undignified conditions.

And coming together to build the power to have these repairs actually happen.

What does that look like on the day-to-day? Like, what are some things that I might not understand is organizing that you—you know, things that you did in your campaign that I might not understand as organizing, but that were effective?

I think a lot of people, they think there’s, like, this wow or flashy thing that happens in organizing that is what makes it like all of a sudden the power has shifted, but really, it is deeply unsexy work. It is the boring phone calls. It is the meetings that could’ve been an email.

It is the thing that, you know, just brings people together and [makes them] feel connected on a human level to build the trust necessary to move toward something else.

And what’s the role of the DSA in that? In that organizing and in that coalition, how did the DSA influence, impact, your race specifically?

Yeah. Well, I mean, DSA is such an incredible organization.

It is a member-based organization, so if you’re part of the DSA, it’s because you love organizing and you love being in that work, and you share this vision of a socialist, or democratic socialist, country. And once they were excited about this race, right, we knew we could win because we all of a sudden had an army of folks who were ready to knock on doors, were ready to talk about a politic that works for working people.

When you say “they” about the DSA, who’s they? I mean, because is it, like, Megan Romer, the co-chair of the national chapter? Is this the mayor?

Well, I’m a member of the New York City chapter of DSA, which is, you know, affiliated but separate from the national DSA. And I think maybe we take a step back and explain what DSA even is.

That’s perfect. Yes. Let’s do that. I had Ta-Nehisi Coates in this chair a few weeks ago, and the conversation went there, and even he was asking, “What is the DSA?”

Well, to Comrade Coates, I can explain that a little bit more.

It is a member-based organization that is focused on building people power in a democratic way.

And so if you share the values of a socialist, which, to us, is making sure that the wealth that working people produce is also wealth that working people can benefit from and enjoy in our society, then, you know, you can come organize with us to make sure that we’re building that world. And when I talk about socialism, I think sometimes people get stuck in the theory of it and don’t understand what that concretely means.

Sure. It’s a tough brand in America sometimes.

Yeah. And I think most of the most popular policies we’ve ever had in this country’s history, including now, are themselves socialist policies. So thinking, for example, of Social Security or Medicare, right? These are incredibly popular policies, to the point where we’re always fighting to defend them, right?

And we should be fighting to expand them. We should be fighting for more socialist policies that make the lives of working people better.

It is not a party. It is an organization.

I mean, many of us are members or are part of the Democratic Party, right? We’re registered as Democrats, but it is—

Yes. And I’m the Democratic nominee. But the DSA is just an organization. It is an organizing organization, right? And organizing, again, is really about shifting power away from a concentrated few toward the many.

The national chapter of the DSA has a published platform that includes aims like abolishing the presidency—

Abolishing the Senate, replacing the Supreme Court, open borders. How do you—do you agree with that? Or how do you address that with people who read that and are frightened?

Mm-hmm. I mean, again, I’m a member of the New York City chapter.

There are some of those things that I really just don’t agree with, like I don’t agree with abolishing the presidency, for example. But I do think that the beautiful thing about DSA is that it is truly a democratic organization. So as a New York City chapter member, right, I can participate in all of the democratic decisions that happen within our chapter.

And anytime we make a decision as a chapter, we are voting on it, right? And the beautiful thing is, if you have an idea for how to shift politics or policies to work for working people, inside the chapter, you can introduce that, right? You can introduce a resolution. And so—

So what I’m hearing is that it’s not—it’s more of an approach.

It’s not one specific policy or goal in mind, or even one specific worldview; that it’s flexible depending on—

Yeah. I mean, there is a huge diversity of thought within DSA, right? And even between national and the New York City chapter, there’s diversity. There’s, like, sometimes disagreement, right?

And that is what democracy is, right? And I think democracy is a practice, it’s an exercise, and it’s something we have to do often, right?

You have often been compared to AOC, also a New York DSA member. You’ve been called the next AOC; AOC but to the left. She recently endorsed you. What’s your relationship like?

It’s a positive relationship. She is someone who has really laid a path for so many people to be able to come into Congress as a working person. And, you know, I have a lot of respect for the congresswoman, and I’m really excited to see what she might do next.

What do you think are the key differences between the two of you?

Yeah. And, again, I think I’m my own person, right? So—

I think, as someone who comes from a different area of organizing but largely agrees with, you know, the vision that she came into Congress with, I think it’s exciting to be able to come in with a new perspective of where we can grow things even further, right?

President Trump has been recently praising AOC. He said—sort of begrudgingly, he said he doesn’t mind her so much, which is funny coming from the leader of the MAGA movement. And, of course, Mayor Mamdani and Trump have had very public meetings, cordial meetings at the White House and then Gracie Mansion.

I am also very fascinated by this. And I think part of it is that he’s a New Yorker, right? And I think that’s something for New Yorkers to also contend with, right? But I think he just also is someone who understands popularity, right? And I don’t mean popularity as like—

Yeah, he likes a winner, and I think he likes it when people are actually willing to stand on what they believe and not just, you know, kiss up to him.

Who do you think gets more from that relationship, him or them? That’s interesting. Specifically Mamdani.

You know, I think it has been really fascinating to watch the way that they’re cognizant of the power disparity.

He’s the president of the United States. That is just the political reality, unfortunately, for all of us, right? But to watch, you know, the mayor and to watch the congresswoman in the way that they interact and really move strategically to make sure that New Yorkers benefit from every interaction that they have with the president has been…

Honestly, I’m taking notes. Because I’m like, eventually, right, that will be something that I’ll have to do.

Yeah. It makes me think of something else too, which is, like, you know, so much of campaigning, certainly organizing, is about principles and ideology, and there’s a purity to that.

When you’re governing, you have to work with people, whether it’s centrist Democrats or even Republicans, and that often means compromise. How do you think about that? Are you prepared to compromise, or do you sort of—are you prepared to compromise?

I think that juxtaposition is funny and interesting because, even in a campaign, you’re organizing with people who you might not always agree with on everything, and that doesn’t mean that you compromise your ideology, that you compromise your vision of the world, that you compromise your principles, right?

I deeply believe that we have to be uncompromising in our principles and willing to move on strategy. Because if we are set on strategy, then what does that mean for our principles? Where are we bending there?

Can you give an example of where you’d compromise, or an example of AOC or someone else who has had to make compromise that you’d be okay with?

You know, I’ll actually point to myself, and that is that I’m running for office. I never thought that I would run for office. I’ve always seen myself as a grassroots organizer, and when I realized we had a strategic opening to meaningfully transform politics in this country, I said, Okay, let’s do this.

Let’s use this vehicle that I had for many years not really—like, that was not my world of organizing, right?

As much as I saw it as valuable, it just wasn’t where I was. But I stepped into it because I saw it as a really important vehicle for how we shift our politics more largely. And after winning, right, people told us that that could only happen in New York, and a week later, Melat Kiros won in Denver.

And then after that, 103 Democrats voted to stop sending arms to Israel. So we’ve already transformed the politics of what’s considered acceptable politics within the electoral world.

Sure. I was gonna say, when you get to Congress, do you see your role as someone that is there to make laws or to move the Overton window to the left so that the laws that are made are closer to what you and your compatriots are after?

Yeah. I think it’s both and. I mean, I think, like, if we are only ever talking about our values, but we’re not shifting the policies, then the world around us doesn’t actually change. And I think about—I mean, we’ll just continue down this example of the issue of the genocide, right?

We can talk about our values.We can convince an entire country of people. We’ve convinced the entire Democratic base on this issue, and yet we still have elected officials who are voting against the interests of their base, right? The material conditions have not changed, right? And that’s only going to happen when we have policy that also shifts with it.

And so that’s why it’s important that we are being, you know, good colleagues, right? And we’re talking to folks, finding those points where we do have something in common, and organizing our colleagues to see reason as well, right? Because it’s—yes, sometimes we have to, like, see reason of why something is difficult for them.

That’s what I was gonna say. Are you ready to see reason?

Yeah. And they have to also see that, like, their Democratic base wants them to move on these issues.

How much time have you spent with your future Republican colleagues?

Well, actually, I haven’t spoken to many, but I’ve spoken to, you know, folks across the spectrum on the Democratic Party at this point.

Have you met any Republican congresspeople?

Not actual members, but I’ve met plenty of Republicans.

Oh, plenty. I mean, I grew up also in Florida.

So there are plenty of Republicans there.

Who’s the Republican that you’ll reach out to first when you get to Congress?

Yeah. No, I mean, I’ll say this. I think that there are no two people who agree on every single issue, even as aligned as you might be, right?

And you’d be surprised how people who think very differently can still find a point of commonality and can build trust.

Exactly, and so that is what organizing is.

Earlier this month, ICE agents attempted to arrest 28-year-old Oscar Belgal in the Bronx.

They shot him in the neck while he was in the car with his five-year-old son in the back seat. As you pointed out in the aftermath of this incident, the shooter, an ICE agent, hasn’t been identified or charged with a crime. Can you talk me through what you think should happen now?

Yeah. I think there should be a full investigation into what happened.

I think Oscar should be released and returned to his family, and so that he could get medical care. He still has the bullet in his body and is losing hearing and feeling in his face. And, you know, I think we need to abolish ICE. I don’t see a world in which an institution that is a rogue militia, that is walking around in crowded streets with no uniform, no identification, masked, and shooting into cars—seven bullets into a car that had a five-year-old child in it—while people were returning home from school and work, is an agency that has any business operating in our country.

We’ve seen so many instances of communities and families being torn apart because of ICE’s actions, and the only reason that it exists at this point is to serve as Trump’s cudgel for fascism.

If the shooter in question was apprehended, what should happen to them?

I mean, they should be prosecuted, obviously.

But I also think—I don’t have faith in that because what we’re looking at is the institution that would oversee that is Trump’s, right? It’s Trump’s DOJ. And so I think what the solution here—

Yeah, I mean, this is attempted manslaughter at best.

It’s attempted murder, right? This was, like, ICE shooting at somebody in broad daylight.

I see a bit of a contradiction between wanting the shooter apprehended and your stance on prison abolition, and where’s the shooter going, if not to jail?

Mm-hmm. You know, abolition is a process and a vision, right?

It is the idea that we are constructing a world where a shooting like that doesn’t happen to begin with. And so when I talk about abolishing ICE, the point is that we are taking away the condition for this to ever happen again. We are taking these arms off the street. We are taking these people away from the power to do that to our communities, right?

And we are reinvesting those resources into things that actually make our communities more safe. Abolition is also the view that we should be proactive in safety and not reactive. Because right now we’re talking about an incident that already happened, where a man was shot, right? The world that we’re trying to build is one where that isn’t something that can come to pass.

I understand that, and I think that is a worthy vision, and to advocate for broad systemic reform to prevent murder is something I think a lot of people can get behind. But that’s a future goal, and I’m just curious about now. Now that you’re gonna become a lawmaker, do you believe that someone who is convicted of murder with intent should be physically confined to protect the public?

But that is what happens in the day-to-day.

And I know that because I’ve worked at a public defender’s office. I’ve seen this happen, right? What I argue, as someone who deeply cares about ending violence in our communities, is that if we don’t begin the process now of building this world where we are proactive in preventing violence instead of reactive once the violence has already happened, then we’re never going to get to that world.

I’m pushing you on this because I think people see this as radical. What you’re saying is an idealistic vision of the future that is worthy. I think most people, many people would think [it’s] a worthy vision.

But they’re concerned about that specific idea now. It feels radical.

Well, again, it’s not something that happens overnight. We’re not gonna depopulate and close Rikers tomorrow, even though the law, right, is that we are supposed to close Rikers, and we’re supposed to do that, like, now, right? But we need to also, of course, do that in a way that actually makes sense and functions for a society, right?

Where are these people gonna go? Do they even have homes to go back to, right? And I think what that is signaling is that we have a larger structural issue that has prioritized incarcerating people over taking care of communities, right? You know, I once worked with a client who would seek to get arrested because it was the only place he could take his bipolar medication.

That is a failure of our medical system, that is a failure of our housing system, and that is also a public-safety issue.

No question. But that ICE agent, in a perfect world where the DOJ behaves as they should, is going to prison.

In a perfect world, ICE wouldn’t exist, is my point, right?

And as a sociologist, we create our world.

We create the infrastructure and the structures that encourage people to behave in particular ways.

Have you ever heard the Tom Wolfe quote? He said, “A Democrat is a Republican who’s been through the criminal justice system, and a Republican is a Democrat who’s been mugged.” [Editor’s note: Wolfe’s quote is, “If a conservative is a liberal who’s been mugged, a liberal is a conservative who’s been arrested,” which builds upon the neoconservative aphorism “a conservative is a liberal who has been mugged.”]

It’s good. Let’s look forward. It’s January 3, 2027, and according to all prediction models, you’re gonna be taking office that day. What are your immediate priorities? Where do you begin?

So our platform was deeply important to our district, and we know that because not only after knocking doors did the folks say, like, “These are the issues that matter to us,” but also because I’d spent a year knocking on doors for the mayor and knowing that those were the issues that our community cares about.

So addressing childhood poverty, investing in our babies and not bombs, signing on to the Block the Bombs Act to stop the genocide, making sure that we are fighting to abolish ICE, and making sure that we’re winning housing for all so that everyone in our district has dignified housing.

You know, I have questions about impeachment. We’ve impeached him twice, and he seems to want impeachment, and I really want meaningful accountability.

And I’m looking at what is actually going to bring that about.

So instead, are you hauling administration officials and others into Congress after investigations? Is that a more meaningful approach?

I think hauling everyone who had any role in any of this corruption into Congress to answer to the American public, that’s what I think was a more meaningful approach, yeah.

So you haven’t committed to backing Hakeem Jeffries for Speaker, but you recently met with him. When it comes to deciding if you will cast your vote for him, what are you weighing? What are your considerations, the pros and cons?

I think this would be a really historic vote, and this would be a very historic moment—not just for myself, as it’ll set the tone for the kind of representative I’ll be for my district, but also for this country, where we’re talking about having the first Black Speaker, right?
