You come back to a finished feature. The button works, the file downloads, the tests are green. You feel like you hired an extra developer for the price of a subscription.

Then you open the diff. There's a new package nobody on your team has heard of. There's a new date formatter sitting next to the one you already have. The export loads every order into memory, and the route never checks whether this user is allowed to see those orders.

Nothing here looks broken in a demo. All of it is a problem in production, and none of it is really the AI's fault.

Now replace "AI" with "the junior who joined on Monday." Would you give them one sentence on their first day, no docs, no design, no acceptance criteria, then disappear until they said "done"? If that went badly, whose mistake would it be?

Then ask a bigger question. Once that feature is live, who watches it? Who notices it getting slower, keeps it maintained, and answers when it breaks next year?

Every product people depend on needs a senior engineer who owns it. AI can assist with almost all of that work, but it can't be the owner.

The tempting belief goes like this: AI writes code quickly, so my job is to ask for features and check that they work. Less typing, less thinking, more output.

It feels right because the speed is real and you can see it. The problem is that the feeling isn't a reliable measuring tool.

In 2025, METR ran a randomized trial with 16 experienced open source developers working on 246 tasks in their own repositories. Each task was randomly allowed or not allowed to use AI. They expected AI to make them 24% faster. They took 19% longer. And after doing the work, they still believed AI had made them 20% faster.

That result needs an honest footnote, because it's easy to misuse. It's a small study of early-2025 tools, and METR itself marks it as out of date. Its 2026 follow-up points toward developers now being faster, but METR calls that data unreliable, partly because many developers refused to work on tasks without AI. So this study doesn't prove AI makes you slower.

It proves something more useful: experienced developers can be wrong about their own speed by a wide margin, in the direction that feels good.

AI doesn't remove the senior's work. It moves your time from typing code to directing and checking it.

A new teammate gets the architecture, the conventions, and a tour of where things live. AI needs the same, written down. Most tools read a project file for this, such as AGENTS.md or CLAUDE.md.

This idea has reached the frameworks too. When I run next dev on this blog, Next.js adds a note to the repo's AGENTS.md that speaks directly to the AI: "This version has breaking changes — APIs, conventions, and file structure may all differ from your training data." It then tells the agent to read the docs that ship with the installed package. The framework's own authors expect AI to be confidently out of date, so they onboard it.

This is the cheapest review you'll ever do. A wrong direction costs one sentence to fix at the plan stage and a whole diff to fix later.

Ask it what it read and what it plans to change. Look for the things a junior wouldn't know to worry about: is it rebuilding something that exists? Is it changing a shared module for one small feature? Did it actually look at the code, or is it assuming?

A new dependency is a long-term decision that takes the AI one line to make. Treat it the way you'd treat a junior's package.json change: it needs a reason.

The same goes for new patterns and tools. If the project uses one state library and the diff quietly adds a second, that's a review comment even if the code works. Every tool works in isolation. Consistency is the thing the AI can't see unless you hold it.

This is the part I feel strongest about: you can't announce "we need feature A" and come back when it's over. You wouldn't do it with a junior, and the AI won't come to your desk when it gets lost.

Watch how it works, especially early on. If it's reading the wrong files, or has tried the same failing fix three times, or starts editing things outside the task, stop it. Correcting course halfway costs far less than unpicking the result.

Work in slices, too. Build the export endpoint, review it, then add the button. A diff small enough to read gets a real review, and a huge one gets a skim and a thumbs up.

This is where most of the long-term value is, and it's the same habit as a good mentor's: don't only fix the mistake, fix why it happened.

When a mistake can be caught by a machine, go further than a rule. A lint rule, a type or a failing test works every time, whether or not anyone remembered to read the docs. And maintain these files like code: rules go stale when the project changes, and a stale rule will mislead the AI as confidently as a wrong comment misleads a person.

You probably do want the end state: hand over a task and get back something you'd happily merge. That's how good juniors grow too. You don't get there by deciding to trust them. You get there by watching them handle bigger jobs without surprises.

Three things about this table are easy to miss.

First, trust is per type of task, not per tool. You might fully delegate writing tests for pure functions and never get past stage 2 on payment code. A good senior gives out work the same way: based on what the task can break.

Second, it's slow. I can't give you an honest number for how long it takes, because it depends on your codebase and how much is already written down. But the time isn't wasted, because everything you put in stays in the repo. Better docs and stricter checks also help your human teammates.

Third, there's no stage 5 where the senior leaves. Delegating a task isn't the same as giving away responsibility for the product. A good tech lead delegates nearly everything and still answers for the result.

So far, everything has happened before the merge. But a product spends its life after the merge, and that's where AI left alone does the most lasting damage.

Go back to the CSV export, and picture it passing every review. A year later, the orders table is far bigger. Exports that took seconds now time out. They hold database connections for so long that unrelated pages slow down. Customers complain before anyone on the team knows.

No test failed, and nobody changed that code. The AI said nothing, because nobody asked it anything.

That's the gap. A coding tool works on the task in front of it. Nobody hands it the job of caring about this product next year, and it can't be held to account for what happens then.

For a starting point on what to watch, Google's SRE book names four golden signals: "latency, traffic, errors, and saturation." On the CSV export, that's how long exports take, how many people run them, how many fail, and how close the database is to its limit. The book's reason for monitoring is the same as this post's: it lets a system "tell us when it's broken, or perhaps to tell us what's about to break."

Reviewing those numbers on a schedule is a real part of the job, and connecting AI to them is a good idea. It can spot the climbing line and summarize a hundred log entries in seconds. But judgment stays with you. Ask for a fix to that timeout with no context, and one valid-looking answer is a longer timeout. A senior asks why it's slow, whether the design still fits the data, and what else this will hurt.

If your team has no senior engineer, bring one in, even part-time, before real users arrive and not after the first outage.

All of this has a cost, and sometimes it isn't worth paying. For a throwaway script or a weekend prototype nobody will maintain, go ahead and ask for the feature and see what comes back. The risk is small, so light supervision is fine.

The junior comparison also has limits. A junior is a person whose growth is part of the point, and this tool has no career to invest in. You can also throw away an AI's attempt and start again without hurting anyone's feelings, which you should do more often than you would with a colleague. The comparison is a guide for how to supervise the work, not a claim about what the tool is.

And the evidence is moving. Most of the studies here are from 2025, and models have changed since. Newer tools will likely make fewer of these mistakes. They won't know your product's unwritten rules unless someone writes them down, and they won't be accountable for what ships.

That weekend prototype leads to the most expensive version of this problem: the one with no senior in the loop at all.

Picture a meeting. Someone from sales or product, with no engineering background, opens a laptop and shows a working app they built with AI. It has a login, a dashboard and real customer data. It's impressive, and they know it. Then comes the sentence:

"I built this without any technical knowledge. You're much better at this than I am, so maintaining it will be easy for you."

Being easy to build says nothing about being easy to maintain. It was easy because the AI made hundreds of decisions and nobody had to look at them: how permissions work, where the secrets live, what the data model assumes, what happens when two people edit the same record. Those decisions didn't go away. Nobody has reviewed them yet.

A better engineer doesn't make those problems smaller. They only see more of them. And the team isn't inheriting code from a colleague they can ask, because the author can't explain why any of it is the way it is. Every question gets the same answer: "I don't know, the AI did that." The team now owns production software with no history, no reasoning, and often no tests.

Even the person who named this way of working didn't claim otherwise. When Andrej Karpathy coined "vibe coding" in 2025, he wrote "I 'Accept All' always, I don't read the diffs anymore," and described it as "not too bad for throwaway weekend projects." The limit was in the original description.

None of this means non-technical people shouldn't build with AI. They should. A working prototype beats a long requirements document, because everyone can click it and argue about something real. The mistake is what happens next, so change the handover:

This isn't really about job titles. A senior developer who merges every AI diff unread is in the same position as the person in that meeting, with a better vocabulary. What matters is whether anyone understands the thing they're asking others to run.

Give every product a senior engineer who owns it, from the first brief to production and beyond. Use AI as a fast junior: brief it, watch it, review it, write down what it got wrong, and give it more as it earns trust. Let it assist with everything, and let it own nothing.
