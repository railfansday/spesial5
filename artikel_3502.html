‘Calvin and Hobbes’ Has the Perfect Christmas Message for Anyone Who Hates the Holidays

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

The holidays can either be a heavenly experience or Hell on earth, depending on your point of view. If you're in the former camp, you probably love meeting up with family for Thanksgiving and Christmas or seeing decorations line stores as well as your own house. If you're the latter, you find yourself counting the days until the New Year's Ball drops. Movies centered on the holidays is no different; Miracle on 34th Street is a heartwarming tale about the power of belief, while National Lampoon's Christmas Vacation perfectly captures the stress of being around one's family during the holidays. It's Calvin and Hobbes that perfectly captures the malaise around holidays with a single strip.

That strip, which made its debut in 1993, takes the form of a rather humorous poem that Calvin receives from Santa Claus. In this poem, Claus tells an overjoyed Calvin that he's repealing his rules about who's "naughty" and who's "nice", encouraging the spiky-haired youth to embrace his naughtier tendencies. However, that's when the strip ends with a twist: it was all part of a dream, leading Calvin to lament, "I hate being good (or trying to fake it)/Six days until Christmas! I don't think I'll make it." While Calvin and Hobbes was no stranger to depicting the holidays, this strip nails the unspoken pressure that the holiday season puts on kids.

Everyone's heard the song "Santa Claus is Coming to Town," which paints Kris Kringle as an all-seeing force that knows when children are are naughty or nice. While this is more or less meant to get kids to behave, it underlines the pressure that's being put on them at an early age. People are human; sometimes you'll say or do something you don't mean, but it doesn't mean you have wicked intentions at heart. There's also the fact that in an age of constant online surveillance, Santa's observations come off as less benevolent and more creepy (no matter how many movies try to explain it.)

Calvin and Hobbes' Santa strip hits hard for this exact reason, especially when you take Calvin's characterization into consideration. Part of the draw of the series is Calvin's boundless imagination, as his flights of fancy lend themselves well to the comic medium but also get him into trouble. For a kid who loves Christmas as much as he does, it must be tough to try and restrain himself. Therefore, the idea of Santa flipping the script so that "naughty" kids triumph would sound like a legit Christmas miracle to him. It's rather fitting, as Calvin and Hobbes creator Bill Watterson seems to excel when he places the titular duo in a wintery setting.

A decade of Calvin and Hobbes strips reveals that some of the best ones are set around Christmastime or a wintery setting, and that's thanks to the way that Bill Watterson approaches the holidays. Instead of the usual heartwarming stories, Calvin and Hobbes fans could see Calvin annoying his parents by waking up early on Christmas morning, or him and Hobbes dealing with an army of grotesque mutant snowmen. They could also expect the strip's signature introspection and wit to be deployed, especially in one moment where Calvin's dad criticizes a televised Christmas special for being about "cola, fast food, and beer." Watterson expressed similar thoughts in the documentary Dear Mr. Watterson, where he spoke on the fact that he never chose to merchandise Calvin and Hobbes.

"My strip is about private realities, the magic of imagination, and the specialness of a certain friendship. Who would believe in the innocence of a little kid and his stuffed tiger if their popularity was used to sell over priced kick knacks nobody needs?"

The holidays can be extremely stressful, putting unneeded pressure on people to be around their families or to act a certain way. Calvin and Hobbes highlighted why this pressure is a bad thing, and is a balm to anyone who prefers saying "bah, humbug!" to "happy holidays."
