The Hidden Toll of Caring for a Chronically Ill Pet

Pet caregiver burden is the negative physical, psychological, social, and financial toll experienced by owners caring for chronically or seriously ill companion animals. The term is adapted directly from human caregiving literature, where it is well established, and it now has validated measurement scales specifically designed for pet owners.

Research has consistently shown that owners of chronically ill pets carry a burden equivalent in psychological impact to those caring for ill human family members (Frenzel et al., 2026). Yet the experience remains largely unrecognised by the people around them.

“For many of us, our animals are our children,” says Courtney Wennerstrom, a Certified Pet Loss Grief Specialist and Companion Animal End-of-Life Doula, and the host and creator of The Hardest Goodbye, a podcast on grief, healing, and the animals who change us. “We feel an enormous responsibility to do everything in our power to protect them and keep them comfortable. But that devotion can come at the expense of our own well-being. We sacrifice sleep, exercise, social connections, and sometimes even our own medical needs because caring for them takes precedence over everything else.”

Jordan Davis, founder of the Canine Stroke Foundation, cared for her Border Collie, Finley, through three strokes, physical therapy, and a cancer diagnosis across the final 14 months of his life. “In his last couple of weeks, his back legs would stop working for a few minutes during potty breaks,” she says. “I supported the back half of his body weight. My back and arms hurt, but I would never have let him fall.”

Caring for a chronically ill pet affects mental health in ways that mirror the human caregiving experience: chronic stress, sleep disruption, financial strain, social withdrawal, and a specific form of anticipatory grief that begins long before any loss has occurred.

A study published in June 2026 in Frontiers in Veterinary Science measured caregiver burden in owners of dogs with chronic neurological conditions and compared them directly to owners of healthy dogs (Frenzel et al., 2026). Caregiver burden was higher among owners of neurologically impaired dogs. What was especially notable was the effect of the social dimension: Owners who received positive feedback about their dog’s condition reported lower burden scores. Validation was not just emotionally supportive, but clinically protective.

A 2025 study of 347 guardians of senior dogs contributed a finding that challenges our assumptions about what drives this burden (Mellor et al., 2025). Anticipatory grief was strongly associated with caregiver burden and emotional closeness to the dog, but it was not associated with the severity of the dog’s condition.

Wennerstrom describes the emotional texture of this period with precision. “Anticipatory bereavement asks us to do two seemingly contradictory things at once,” she says. “Remain fully present with our animals and cherish the time we have left, while simultaneously trying to prepare ourselves for a future without them. We’re navigating love, hope, fear, uncertainty, and grief, sometimes all in the same moment.”

There is also, she adds, the pressure of suddenly becoming a nurse without any medical training: “Caregivers find themselves managing complex medication schedules, dressing wounds, monitoring symptoms, and remaining constantly vigilant for signs of a medical emergency. They’re also making difficult medical decisions, often terrified of making a mistake that could cause additional suffering.”

The term for grief that society does not generally recognise or validate is disenfranchised grief. It describes losses that fall outside what the world treats as legitimate mourning: the end of a relationship that was never acknowledged, the grief of a miscarriage, or the mourning of an estranged parent are just a few examples.

The anticipatory grief and caregiver burden that precede the death of a pet falls in this category for most people.

A 2025 qualitative study of 31 animal caregivers found three consistent themes (Cameron, 2025): the psychological toll of having their grief dismissed, the work of finding meaning during and after illness, and the healing that came from social recognition. The third theme was not incidental. People described being believed as restorative. Being told that what they were going through was real changed how they were able to cope.

Wennerstrom sees a pattern emerge among the people she supports: a gradual narrowing of the world. “As our animals become increasingly dependent on us, we stop making plans, decline invitations, and withdraw from activities we once enjoyed because we’re afraid to leave them alone,” she says. “Over time, that isolation can become profoundly debilitating.”

Davis experienced this directly. “Others said he was old and had lived a good life, as if that meant I should be ready to let go,” she says. “They saw his wobbly walk and assumed he was in terrible pain. He wasn’t. I wish people had commented on how well he was doing: that he had overcome so many obstacles and was still full of joy.”

What helped, she says, was someone finally putting a name to what she was going through: “Being told about caregiver burden made me feel less alone, and less like I was making too big a deal of everything.”

Davis's dog, Finley, suffered his first stroke in August 2025, and was subsequently misdiagnosed. When she researched what resources existed for owners in her situation, she found the field lacking and veterinary professionals often underprepared to support the psychological experience of caregivers. Finley passed away in October 2026, at age 14.

Davis describes his last year as structured entirely around his needs: stretching, massage, and balance work woven through every day, two rounds of medications, and short walks in which he led and she steadied him if he stumbled. “Emotionally, it started after Finley’s first stroke,” she says. “I worried constantly that he would have another and might not survive, and anticipatory grief made it heavier.” After his second stroke, the anxiety became physical. “My heart raced constantly, and any time I had to leave the house I was trying to hurry back to him.”

She founded the Canine Stroke Foundation as a response to that gap, and as a tribute. “The foundation exists because of him,” she says. “He was my inspiration. Because he was resilient and kept going, I knew I could keep going too.”

The same research that documents the burden identifies what reduces it. Social validation from people in the caregiver’s life is the most consistently protective factor across studies. Positive acknowledgement from veterinary teams, addressing the caregiver’s experience alongside the animal's condition, lowers burden scores.

Wennerstrom encourages people to lean on community actively rather than allowing their world to contract. “If leaving your animal alone feels impossible, invite friends over for dinner, watch a movie together, or organize a game night,” she says. “We need opportunities to laugh, experience joy, and remember that our lives still exist beyond caregiving.”

She also advocates for caregivers to maintain their hobbies wherever possible. “Our psyches can only absorb so much emotional pain at once, and we need moments when we can quiet our minds and simply exist outside of our grief. Even 20 minutes spent doing something we enjoy can help us recharge.”

Her most important recommendation concerns end-of-life decisions. Wennerstrom urges families to assess not only the animal’s quality of life but their own. “When an animal is seriously ill and the demands of caregiving have become unsustainable despite efforts to find support, that reality may also factor into the difficult decision about whether euthanasia is the most compassionate path forward.”

Davis tracked Finley’s quality of life using dedicated tools, counting good days, good days with difficult moments, and harder days. When the balance shifted, she made the call. His regular vet came to their home. “He was on his bed with his head in my lap, listening to his song,” she says. “He was at total ease, and it was peaceful.”
