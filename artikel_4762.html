You’re Making Big Decisions for a Person You’ve Never Met

Posted November 10, 2026 | Reviewed by Gary Drevitch

The life I had at 22, along with the one I pictured ahead of me, no longer exists.

I was living in New York City, auditioning weekly for commercial dance roles and cruise-line troupes while pursuing my degree in creative writing. I’d spent years learning how to pick up choreography in 15 minutes and write a poem that holds a feeling it never names. If you had told that person she would end up doing neuropsychological evaluations in a Boston hospital, she would not have believed you, because she could not have pictured it.

She knew who she was at that time, but she was simultaneously wrong about what her future would look like.

Psychologists have a name for this failure of vision: the end-of-history illusion. It's the tendency to recognize how much you have changed while predicting that the changing is now largely over. In six studies with more than 19,000 people aged 18 to 68, participants at every age reported substantial change over the previous decade and yet forecast far less for the decade ahead. A 30-year-old looks back at 20 and sees a different person. She looks forward to 40 and sees her present self, slightly adjusted.

The pattern held for personality traits, core values, and everyday preferences. It held for the 18-year-olds and for the 68-year-olds. Everyone, apparently, believes they have just now arrived at the finished version.

The lease is 30 years long, and you are signing it on behalf of someone you have never met. That is the practical problem, as the decisions that bind hardest tend to cluster in your late twenties and thirties: the mortgage, the specialty, the city, the degree, the name, the person. You make them with full information about your current preferences and almost no imagination for your future ones.

The more unsettling finding is who we think we are predicting for.

Emily Pronin and Lee Ross found that when people describe their future selves, they reach for the same explanatory style they use for strangers. We explain our current behavior by pointing to circumstances: the week was brutal, the request was unreasonable. We explain other people’s behavior by pointing to their character. And we explain our future selves’ behavior the way we explain others: from the outside.

A 2024 study found the end-of-history pattern in children as young as four, across two experiments with 256 participants, including a design in which the same child was asked about both past and future. A third experiment asked children about other people. In that study, the illusion disappeared. Children can imagine that someone else will change, but can’t see their own change coming.

What we learned from this study is that the future version of you gets handled by the part of your mind that imagines people you have not met.

There is a whole literature on closing the gap, built around what researchers call future self-continuity, or how connected you feel to the person you will become. The interventions are inventive: writing a letter to yourself 20 years out, viewing an age-progressed photograph. More recently, a team at MIT built a system that lets you hold a conversation with an AI-generated version of yourself at 60; in a preregistered study of 344 young adults, a single session lowered reported anxiety and raised a sense of connection to the future self.

However, in late 2025, a team went looking for studies that had tested these techniques. They screened more than 1,200 papers and found 14. Fewer than half of those bothered to check whether the exercise had actually changed how connected participants felt to their future selves, which was the whole premise. And nearly all the participants were college students in their twenties. Not one study asked whether this works differently at 40.

In other words, the technique that makes you feel closer to your future self has not been shown to make you better at predicting it.

I don’t think you need to successfully forecast the person you will be in 2036, and the research gives no reason to think you could.

The goal is to have humility about the forecast you are making. When you make a decision that binds, beyond choosing between options, you’re making a prediction that the person living with the consequences will want what you want today. The research has shown that prediction is almost certainly too confident.

Decisions differ in what they cost if you change. A lease and a mortgage both bind you, but one releases you in a year. A degree and a license both take years, but only one of them travels. That difference is a property of the decision rather than of the person making it.

So extend some grace backward. The decisions you now find baffling were made by someone operating with the same blind spot you have right now, and they were doing their best with a self they could not see past.

My creative-writing major self was not wrong. She just could not have known that learning to read a line for what it does not say would turn out to be useful in a room where someone is describing their memory loss and leaving out the part that frightens them.
