Jensen Huang has argued execs need less supervision. Then a scandal made him step down as Nvidia CEO

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

The spectacular downfall of superstar Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang—who resigned this morning after admitting to an extramarital affair with an alleged Chinese spy and amid reports of another affair with one of his executives—casts a harsh light on a key tenet of Huang’s own management philosophy: that senior executives need little supervision.

Huang often used this belief to justify Nvidia’s unique management structure, in which 50 to 60 senior executives reported directly to him. He argued that senior leaders already have a proven ability to make decisions, manage people, and solve problems on their own.

“CEOs should have the most reports by definition, because the people that report to the CEO require the least amount of management,” he said during an appearance at Stanford Graduate School of Business in 2024. “It makes no sense to me that CEOs have so few people reporting to them.”

The approach, which Huang often reiterated in public appearances, reflects a broader Silicon Valley ideal: hire extraordinary people and give them free rein to do their best work. A classic example is Google’s 20%-time policy, which allowed employees to spend a portion of their time exploring their own ideas and innovations.  

Huang’s abrupt exit shows the drawbacks of this philosophy—at least when it comes to the behavior of top executives. While experienced leaders may not require micromanagement, allowing them to operate with very little oversight exposes organizations to major risks.

As Huang’s actions have illustrated, even the most capable executives can show extremely poor personal judgment. This may be especially true in corporate cultures like Nvidia’s, where the line between the personal and the professional is often blurred. 

Over the years, reports have described Nvidia as an intensely personal workplace in which Huang regularly interrogated junior employees about their work, sent emails late into the night, and exerted enormous influence over the company’s culture.

Senior leaders also have fewer people who are willing to rein in their behavior—something that was clearly the case at Nvidia. Last spring, proxy advisory firm Institutional Shareholder Services assigned its riskiest rating to Nvidia’s board structure. According to The Wall Street Journal, ISS cited the board’s multiple long-tenured directors, its lack of a chairman, the absence of a mechanism to refresh the board, and other factors.

Finally, the consequences of misconduct increase with seniority. In Huang’s case, the fallout could extend even beyond Nvidia, which manufactures chips crucial to the AI boom and has been central to American leadership in the area.

Management theorists have long struggled to balance meaningful oversight with executives’ autonomy, and some have concluded that no one-size-fits-all approach ensures proper board oversight. Boards must tailor oversight to individual CEOs and circumstances rather than mechanically apply standardized rules.

In Nvidia’s case, at least, the board of directors failed to properly assess the risks of Huang’s personal behavior or plan adequately for succession. And the lesson for others is clear: While senior executives don’t need micromanagement, they still need someone to answer to.
