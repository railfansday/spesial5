Smoke flavorings succumb to EU grilling

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

A food ingredients supplier challenged how regulators assessed its products, but judges found the studies provided did not resolve concerns about DNA damage.

Smoke flavorings deliver taste without the smoker. Made from processed wood smoke, they give foods such as cheese, chips and sauces a smoky flavor, including foods that would never traditionally be smoked.

But keeping those products on the market meant showing they were safe, the court held. The responsibility rested with Kerry; regulators did not have to prove that consumers had already been harmed.

The European Food Safety Authority had identified two ingredients shown in oral-exposure tests to damage DNA, the genetic material inside cells. Years on the market did not guarantee another approval. EU permissions lasted 10 years, and renewal required safety evidence updated to reflect scientific advances. Kerry applied in June 2022 and supplied more data the following February.

When the authority reassessed eight flavorings in 2023, it identified DNA damage concerns in six and lacked enough information to rule out concerns for the other two. The commission refused renewals in July 2024, giving businesses transition periods to adapt recipes and production.

Natacha Cingotti, campaign strategy lead working on consumer protection at foodwatch international, said the organization had welcomed that precautionary approach. She wanted similar caution applied more often when approving flavorings and additives, arguing that too many substances raising health concerns are still allowed in food.

Kerry also argues restrictions could push manufacturers back toward potentially riskier traditional smoking. The court found the companies had not demonstrated such a shift would happen. Even if it did, that would not justify approving flavorings whose safety had not been established. Transition periods addressed the economic consequences.

She stressed that considering a company’s evidence did not mean accepting its conclusions: The court’s acceptance of EFSA’s assumption that DNA-damaging substances have no safe exposure threshold made scientific uncertainty especially important, while its rejection of Kerry’s alternative method showed how difficult it is to challenge established science without a validated approach.
