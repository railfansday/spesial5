Court to hear argument on whether school employees may bring suit under Title IX

The federal law known as Title IX prohibits sex-based discrimination by schools that receive federal funding. On November 30, the Supreme Court will hear oral argument in a dispute over whether the law allows school employees to bring lawsuits alleging sex discrimination, or whether such employees must instead rely on a different federal law, Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, that specifically provides a remedy for sex-based employment discrimination but also imposes constraints not found in Title IX. The employees seeking to bring the lawsuits under Title IX will face a bench that has become increasingly reluctant to permit private plaintiffs to bring lawsuits unless the federal statute at issue clearly authorizes them.

The plaintiffs in the case are two former employees of Georgia’s state university system. Thomas Crowther worked as an art professor at Augusta University. When he was accused of inappropriate conduct by several students, including sexual harassment, the university initially suspended him for one semester and then declined to renew his contract, prompting Crowther to file a lawsuit against the university for sex discrimination and retaliation.

Machelle Joseph was the head women’s basketball coach at Georgia Tech. She was fired in 2019 after an investigation raised concerns about mistreatment of players. Before her termination, Joseph had repeatedly complained about her team receiving fewer resources than the men’s team. Joseph filed a lawsuit against the university for sex discrimination and retaliation.

The U.S. Court of Appeals for the 11th Circuit considered Crowther’s and Joseph’s cases together. It held that Title IX does not provide a right – either explicitly or by implication – for employees to sue for sex discrimination. When the full court declined to reconsider their case, Crowther and Joseph asked the Supreme Court to weigh in, which it agreed in May to do.

Crowther and Joseph urge the justices to reverse the 11th Circuit’s decision and allow their lawsuits to go forward. They point to the text of Title IX, which provides that “[n]o person … shall, on the basis of sex, be excluded from participation in, be denied the benefits of, or be subjected to discrimination under any education program or activity receiving Federal financial assistance.” That language, they say, creates a broad ban on sex discrimination by schools that receive federal funding; moreover, they add, Title IX “also contains ‘a list of narrow exceptions’ that does not include employment discrimination” – further evidence that Congress intended Title IX’s ban on sex discrimination to apply to both employees and students.

The Georgia university system agrees that Title IX bars sex discrimination in employment, “and that federal agencies may deny funding to violators.” But that is not the question before the court, it stresses. Instead, it emphasizes, the issue is whether Title IX allows employees to bring private lawsuits alleging that they were the victims of sex-based discrimination. The answer, the university system says, is no.

To support their position, Crowther and Joseph cite several past Supreme Court rulings, including its 1982 decision in North Haven Board of Education v. Bell. In Bell, the court upheld regulations, meant to implement Title IX, that barred sex-based discrimination against employees (as well as students) at federally funded educational institutions. That decision, they say, demonstrates that Title IX prohibits sex-based discrimination in employment. “The Court’s cases, read together,” they continue, “lead to the inexorable conclusion that an employee who is a victim of intentional sex-based discrimination by a federally funded education program or activity has a private right of action to seek damages under Title IX.”

Crowther and Joseph add that the Supreme Court has allowed employment discrimination lawsuits under other federal antidiscrimination statutes that apply to the recipients of federal funds – Title VI of Civil Rights Act of 1964, which prohibits discrimination based on race, color, or national origin, and the Rehabilitation Act of 1973, which prohibits discrimination based on disability. Those laws, they write, contain “virtually identical” language – further evidence that Title IX allows lawsuits like theirs.

The university pushes back against the employees’ attempt to draw comparisons with other federal antidiscrimination laws. It writes that Supreme Court “has assumed,” but has never specifically held, that “Title VI permits private suits for the narrow category of employment discrimination that statute prohibits.” But in any event, it says, that assumption does not indicate anything about whether Congress created a private remedy in Title IX. The university system also emphasizes that “[t]he Rehabilitation Act has always been a far more detailed and targeted statute than Title IX” – for example, it “expressly ban[s] disability discrimination by the federal government as [an] employer.”

Similarly, the university system continues, the Supreme Court’s decision in North Haven Board of Education v. Bell does not resolve the question before the justices now. The court in that case, it writes, “had no reason to, and did not, consider whether Congress created a private remedy atop the express one the challenged regulations implemented.”

Whether the availability of another remedy forecloses private lawsuits under Title IX

The Supreme Court, the university system writes, has made clear that the right to bring a private lawsuit hinges on whether there is “evidence confirming that Congress sought to” provide such a right. And when Congress has created “an express remedy” in another statute, there is “a strong ‘presumption’ that Congress did not imply others” – particularly when that “express remedy” involves a “‘comprehensive’ remedial scheme.”

That is exactly what happened here, the university system argues. “Mere weeks before Congress passed Title IX,” the university contends, “it extended … [the] protection—and reticulated enforcement scheme” in Title VII to school employees, which bars employment discrimination based on (among other things) sex. Therefore, the university system says, allowing lawsuits like Crowther’s and Joseph’s to go forward under Title IX “would attribute to the same Congress an inexplicable about-face: Immediately after giving a set of employees a private employment-discrimination remedy mediated by a ‘detailed administrative and judicial process,’ it silently gave the same employees a second private right of action, to attack the same basic harm.” And it did so, the university system suggests, without requiring employees to comply with any of the “strings attached” to private lawsuits under Title VII – including, but not limited to, the requirement to first file a complaint with the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission before going to court and its limits on the damages that can be recovered.

Crowther and Joseph counter that “Title IX and Title VII are ‘vastly different.’” The prospect “that an employee might be able to bring a claim under both Title IX and Title VII does not justify” a determination that a private right of action is not available under Title IX. Rather, they write, the Supreme Court “has recognized that Congress has enacted overlapping remedies to eradicate employment discrimination, and Congress chose not to exclude employment discrimination from Title IX’s broad reach.”

As additional support for the idea that Title IX does not provide a private right of action for employees, the university system notes that the law was enacted pursuant to Congress’ power under the Constitution’s Spending Clause, which gives Congress the power to impose taxes and spend money for (among other things) the country’s “general welfare.” In exchange for the federal funds, the recipients therefore agree to comply with the conditions that Congress imposes, and Congress can terminate the funding if a recipient fails to do so. However, in cases such as last term’s Landor v. Louisiana Department of Corrections and Public Safety, the Supreme Court has held that additional sanctions for violating the conditions in a law, such as the ability to bring a private lawsuit, are only available if the recipient has knowingly and voluntarily agreed to those sanctions. Here, the university system asserts, “Title IX does not unambiguously authorize private employment-discrimination suits,” and so it could not have known that it could be sued by employees, much less have agreed to that possibility.

Crowther and Joseph reject this argument. They say that recipients of Title IX funds “‘have been on notice that they could be subjected to private suits for intentional sex discrimination under Title IX since 1979.’” As a result, they write, the Supreme Court’s “Spending Clause precedents ‘do[] not preclude private suits for intentional acts that clearly violate Title IX.’”

A “friend of the court” brief from West Virginia and 20 other states attempts to refute this assertion. The states tell the justices that the states “are best positioned to say what Title IX’s bargain contains, as they’re the ones who’ve accepted it.” And they “can say with firsthand authority that the deal they made did not include this,” they write. “To the contrary, all the circumstances suggested to the States that Title VII [procedures, not private lawsuits] was the rightful home for those cases.”

The Trump administration, which filed a “friend of the court” brief supporting the university system, urges the justices not to reach the Spending Clause question at all. Instead, U.S. Solicitor General D. John Sauer tells the justices, they “should decide this case on the ground that” the employees’ “claims fail as a matter of ‘statutory interpretation’ alone.” Therefore, he writes, it is “unnecessary to reach, and prudent to avoid, the parties’ further dispute over the constitutional notice requirement for legislation enacted under Congress’s spending power.”

The possible effects of the court’s decision

Both the Trump administration and lawyers representing public school districts warn that a ruling in the employees’ favor could have “significant adverse consequences.” The federal government suggests that allowing lawsuits like the employees’ to go forward “would crowd judicial dockets with premature lawsuits that could often be avoided through proper use of Title VII’s administrative process, which successfully resolves numerous cases. And it would undermine the government’s primary authority to enforce both Title VII … and Title IX.”

For its part, the National School Attorneys Association emphasizes that “public school districts typically operate with limited legal and administrative staff. Expanding Title IX’s implied private right of action to cover claims against employees,” it says, “would impose disproportionate compliance and litigation burdens on public school districts and diminish the limited resources they have to educate students.”

But a brief from the National Education Association, supporting the employees, takes a contrary stance. It tells the justices that upholding the 11th Circuit’s decision “would remove a critical deterrent against sex-based mistreatment of educators, exacerbate an already dire workforce crisis, and ultimately compromise the quality of education available to millions of students nationwide.” Lawsuits under Title VII, it writes, are not enough because that law “does not offer the full protections to which employees of education programs are entitled.”

A decision in the case is expected by late June or early July.

Amy Howe is the co-founder of SCOTUSblog and its primary reporter.
