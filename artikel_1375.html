A major goal in neuroscience is to map the transformation of information as it flows through the brain. For example, neurons at many levels of the visual hierarchy are attuned to motion direction1,2,3,4,5. However, links between motion-selective computations and cell types at various stages of the visual hierarchy remain unidentified. A key first step in delineating these relationships is to map the wiring diagrams that connect different motion-selective cell types. Here we establish a systematic, cell-type-specific circuit-mapping method, combining an anterograde trans-neuronal fluorescent tracer (mWGA–mCherry)6 with spatial transcriptomics (multiplexed error-robust fluorescence in situ hybridization (MERFISH))7,8 to establish TransA-MERFISH. We used TransA-MERFISH to comprehensively map the synaptic target cell types of four retinal ganglion cell subclasses in superior colliculus (SC) at cell-type resolution. These experiments unexpectedly revealed that retinal on–off direction-selective ganglion cells (ooDSGCs) innervate several direction-selective SC cell types, but do not innervate a SC cell type that was previously reported to display strong direction-selective responses. We validated these results using orthogonal approaches and demonstrated the sensitivity and specificity of TransA-MERFISH. Functional studies of SC activity before and after ablation of retinal direction-selective circuits confirmed that these computationally distinct, ooDSGC-dependent and -independent direction-selective cell types are commingled in SC. Together, our study establishes TransA-MERFISH as a robust framework for mapping wiring diagrams at the cell-type level and uncovers divergent computational mechanisms of motion detection in early visual processing.

A central objective in neuroscience is to characterize the computational transformations that occur across successive nodes of neural circuits. In the visual system, this requires defining the mechanisms by which retinal signals are integrated and processed as they ascend to higher brain centres. Among these, motion signals are particularly salient, providing the sensory substrate for critical behaviours such as predatory pursuit, escape and spatial navigation9,10. Over the past half-century, neurons in various brain areas have been found to respond to visual motion. A particularly common form of motion sensitivity is a preference for stimuli moving in a certain direction, a property known as direction selectivity that is observed in the retina, SC and cortex1,2,3,4,5. However, relationships between direction-selective cell types in different brain areas are unknown, leaving equally unclear how direction-selective activity is used and transformed across different stages of the visual hierarchy. A key obstacle has been the lack of a wiring diagram to guide understanding of information flow between the tens of different retinal ganglion cell (RGC) types and similarly diverse neuronal cell types in target areas such as SC. Here, we describe the development of a technological platform that enabled us to address these knowledge gaps.

Efforts to map neural circuits have yielded a diverse array of methodologies, yet existing ‘gold-standard’ approaches face considerable scalability and molecular identification constraints. Although electron microscopy provides high-resolution reconstructions11,12,13,14, it remains incompatible with the molecular profiling of pre- and postsynaptic partners and is currently impractical for brain-wide mammalian applications. Similarly, physiological techniques such as channelrhodopsin-assisted circuit mapping (CRACM)12 and Patch-seq15,16 provide precise functional data but are limited by low throughput. Recent sequencing-based strategies (such as Retro-Seq, Project-Seq, VECTOR-Seq, MAPseq and BARseq)17,18,19,20,21 have advanced the field by using RNA barcodes to define projection targets of starter neurons at high throughput, but these strategies trace axonal projection targets in the brain, and do not identify their synaptic target cell types within those areas. Additionally, these methods often rely on tissue microdissection, thereby losing anatomical context. These limitations underscore the need for next-generation, readily adoptable methods for comprehensive connectivity mapping at cell-type resolution.

To fill this gap, with a particular focus on comprehensive mapping of synaptic target cell types, we integrated a genetically encoded anterograde fluorescent trans-neuronal tracer, mWGA–mCherry (mWmC)6, (Fig. 1a) with the spatial transcriptomics technology, MERFISH7,8 (Fig. 1b). This combined platform, which we named TransA-MERFISH, required two major technical advances. First, we established an experimental workflow that enables the combination of anterograde trans-neuronal tracer immunostaining and MERFISH-based spatial transcriptomics. Second, we developed an automated data processing pipeline to align connectivity and transcriptomic data—that is, to overlay fluorescent signals from anterograde tracers on the spatially resolved neural identity data derived from multiplexed RNA signals in MERFISH (Fig. 1c). We then applied TransA-MERFISH to investigate the aforementioned half-century-old unresolved problem in visual neuroscience: the relationship between direction-selective neuron types and computations at different stages of the visual hierarchy. We used TransA-MERFISH to establish a comprehensive, cell-type-resolution wiring diagram downstream of ooDSGCs, a well-studied RGC subclass attuned to stimuli moving in one of the four cardinal directions: nasal, temporal, dorsal or ventral22,23. Using Cre transgenic mice, we mapped the SC synaptic target cell types of ooDSGCs that prefer particular directions24,25,26. We unexpectedly found that although ooDSGCs strongly innervate several SC cell types, they do not innervate the cell type that a previous study27 claimed was most direction-selective in SC. These surprising results were validated using multiple genetic, anatomical and electrophysiological tests. Subsequent in vivo imaging studies of SC activity before and after ablation of retinal direction-selective circuits confirmed that computationally distinct ooDSGC-dependent and ooDSGC-independent direction-selective cell types are commingled in SC. Single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq)-based methods have laid the groundwork for understanding neuronal taxonomy, including scRNA-seq studies covering the entire SC region28,29. However, these methods require tissue dissociation and microfluidics-based single-cell isolation, which reduce spatial resolution, the completeness of cell-type identification, and scalability for large-scale mapping. We therefore developed a preparation to combine anterograde trans-neuronal tracing with spatial transcriptomics (MERFISH). Because the established MERFISH protocol uses fresh-frozen tissue to maximize RNA preservation30,31, it is incompatible with immunostaining for protein markers such as the anterograde trans-neuronal tracer mWmC. Therefore, we developed a protocol to perform MERFISH on fixed tissue. To benchmark this protocol, we sought to create a reference atlas of SC neurons in the retino-recipient superficial SC (sSC) (Fig. 1c). We referred to existing scRNA-seq data6,19,28 to select 140 neuron-type marker genes to classify retino-recipient neurons in the superficial SC (sSC) (Fig. 1b, Extended Data Figs. 1e,f and 2f,i and Methods). We developed a perfusion–fixation MERFISH protocol that ensured that RNA integrity and neuronal morphology were not compromised compared with fresh-frozen tissue, while preserving protein localization (Extended Data Fig. 1a–d and Methods). We then performed MERFISH on sSC, followed by Cellpose-based segmentation (as in past MERFISH studies in the mouse brain31), which covered all sSC neurons (Fig. 1h,i and Methods). This pipeline classified sSC neurons into 8 excitatory cell types (ENC1–8, marked by Slc17a6) and 11 inhibitory cell types (INC1–11, marked by Gad1) (Fig. 1d–g and Extended Data Fig. 1g–j). Data quality for SC cell classification from the modified TransA-MERFISH protocol (Extended Data Fig. 3a–e) closely matched that of several published scRNA-seq datasets19,27,28 (Extended Data Fig. 2c,e–j), as well as the cell types and supertypes reported in the Allen Brain Atlas dataset30 (Extended Data Fig. 2a,b). Together, these data validate the feasibility of performing MERFISH in fixed tissue.

Analysis of spatial distributions found that each ENC cluster is restricted to a specific sublamina along the dorsal–ventral axis (Fig. 1f and Extended Data Fig. 4f), consistent with past observations in rodents and zebrafish6,32,33,34. INC1, INC2, INC5 and INC6 are also restricted to specific sublaminae along the dorsal–ventral axis, whereas INC3, INC4 and INC7–11 are spread across multiple sublaminae (Fig. 1f and Extended Data Fig. 4g). In addition, we observed a consistent distribution of all ENCs and INCs along the anterior–posterior and medial–lateral axes, which largely follows the sublaminar arrangement, without layer swapping (Extended Data Fig. 4a–e). Collectively, the MERFISH data map the sublaminar distribution of sSC neurons along the dorsal–ventral axis at single-cell-type resolution. Each cluster is further compared and quantified in Extended Data Table 1.

Having established a MERFISH protocol compatible with immunostaining and created an atlas of sSC cell types, we next sought to overlay cell connectivity information onto it. To do so, adeno-associated virus (AAV) encoding mWmC was delivered to the left eye to yield nearly complete coverage across all RGC types (Fig. 1a and Extended Data Fig. 6a). mWmC protein was transferred anterogradely and monosynaptically to the synaptic targets of RGCs in the contralateral (right) sSC, which receives innervation from around 90% of RGCs35. We then performed MERFISH on fixed tissue followed by anti-RFP immunostaining on the same microscope to facilitate registration with the MERFISH data. As a result, mWmC signals identified by anti-RFP immunostaining could be clearly registered with the RNA-based Cellpose segmentation masks (Fig. 1i,j and Methods). All mWmC+ neurons in the SC downstream of a given RGC starter neuron could be captured by imaging approaches and molecularly identified using MERFISH. Based on co-registered mWmC signals and MERFISH transcripts, we established a computational pipeline to assign mWmC signals to segmented neurons at single-cell resolution, followed by manual proofreading (Fig. 1i,j and Methods). The optimized workflow enabled high-throughput registration. This computational pipeline determines the percentages of mWmC+ neurons within each neuronal cluster, providing a quantitative measure of connectivity at cell-type resolution (Fig. 1k and Methods). We termed this integrated workflow TransA-MERFISH because it combines anterograde trans-neuronal tracing with MERFISH-based cell classification to read out connectivity.

Using TransA-MERFISH, we examined the synaptic target cell types of all RGCs. Roughly half (49 ± 3%, mean ± s.e.m.) of sSC neurons had a detectable mWmC signal (Fig. 1k). Across sSC neuron types defined by MERFISH, 5 out of 8 ENCs and 6 out of 11 INCs were classified as retino-recipient neurons based on mWmC signals above baseline, including a cell type that we recently showed to be retino-recipient (Npnt+)6 (Figs. 1k and 2a,c and Extended Data Fig. 3f–h). Notably, multiple ENCs and INCs in the sSC did not receive mWmC and were therefore not categorized as recipient neurons of RGCs, including a cell type that was previously6 characterized as not retino-recipient (Etv1+) (Figs. 1k and 2c,o). These data thus suggest TransA-MERFISH possesses the sensitivity and specificity to map synaptic connectivity at scale.

The spatially resolved map of all retino-recipient neurons in SC (Fig. 2b) generated with TransA-MERFISh enabled direct comparison of connectivity across cell types within shared sublaminar contexts. Notably, several SC neuron pairs exhibited divergent RGC connectivity despite occupying similar sublaminar distributions (Figs. 1f and 2a,b,g–o). For example, in the most superficial SC, ENC1 and ENC2 neurons were retino-recipient, whereas neighbouring INC1 neurons were not; similarly, in slightly deeper layers, ENC5 and INC6 neurons received retinal input, whereas neighbouring ENC6 neurons did not. These observations suggested that sublaminar position alone is insufficient to explain synaptic targeting by RGC axons. To test whether molecular cell-type identity contributes to synaptic specificity beyond sublaminar organization, we implemented a permutation-based framework in which cell-type labels were randomly shuffled within depth bins along the dorsal–ventral axis (Fig. 2d and Methods). This procedure preserves the sublaminar distribution of mWmC labelling while disrupting the association between molecular identity and connectivity. Cell-type specificity was quantified by comparing observed mWmC enrichment or depletion for each cell type against the permutation-derived null distribution. Using this approach, we identified multiple retino-recipient clusters, including ENC1, ENC2 and ENC5, with significant enrichment of mWmC labelling after accounting for sublaminar positions (Fig. 2e). By contrast, multiple non-retino-recipient clusters, including INC1 and ENC6, characterized here, showed significant depletion relative to sublaminar expectations (Fig. 2e,p). Together, these results indicate that RGC axons innervate SC neurons in a cell-type-specific way rather than simply according to their sublaminar distributions; that is, molecular identity, rather than sublaminar distribution, determines wiring specificity from the retina to SC.

We validated the sensitivity and specificity of TransA-MERFISH using CRACM. We first examined ENC5, which corresponds to genetically labelled Npnt+ wide-field neurons6 (Extended Data Fig. 5i and Extended Data Table 1). ENC5 was known to receive strong RGC input: 54 ± 6% (mean ± s.e.m.) of Npnt+ neurons contained mWmC labelling (Fig. 1j,k). CRACM quantitatively validated the TransA-MERFISH prediction, with a comparable percentage (52 ± 2%, mean ± s.e.m.) of Npnt+ wide-field neurons innervated by RGCs (Extended Data Fig. 5a,b,d). By contrast, ENC6 neurons, marked by Etv1, were in the same sublamina as ENC5 but did not receive direct retinal input in the TransA-MERFISH data (Fig. 1k). Consistently, these neurons did not receive RGC input in CRACM experiments (Extended Data Fig. 5a,c,d). These results validated the finding that ENC5 neurons receive input from the retina, whereas ENC6 neurons are not innervated by RGCs (Extended Data Fig. 5e–h). Together, these data support the notion that TransA-MERFISH provides a high-throughput platform for quantitatively defining neuronal synaptic target cell types at cell-type resolution.

To demonstrate the power of TransA-MERFISH to enable the discovery of synaptic target cell types of genetically defined starter cells, we decoded synaptic target cell types in SC of four major RGC subclasses that detect distinct visual features36: αRGCs (Kcng4-cre); ooDSGCs (Cartpt-cre); F-RGCs (Foxp2-cre); and T5-RGCs (Tusc5-cre)24,37,38,39,40 (Fig. 2f and Extended Data Fig. 6). These parallel TransA-MERFISH experiments yielded a comprehensive connectivity matrix at cell-type resolution. We observed highly distinct synaptic target cell-type compositions for different RGC subclasses (Fig. 2g–n). For example, ENC5 (Npnt+) neurons receive input from αRGCs, but not from ooDSGCs, consistent with our CRACM and trans-neuronal tracing results, and ENC6 (Etv1+) neurons receive no input from any of the four RGC subclasses (Figs. 1k, 2f–n,p and Extended Data Fig. 7). In addition, because different RGC subclasses arborize in distinct sSC sublaminae, we sorted the synaptic target cell types by their sublaminar distributions and connectivity frequencies (Fig. 2b,h,j,l,n and Extended Data Fig. 7). We found that retino-recipient neurons largely share the sublaminar distributions of the axonal arbors from that RGC subclass. For example, robust connectivity was reported from αRGCs to ENC4 and ENC5 neurons in lower stratum griseum superficiale (SGS) and stratum opticum (SO) (Fig. 2g,h and Extended Data Fig. 7b), with αRGC axons primarily ramifying in the same sublaminae24,26,40,41 (Extended Data Fig. 6i). By contrast, ooDSGCs, F-RGCs and T5-RGCs primarily innervated superficial ENC1 and ENC2 (Fig. 2i–n and Extended Data Fig. 7b–f), correlated with their axons primarily innervating the upper SGS (Extended Data Fig. 6i). Collectively, the TransA-MERFISH data provided a detailed neuronal connectivity map from the retina to SC at the level of cell types, offering functional insights into parallel retinotectal circuit organization and further validating the sensitivity and specificity of this tool for mapping comprehensive connectomes in a high-throughput manner.

A notable finding emerged from analysis of the synaptic target cell types of Cartpt-cre-labelled RGCs, which are ooDSGCs that are reported to prefer upward (dorsal) or downward (ventral) motion24,42. Cartpt+ ooDSGCs innervated 3 ENC clusters (ENC1, ENC2 and ENC4) as well as 4 INC clusters (INC3, INC4, INC5 and INC9) (Figs. 2i,j and 3c). The strongest targets of ooDSGCs, ENC1 and ENC2, are excitatory clusters marked by Cdh7 (Figs. 1g and 3a–d and Extended Data Fig. 2d). They are in the most superficial 100 µm of SGS, commingled with INC1 neurons, which are marked by Cbln4 (Fig. 1g) and are negative for mWmC labelling (Fig. 3c,d). Notably, the permutation test on TransA-MERFISH data (Fig. 2d,e) also showed that ENC1 and ENC2 have the highest synaptic enrichment for innervation from the retina, whereas INC1 neurons have the lowest (Fig. 2e), emphasizing the importance of cell-type-specific molecular properties among these neuron clusters in recruiting ooDSGC input.

The lack of mWmC labelling from ooDSGCs in INC1 was unexpected, because a recent study combining calcium imaging with post hoc in situ hybridization found that Cbln4+ SC neurons were strongly direction-selective31. Moreover, other studies have suggested that SC direction selectivity depends on ooDSGCs43,44. Together, these results appear to predict that the strongly direction-selective Cbln4+ SC neurons would be innervated by ooDSGCs. Therefore, to verify these findings, we generated the FlpO knock-in lines Cdh7-FlpO (Fig. 3e–h, Extended Data Fig. 5j,k,p,q and Methods) and Cbln4-FlpO (Fig. 3i–l, Extended Data Fig. 5l–o and Methods), and performed CRACM, mWmC anterograde tracing and rabies retrograde tracing. All of these methods confirmed strong monosynaptic input from ooDSGCs to Cdh7+ SC neurons (Fig. 3m,n,r,s and Extended Data Fig. 8a–c,h,j,l) but not from ooDSGCs to Cbln4+ SC neurons (Fig. 3m,o,r,t and Extended Data Fig. 8d,e,i,j,m). Moreover, mWmC anterograde and rabies retrograde tracing found negligible input from RGCs in either the contralateral or ipsilateral eye to Cbln4+ SC neurons, and CRACM using all RGC expression of ChR2 did not detect monosynaptic input from contralateral RGCs to Cbln4+ SC neurons, consistent with the TransA-MERFISH data (Figs. 2a and 3p,q,u and Extended Data Fig. 8f,g,k,n,o). These experiments thus validate the unexpected finding from TransA-MERFISH that the strongly direction-selective Cbln4+ SC neurons are not innervated by ooDSGCs from either the contralateral or ipsilateral eye (Figs. 2f,i,j and 3q and Extended Data Fig. 8d,e,k,o). These results demonstrate the specificity and sensitivity of TransA-MERFISH for systematic synaptic target cell-type mapping, enabling biological discovery.

The preceding circuit tracing data suggested that Cdh7+ SC neurons may inherit their direction selectivity from ooDSGCs, whereas Cbln4+ SC neurons may not. A key functional prediction of this differential wiring is that the direction selectivity of Cdh7+ SC neurons depends on the retinal ooDSGC circuit, whereas that of Cbln4+ SC neurons does not. To test this prediction, we used two-photon calcium imaging (Fig. 4a,d,i,l) to analyse the direction selectivity of both SC cell types at baseline and after ablation of starburst amacrine cells (SACs), which reduces direction selectivity of ooDSGCs45 (Fig. 4b,c,j,k,q,r and Extended Data Fig. 9a–f). Consistent with predictions from our TransA-MERFISH experiments, SAC ablation reduced the direction selectivity of Cdh7+ SC neurons (Fig. 4c,f–h) but not of Cbln4+ SC neurons (Fig. 4k,n–p). We further examined the contribution of ipsilateral retinal ooDSGCs by covering the ipsilateral eye and ablating SACs bilaterally (Extended Data Fig. 9m,o) and found no changes in direction selectivity in Cbln4+ SC neurons (Extended Data Fig. 9n,p). These data indicate that the direction selectivity of Cbln4+ SC neurons is independent of retinal ooDSGCs, whereas Cdh7+ SC neurons inherit direction selectivity from retinal ooDSGCs. Together, these data support the notion that multiple mechanisms underlie computationally distinct direction selectivity across different SC neuron types, including retinal ooDSGC-dependent direction selectivity in Cdh7+ SC neurons and retinal ooDSGC-independent direction selectivity in Cbln4+ SC neurons. Analysis of the preferred directions of these two populations revealed another marked difference: in the imaged region of SC (posterior and medial), Cdh7+ SC neurons showed a broad distribution in preferred directions (Fig. 4q and Extended Data Fig. 9g–i), whereas Cbln4+ SC neurons preferred a very narrow distribution of direction, mainly preferring dorsal-to-nasal motion (Fig. 4r and Extended Data Fig. 9j–l). These differential population biases suggest that these neurons are likely to participate in distinct circuits (Fig. 4s).

Here we establish TransA-MERFISH as a high-throughput platform for systematic circuit mapping in the mammalian brain. Using this platform, we implemented a stepwise strategy to decode direction-selective circuits in the early visual pathway. First, we applied TransA-MERFISH to four major RGC subclasses and generated a comprehensive wiring diagram of their synaptic target cell types in the SC at the resolution of individual neuron types. Second, by focusing on the downstream targets of ooDSGCs, we identified two distinct, genetically defined SC neuron populations within the same sublamina of the sSC: Cdh7+ SC neurons, which receive monosynaptic ooDSGC inputs, and Cbln4+ SC neurons, which do not receive such inputs. Third, we used in vivo two-photon calcium imaging to show that selective removal of direction-selective responses from ooDSGCs reduces direction selectivity in Cdh7+ but not in Cbln4+ SC neurons. This result reveals that the SC contains intermingled cell types whose direction selectivity arises from distinct circuit origins and computational mechanisms. Finally, we note that the TransA-MERFISH approach introduced here could be generalized to other regions of the mammalian central nervous system (CNS)46 to directly merge connectivity and cellular taxonomy at cell-type resolution. Scaling up the TransA-MERFISH approach may offer an efficient pathway to achieve comprehensive mammalian connectivity mapping at neuron type resolution, by benchmarking with recent connectomic milestones in zebrafish34 and Drosophila47.

Our study presents the following technical innovations. First, TransA-MERFISH bridges cell identity and connectivity, enabling high-throughput, single-cell-type connectivity reconstruction while reducing cost and complexity. Its throughput lays the groundwork for scalable mapping of synaptic target cell types throughout the CNS. Validation using orthogonal methods, including CRACM and rabies tracing, confirmed the sensitivity and specificity of TransA-MERFISH. Second, we applied TransA-MERFISH to generate an atlas of retinal-to-SC wiring that links molecular identity, connectivity and function, thereby assigning genetically defined cell types to their roles in motion processing across visual relays. These datasets complement existing large-scale resources such as the Projectome Atlas48, which lacks postsynaptic specificity, and mesoscale input atlases that do not specify presynaptic molecular identities. Collectively, TransA-MERFISH offers a scalable method for multi-region circuit mapping that combines molecular, anatomical and physiological brain features, providing a way to substantially improve understanding of the formation and function of networks of genetically defined neurons. Future applications of this approach should also help us to better understand circuit degeneration, such as in glaucoma49,50, or circuit reconstitution, such as in regenerative therapies for optic neuropathies51.

Our study establishes a streamlined, scalable workflow for circuit mapping by combining single-component anterograde trans-neuronal tracer (mWmC) with hybridization-based spatial transcriptomics technology (MERFISH). Although mWmC-mediated anterograde tracing6 and TransA-MERFISH yield non-zero false-positive and false-negative rates, these are largely negligible at the level of specific cell types and frequently reflect inherent biological variability in cell-type connectivity, as also evident in our CRACM data. Furthermore, we independently validated these connectivity predictions using complementary genetic and functional experiments. mWmC-mediated anterograde circuit decoding can be generalized to various circuits in the nervous system, including thalamocortical circuits46, hypothalamic circuits52, hippocampal circuits53 and enteric circuits54. Thus, TransA-MERFISH will serve as a scalable circuit-mapping approach that will be broadly applicable across the mammalian nervous system, beyond retina-to-brain connectivity mapping6,46. Ultimately, these experiments establish a powerful proof of principle for pairing anterograde trans-neuronal tracing with spatial transcriptomics. This generalized framework is highly adaptable to other spatial transcriptomics platforms, as well as to other anterograde trans-neuronal tracers, including genetically encoded reporters with AAV1-Cre/Flpo55,56 for regional targeting or ATLAS57 for isolating strictly excitatory synaptic connections.

Using TransA-MERFISH, we gleaned critical insights into how motion information encoded by retinal direction-selective circuits is transmitted and transformed along mammalian early visual pathways. Since their discovery more than half a century ago1, ooDSGCs have served as a powerful model for dissecting the circuit mechanisms underlying motion selectivity in the retina. However, how downstream cell populations process this information remains unclear. SC has also been extensively studied for its direction selectivity, with abundant direction-selective responses observed across species from mice to primates. Direction selectivity in SC is thought to depend on ooDSGCs because SAC perturbations reduce overall direction selectivity in SC. However, these effects were not analysed at the level of genetically defined SC cell types43. Using TransA-MERFISH to systematically map connectivity between ooDSGCs and SC cell types, we found an unexpected relationship between the reported direction selectivity of SC cell types and their connectivity with ooDSGCs. Most notably, the cell type (Cbln4+) that was previously reported as the most strongly direction-selective in SC receives little to no input from ooDSGCs. This wiring diagram yielded testable predictions, which functional experiments confirmed: direction selectivity of Cbln4+ SC neurons is ooDSGC-independent, whereas the direction selectivity of other SC cell types is ooDSGC-dependent. Notably, a prominent body of literature has debated the relationship between retinal and cortical direction selectivity, revealing that the primary visual cortex mostly computes direction selectivity de novo through intrinsic cortical circuitry while also containing a subset of neurons that inherit hardwired, specialized retinal motion streams via the dorsal lateral geniculate nucleus58,59. Recent findings suggest that these parallel channels serve distinct functional roles: inherited retinal pathways appear to sense more local motion, such as object motion, and non-inherited retinal pathways appear to sense more global motion, such as optic flow60. Our findings demonstrate that parallel inherited and computed direction selectivity responses are a fundamental strategy used by both cortical and subcortical structures that may support detection of different forms of behaviourally relevant motion, such as self-generated versus external motion. Together, our findings suggest that SC comprises intermingled subnetworks of disparate genetically defined direction-selective cell types: one downstream of ooDSGCs and a second ooDSGC-independent. Thus, TransA-MERFISH enables systematic, high-throughput discovery of neuronal wiring diagrams at cell-type resolution to reveal circuit mechanisms.

The brain preparation for TransA-MERFISH was optimized to preserve immunohistochemical staining and maximize RNA quality for subsequent RNA multiplexing experiments. Young adult mice were deeply anaesthetized and transcardially perfused with ice-cold saline (0.9% NaCl solution), followed by ice-cold 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4). After dissection, the brains were post-fixed in the same fixative solution overnight at 4 °C. The target brain regions (SC in this study, about 10 mm3 in volume) were micro-dissected, with anteroposterior and dorsoventral orientations carefully marked. This was followed by cryoprotection in 30% sucrose in PBS at 4 °C until the tissues sank (2–4 h). The target regions were embedded in OCT compound and rapidly frozen in a dry ice-cooled 2-methylbutane (Supelco, MX0760-1). Then, approximately 12 µm coronal sections were prepared on a cryostat and collected under RNase-free conditions on MERSCOPE Slides (Vizgen) for RNA-grade processing.

For each mouse, about 10 coronal sections were obtained using the right SC, which receives contralateral retinal input from the left eye injected with mWmC tracer, from the perfusion–fixation protocol described above. Sections were collected at intervals of every 9 slices (12 µm each, totalling about 100 µm), spanning the anterior-to-posterior axis of the right SC. The coronal sections encompassing the SC were selected based on the Allen Mouse Brain Atlas, corresponding to stereotaxic coordinates relative to Bregma ranging from −3.0 mm to −5.0 mm. The section captures the full dorsoventral extent of the SC, from the superficial visual layers stratum zonale (SZ), SGS, to deep motor-associated layers stratum griseum profundum (SGP) and stratum album profundum (SAP).

To design an SC-specific MERFISH gene panel, we first assessed the number of genes required to classify SC neuron types. To this end, we implemented a simulation framework based on gradient boosting (XGBoost) using SC single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) data. In brief, for each neuron cluster, the top k non-redundant specific genes were selected to form a panel. The validation accuracy (using a 70:30 training:test split) was evaluated until a plateau was reached. For each 12 µm tissue section, the MERFISH protocol generated seven 1.7 µm optical sections (z0, z1, … , z6) for DAPI, polyT and mCherry/RFP channels, respectively, as Tiff files under the images folder, together with the micron_to_mosaic_pixel_transform.csv to link the micrometre scale (transcripts and MERSCOPE visualizer) to mosaic pixel scale (Tiff files). Each MERFISH data folder also contains the detected_transcripts.csv file, which includes coordinates for detected transcripts along with their spatial coordinates, as well as other metadata and cell-by-gene information. Cell segmentation information is also generated by the Vizgen Post-processing Tool (VPT). To improve cell segmentation accuracy, we performed independent segmentation using custom scripts and a fine-tuned Cellpose2.0 model tailored to the SC, as described below.

The following steps were conducted: (1) DAPI image preprocessing and initial region-of-interest (ROI) selection. The DAPI image of z-plane 0 for each region is loaded into a Python Jupyter notebook (tifffile.imread), and overexposed pixels are filtered using a cut-off at the 0.95 quantile (np.quantile) to enhance the signal for downstream ROI selection. For each region, the initial ROI was restricted to the right SC hemisphere as our AAV2-mWmC injection was onto the left eye (Fig. 1a) and manually delineated using the processed DAPI image in ImageJ (Analyze-Tools-ROI manager), from the pial surface to encompass superficial SC layers (SZ and SGS) medially toward the midline, extending ventrally to the level of the cerebral aqueduct. This ROI includes both superficial and deeper layers as defined by cytoarchitectonic boundaries in the Allen Mouse Brain Atlas. We then cropped the original DAPI Tiff images of each seven z-plane images of one section to the delineated ROI (using the read_roi function, the matplotlib.patches module’s Polygon class, and the PIL package’s Image module). A maximum z-projection approach was performed over optical sections to enhance cell segmentation signals. To this end, the maximum-intensity projection of all seven z-plane optical sections for each slice was generated using either ImageJ (Images-Stack-Z Project) or Python (numpy.max). Meanwhile, we subset the detected_transcripts.csv file from MERFISH to correspond to the selected ROI (read-roi to load ImageJ ROI file, followed by matplotlib.path.Path in Python), using the micron_to_mosaic_pixel_transform.csv file to transform transcript coordinates (global_x and global_y coordinates) to the mosaic pixel coordinates of the Tiff images, along with gene symbols for each detected transcript.

Segmentation was performed as in previous MERFISH studies of the mouse brain31. We constructed a labelled image by combining the transcript dots and maximum-intensity projection DAPI images using the get_img function (windowsize=2000, margin=10%) of the ClusterMap package, followed by splitting the data into small tiles (split function) to improve downstream computational efficiency. We set a threshold of 3,000 transcript counts to focus on tiles with denser transcript accumulation. The split tiles were then saved as separate PNG files with DAPI signal and transcript dots overlaid. A customized Cellpose model66 (models.CellposeModel pretrained_model) was used to perform cell segmentation on each tile (gpu=True, channels = [0,0], diameter=80). More specifically, the fine-tuned Cellpose model was generated based on the ‘cyto2’ model using the Cellpose graphical user interface, trained with five regions of interest (containing about 200 manually curated cells each), to improve the performance for dimly/darkly stained cells, smaller cells, and separating multiple cells that were segmented as one. The segmentation output for each tile was subsequently generated using the masks_flows_to_seg function from cellpose.io and saved as a.npy file. The separate tiles were then stitched by merging adjacent patches, while avoiding cell ID collisions and duplication in overlapping regions, to form the full segmentation mask (reassigned_masks.npy), which encompassed the entire tissue section. After all tiles were stitched, connected components in the combined mask were relabelled (skimage.measure.label and regionprops) to ensure sequential, non-overlapping cell IDs. We then assembled the AnnData for downstream analysis by constructing a ClusterMap model for each section, using the transcriptomic spot information (xy_radius=30, z_radius=0, fast_preprocess=False). Centroid positions were calculated from labelled masks using skimage.regionprops, and used to initialize cell-centred AnnData objects. Cells with no transcriptomic counts were excluded by default, and spatial coordinates were stored in the obsm[‘spatial’] field of the AnnData object. We further double-checked the accuracy of the assembled AnnData by comparing the transcript distribution and the segmentation of a specific cell to the combined raw data for DAPI, PolyT, and transcript dots from the same cell in the MERSCOPE visualizer. We then integrated the mean mCherry fluorescence intensity, mWmC registration-related metadata, and the estimated depth (in µm; distance from the pial surface, as described below) into a unified AnnData object.

Last, related to the entire TransA-MERFISH data integration, for consistency with the ROI of other data modalities, such as electrophysiology and calcium imaging, we also further restricted the above-selected ROI to rectangular regions, avoiding the midlines and lateral regions, encompassing from the pial surface to SO by examining the spatial expression of Npnt and Etv1 (Fig. 1j). Subsequent clustering and transcriptomic analyses of the resulting cell-by-gene matrix were performed using Scanpy (v1.10.4) and Squidpy (v1.6.2), as detailed in the following sections.

To register mWmC+ cells in the TransA-MERFISH dataset, we implemented an automated registration workflow based on per-cell mCherry intensity, supplemented by manual curation. First, we derive the per-cell mean mCherry intensity by aggregating the mean mCherry pixel intensity at each z-layer (z = 1:6, excluding z0 to avoid potential optical noise). The aggregated value was then divided by the number of z-planes (six in this case, as the maximum-intensity projection-derived cell segmentation mask was shared across z-planes), yielding a per-cell mean mCherry intensity across z-planes. The frequency distribution of the per-cell mean mCherry intensity showed a predominantly bimodal distribution of red fluorescence. Second, we assigned an mWmC-positive score using the mean intensity value from the inner cytoplasm ring area and an adjacent out-of-cell annulus as a local background estimate. The positive cells were identified by applying a threshold based on the distributions from a control ROI (in the lower, non-retino-recipient area with the lowest red fluorescence signal) on the same slice. Last, mWmC+ cells were proofread by two independent annotators for all slices, who examined the red fluorescence overlaid on the DAPI and PolyT labelling, with or without segmented cell masks, using Vizgen MERSCOPE Visualizer. First, background correction: to remove uneven illumination and background fluorescence (especially signals in axons, which reside in the same lamina as the recipient neurons), we applied a multi-threaded rolling-ball background subtraction algorithm to the RFP channel image. For each section, the maximum-intensity projection of z0–z7 optical planes was used as input. The image was processed in overlapping tiles (4,096 × 4,096 pixels) with a rolling-ball radius of 80 pixels. For each tile, the background surface was computed using the skimage.restoration.rolling_ball function, and the results were then merged across tiles to form a continuous background field. To perform clustering and dimension reduction, we first applied SCTransform-based normalization67 to the expression matrix for each MERFISH section. All statistics were calculated by GraphPad Prism 9. Peak currents between the two groups were compared using a two-tailed Student’s t-test.

Optomotor response was described previously75. The OptoDrum (Striatech) comprises a box with four screens to simulate drifting gratings. An IR camera mounted on the top of the box was used to record mouse movements. Mice were first acclimated to the room in their cages for 30 min. Then, freely moving mice were placed on a platform and allowed to acclimate to the box for at least 5 min. Drifting gratings (12° s−1) in the clockwise direction can trigger left-eye-mediated body and head movements, while drifting gratings in the counterclockwise direction were used to test right-eye-mediated movements. Drifting gratings were presented from low spatial frequencies (0.056 cycles per degree) to high frequencies (0.3–0.5 cycles per degree) to determine the threshold at which a mouse could track. OptoDrum’s software can detect the body shape of mice and label the positions of head, body, and tail of mice. The stimulus and video tracking were paused if a mouse engaged in grooming behaviour or dropped off the platform and resumed later. The software can score the optomotor response and determine the spatial-frequency threshold. False positives were manually identified as invalid, and the same frequency of drifting gratings was repeated. We compared the spatial-frequency threshold of drifting gratings to trigger optomotor responses in the left eye (ablation) with the right eye (control) to assess visual acuity differences.

We obtained measurements from distinct biological samples. No covariates were tested. No formal test of normality was performed; statistical tests were selected based on the distributional assumptions of the data. Statistical methods and the number of animals tested for each configuration are documented in the figure legends for each experiment. When comparing two groups, statistics were derived using an unpaired two-sided Student’s t-test, Kolmogorov–Smirnov test or Fisher’s exact test, as indicated in the figure legends. For comparing cell-type proportions across multiple reactions, we used the Kruskal–Wallis H test (scipy.stats.kruskal, Python). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed in GraphPad Prism 9 to assess the statistical significance of differences among multiple measurements.

Fisher’s exact test was performed using the Easy Fisher Exact Test Calculator (https://www.socscistatistics.com/tests/fisher/default2.aspx).

Statistical significance was defined as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001 and ****P < 0.0001. All values indicate mean ± s.e.m., unless otherwise noted.
