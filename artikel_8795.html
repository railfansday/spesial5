Grip sets the table for what comes next because where you place your hands affects wrist position, elbow path, shoulder comfort, stability, and often good form.

Your arm length, shoulder and wrist mobility, and training goal can all influence your grip. Grip is essential for rows, deadlifts, and curl variations, but for presses and squats we often ignore it.

However, paying special attention to it will pay dividends. The goal is to find a grip that makes the lift feel comfortable, strong, and minimizes discomfort.

Here, we’ll break down the best hand positions for the barbell bench press, overhead press, back squat, and front squat, including the grip options for each and how to decide which one fits you best.

We often only think about grip when it fails, but taking the time to set it often makes the difference between a good set and a bad one.

Let’s begin here. Your bench press grip affects wrist position, elbow path, range of motion, shoulder stress, and which muscles work hardest.

Where the Bar Should Sit: Start by placing the bar toward the heels of your palms. This keeps the bar stacked over your forearm and helps prevent wrist hyperextension. Think of your hand, wrist, and forearm as a unit. The better your forearm and wrist stack, the better your bench press.

Grip Width: Begin with your pinky finger wrapped around the barbell ring. This grip is the best starting point for most lifters. At the bottom of the rep, your forearms should be nearly vertical.

A wider grip shortens the range of motion and emphasizes the pecs, but it also moves the shoulder into greater abduction. That can feel strong for some but cranky for others.

A narrower grip increases range of motion and puts more demand on the triceps because the elbows stay closer to the torso and get a deeper stretch. Neither one is better or worse. It depends on comfort and your training goal.

Full Grip vs. False Grip: A full grip, with the thumb wrapped around the bar, gives you the best bar security and should be your default. However, a false grip (without the thumb) may provide more comfort for some lifters, but the tradeoff is less bar security.

How to Know Which Grip Is Best for You: Use the grip and choose the grip width that best balances shoulder comfort, wrist alignment, and stability.

Bar placement and grip width are vital for the barbell overhead press. Maintaining the wrist, forearm, and elbow stack will help you press with power without driving your upper-body joints mad.

Where the Bar Should Sit: The bar should sit toward the heel of the palm, not near the fingers. This places the load over the forearm and helps prevent wrist hyperextension, but some wrist extension in the OH press is normal. The goal is not to force the wrist straight, but to avoid letting the bar roll so far back that it becomes uncomfortable.

Grip Width: For most lifters, a grip just outside shoulder width is a good place to start. If it’s too wide, you shorten the range of motion and may lose leverage. If it’s too narrow, your elbows and shoulders can feel crowded at the bottom. From the front, your forearms should be close to vertical, with the elbows slightly in front of the bar.

Full Grip vs. False Grip: A full grip gives you the most security and should be the default for most lifters. A false grip can feel better for some people because it may reduce wrist bend. The tradeoff, again, is less bar security.

How to Know Which Grip Is Best for You: Use the grip that lets you start with a solid wrist position, keep the forearms vertical, and press without shoulder or elbow irritation.

Grip is secondary to the squat, but your hands secure the bar and help create upper-back tension while your back supports the load.

Use a False Grip: For the back squat, I prefer a false or thumbless grip, with the thumb resting on the same side of the bar as the fingers.

Why? It makes it easier to keep the wrist in line with the forearm instead of letting the wrist bend back under the bar. This helps prevent your hands from taking the load that should be on your back.

Grip Width: A narrower grip can increase upper-back tension by pulling the shoulder blades together and locking the bar in place. But narrower isn’t always better.

If you don’t have the shoulder mobility to reach that position, forcing your hands too close can irritate your wrists, elbows, or shoulders. Moving the hands slightly wider often gives you enough room to keep the wrists aligned while keeping the upper back tight.

If you’re getting wrist or inner-elbow soreness before you squat, widening the hands a little is the better solution.

The front squat gives you more grip options than the back squat because grip is secondary. The goal is to have the bar resting securely across the front of your shoulders while your hands support it.

There are three practical ways to do that.

The best front-squat grip keeps the bar secure, your elbows high, and your torso upright without causing unnecessary discomfort. There’s no need to force-feed one grip when you have options.

Here are the key points to pay attention to with barbell squats and presses.

Does it improve joint alignment? Your wrists, elbows, and shoulders should feel natural and not engaged in a game of Twister.

Does it reduce pain or irritation? A grip that bothers your wrists, elbows, or shoulders is worth changing. Adjust your grip width, switch from a full grip to a false grip, or try a different front-squat setup to fix it.

Are you stronger with it? If one grip gives you better control and lets you get after it without sacrificing technique, then go with that.

Can you maintain the intended range of motion? If your grip shortens your range of motion or forces you into bad lifting positions, it probably needs adjusting.

The key is to make small changes and judge them by how the whole lift feels and performs. A good grip should increase stability and express strength, not add another thing to fight against.

Your grip should make the lift better, not give you another problem to solve.

Paying attention to joint alignment and discomfort before you lift puts you in the best position to succeed. So don’t be afraid to adjust if you’ve been told to grip a certain way; a small grip change can improve comfort, stability, and strength more than most lifters expect.
