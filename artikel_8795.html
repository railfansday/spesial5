Our Editors Tested Winter Cycling Gear in the Coldest Temps. Which 11 Picks Deliver Real Warmth?

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

We rode through biting wind and bone-chilling cold to find the jackets, gloves, tights, and layers that keep you warm without trapping sweat. These 11 editor-tested picks are the ones worth buying for winter rides.

Cold weather doesn’t have to mean putting your bike away until spring. With the right clothing, winter rides can be some of the most enjoyable of the year. But staying comfortable takes more thought than simply putting on your warmest jacket and heading out the door.

The challenge is balancing warmth with breathability. Dress too lightly, and you’ll be cold and miserable. Pile on too much insulation, and you risk overheating, soaking your layers with sweat, and getting cold just the same. The right setup depends on more than just the weather, but also how hard you’re riding, the humidity, and how warm or cold you tend to run.

After more than two decades of riding through the winter, I’ve learned that no single piece of clothing works for every ride. Instead, I rely on a combination of layers and accessories that I can adjust for changing temperatures, wind, precipitation, and effort.

Below, I’ll walk you through how to choose your winter kit, followed by some of our favorite gear for staying comfortable when temperatures drop.

Before I get dressed, I check the forecast as close to the start of the ride as possible. Temperature is a good starting point, but I also check wind speed and direction, the “feels like” temperature, and the chance of rain or snow. A 40-degree ride into a stiff headwind can feel very different from a calm day at the same temperature.

Next, think about the ride itself. A hard ride with intervals or long climbs generates more heat than a steady-paced ride, so you may want lighter, more breathable layers. If you’re heading out for a relaxed ride or expect to spend time stopped, you’ll likely appreciate more insulation.

I generally aim to feel a little chilly when I first roll out. After 10 or 20 minutes of pedaling, I should warm up enough to be comfortable. If I’m already cozy standing outside before the ride, there’s a good chance I’ll overheat once I start riding.

Conditions can change during a ride, too. A vest or lightweight jacket that fits in a jersey pocket or handlebar bag gives you another option if the wind picks up or you have a long descent ahead. On rides with big temperature swings, I’ll sometimes bring a second pair of gloves so I can swap to a warmer or lighter pair as needed.

Layering lets you adjust your clothing to the conditions instead of relying on one jacket to do everything. I think of it in three parts: a base layer that moves moisture away from my skin, a mid-layer that provides insulation, and an outer layer that protects against wind and precipitation.

Base layer: This sits closest to your skin and should fit snugly enough to move moisture away from your body. I typically start with a thin synthetic layer, even on cold days. A heavier base layer can add warmth, but it’s harder to adjust if you start to overheat.

Mid-layer: This is where you can add or subtract insulation for the temperature and your expected effort. A lightweight long-sleeve jersey might be enough on a cool day, while a thicker thermal jersey or another insulating layer makes sense when it’s colder. Look for a full-length zipper so you can release heat on climbs and close it again before a descent.

Outer layer: A vest, softshell, or winter jacket protects you from wind and weather. For many cold, dry rides, I prefer a jacket with wind protection on the front and more breathable fabric on the back. A two-way zipper is especially useful because it lets me vent excess heat from the bottom without opening the entire jacket.

For steady rain, a waterproof shell provides more protection, but even the best rain jackets are less breathable than lighter layers. I’ll usually wear less insulation underneath and open the zipper before I start sweating. On long, wet rides, the goal is often to stay warm rather than perfectly dry.

Even if your core is comfortable, cold fingers or toes can cut a ride short. Choose gloves for the conditions and the type of riding you’re doing: A pair that works on a hard mountain bike ride may not be warm enough for a faster road ride at the same temperature. Fit matters, too. Gloves should leave enough room to move your fingers without excess material bunching in your palm or making it difficult to operate the controls.

For your feet, start with socks that fit comfortably inside your shoes, then add shoe covers for wind and cold. Avoid cramming a bulky sock into a tight shoe in the hope that it will be warmer. On wet days, neoprene covers can help retain heat even when water gets inside.

A cap that fits under your helmet helps protect your head and ears, while a neck gaiter can seal the gap at your collar. Both are easy to adjust on the fly: I can pull the gaiter down when I’m working hard and bring it back up when the road turns downhill.

The right combination takes some trial and error. Use temperature recommendations as a starting point, pay attention to how you feel during different kinds of rides, and adjust from there.

These Assos gloves feel like they shouldn’t be this warm. But don’t let their thin, stretchy, low-bulk material fool you. These gloves have kept my hands toasty and comfortable into the high 30s, and I have mountain biked in them at near-freezing temperatures with no issues.

They use soft fleece fabric inside and have an outer layer with excellent wind resistance. Assos also applies a water-repellent treatment to manage light rain and spray from damp roads. I’m a big fan of the palm of these gloves. They feature very minimal padding that helps with grip and bar feel. Fit and construction are excellent, with long cuffs to keep cold air out of your jacket, plus touchscreen functionality that works very well. The only knock against these gloves is their price. However, my personal pair is now in its fourth winter with no signs of wear, making these an easy recommendation if you want a good pair of gloves that will last.

At around $100, the Pearl Izumi Quest AmFIB is a great choice if you want a warm, simple jacket for winter rides without spending a lot or piecing together separate windproof and insulating layers. Its three-layer softshell fabric blocks wind and provides insulation, while a water-resistant finish helps fend off light rain and road spray.

The relaxed fit leaves room for additional layers underneath, and the full-length zipper lets you vent some heat when you start to warm up. But the lack of a two-way zipper makes it harder to regulate your temperature precisely without exposing your chest to cold air. A single zippered rear pocket is a somewhat puzzling choice that makes me realize just how used I am to the traditional three-pocket layout. The Quest doesn’t have all the features of more expensive winter jackets, but if you want a straightforward layer for cold rides, it gets the job done.

Yes, the Velocio Luxe Thermal Bib Tights are expensive. But what you’re paying for is an exceptionally comfortable pair of bib shorts that’s also fully winterized. And after spending enough cold rides in tights with cheap chamois pads or fabric that bunches and chafes, I can tell you that skimping here can make winter riding uncomfortable in a whole new way.

Velocio also offers free repairs, which helps make the high upfront cost easier to justify if you plan to keep riding in them for many winters to come.

If I’m heading out for a ride in the rain, the Castelli Perfetto RoS 3 is the jacket I’m most likely to reach for. Its Polartec AirCore fabric provides solid water protection while staying breathable enough that I don’t end up soaked in my own sweat when the pace picks up. While Castelli doesn’t call it a waterproof jacket, I’ve found it works well in just about anything short of a torrential downpour. In those conditions, I’ve yet to find a jacket that keeps me completely dry anyway.

Pick the right base layer for the temperature, and the Perfetto can keep you dry and comfortable through a wide range of chilly, wet rides. Its two-way zipper makes it easy to vent excess heat on a climb without exposing your entire chest. When temperatures drop further, add a thermal mid-layer underneath. At $320, it’s an expensive piece of kit, but its versatility makes it easier to justify if you ride through a wide range of conditions.

Specialized’s Neoprene Shoe Covers are my favorite choice for cold, wet winter rides. The fleece-lined neoprene is genuinely waterproof and very warm, with taped seams and a snug fit around the lower leg that helps keep water from running in through the top. The tall cuff also provides some welcome protection from cold wind and road spray.

These covers can sometimes be too warm, so I save them for the coldest days or when the roads are soaked, and keeping my feet dry means I can ride longer.

Big glasses do more than you might think to make cold rides comfortable. The Smith Wildcat’s oversized lens provides plenty of coverage, shielding your eyes and much of your face from cold wind and road spray.

For winter riding, I’d choose the photochromic lens, which adjusts its tint as light conditions change. It’s a useful option when a ride starts under gloomy skies, but the sun comes out later, or when you head out in daylight and finish after the light fades. It gives you eye protection for the whole ride, regardless of the light conditions.

DeFeet’s Woolie Boolie socks might have been one of the first pieces of cycling-specific gear I ever bought. When I started working as a bike messenger, I quickly learned what it felt like to spend a winter day riding around with cold feet. These wool socks were one of my first attempts at solving that problem.

The Woolie Boolie is a classic for a reason. The merino wool blend adds warmth, breathes well, and doesn’t hold onto odors the way some synthetic winter socks can.

The Velocio Alpha Zero is easily the warmest cycling jacket I’ve ever tested. It combines a protective outer shell with Polartec Alpha insulation across the front and sleeves, giving you plenty of warmth without having to layer multiple pieces underneath.

For me, it’s a jacket for the coldest winter rides. In fact, I find it’s probably too warm for anything above freezing. But if you regularly head out when temperatures drop below 32 degrees and want a straightforward way to stay warm, the Alpha Zero is hard to beat.

Pearl Izumi’s PRO Alpha Layer is one of my favorite ways to add warmth without sacrificing breathability. Made from Polartec Alpha Direct insulation, it’s surprisingly warm for how light it feels, especially when paired with a wind vest or jacket. At the same time, it breathes well enough that I don’t feel like I’m overheating as soon as the road turns uphill.

That combination makes it a versatile piece for winter riding. Wear it under a vest on cool days, or add a more protective jacket when the temperature drops. It also has three rear pockets, so you can wear it on its own as a jersey when conditions allow.

Ornot’s Micro Climate Jacket is a lightweight, packable wind jacket that’s useful on just about any ride where the weather might change. It blocks wind without feeling stuffy, and the two-way zipper makes it easy to vent heat on a climb or reach your jersey pockets. When you don’t need it, the jacket packs into its own rear pocket.

The fit is part of what makes the Micro Climate so versatile. Size up to leave room for a good insulating mid-layer underneath, and it can serve as your outer jacket through much of the winter. Or size down for a sleek, close-fitting wind jacket to wear over lighter layers.

I’ve been using Pearl Izumi’s AmFIB Lobster Gloves for more than a decade. And they’re still one of the best gloves for when temps plummet.

The gloves are made with 170g PrimaLoft Gold insulation, AmFIB soft-shell polyester-polyurethane fabric, and Ax Suede Laredo synthetic leather. It has the kind of exceptional warmth you’d get in a mitten. Yet the AmFIBs still allow for ample dexterity so you can operate both your shifters and brake levers. There’s also a gel palm pad for comfort while gripping the handlebar, plus touchscreen compatibility for the index finger and thumb.
