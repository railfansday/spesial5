The Art of Breakfast: What American Hotels Can Learn From Europe

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

From Gstaad to Lake Como, the best hotels treat the morning meal as the main event.

There are few things more revealing about a hotel than its breakfast. A property can have beautiful rooms and a marble-clad lobby, but the morning meal is where hospitality is really put to the test. And while American hotels have become very good at building elaborate breakfast buffets, there is often something missing: the sense that anyone has given the experience that much thought. Too often, guests are greeted with scrambled eggs under a heat lamp, a few slices of wet bacon, and a $38 bill. In Europe, meanwhile, breakfast can involve silver cake stands, homemade jams, excellent coffee, and a real sense of place.

At Gstaad Palace, the grand Swiss hotel, breakfast is, like everything else, a dignified affair. The buffet is beautiful but not extravagant, and the selection spans house-made juices, jams, and pastries, eggs cooked to order, and service that runs more smoothly than a Patek Philippe. And yet, despite the formality of the space, the experience feels wonderfully relaxed.

“Guests should feel like they are in their castle or palace, and they should be welcomed like it’s their house and it’s their best friends serving them,” said Andrea Scherz, general manager, whose family has owned the hotel since 1947. “It shouldn’t be a procedure.”

Scherz recently had breakfast at an American hotel that left him less than impressed. The food wasn’t necessarily the problem—it was the little things that were missing, the gestures that make a guest feel looked after rather than processed. A hostess took his name, checked it off a list, and asked him to wait while she set a particular table, despite there being an abundance of available tables already set. It was nearly half an hour before he was sipping his humble cappuccino. “They didn’t go the extra mile; they didn’t even go the extra meter,” he recalled.

That gets to the heart of what American hotels often get wrong about the most important meal of the day: they confuse abundance with luxury. More eggs, more bacon, more potatoes—but bigger isn’t always better. “Great breakfast is often about something less complicated,” said Nick Hatsatouris, owner at Caffé Tusk, a European-style café inside the Evelyn Hotel in New York City. “A great breakfast is a simple menu with quality ingredients, the ritual of the service, and the atmosphere of the room.”

There’s also the question of price. In the United States, breakfast is frequently an additional à la carte charge, even at hotels where the room rate can feel steep. At most European hotels, the charge is folded into the room rate in advance; for guests booking through a travel advisor, it is often waived.

“For American hotels, I think the lesson is about more than including breakfast in the rate,” said Amy Brandwein, the James Beard Award-nominated chef-owner of Washington, D.C.’s Centrolina. “It’s about recognizing breakfast as an essential part of hospitality and an opportunity to express a hotel’s identity, its connection to local producers, and its commitment to the guest’s well-being.”

Beyond price, the best hotel breakfasts in Europe also reflect where guests are. At Gleneagles in Scotland, for instance, the Scullery puts a distinctly Scottish spin on the morning buffet with local charcuterie, house-made sausages and haggis, and freshly prepared eggs and pancakes. There’s a Bloody Mary station, too, as well as undeniably cozy and humble Scottish porridge. Adare Manor in Ireland serves its breakfast of Irish breads, cheeses, and hot dishes in a 132-foot-long gallery. And at Passalacqua on Lake Como, guests can take breakfast in the garden or beneath frescoed ceilings, with fresh fruit grown in the property’s gardens, eggs from the property’s own chickens, and 11 varieties of regional cakes arranged on silver tiered stands.

Of course, not every hotel needs a sprawling buffet or a kitchen turning out elaborate dishes. Smaller, more thoughtful changes can go a long way.

Brandwein remembers European breakfasts for their balance: beautiful cappuccinos, whole-grain breads, oats, flaxseeds, fresh fruit, and excellent local honey. “I find the typical American hotel breakfast rather heavy and unimaginative,” she said, pointing to the usual emphasis on eggs, potatoes, bacon, and sausage. She would like to see more whole grains, fresh produce, and lighter preparations. The ingredients don’t need to be complicated; in fact, the simpler they are, the better. Quality and presentation go a long way.

A hotel breakfast doesn’t need 40 items on a buffet or a pastry chef working through the night. It needs good coffee, something fresh and local, and a staff who seem happy to have you there.
