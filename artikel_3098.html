What Berkshire Hathaway's Third-Quarter Report Says About Its Insurance Business

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Even if investors saw it coming, a headwind is still a headwind regardless of the reason.

By James Brumley – Nov 19, 2026 at 5:30AM EST

10 stocks we like better than Berkshire Hathaway ›

It's been roughly two weeks since Berkshire Hathaway (BRKA -0.95%) (BRKB -0.77%) posted its third-quarter results. And yet, there's still plenty about this complex conglomerate's numbers left to dissect.

Today's topic? Berkshire's insurance business, which -- depending on the quarter -- can be the company's single-biggest source of operating income. Only this time around, it suffered a measurable dip in the bottom line.

You may know it best as a collection of Warren Buffett-approved stock picks. That's hardly all that Berkshire Hathaway is, however. It owns several dozen privately held businesses like Shaw flooring, Duracell batteries, railroad BNSF, Pilot Travel Centers, Clayton Homes, and GEICO insurance, just to name a few. These outfits collectively make up about 40% of Berkshire's total market value of $1.25 trillion and account for all of its operating income, which is on pace to reach $48 billion to $50 billion this year alone.

And insurance is a huge piece of this profit. Between underwriting and investment income stemming from its insurance business, this arm delivered nearly 45% of last year's operating earnings.

This year, though, isn't nearly as impressive. Through the first three quarters of the year, insurance-related investment income of just under $8.5 billion is down 10% from year-earlier comparisons, while underwriting income of $4.8 billion is lower to the tune of almost 16%.

Image source: Chart by author. Data from Berkshire Hathaway.

GEICO is a key contributor to this weakness. After a couple of banner years, it was finally forced to increase commissions for brokers and agents as well as advertise more. Claim numbers are also up, reflecting the growing price of ... well, everything.

It's not just the insurance-specific aspect of this arm, however, that's running into a headwind. Berkshire also earns money on the cash it keeps on hand to pay claims. While they're up now, the lower interest rates compared to earlier this year are still taking their toll. Indeed, practically all of this year's 10.3% dip in Berkshire's insurance-related investment income is the result of lower interest rates on shorter-term bonds, which were higher at this point last year.

So what do Berkshire Hathaway's Q3 numbers say about its insurance business? A lot. More than anything, however, it says this business isn't exactly consistent even if its ebbs and flows are somewhat predictable from one quarter to the next.

Oh, certainly experienced investors have seen more erratic income trends. By some standards, in fact, Berkshire's insurance operation actually is reasonably consistent.

For Berkshire Hathaway and its shareholders, though, this isn't the sort of inconsistency that's easy to digest from one quarter to the next. The other businesses it also owns don't necessarily offset its insurance operation's earnings headwinds at the right time either, as Berkshire's erratic total operating earnings data, plotted on the chart above, shows us.

Just don't lose sight of the bigger picture. One disappointing quarter -- or even one disappointing year -- doesn't necessarily mean this business is being poorly managed. The longer-term growth trend here is far more important, and it still looks fine even if the recent post-earnings rhetoric hasn't been overwhelmingly bullish.
