Sara Paretsky on inventing languages and feeling kinship with older characters

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

For more than four decades, V.I. Warshawski, the fictional private investigator created by Sara Paretsky, has aged slowly. In her late twenties and early thirties in the first novels, Warshawski is now about fifty years old. Unfortunately for Paretsky and the rest of us, time has flown by at a normal pace. In her mid-thirties when she published her first novel, Indemnity Only, in 1982, Paretsky is now seventy-nine and has less in common with her more youthful character.

Paretsky’s newest book, Bad Company, introduces a protagonist more in line with her age: Lily Sedaka, a former CIA field agent now in her seventies. The book marks her twenty-fifth novel and the first non-Warshawski novel since Ghost Country in 1998.

Paretsky will continue writing novels about Warshawski, but this new character will enable the author to tap into her own lived experiences—complete with the aches and pains that come with age.

Like her Warshawski novels, Bad Company takes place in Chicago but with an added worldview. Sections are set in the fictional Central Asian country of Hayalistan, where Sedaka once was stationed. In an interview conducted over Zoom, Paretsky reflected on the decision to introduce a new character and the difficulty that came with inventing a language.

L. Wayne Hicks: I wanted to ask you what made you decide to unveil a new character after so much success with the V.I. Warshawski novels.

Sara Paretsky: Oh, you know, it wasn’t a deliberate decision. But she came to me. I was on vacation in Paris, which makes me sound more glamorous than I am. But especially because what happened was that I fell out of bed, which is totally not glamorous. And as I was rubbing my sore hip, I was thinking, what would happen if V.I. got to be my age, seventy-something, and injured herself and could no longer race around the streets after bad guys? And then I thought, I really don’t want V.I. to be old and creaky. I want her to stay kind of fifty-ish.

And really, in that moment, Lily came to me with her name, Lily, this ex-CIA agent who now was on the run from the CIA. The whole storyline took years to develop, and many false starts, but that was how it happened.

LWH: Is Lily going to be a recurring character?

SP: I hope so. You know, everything depends on my being able to write faster than I do because I’m under contract to do a new book in the V.I. series. I wrote 10,000 words that I threw out. I wrote ten thousand more words and threw out eight thousand. But I’ve started a file on Lily. I have a storyline that I’m really excited about for her that is totally hers. It’s not the kind of thing V.I. would be involved with.

LWH: I was wondering since Lily and V.I are both in Chicago, whether there’d ever be a crossover or meetup.

SP: That’s a delicate question. I don’t know if I should say this or not. I mean, I don’t know if this is politically advisable to say this, but Disney owns the rights in perpetuity to V.I. and any characters that hover around her. So, I want to own Lily, and that means that much as I would like to have a lot of crossover, that’s just not going to happen.

LWH: Okay, and Disney has the rights because of the movie? (The 1991 film V.I. Warshawski, starring Kathleen Turner.)

SP: Yes, exactly. And I was young, ignorant, relied on advice. It wasn’t great advice, and here I am.

LWH: Do you feel more of a kinship to Lily than you do to V.I. since you’re both in your seventies?

SP: When V.I. and I were the same age, I don’t think I felt a kinship with her. I was thinking about that this morning. I have a cousin who’s my age, whom I admire greatly. I love her. She’s a wonderful woman in every way. She’s kind of more like V.I. She just got back from backpacking, carrying a thirty-pound backpack through the Boundary Waters in northern Minnesota, and she’s done many wonderful things in her life for women, so I admire her and I admire V.I. in that way.

But I have to say that I actually actively envy Lily. I would like to be Lily. I never wanted to be V.I. I’d like to be that person who could insert herself into groups the way she did when she was actively working as a CIA agent. She scampers up and down mountains. She bathes naked with local women under the full moon and the glacier waters in the country where she worked.

And I mean, like V.I., she takes a lot of risks, but unlike V.I., she’s a team player to a certain extent, insofar as the CIA lets her have a team, and then she’s got these incredible linguistic gifts, so I would like to be Lily, whereas I don’t want to be my cousin. I just like to admire my cousin.

LWH: And you had to create an entirely fictional country for Lily to have had her adventures in.

SP: Yes, I realized if I was going to write a book that was set in the CIA world, sort of set in the CIA world, if I wrote about any of the things that the CIA has really been involved with, I would spend the rest of my life answering letters from people who said, “Well, you got this detail on, and I was there from 1950 to 1990,” and so I thought, okay, the way around that is to create my own country, and then created my own language.

That was a mistake because when the audio recorder was doing the reading, they said there are sixty words in the Hayali language that are in the book that they needed to know how to pronounce. And I’m looking at them and thinking, why did I create a language that was Qs, Zs, and Ws? I don’t know how to pronounce these words. Figure it out yourself. But anyway, I made a stab at it, and I thought, well, if I ever do that again, it’s going to be a language that looks like French.

LWH: You could have made Lily a retired CIA agent who doesn’t have a lot of health issues. She doesn’t have a bum shoulder. But you gave her things that people in their seventies would be going through. Is that just to make it more realistic?

SP: Well, yes, and you know it’s sort of a reflection of my own lived experience. I feel lucky to be in as good shape as I am with eighty kind of lurking around the corner, but I was badly injured in a car crash some years ago, and I have residual problems that keep me from scampering up and down mountains, and I just think if you get to be my age and you don’t have arthritis or joint issues or something that’s slowing you down, you have lived a lucky life.

So, I wanted that lived experience to be something that people could relate to as much as I relate to it, or in the same way that I relate to it. We all have these issues that we’re dealing with, and yet people carry on. And I think that was what I wanted to do with Lily to show that you can just keep on keeping on, even when it’s not so easy.

LWH: When you started writing the V.I. series, you and she were roughly about the same age, but you’ve made her age a lot slower than the rest of us mortals.

SP: Right. It’s just one of her superpowers. I had no imagination about what that series would be. I didn’t know it was going to be a series. I wanted to create a woman detective who was sort of turning the tables on the standard roles for women in crime fiction forty-plus years ago.

I thought I would write this book and then I would go back to whatever else I was doing in my life, which at that time was selling computers to insurance agents. I don’t know if I would have made different decisions if I’d imagined that there would be twenty-three more books about V.I. coming down the pike.

I’m not sure when you’re thirty that you can imagine seventy. I looked at my mother who had terrible health issues. She was in her fifties then, and I thought, oh well, you know, it’s just a question of taking care of yourself. And then I got to be fifty and had my own issues. Life gives you these slaps in the face to tell you to stop being so arrogant.

LWH: Why do you think it was important to have Lily suffering from some of the physical ailments that she does versus making her full of vim and vigor and in great physical shape?

SP: Well, I want a character who readers can connect to. I think everyone second-guesses themselves. Everyone makes choices that they may look back on with regret, as Lily does. She’s thinking back over her long life of service and realizing that there were things that she just didn’t see. They were going on in front of her and she didn’t see them.

I hope people can see their own experience in her, even if their experiences are, I don’t know, in a small town in the Midwest, as opposed to hiking up and down mountains in Central Asia. They’ll see that fundamentally she’s the same kind of person they are and if she can achieve her aspirations, other people can achieve their own aspirations.

LWH: What can you do with a the character in her seventies that you couldn’t or wouldn’t be able to do with with V.I.?

SP: One reason that I took a break from V.I. was that she was getting to be too much like me. Too worried, too feeling overwhelmed by responsibility, too much questioning her judgment. V.I. is supposed to be a lot more—I don’t know if carefree is the right word, but bold. V.I. is supposed to be bolder.

Lily is effective, but she’s not bold in that way. She’s someone who is more emotionally akin to me, I would say. And I was letting V.I. get too much into that space, and it was—I don’t want to say destroying her as a character, but it was making her character less what I wanted her to be. Bringing Lily on board and letting her have that part of my emotional landscape now makes it possible to have V.I revert more to what should be her character.

LWH: Does Lily being as old as she is make her sort of invisible to people and let her go about her skullduggery or whatever she needs to get done?

SP: Yes. There is no disguise like being an old woman in America. I’ll tell you one of the things that I loved about Grace and Frankie in the first episode with Lily Tomlin and Jane Fonda, is they’re in a liquor store and they cannot get the attention of the person behind the counter because he’s talking to some young person and he just doesn’t register two old women and so they shoplift. It’s like: why not?

LWH: There are going to be readers who read that Sara Peretsky has a new novel out, but they might be upset that it’s not another V.I. story. Do you think there’s going to be appeal, that people are going to enjoy reading both characters, both series?

SP: Well, I hope so. I mean, I’ve had very good feedback from the people who read the book so far. It was published September twenty-four in England. They usually publish the same month as my American publishers, but for some reason they were two months ahead or six weeks ahead. But they sold out their first printing the first week it was on sale, so that that’s encouraging that English readers were willing to take the chance on a new character.

LWH: There are many novelists who have died but their characters keep going. Robert Parker, his Spenser novels are still being written by other people, but Sue Grafton said no, the alphabet ends with Y. What is your thinking? What is your preference?

SP: Oh, I’m with Sue on that. You know, this is my voice. This is my presence in the world. V.I. is too much part of my life, and I feel the same way about Lily. There are other people who write better than I do. There are other people who write faster, more creatively, but it wouldn’t be my voice.

Years ago, the Japanese took over the Macallan whisky company. I was talking to one of the master distillers in the Highlands, and he said that that Suntory was bringing him over to be their lead tester on their chemically created version of Highland whisky, and he said, “They have the Scottish water, they have the Scottish peat, they have the Scottish barley, but they don’t have the Scottish air, and it is not Scotch.”

That’s how I feel about my character. You might have the storyline, you might have the name, but you don’t have my spirit, and so it is not V.I.

L. Wayne Hicks is a freelance writer who covered real-life crimes for newspapers in Florida and Colorado, and has written profiles of many mystery writers including Sara Paretsky, Michael Connelly, John Dunning, Donald J. Sobol, and Stephen White.
