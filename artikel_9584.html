Cellular coverage ends where the map gets interesting: hiking trails, open water, disaster zones. Satellite direct-to-device fills that gap, and the first use cases are deliberately narrow: emergency SOS, remote tracking, short messaging. Narrow is fine. When someone is lost with no bars, a 160-character message to a satellite is everything.

The standard path runs through 3GPP Release 17, which adapted NB-IoT protocols for non-terrestrial networks. NB-NTN gives device makers a defined air interface instead of a proprietary one. But the standard only describes the conversation. It doesn't solve the physics of having it from a wrist.

Smartwatches are the hardest NTN platform in consumer electronics. The ground plane is tiny, the battery holds a few hundred milliamp-hours, and the antenna sits against a human body that absorbs RF for a living. Everything that follows is about those three constraints.

This article examines the physical-layer realities, RF front-end architectures, and empirical link budgets required to establish and maintain a 3GPP Release 17 NB-NTN connection directly from a wrist-worn form factor.

Standard satellite downlinks utilize Circular Polarization (typically RHCP) to eliminate Faraday rotation effects in the ionosphere and to mitigate orientation-dependent cross-polarization fading. Generating high-purity circular polarization (axial ratio < 3 dB) across a hemisphere requires either:

Dual-point fed stacked patch antennas, or

Multi-arm crossed planar inverted-F antennas (PIFAs).

In a 42-46 mm chassis  accommodating a patch antenna thick enough to achieve usable bandwidth at S-band (e.g., 3GPP Band n256: 2170–2200 MHz DL, 1980–2010 MHz UL) or L-band (Band n255: 1525–1559 MHz DL, 1626.5–1660.5 MHz UL) is physically prohibited by the Z-height consumed by the OLED panel, touch sensor, logic board, and battery.

Consequently, wearable implementations frequently make a deliberate trade-off: implement a linear (or imperfectly elliptical) radiator and accept a theoretical 3 dB polarization mismatch loss against the satellite's circular wave. Real-world axial ratios on wearable antennas typically degrade to > 8 dB off-boresight, meaning the front-end budget must explicitly absorb this 3 dB hit rather than burning PCB volume on complex phase-shifting networks

At 2 GHz, the free-space wavelength (λ) is 150 mm. A resonant half-wavelength dipole requires 75 mm. A typical watch PCB provides a continuous conductive chassis of only 30 to 40 mm (0.2λ to 0.26λ). Because the ground plane cannot support resonant return currents, the entire metal perimeter of the watch—including the exterior bezel and titanium or aluminum mid-frame—must serve as the radiator.

A common implementation is a chassis-integrated slot antenna or multi-segment inverted-F antenna (IFA) where mechanical isolation breaks in the frame act as tuning apertures. In free-space, these designs can achieve peak gains of +1 to +2 dBi. However, once placed on the body, biological loading reduces the total radiated power (TRP) and total isotropic sensitivity (TIS) significantly.

The defining number in NTN work is path loss: more than 160 dB between a wrist-worn device and a low-earth-orbit satellite. Every dB in that budget is contested. Antenna gain, front-end losses, and transmit power all get spent against it, and there's no margin to waste.

The power amplifier is where the budget meets the battery. The link needs enough effective radiated power to close, but the PA has to deliver it without overheating the device or draining a 300 to 500 mAh battery in minutes. PA efficiency isn't a nice-to-have here. It's the difference between a feature and a demo.

Link margin management is the operational answer. Under foliage, body blocking, or bad orientation, the device trades data rate for stability: lower-order modulation, more repetitions, narrower bandwidth. The link gets slower so it can stay alive. Designing that fallback ladder, and deciding when to climb it, is core NTN firmware work.

To evaluate feasibility, we analyze an uplink and downlink link budget for a Low Earth Orbit (LEO) satellite at a 600 km orbital altitude, observed at a 30° elevation angle.

Slant Range and Free Space Path Loss (FSPL)

The slant distance d between the wearable terminal and the satellite is calculated as:

d = sqrt(R_E^2 * sin^2(θ) + 2 * R_E * h + h^2) - R_E * sin(θ)

FSPL = 20log10(d) + 20log10(f) + 20*log10(4π / c)

At an S-band uplink frequency of f = 2.0 GHz:

FSPL = 20log10(1.093 x 10^6) + 20log10(2.0 x 10^9) - 147.55 ≈ 159.27 dB

At S-band downlink (2.18 GHz), FSPL ≈ 160.02 dB.

However, if the user turns their body so that torso shadowing occurs (inducing an additional 10 to 15 dB attenuation), the link margin falls below zero. The modem must drop down the Modulation and Coding Scheme (MCS) ladder and engage repetition coding (N_rep = 4, 8, 16 ... 128), as standardized in 3GPP TS 36.211.

LEO satellites move fast, and that motion shows up as Doppler shift. The device has to track the frequency offset continuously and compensate, or the signal slides out of the receiver's window mid-message. Doppler compensation isn't optional in NTN. It's part of the baseband's job description.

The watch's own GNSS receiver helps more than you'd expect. Position and velocity from GPS give the modem a head start on satellite acquisition and synchronization: it knows which satellite should be overhead and roughly what Doppler to expect. Sensor fusion between GNSS and the NTN stack shortens the search and saves battery.

Then there's the wrist itself. Antenna impedance changes as the wrist moves, as skin contact changes, as the strap tightens. Dynamic impedance matching adjusts the antenna tuning in real time against body loading. Without it, the carefully tuned lab performance evaporates the moment someone actually wears the device.

Validation cannot rely on standard terrestrial multipath chambers. The long propagation delays (10 to 40 ms) and dynamic orbital trajectories require specialized emulation methods.

Testing must use hand and wrist phantoms formulated to mimic human tissue dielectric properties per CTIA OTA Test Plan standards:

Relative permittivity (εr): 35 to 42 between 1.5 and 2.2 GHz.

Measurements must capture three-dimensional radiation performance:

Upper Hemisphere Total Radiated Power (UH-TRP): Quantifying total energy emitted into the skyward hemisphere (2π steradians), rather than standard total spherical integration (4π), since power absorbed by or directed through the arm does not contribute to the satellite link.

Upper Hemisphere Total Isotropic Sensitivity (UH-TIS): Evaluating the receiver's sensitivity specifically against signals originating above the artificial horizon.

Human-in-the-loop testing is the part teams underestimate. Phantom wrists, tissue-equivalent arms loaded with the device, let you measure total isotropic sensitivity and total radiated power across arm positions without booking field time. Field testing still matters, hiking trails and open sky, but the phantom catches the regressions first.

Two test dimensions deserve their own plans. The wrist-to-sky geometry changes everything: a hiker's raised arm and a runner's swinging wrist are different RF environments, and emergency use has its own posture. Multi-satellite handover testing verifies session continuity as the device moves between satellites, because a dropped session during an SOS is the failure that matters most.

SAR compliance constrains the uplink directly. High-power satellite bursts have to stay within RF exposure limits, which caps how aggressively the device can push power to close a marginal link. The regulatory team isn't a downstream reviewer here. They're a design input.

The sky-view user experience is a design problem most cellular engineers never face. The user has to orient the watch toward the satellite, which means the UI has to guide them: where to point, when the satellite passes, how long the window lasts. An SOS feature nobody can aim is a failed feature.

Battery budgeting closes the loop. Deep-sleep modes and wake-up signals have to be tuned around satellite paging latency, which is nothing like cellular paging. Every milliamp spent listening for a satellite that isn't there is battery the SOS won't have later.

The trajectory points from NB-NTN toward 5G RedCap NTN: higher bandwidth, richer wearable applications, the same physics. The antenna and link-budget disciplines don't change. The data rates do.

There's a reasonable future where satellite-to-wearable becomes a standard outdoor safety feature, as unremarkable as GPS is today. Getting there runs through the unglamorous work: antenna-chassis co-design, software-defined tuning, and validation that treats the wrist as part of the RF system.

Direct-to-satellite connectivity in a smartwatch cannot be solved through brute-force RF power scaling. Operating at the limits of battery capacity and chassis volume requires balanced physical compromises:

Closing this link demands deep co-design across mechanical packaging, structural RF, antenna tuning, and baseband firmware. Wearable NTN terminals demonstrate that even highly constrained link budgets can be made operational through targeted RF architecture and disciplined physical integration.

The watch is the smallest satellite terminal ever mass-produced. Making it work took every dB the physics allowed and a few the engineering created. That's the job.
