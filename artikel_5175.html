Singapore and Germany Test CBDC Settlement Without Joining Their Ledgers

Singapore and Germany have tested a way to settle foreign-exchange transactions using digital central-bank money while keeping their underlying systems separate. Project Simbolon, announced on November 16, uses cryptographic proofs to coordinate the two payment legs. Its interest lies in a practical design choice: institutions may be able to exchange value without first agreeing to run everything on one shared ledger.

The Monetary Authority of Singapore’s release says DBS and Deutsche Bank participated, with technology from Partior. The experiment covered euro–Singapore dollar payments in the central banks’ test environments. Zero-knowledge proofs verified settlement conditions without direct technical integration between the ledgers.

The intended payment-versus-payment arrangement makes each final transfer dependent on the other. Imagine a bank paying euros to receive Singapore dollars: it wants assurance that its counterparty’s payment will complete before its own funds become irretrievable. Making both legs conditional addresses that exposure at settlement. It does not make an unfunded account suddenly able to pay.

The central banks’ November 2025 cooperation agreement set out an ambition to improve cross-border transfers and develop common standards for payments, FX and tokenized securities. Simbolon is a milestone within that work, rather than the launch of a generally available international payment service.

Keeping ledgers independent can make collaboration more manageable. Each operator may retain its own infrastructure, access rules and upgrade process. The connecting arrangement then needs precise answers about which evidence is accepted, how long an instruction remains valid and what happens when one environment stops responding.

Those questions are related to the Meridian FX APAC trial covered by NewsBTC, although the two projects should not be conflated. Both address coordination across settlement systems; Simbolon’s announcement specifically centres on the euro–Singapore dollar test and its use of cryptographic proofs.

A treasury desk usually wants to know when it must commit funds and when it can use the proceeds. An arrangement that reduces uncertainty could allow it to fund a transaction closer to completion. The economic advantage would come from the timing of usable balances, not simply a faster message between computers.

MAS’ broader Project Guardian framework treats interoperability, custody, legal finality and operational resilience as connected requirements for tokenized markets. It spans several kinds of assets and money. That breadth matters because a securities transaction may involve more than a currency exchange: ownership records and collateral obligations must also remain consistent.

The challenge resembles the one behind delivery-versus-payment tools for tokenized securities. Coordinating asset and cash movements can reduce exposure between them, but the contractual rights associated with both sides still require a clear legal basis.

The central banks identify performance, legal, regulatory, operational and governance work as areas for further exploration. They also say a fuller technical report will follow. No commercial launch date or measured cost reduction is established by the announcement.

That leaves useful questions for the next stage. A production arrangement would need to explain recovery after an interrupted transfer, the treatment of expired instructions and which institution resolves a disagreement about completion. Testing those situations would show whether the connection is dependable when conditions become awkward.

Simbolon provides evidence that independent digital infrastructures can coordinate an FX settlement in a controlled setting. Turning that result into a financial service will require the institutions to make the same coordination reliable, legally effective and understandable to the people managing real balances.
