Shoppers ask AI for Black Friday deals, but few let the bots pay

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Black Friday is the biggest test yet for AI shopping agents. Amazon is blocking Meta’s Muse, and only 23% of US shoppers trust AI to pay for them.

Traffic from AI services to US retail sites more than doubled on Thanksgiving from last year, Adobe told the Associated Press. Shoppers spent a record $7.1bn online that day, up 10.4%. Adobe expects $12.9bn on Black Friday itself.

Purchases made by AI agents are a far smaller slice. They made up 0.4% of global e-commerce transactions and 0.2% of their value, according to Bain & Co research. Annie Palmer reported the figures for CNBC.

This weekend is the biggest test yet for the shopping agents from Meta, Google, OpenAI and Amazon. Each wants to help people find gifts and, in time, buy them. The early evidence points to three problems. Shoppers do not trust the bots with their money. Many retailers will not let them in. And nobody agrees who pays when an agent gets it wrong.

A Morning Consult poll for Visa found that 54% of adults have used AI for at least one shopping task, up from 47% last year. Only 23% trust generative AI to make payments for them, Phil Wahba reported for Fortune.

The price of the item matters too. In an American Express survey, 52% of US consumers were happy for an agent to buy cheap items such as groceries. Only 15% felt the same about electronics and appliances, the big sellers of Black Friday.

That caution has some basis. Visa sent 22 AI models on 15 shopping tasks earlier this month, and they failed 42.8% of their runs. In more than half of the failed runs, the agent still bought something.

British shoppers are trying the tools too. Meta launched Muse in the UK this week, with partners including Marks & Spencer, Sainsbury’s, JD Sports and Currys. A third of people planning to shop in the sales intend to use AI, up from 19% last year, according to a YouGov poll. Almost half of the shoppers who use AI worry about biased or sponsored results, Sarah Butler reported in The Guardian.

Even shoppers who want an agent to buy for them may find it cannot. About half of big retailers and popular brands do not let AI assistants shop their sites. London software firm Human Forward found this after testing ChatGPT, Gemini, Muse and other agents on 200 websites, Spencer Soper reported for Bloomberg.

Amazon has gone furthest. It blocked Muse from its store in September. It is also suing Perplexity over the shopping agent in its Comet browser, in a case due for trial next year. Amazon is reportedly in talks with Meta about Muse, but the block is still in place.

“Agentic third-party applications such as Muse have the same obligations, and we’ve requested that Meta remove Amazon from the experience,” an Amazon spokesperson told Bloomberg.

Amazon’s advertising business is expected to bring in $81.9bn this year, Bloomberg reported. Bots that browse for shoppers could undercut it. Chief executive Andy Jassy told investors in October that outside agents were not meaningfully hurting its ads business.

“The natural question is whether agentic shopping from external horizontal agents is meaningfully impacting Amazon ads, and the answer is no,” Jassy said on Amazon’s earnings call.

Other companies limit agents rather than ban them. eBay, United Airlines and Kohl’s restrict what agents can do or block them, CNBC reported. Some retailers let bots browse but stop them at the checkout. Agents that do get in can still get stuck at logins and CAPTCHA tests.

Maia Bittner, chief product officer at fintech startup Imprint, told CNBC she asked Muse to buy windscreen wipers from AutoZone. Seventeen hours later, the agent said AutoZone had cancelled the order over an error. Its suggestion was to buy them on Amazon herself.

The bigger question for retailers is liability. Some have written their answer into their terms of service. Best Buy treats an agent’s purchases as if the customer had made them.

“Best Buy is not responsible for conduct, errors, actions, or inaction by any Digital Agent,” the company’s terms state.

Card and payment companies are taking on some of that risk. American Express has said it will protect eligible customers from charges caused by an agent’s errors. Stripe, whose Link wallet works with Muse, offers a refund guarantee on some agent purchases. JPMorgan Chase went the other way last month and blocked outside agents from acting for its customers, citing security, CNBC reported.

In the UK, TrueLayer is testing another route. Its pilot lets an agent pay by bank at Papa John’s, under the FCA’s watch.

“In Europe especially, retailers are asking a simple question: if the bot gets it wrong, who’s liable?” Mark Howell, director of European retail at e-commerce firm Rithum, told The Guardian.

Meta says that in the UK, Muse must ask for approval before it buys anything. A separate “security agent” checks its actions against the permissions a user has granted.

The tech companies are pressing on. Muse reached 10 million users in the US and Canada about six weeks after its September launch, Bloomberg reported. Mark Zuckerberg told investors in October that shopping was its most common use, with users spending $200 on average. He expects Meta to take a small fee from transactions. Google has launched Universal Cart, which puts items from several retailers in one basket.

McKinsey estimates agentic commerce could generate $1tn of US economic activity by 2030. Retailers, meanwhile, are bracing for more AI-driven cyberattacks around the sales.

Matt Kochmann, a tech executive, tried ChatGPT, Muse and Claude to find a case for his new iPhone. None of them stood out, he told Bloomberg.

“It just doesn’t feel magical yet,” he said.
