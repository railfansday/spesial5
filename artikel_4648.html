Is It True That PDA Is Driven by Anxiety?

Posted November 26, 2026 | Reviewed by Lybi Ma

Pathological demand avoidance (PDA) has quickly become the prevailing explanation in online parenting communities for children with challenging behavior, while oppositional defiant disorder has taken a backseat.

Parents of both describe very similar challenges: kids who are highly emotionally reactive, refuse everyday requests and expectations (“demands”), argue with adults, become easily frustrated, and blame others for their own behavior.

Despite the challenges being very similar, the online narratives that supposedly underlie these behaviors are quite different between PDA and ODD. ODD is often portrayed on social media as willful, anger-driven behavior, while PDA is presented as an involuntary response to anxiety or a threat to autonomy.

Parents seem to gravitate to the second explanation because it offers a more compassionate perspective. Seeing our kids as scared invites us to comfort them, but seeing them as angry makes us feel we need to correct them.

An important distinction that gets lost in this discussion is that psychiatric diagnoses do not include proposed causes for the condition and instead describe patterns of symptoms and behaviors.

For example, take major depressive disorder. The diagnostic criteria include sadness, hopelessness, loss of interest in formerly enjoyable activities, and so on, but the criteria do not include a “chemical imbalance.” The DSM or ICD don't include proposed causes because a clinician must give a diagnosis even if the underlying mechanisms are not well understood.

The same principle applies to ODD. The diagnosis describes patterns of argumentativeness and resistance to following adult directions, but, like every other diagnosis, it doesn’t require us to establish an underlying motivation or driver.

But maybe PDA really is driven by anxiety? Although this has not yet been established scientifically (it’s been proposed, not established), a child who meets the full diagnostic criteria for ODD can still receive that diagnosis. If the criteria for ODD don’t require anger to be the underlying cause, then identifying anxiety doesn’t automatically invalidate it.

Of course, we should continue to better understand possible underlying causes, including anxiety-driven avoidance. But “the behavior is driven by anxiety” is not, by itself, a reason to conclude that ODD isn’t an appropriate diagnosis.

The idea that PDA is driven by anxiety while ODD is driven by anger sounds pretty straightforward, but the evidence doesn’t support such a clear-cut distinction.

Children with ODD can also experience significant anxiety (Boylan and colleagues, 2007). Conversely, parents interviewed about children with PDA-type behavior also report that they are often angry (O’Nions and colleagues, 2018).

As I’ve proposed elsewhere (Sunseri, 2026b), anxiety might not drive demand avoidance at all. Instead, anxiety could very well be the natural byproduct of years of conflict and upsets with adult authority figures (“anxiety is the exhaust, not the engine”).

As a clinician, I might watch a child refuse to follow a direction, start yelling, and move into having a tantrum. However, I only know what the child did and can’t tell from the behaviors alone how much anxiety, anger, or both contributed to the upset.

I was curious to see how well represented PDA and ODD are on Facebook. I hypothesized that parents are being exposed to much more PDA content, so I searched for groups that were devoted exclusively to PDA or ODD. I was able to identify 44 PDA-related groups and only seven ODD-related groups.

Now imagine you’re a parent raising a very challenging child, and you want to learn more. You might join a group (or several), and you repeatedly find the same “explanation.” Support and community can help, of course, but if someone keeps reading the same information over and over, it will start to feel like a fact. Even if the parent first lands on an ODD group, invariably someone there will comment, “This sounds like PDA.”

PDA groups are also quite insular and often very hostile when someone challenges any of the prevailing narratives. As I have discussed elsewhere, researchers and clinicians are attacked and ultimately removed from the group (Sunseri, 2026a), leaving parents with the false impression that other views don’t exist.

Commercial influences have a strong incentive to maintain the “PDA is anxiety, and ODD is anger” argument: “Your child has a fundamentally different condition. Therapists don’t understand it, but I do. Conventional treatments don’t work, but I can help you.”

For the hundreds of PDA “coaches” advertising in these PDA groups, acknowledging that PDA and ODD are likely the same condition undercuts their business model. If well-researched and established treatments exist for kids who resist following directions, are highly emotionally reactive, and engage in near-constant conflict with others, why would a parent pay for their alternative?

Challenging the well-publicized commercialized narrative is similar to what my wife encountered working in public health; she and her colleagues tried to encourage families to adopt healthier eating habits. Tremendous resistance from well-funded, highly visible fast-food companies with a strong financial incentive drowned out voices advocating healthier alternatives. It’s hard to convince a kid to eat an apple when there’s a McDonald’s on every corner.

It’s important to note that there is truth in what parents say about traditional therapy not helping them and providers dismissing the seriousness of their child’s behavior. Treatment can fail when it is poorly delivered or adapts insufficiently. These kids are legitimately hard, and parents’ lived experiences are real, but an unsuccessful course of treatment does not establish that evidence-based approaches do not exist or that a coach selling a more reassuring story, free of any obligation to report measurable outcomes, is a better alternative.

Effective treatments do exist. Parent behavior training has demonstrated benefits for disruptive behavior and noncompliance in autistic children (Bearss and colleagues, 2015). Coaches routinely tell parents that “behavioral approaches don’t work with PDA,” but the right ones are highly effective.

A 2026 review proposes a comprehensive, family-focused approach to addressing behavioral difficulties, anxiety, and family distress in autistic children (Shehu and Orhani, 2026). The authors recommend parent-mediated behavioral interventions as the treatment foundation, coupled with family-based CBT for co-occurring anxiety and systemic family therapy for communication and relationship difficulties.

Families don’t need to settle a debate about labels before seeking help for their child. They need real treatment guided by science, evidence, and measured outcomes, and not by presumed mechanisms pushed by commercial interests.

The question isn’t which explanation sounds better. It’s which approach actually helps your child.
