New Research Strengthens the Case for Emotional Dysregulation in ADHD Criteria

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

The new “severe-combined” ADHD subtype identified in a landmark study is marked by emotional dysregulation, a symptom the DSM-5 doesn’t include in its diagnostic criteria.

When the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) was updated in 2022, officials believed that research was lacking to expand ADHD’s definition to include the common and extremely impairing symptom of emotional dysregulation. Consequently, the overwhelming and uncontrollable emotions reported by many patients remain largely unrecognized as ADHD manifestations.

Now research appears to be catching up to patient reports.

A new landmark study has revealed three ADHD subtypes that reflect neurobiological differences: predominantly inattentive; predominantly hyperactive/impulsive; and the new severe-combined, which is marked by emotional dysregulation.1

Using neuroimaging and computational modeling to map the pathways of ADHD, researchers found that the severe-combined type had 45 areas of brain abnormality tied to emotion and control compared with 26 each for the two other types. The study, which analyzed 1,154 brain scans of children and adolescents with ADHD, was published recently in JAMA Psychiatry.

The findings are “an example of recent insights emerging from neuroimaging that may impact clinical care, which has been an elusive goal,” says F. Xavier Castellanos, M.D., professor emeritus of child and adolescent psychiatry at New York University’s Grossman School of Medicine. He was part of the working group that contributed to the DSM-5.

“Still, it’s too early to assume that their intriguing results are real in the sense of replicating repeatedly in independent datasets, particularly by investigators who are not allied or collaborating with the original research team,” says Castellanos. He, like the study authors, calls for prospective studies to validate their biotypes and determine whether they may influence treatment.

Findings that reveal a new severe-combined subtype may come as no surprise to the thousands of parents of children with ADHD, and adults with the condition, who have named emotional dysregulation as the most impairing feature of ADHD in ADDitude surveys.

But, as David Anderson, Ph.D., senior psychologist at the ADHD and Behavior Disorders Center at the Child Mind Institute, points out, parents’ anecdotal feedback precedes scientific validation.

“Finding evidence of a subtype in the brain leaves open questions about the balance of nature versus nurture. Is there a biological basis for subtypes and is it reliable across the span of development?” Anderson asks. “Emotion dysregulation can be biological for kids who are more prone to emotional behavior from an early age, while some are frustrated with the world due to negative feedback they’ve gotten since kindergarten, so it’s environmental. There are multiple pathways to come to this.”

If the study’s findings are replicated, Castellanos says, it would become a new presentation in a future version of the DSM. “I served on the DSM-5 committee, and we were explicitly reminded that the DSM follows the field; it does not lead it. It’s only when the scientific evidence is strongly supportive that DSM changes are allowed. Otherwise, it’s too disruptive.”

Stephen V. Faraone, Ph.D., distinguished professor at SUNY Upstate Medical University, said he believes that emotional dysregulation was not included in the DSM-5 because it might have made differential diagnosis with anxiety and mood disorders more difficult, especially in primary care. He also said he was skeptical that ADHD can be divided into biological subtypes, adding that underlying ADHD brain mechanisms exist along a spectrum and do not fall neatly into separate categories.

The study’s researchers said the findings may eventually create a path toward developing personalized therapeutic strategies.
