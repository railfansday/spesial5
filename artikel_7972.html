Ten Deaths & 5,400 Cases Later, The U.S. Has Lost Its Measles Elimination Status

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

The Pan American Health Organization (PAHO), a subgroup of the World Health Organization, will make the official announcement today.

Measles has made headlines time and time again over the past two years, as cases have hit record highs and vaccination rates have reached record lows. Now, the looming threat has come true: according to sources familiar with the vote, the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) revoked the United States’ measles elimination status.

The group is expected to make a formal announcement today.

To date, the U.S. has racked up more than 5,400 confirmed measles cases in 2026, the most to be reported in the United States in 35 years. There have been 10 reported measles-related deaths so far, including an infant in Wisconsin earlier this month. Measles was considered officially eliminated in the U.S. in 2000, meaning there had been no large outbreaks or uncontrolled domestic transmission of the virus for at least 12 months prior.

America’s current measles outbreaks first began in January 2025 in West Texas. By January 2026, the spread continued unchecked. PAHO’s meeting to discuss the country’s measles elimination status was originally scheduled for April 2026, KFF reports, but the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) asked for more time to determine whether measles cases were being transmitted domestically or “imported” (for example, if someone contracted measles abroad and then brought it to the U.S.). According to NBC, measles is now considered endemic to the U.S.

Many issues have contributed to the country’s ongoing measles outbreaks, from sweeping funding and staffing cuts to public health agencies to a lack of leadership — there was no Senate-confirmed director of the CDC for an entire year during this measles outbreak. But the main driver is declining vaccination rates, which experts blame on the Trump administration, specifically HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr, as Scary Mommy previously reported.

The CDC and American Academy of Pediatrics both recommend children get their first measles vaccine dose between 12 and 15 months of age, and the second dose between 4 and 6 years of age before starting school. According to CDC data, 96% of measles cases in 2026 have occurred in unvaccinated individuals. A KFF poll found that roughly one in five adults believe getting the measles vaccine is more dangerous than becoming infected with the measles. Parents are commonly exposed to vaccine misinformation, and data shows it is affecting their health decisions: the number of kindergarteners with vaccine exemptions rose to 4.2% in the 2025-2026 school year, the highest in the country’s history, with 39 states falling below the 95% vaccinated threshold needed to prevent outbreaks.

“The expected loss of America’s status as having eradicated measles will be a tragedy because it reflects the preventable loss of human lives. It is unthinkable that people in the United States should be dying from measles in 2026,” said Wendy S. Armstrong, M.D., president of the Infectious Diseases Society of America, in a publicly issued statement. “Our government’s leaders and public health officials must heed this urgent warning and take actions that slow the spread. That starts with promoting vaccination against measles, which is proven to be safe and effective.”

The largest outbreak in the U.S. since it earned measles elimination status, prior to the current one, was in 2018-2019. It was curtailed through a strong federal and state-level public health response, including closing schools where transmission occurred, prohibiting unvaccinated children from attending school, and fining parents for not vaccinating their children, KFF reports. The CDC also declared a public health emergency and made “clear statements about the importance of measles vaccinations.”

But last week, CDC Director Dr. Erica Schwartz told Reuters that the agency will not make any major changes to their handling of the outbreak regardless of PAHO’s decision. The CDC will continue to “help [the states] provide awareness about ​measles, emphasizing, reemphasizing that the number one way to prevent measles is the MMR (combined measles, mumps and rubella) vaccine,” she said.

She attributed the ongoing spread of measles to “closely knit communities” — several outbreaks have started in religious communities, like among the Pennsylvania Amish, where vaccination rates are lower than other parts of the country.

“These communities typically don’t vaccinate their children...their families were making decisions about the MMR vaccine 20 to 30 years ago and we’re just now seeing the results of those decisions,” Schwartz said.

In response, the AAP and nearly 150 other health care organizations published a letter calling on Kennedy Jr. and Schwartz to develop and implement a plan to eliminate measles in the United States. Their recommendations include restoring evidence-based immunization policies, proactively encouraging measles vaccination, and conveying accurate information about measles and MMR vaccines.
