Unconventional resistivity reduction in NbP semimetal nanoribbons

Nature volume 659, pages 979–986 (2026) Cite this article

Modern and future electronics harbour a key challenge: as conventional metal wires shrink to nanoscale dimensions, their resistivity increases sharply1 owing to surface and grain-boundary scattering. To circumvent this unwanted behaviour, here, we explore the fundamental limits of charge transport in topological semimetal niobium phosphide (NbP) nanoribbons down to approximately 6-nm widths, with industry-friendly top-down nanofabrication. The extracted NbP nanoribbon resistivity reaches 18 ± 4.1 μΩ cm at room temperature with an approximately 9-nm2 cross-sectional area, lower than that of conventional metals and semimetals with comparable size. The NbP nanoribbon resistivity decreases with decreasing width, unlike the classical behaviour of metals such as copper. This trend suggests a growing relative contribution of surface and edge states as the nanoribbon cross-section is reduced, despite a certain amount of disorder, including edge roughness. Our findings advance the understanding of size-dependent transport and scaling in this class of semimetals, offering a platform for nanoscale wires and interconnects in future nanoelectronics.

a, Schematic of the scaling mechanism for conventional metals, with surface scattering and grain boundary scattering. b, As thickness and width of NbP are reduced, the conduction is consistent with the growing dominance of surface carriers, which are disorder-tolerant, with reduced scattering. The total sheet conductance in this case is a sum of the sheet conductance contributed from bulk carriers and surface carriers. c, For thick NbP, bulk carriers dominate the transport, and thus conventional scaling laws apply, with pronounced surface and grain boundary scattering. d, The Fuchs-Sondheimer (FS) and Mayadas-Shatzkes (MS) model fitting of 3.4 nm Nb seed layer, showing an excellent fit. The extracted resistivity with increasing width (W → ∞) is 56.3 μΩ∙cm, which matches the literature results. e, Sheet conductance (GNbP,□) of 1.5 nm NbP as a function of width (red curve), extracted from Fig. 2d of the main text. The sheet conductance from bulk carriers (extracted from Hall measurement) follows the conventional FS-MS scaling model (black dashed line), where the sheet conductance from surface carriers increases as the width decreases due to the increased surface area/volume ratio, as indicated by the purple dashed line. At this ultrathin thickness (1.5 nm), bulk carriers experience strong scattering, resulting in very small bulk carrier sheet conductance. f, GNbP,□ of 10 nm NbP as a function of width (blue curve). At NbP thickness of 10 nm, 3D bulk carriers and 2D top and bottom surface carriers contribute comparably to transport, as shown in Fig. 4b. At this thickness, as the nanoribbon width is reduced, the enhancement in surface conduction is insufficient to offset the increased scattering of bulk carriers. Consequently, bulk transport dominates in this regime, leading to a net reduction of sheet conductance (i.e., increased resistivity) with decreasing width, more similar to conventional metal wires. We note that there could be subtle differences in sidewall roughness and damage in the thicker nanoribbons compared to the thinner ones. See Supplementary Note 2 for the modelling details and more discussion.

a, Slab model from the NbP (001) film with thickness d = 1.5 nm and width W = 4.4 to 5.7 nm. b, The one-dimensional (1D) band structure of a W = 5 nm nanoribbon along ky direction. The edge projection is highlighted in purple to show the edge states, while the bulk states are shown in pale blue. Near EF, the edge-projected subbands form an electron-like pocket, consistent with the negative pedge values (panel f). c-e, The spin projection to Sx (c), Sy (d), and Sz (e) components, respectively, for the edge state. The blue and red represent opposite signs of spin polarization. These edge states exhibit a pronounced spin texture arising from strong spin-orbit coupling (SOC) in noncentrosymmetric NbP where Sx and Sz components are locked to the ky momentum in a Rashba-type SOC. This reduces the wavefunction overlap between counter-propagating modes, thereby suppressing the leading backscattering channel, which indicates high-mobility edge conduction. f, The carrier densities from edge (pedge) and 2D film (p2D) are extracted from linear fitting of the total ribbon density (p1D) by p1D = pedge + p2DW, at varying W. Wc denotes the cross-over width at which the edge carrier density would dominate the transport over the top and bottom surface carrier density. Here, Wc ≈ 0 indicates that the enhanced edge conduction of ultrathin films is not primarily driven by an increased edge carrier density. Instead, it is more plausibly attributed to an enhanced carrier mobility arising from reduced backscattering as the edge dimension approaches the quasi-1D limit. See more details in Supplementary Notes 2 and 8. g, Agreement between the nanoribbon fitting (\({p}_{2{\rm{D}}}^{{\rm{NR}}}\)) and that obtained from independent slab film calculations (\({p}_{2{\rm{D}}}^{{\rm{slab}}}\)) which corresponds to the d = 1.5 nm thickness in Supplementary Fig. 10. This agreement supports the self-consistency of our calculations across both the nanoribbon and thin-film geometries.

a, Temperature-dependent effective resistivity (ρeff) of NbP films with different thicknesses up to 90 nm, measured from Hall bar devices. These films have a 3.4 nm Nb seed layer, whose resistivity is measured separately (black symbols). b, Low-temperature limit (2-10 K) of panel a. The smallest superconducting transition temperature Tc (~2.2 K) is in the 3.4 nm Nb layer (black symbols), while higher Tc are observed in the NbP/Nb films, and Tc decreases monotonically with thicker NbP. The symbol colours follow the convention in panel a. c,d, Temperature-dependent sheet conductance (GNbP,□) (c) and resistivity (ρNbP) (d) for all NbP thicknesses, after subtracting the Nb seed contribution. e, ρNbP as a function of NbP thickness at 300 K and 10 K. For comparison, the ρNbP values of nanoribbons with the widest widths (Fig. 2d of the main text) are slightly higher than those from Hall measurements at 300 K, possibly due to process-induced damage during nanofabrication. f, Temperature-dependent ρeff of a ~5 nm-thick Nb control film, measured from a Hall bar device. The residual resistivity ratio (RRR) is 1.48, and the Tc is ~4.2 K. Inset: enlarged view of the low-temperature region. The presence of NbP appears to promote superconductivity in NbP/Nb films. The superconducting proximity effect is one plausible interpretation, although changes in disorder and enhanced inter-grain coupling (in the presence of NbP layer) could also contribute. We also cannot fully rule out the formation of an interfacial superconducting layer at the NbP/Nb interface. The NbP sheet conductance is obtained by subtracting the sheet conductance of the Nb layer (measured independently on control Nb films) from the total sheet conductance of the NbP/Nb stack. The extracted NbP resistivity is then obtained by dividing the NbP thickness by the NbP sheet conductance, i.e. ρNbP = dNbP/GNbP,□, where GNbP,□ = Gtot,□ - GNb,□ and Gtot,□ = dtot/ρeff, where Gtot,□ is the total sheet conductance of the NbP/Nb stack, dtot is the total stack thickness and ρeff is the measured effective resistivity (panel a). The GNbP,□ (panel c) represents the NbP sheet conductance after subtracting the independently-measured Nb seed contribution. Additional validation of the subtraction method using Pt/Nb stacks is provided in Supplementary Fig. 19. Control experiments using Pt/Nb stacks validate that the subtraction procedure itself does not generate an anomalous resistivity trend. Nevertheless, the conductance of the Nb seed layer within NbP/Nb stacks cannot be independently measured and it cannot be said, with absolute certainty, that its behaviour is identical to that of the separately measured Nb control film. Thus, the extracted NbP resistivity relies, by necessity, on the model used.

NbP films exhibit a pronounced increase in mobility upon cooling due to suppressed phonon scattering, whereas the 3.4 nm Nb film shows only a slight rise in mobility, indicating that its transport remains strongly limited by surface scattering.

All measurements were performed with the magnetic field applied perpendicular to the film plane and current direction. At low temperatures, all films exhibit a pronounced negative cusp near low field regions, arising from the interplay of superconducting fluctuations and weak antilocalization. d-e, Longitudinal sheet resistance (Rxx) vs. magnetic field (B) measured at 1.8 K for both 1.5 nm thick NbP (on 3.4 nm Nb seed layer) and for the Nb layer (3.4 nm) evaluated separately. Measurements were performed with B field perpendicular to the film plane and current direction (d), and with B applied in-plane (e). The superconducting transition temperatures are Tc ~ 6 K for the 1.5 nm NbP (on Nb) and ~2.2 K for the 3.4 nm Nb film (see Fig. 3d of the main text). The upper critical field Hc2, defined as the midpoint between the normal state resistance and zero resistance, is extracted. At 1.8 K, the out-of-plane critical field \({H}_{c2}^{\perp }\) is 2.2 T for NbP (1.5 nm) and 0.38 T for Nb (3.4 nm). The in-plane critical field at 1.8 K exceeds 9 T for NbP (1.5 nm) and reaches 4.44 T for Nb (3.4 nm). The superconducting coherence length estimates are provided in the supplement. The sense current used for Hall bar measurements is 0.5 mA, corresponding to a current density of ~0.1 MA/cm2 for the NbP (1.5 nm) film and ~0.15 MA/cm2 for the 3.4 nm Nb control, with the same 100 μm Hall bar device width. We note that this sense current is well below the superconducting critical current: even when we increased the sense current up to 10 mA, we were unable to suppress the superconducting state at 1.8 K.
