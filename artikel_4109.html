Review: The Legend Of Zelda: Ocarina Of Time (Switch 2) - A Stunning Remake, But Not Quite Definitive

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

You’re not ready for this. No matter how many trailers you’ve watched or how much pre-release footage you’ve pored over, walking out into Hyrule Field for the first time in the Zelda: Ocarina of Time remake is a revelatory experience.

It’s easy to forget how impactful Hyrule Field really was in the N64 original. While the game itself is still held in remarkably high regard, its main hub world has arguably been supplanted by later entries, particularly Breath of the Wild's enormous, immersive open world. It’s pioneering, yes, but it’s also quite small and empty when viewed through a modern lens.

The remake, with its breathtaking visuals, modernised controls, and revamped soundtrack, makes it feel new again. Standing at the entrance to Kokiri Forest and panning the camera around to soak in all of the details, I had to take a beat to stop myself being overcome by the enormity of the moment.

Making everything feel new again is really at the heart of the Switch 2 remake. I want to be clear: this is basically the same game, but Nintendo has tweaked just enough to make it feel fresh. Whether it’s the slightly different chest location for the Kokiri Sword or the Cucco hiding spots in Kakariko Village, there will always be something — however small — to give you pause.

The biggest change is the visuals. Much like Star Fox, Ocarina of Time is a staggering upgrade over the original, offering an almost overwhelming amount of detail across the board. From the small trinkets on Link’s tunic (who, by the way, is definitively 'Link' here, with no renaming options) to the way shards of broken pots stay on the ground and move underfoot, every aspect has been overhauled to take advantage of Switch 2’s power.

Yet despite the almost hyperrealistic visuals, it retains the original's spirit. There was a lot of discourse about the presentation when the remake was first properly revealed, particularly around characters’ faces. Any doubts about the approach will fade away as soon as you start playing. Everything looks right; whether it’s Link’s design or Zora's Domain, this is Ocarina of Time through and through. If you’re one of the many fans who were hoping to see the Spaceworld 2000 and Wii U tech demos properly realised, this is it and more.

The visual upgrades extend to the dungeons, of course, and it's crazy how impactful they are on your enjoyment. Inside the Deku Tree feels like a living world with realistic bark walls and copious spider webs. Likewise, the shimmering reflections and ethereal soundtrack completely transform the Water Temple. Despite minimal changes to the gameplay and structure — minor alterations to puzzles ultimately have little impact — the dungeons are a joy to explore once again.

It’s not all perfect, though. Keeping such a stunning game running at 60fps on a handheld takes some jiggery-pokery, and Nintendo's solution has resulted in a few visual blemishes. Assets in the distance — mountains and trees — look overly smooth and there’s some shimmering going on when you pan the camera around. It’s nowhere near as severe as Xenoblade Chronicles X on Switch 2, but it’s definitely something you’ll notice occasionally, particularly during handheld play.

Overall resolution takes a notable hit undocked, too, to the point where even Link himself can look a little fuzzy at times. It definitely needs mentioning, although it wasn't significantly detrimental to my experience. Keeping the game running smoothly feels like the more important priority, and Nintendo has nailed that aspect.

The audio has also been completely revamped, as well. The soundtrack, while familiar, has been reworked with new orchestral renditions, and everything from the Kokiri Forest track to the final, epic battle sounds incredible. Key cutscenes also feature voice acting, and it's some of the best I've heard from a first-party Nintendo game in quite some time. Your fairy companion Navi, whose in-game interruptions have been drastically cut down, is delightfully cheerful.

Exploring Hyrule and taking in the new visuals is made all the more satisfying thanks to the new control scheme. I’ve mentioned panning the camera around a couple of times, but it’s worth stressing that full camera control is completely new - a strange thing to highlight in 2026, but the ability to properly absorb everything this world has to offer really enhances your relationship with it. You feel so much more involved, and it helps that locations like Hyrule Castle Town have been upgraded to take advantage: no more pre-rendered backgrounds, you can now soak in all of the glorious detail.

The other major addition is the ability to jump whenever you please. Again, how quaint! Yet leaping at the press of a button gives you so much more autonomy over Link’s movements, even if it doesn’t open new routes or opportunities during your quest. You still jump at the same moments to scale platforms and solve puzzles, but doing it yourself without having to run off a ledge to trigger it is both quicker and more satisfying.

Combat feels comfortingly familiar: you lock onto your enemy and slash at them with ‘Y’, altering the direction of your swing by tilting the left analogue stick. You can hop about, perform a backwards somersault, and execute a leaping attack with either ‘X’ (the default jump button) or ‘A’, while holding and releasing ‘Y’ triggers the spin attack. It’s basically the same – why fix what isn’t broken, right?

Yet every attack now lands with such ferocity — accompanied by a searing red flash and a deafening crash — that combat feels completely overhauled. Again, old is new. Indeed, it’s so effective that it even makes Tears of the Kingdom's clashes feel comparatively tame.

Making use of your various weapons and items is also much easier than before. By holding down ‘R’, you’ll bring up an item inventory that instantly looks familiar if you’ve played TOTK or Echoes of Wisdom. Displayed in a single line, you quickly scroll left and right for your weapon of choice, whether it be a Deku Stick, a Bombchu, or the Megaton Hammer. The selection is relatively limited compared to, say, Echoes of Wisdom, so I never felt the UI was unwieldy or overwhelming; I always found what I was looking for straight away.

Meanwhile, key items like the Ocarina, masks, and Iron Boots are mapped to the D-pad. The latter is especially useful in the Water Temple, which gained its reputation as a major sticking point partly due to the need to repeatedly access the main menu to equip and unequip the boots. Tapping down on the D-pad is a lot more intuitive and makes for a breezier experience.

You can also do this for the newly-designed Lenses of Truth, which function exactly as the original Lens of Truth but have been revamped as a pair of goggles instead of a magnifying glass. It makes so much more sense!

The game is littered with similar tweaks, many of which have a noticeable quality-of-life impact. Reloading now drops you in the exact location you last saved (instead of at your house as Young Link and the Temple of Time as Adult Link). Elsewhere, the new Echo Gem, a reward from the House of Skulltula quest that replaces the Stone/Shard of Agony, shines and vibrates as you approach any Gold Skulltulas.

As for the Ocarina, you can play tunes in a variety of ways, the most basic of which is by simply pressing specific inputs via the face and shoulder buttons. Holding down ‘ZL’ brings up a menu of songs you’ve learned so far - handy for a quick reminder at any given moment.

However, you can also use the Switch 2’s built-in mic to input tunes using your own voice. Simply hum the tune you want and it’ll mostly pick it up with no fuss. You have the option to practice tunes if you wish, and it makes for a cute, if gimmicky way to use the Ocarina.

I’ve not been able to test Hori’s Electric Ocarina (and who knows where Nintendo’s official instrument is at), but I whipped out my guitar to test a few songs, and lo and behold, it actually worked. It certainly won’t be my go-to method, and chances are I’ll just stick to the buttons, but it’s neat.

I've not been able to test the Zelda Notes features during the review period, but you might find it a worthwhile addition if you're keen to create and share Ocarina songs. If you have some Zelda-themed amiibo lying about, tapping one during gameplay yields a few bonus items (arrows, recovery hearts, bombs, etc.), but thankfully nothing that could be deemed essential. All welcome additions, though.

What hasn’t been added for the remake is the Master Quest, and this is a real bummer. Available via a special GameCube release and as an unlockable in the 3DS remake, Master Quest proved an excellent way for veterans to test their skills upon completing the game. Given how closely the Switch 2 remake sticks to the core structure of the original, veterans will breeze through with minimal issues. And if you do ever get stuck, the new Threads of Time feature, which essentially documents your progress, will help you get back on track.

I died just once during the entire journey because I just got caught off-guard by an Iron Knuckle in the Spirit Temple. You can increase the difficulty slightly by altering the UI to show either minimal information or no information at all, but it’s no replacement for the changes that Master Quest could have brought. I'm hopeful that it will be added via an update, but it’s a glaring omission.

The lack of multiple save files is also disappointing. Yes, you can just create additional profiles at a system level, but the game allows you just one manual save along with several autosaves. Sometimes you just want to experience the start of the game again without impacting your main save file, so this is a shame.

The one last major addition is The Hall of Fallen Foes, and this is a cool, albeit inconsequential bonus. Accessed by sleeping in a bed after a specific campaign moment, here you can gaze upon statues based on enemies you’ve slain. It’s a lovely way to properly appreciate their updated designs, and you’re also given the opportunity to replay boss fights or even take on multiple bosses in succession. The rewards for doing so are useless, but the act of optimising your battles and beating your time is fun enough on its own.

And so, even with all that knowledge, you're really not ready for this. The visuals, the gameplay, the audio – everything has been thoughtfully upgraded to such a degree that this will completely replace the original for many. While the lack of Master Quest means it falls short of being definitive, it's still a remarkable remake, and a must-buy for Switch 2.

Ocarina of Time on Switch 2 is a stellar reimagining of the original, one that completely revamps the experience with stunning visuals, modernised gameplay, and vastly improved audio. Despite some minor blemishes, this is comfortably the most visually stunning work Nintendo has ever put out, and it’s unquestionably the best way to play one of the most seminal games of all time.

That said, it is a very safe remake. Despite lots of minor changes throughout, it’s still essentially the same game you’ve likely already played, and so you might find it offers little challenge if you’re familiar with the N64 original or 3DS remake. This makes the lack of Master Quest — or really any kind of hard mode — particularly glaring, but I’m hopeful it’ll be added in the future.
