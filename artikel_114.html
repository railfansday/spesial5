In further comments to Russia’s Vesti news, Medinsky said there is no prospect of territorial exchanges between Russia and Ukraine as part of a peace deal.

“The topic of possible territorial exchanges is periodically raised from various sides,” Medinsky said.

“After Ukraine’s invasion of the Kursk region, a decision was made – the president has spoken about this repeatedly – to create a security zone along the border with Russia. This zone is now quite substantial, it is expanding, and will continue to expand. I see no possibility of exchanging these territories.”

He was referring to Ukraine’s incursion into Russia’s Kursk region in August 2024, when Ukrainian forces crossed the border and seized territory in one of the most significant cross-border operations of the war. Zelenskyy said at the time that the operation was aimed at creating a buffer zone to protect communities in Ukraine’s neighbouring Sumy region from Russian attacks. The incursion was also widely seen as potentially giving Kyiv leverage in future negotiations.

Thousands of people are marching through Warsaw to mark the 108th anniversary of Poland regaining independence in 1918.

The annual march which brings together thousands of Poles remains deeply divisive, given some violent clashes in the past and the attendance of nationalist and far-right groups from Poland and abroad.

But despite regular criticism, the march has in recent years become increasingly mainstream, taking the role of the largest mass independence day celebration in the country and attracting more conservative Poles to take part.

Poland’s conservative president Karol Nawrocki was seen walking in today’s procession, which organisers hoped would attract up to 500,000 people.

By mid-afternoon, no major incidents had been reported, other than the widespread use of red flares along the route of the march, Poland’s state broadcaster TVP Info reported.

As Reuters noted, organisers argued that the ⁠gathering, held under the motto “A Pole is the host in Poland,” is their way of expressing national pride.

“We want a sovereign Poland, independent of foreign capitals ... We also ​envision a Poland that ​is as monoethnic as possible,” a spokesperson for the march organisers Rafał Buca said.

Poland’s centrist prime minister Donald Tusk – a regular target of chants, banners and often abusive taunts at the march – stayed clear of the event, attending a smaller celebration in Szczecin instead.

“I ‌address everyone, including those who will be gathering in Warsaw. We may hold different views, ‌we may keep each other in check, and at times we may even be political opponents; yet, anyone who truly cares about Poland must understand that this space, defined by independence, freedom, and a secure Republic, is a common good,” he said.

The march comes less than a year before the 2027 parliamentary election, which will see the current centrist government, led by Donald Tusk, face a group of conservative, nationalist and libertarian right parties in what is likely to be a polarising, bruising contest.

Opposition leader and former prime minister Jarosław Kaczyński told Onet that he hoped to maintain the unity of right-wing forces ahead of the election, despite the fact that “even today, during this march, there are some unpleasant elements.”

Bulgaria is marking a day of national mourning today after eleven people died and more than 50 were injured in a mass pileup in thick fog on a motorway in southeastern Bulgaria, AFP reported.

Crashes snared around 50 cars and trucks heading in both directions of the highway linking the capital Sofia to the Black Sea city of Burgas, regional governor Georgi Chalakov told the BTA state news agency.

The incident took place in thick fog, drastically reducing visibility for drivers.

Bulgaria saw 71 road fatalities per million inhabitants in 2025, the highest rate in the European Union, according to European Commission figures.

In neighbouring Spain, millions of people across the country have joined the country’s first general strike in 14 years, setting up picket lines to protest against soaring housing prices before an election in which the far right could be catapulted into power.

At the centre of Wednesday’s nationwide 24-hour stoppage, singularly backed by the country’s largest unions as well as tenants’ associations, is a pushback against Spain’s deepening housing crisis.

Sandra Mora, of the Madrid Tenants’ Union, told the broadcaster RNE:

“It’s extremely important to remind people that we, the workers – who are also renters or who also pay mortgages – are the ones with the power to halt production and bring the economy to a standstill.”

While workers’ unions reported high participation in the strike across several sectors, including 90% in the automotive and port sectors and 82% in waste collection, they said participation was uneven across the country.

The strongest support was reported in coastal regions, such as Catalonia and Andalusia, where the soaring cost of housing has been particularly acute.

Portugal closed all schools and shuttered non-essential public services on Wednesday as a major Atlantic storm headed for the country’s mainland, Associated Press reported.

The government told people to stay at home as heavy rain and rough seas were expected to batter the Portuguese coast, with wind gusts of up to 140 kph (85 mph).

It was the first time in living memory that Portugal’s roughly 8,000 schools had canceled classes nationwide over a storm forecast, the AP said. An international technology conference in Lisbon attended by around 70,000 people called off the day’s events.

Airlines ⁠cancelled more ​than 200 flights in and out of Portugal, Reuters reported.

The brunt of Storm Charles was expected to hit the Atlantic coast on Wednesday afternoon and evening, coming from the southwest after it had struck the island of Madeira on Tuesday with 130 kph (81 mph) winds.
