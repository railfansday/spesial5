Why the American West Is the Perfect Setting for a Crime Novel

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Before we can answer the question of why the American West is the perfect place for a crime story, we need to clarify what the American West is. This is necessary because there seems to be a great deal of confusion on the subject. You can see this in almost any man-on-the-street interview which quickly reveals that the average American’s understanding of geography is less a body of knowledge than it is a collection of strangely confident opinions. For instance, people who live where I do, in Nevada, might look at Boston, New York, Philadelphia, Baltimore, and Washington and think, “You people are practically sharing a driveway.” Similarly, many people who live along the Eastern Seaboard believe that anything west of the Mississippi River qualifies as the West.

For purposes of crime fiction, I propose that the American West should be considered any place that meets all five of the following criteria:

(1) It’s a Place Where Nobody Can Hear You Scream

I’m not kidding. The West has a lot of empty space. In Tallahassee or Atlanta, if someone screams at 2:17 a.m., seventeen people call 911. In Nevada, there may not be another person within 47 miles. This is both atmospheric and convenient for murder. And for crime authors. The amount of open space is incomprehensible to people who live in tighter confines and where the horizon often consists of a strip mall, a water tower and a billboard advertising personal injury attorneys.

Now, don’t get me wrong. There are a lot of desolate areas out here. But while there might not be any humans within earshot of your scream, rest assured the coyotes, wolves, bears, and other high school mascots can hear you. When you scream in the West, be careful, lest you inadvertently ring the dinner bell for some other kind of serial killer.

(2) It’s a Place Where You Can Hide a Body Almost Anywhere

When human remains are discovered in urban areas, there’s a good chance they’ve haven’t been hidden for more than ten minutes. That’s because there’s no good place to dispose of them without being caught on someone’s cell phone video. If you hope to hide your victim, you have to have a space in grandma’s wall or have a large, airtight cooler. In the West, you have deserts and mountains, some with places where no human has ever left a track. You have canyons so vast that the chances of finding a body begin to depend more on plate tectonics than police work. You have ghost towns with mine shafts so deep that any body placed in one will have to be carbon-dated if and when it’s ever found.

(3) It’s a Place Where Everyone Has a Truck

This solves an enormous number of plot problems for western crime authors. Need to transport a body? Truck. Need to follow someone into the wilderness? Truck. Need your protagonist to spend three chapters driving while contemplating the meaning of life? Truck.

Why does everyone in the American West have a truck, you ask? Because daily life demands one. Just watch any of those YouTube channels featuring people who camp in places a normal vehicle could never get to. I’m convinced many of these YouTube campers are serial killers. Also, in a lot of places, there are so few people that everybody knows everybody, so any crime committed has to be hidden where the eyes of prying neighbors aren’t focused. To get there, you need a truck. Trust me.

(4) It’s a Place Where Everyone Owns a Gun

As a writer of crime fiction, this is particularly useful because you can eliminate several pages of explanation.

In the American West, there are more guns than there are people. Most people own multiple firearms, and children are introduced to them shortly after learning to walk. Sometimes earlier. You may argue the merits of this…until little Tommy uses that .22 you bought him to shoot the rattlesnake that’s about to bite his sister. If, some years later, Tommy turns that same rifle on a person, well, that’s the makings of a pretty good crime story.

(5) It’s Place That Gives Your Detective Something Beautiful to Look at While Brooding

And this is, perhaps, the most important thing. The West is practically a literary service.

Mountain ranges. Sunsets. Deserts. Snow. Big sky. Canyons.

A detective in the West is never more than a few minutes away from something worth staring at while contemplating a murder, a missing person, a regrettable decision or the general decline of civilization.

Try doing that on the Jersey Turnpike. A detective there walks outside, looks dramatically into the distance and sees a Wawa.

The West gives a crime novelist something even more than just scenery: space. Space for a crime to happen. Space for a secret to survive. Space for a suspect to disappear. Space for a sheriff to chase him. And, most importantly, space for a character to stand on a ridge at sunset and wonder what the hell happened to his life.

For me, geography is everything. It’s as much a character in my novels as my protagonist, Porter Beck. When I build an outline for a new story, I plot the scenery just as I do my character biographies. I plot the time of year, the colors, the smells, the flora and fauna. In some cases – most actually – the physical setting is as much an adversary to Beck as the person he’s trying to stop. It might be snow drifts or wildfires or the relentless summer heat.

It’s the opportunities this landscape provides that make the American West the perfect place to stage a crime novel. Out here, there is room for everything a crime story needs.
