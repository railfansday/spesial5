Hackers have cut power in Ukraine, but could they take down the U.S. grid? 10 cases

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Whether hackers can take down the U.S. power grid has no simple answer, and these 10 cases, safeguards and weak points show what cyberattacks have done and what limits them

A cyberattack has already caused a blackout — just not in the U.S. In December 2015, attackers took over operator workstations at three regional distribution companies in Ukraine and opened breakers. The breaches cut power to roughly 225,000 customers. The outage lasted hours, not days. A year later, malware called Industroyer cut power for about an hour at a transmission substation near Kyiv.

Those attacks raise a question that returns with each new federal warning: could hackers take down the U.S. power grid? The answer depends on what an attacker can reach, how far a failure can spread and how quickly operators can restore service. None of those factors reduces to a simple yes or no.

Stuxnet, discovered in 2010, showed that code can physically damage industrial equipment. It targeted Siemens control systems and damaged centrifuges at Iran's Natanz enrichment plant. It was not aimed at a power grid. In 2023 and 2024, CISA, the FBI and other agencies issued warnings about a China-linked group called Volt Typhoon. The agencies said the group had pre-positioned itself in U.S. critical infrastructure networks, including some in the energy sector. The U.S. government said the aim was the ability to disrupt operations in a future crisis. That kind of access is not the same as an outage.

The shape of the U.S. grid matters too. It consists of three major interconnections, Eastern, Western and Texas, run by thousands of utilities with different systems. That fragmentation limits how far a single intrusion can spread. It also makes uniform defense hard. The North American Electric Reliability Corporation, or NERC, sets mandatory Critical Infrastructure Protection standards for the bulk power system, and FERC approves them. Smaller distribution utilities face fewer mandatory requirements.

Large outages do not require an attacker. The 2003 Northeast blackout affected about 50 million people in the U.S. and Canada. A software bug in an alarm system at FirstEnergy $FE in Ohio, along with overgrown trees touching power lines, contributed to the cascade. Recovery tools exist as well. Black start procedures let utilities restart plants without outside power. GridEx, a NERC-run exercise held every two years, tests how utilities respond to cyber and physical attacks.

The 10 items below are a selection, not a ranked list of threats. They explain what cyberattacks on electric utilities have done, where the weak points sit and what limits the damage an intrusion can cause.

In December 2015, hackers cut electricity to roughly 225,000 customers in Ukraine. It was the first publicly confirmed case of a cyberattack causing a power outage.

The intruders did not need to destroy any equipment to cause the outage. They took control of operator workstations at three regional distribution companies. These are the computers that control-room staff use to monitor and operate equipment across a utility's network from a central location.

From those screens, the attackers opened breakers. A breaker is a switch that stops current from flowing along a line. Operators open breakers as a routine step during maintenance or to isolate a fault. In this case, that ordinary command was the method of the attack.

The method is central to the question of whether hackers could take down the U.S. power grid. The attackers turned the utilities' own control tools against them. Once they held the workstations, they could send the same kinds of commands a legitimate operator would.

The targets were distribution companies. These utilities handle the final stage of electricity delivery. They carry power from the high-voltage network to homes and businesses in their regions. The outage therefore reached customers served by three separate companies.

The U.S. government and the E-ISAC later attributed the attack to Russian-linked actors. The E-ISAC, the Electricity Information Sharing and Analysis Center, gathers and shares threat information for the power sector. The center is operated by NERC, the North American Electric Reliability Corporation.

The outage lasted hours, not days. Remote access to control systems was still enough to switch off electricity for a large number of people. Both facts bear on the question of what a cyberattack on the electric grid can and cannot do.

So far, Ukraine is the only country where cyberattacks have been publicly confirmed to cause blackouts. No confirmed cyber-caused blackout has occurred in the U.S.

In December 2016, malware known as Industroyer, also called CrashOverride, cut power from a transmission substation near Kyiv, Ukraine, for about an hour. The tool itself set the attack apart. It was code that could send commands directly to substation equipment in that equipment's own industrial protocols.

Industrial protocols are the languages control systems use to communicate with physical devices in the field. A utility's control software relies on them to read the status of equipment and to issue commands, such as opening or closing a breaker. Most everyday malware has no use for these languages. It steals data, encrypts files or spreads across office networks. Industroyer was written to talk to the hardware that moves electricity.

That was a step beyond Ukraine's first grid hack a year earlier. The December 2015 attack depended on intruders operating utility controls themselves. Industroyer moved much of that work into software. A program that issues breaker commands on its own reduces the need for attackers to work the controls by hand. It also packages the knowledge needed to manipulate grid equipment into code.

The level of the grid that was hit also differed. The 2015 attack struck distribution companies, the utilities that deliver power to homes and businesses at lower voltages. The 2016 attack struck a transmission substation. Transmission is the high-voltage part of the grid that carries electricity in bulk over longer distances. Power is stepped down from there for local delivery. A transmission substation therefore sits upstream of the lines that serve customers.

The physical result was limited. The outage was brief, and the incident did not become a lasting regional blackout. For anyone asking whether hackers could take down the U.S. power grid, the case points in two directions. Malware built specifically for grid equipment exists and has worked against a real substation. That single strike still produced a short outage rather than a prolonged collapse.

Industrial protocols are not unique to Ukraine. Utilities in many countries, including the U.S., use control systems that communicate with field equipment in this way.

Stuxnet, discovered in 2010, was malware that damaged centrifuges at Iran's Natanz enrichment plant. It did this by targeting Siemens control systems. That made it a clear case of software breaking physical machinery.

Many cyberattacks stay inside the digital world. They steal data, lock files or knock websites offline. Stuxnet went further. It reached the systems that tell industrial machines how to behave, and the harm landed on the equipment itself.

Centrifuges at an enrichment plant spin at very high speeds to enrich uranium. Control systems govern that motion. Stuxnet attacked that control layer. The result was broken hardware, not only a disrupted network.

That difference shapes recovery. A disrupted network can often be brought back once malicious code is removed and systems are rebuilt. Damaged machinery cannot be restored that way. It has to be repaired or replaced before it runs again.

Stuxnet was not aimed at the power grid. It did not cause a blackout. Its target was a uranium enrichment facility in Iran, and no electric utility was involved.

Its relevance to electricity comes from what it demonstrated. Power plants and substations also run physical equipment through industrial control systems. Circuit breakers open and close on command. Generators respond to control signals. Stuxnet proved that malicious code can reach that kind of control layer and turn digital commands into physical consequences.

The same distinction would apply on the grid. An intrusion that only opens breakers leaves equipment that operators can close again. An intrusion that damages generators or substation hardware would leave a repair job behind it.

That proof has limits. The code targeted Siemens control systems, and its known damage was at Natanz. An attack on a U.S. utility would have to match the equipment and software that utility actually uses. That is a separate challenge, and the Natanz case does not answer it.

The U.S. power grid is not one machine that a single switch could turn off. It is made up of three major interconnections — Eastern, Western and Texas, known as ERCOT. Thousands of utilities run the equipment inside them.

That structure shapes any answer to whether hackers could take down the U.S. power grid. An attacker who breaks into one utility's network has reached one operator, not the country. The division into separate interconnections also sets boundaries on where trouble can travel.

Those boundaries change what a nationwide outage would require. An attacker aiming at the whole country would need footholds inside all three interconnections, not just one of them.

The utilities inside those interconnections do not share one common set of computers or software. Each runs its own systems. An intrusion built around one company's setup may not carry over to the company next door.

For anyone trying to cause a nationwide blackout, that variety is an obstacle. One piece of malicious code or one stolen login does not open every control room at once. Reaching more operators means getting past more separate systems.

The same fragmentation creates a problem for the people defending the grid. With thousands of operators running different systems, there is no single standard setup to lock down. Security work has to happen utility by utility rather than all at once.

The result is a trade-off rather than a simple answer. The grid's patchwork design limits how far one breach can spread. It also makes it hard to raise defenses evenly across the whole network.

The three-interconnection layout is part of the answer to how vulnerable the power grid is. A breach at one utility reaches that utility's systems. Spreading an outage beyond it runs into separate operators, different technology and the boundaries between the Eastern, Western and Texas interconnections.

NERC, the North American Electric Reliability Corporation, sets mandatory cybersecurity rules for the bulk power system. These rules are the Critical Infrastructure Protection standards, known as CIP. FERC, the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission, approves them.

The arrangement splits the job in two. NERC writes the requirements, and a federal regulator signs off on them. Utilities within their scope must follow them. They are not optional guidance. The mandatory status means compliance does not rest on each company's own judgment about what is worth doing.

The bulk power system is the high-voltage backbone of the grid. In general terms, it includes large power plants along with the transmission lines and substations that carry electricity over long distances. A failure at that level can affect customers across a wide area.

The CIP standards are written for that backbone. A compromised control system at a major transmission substation can affect far more people than a fault on one neighborhood line. The rules concentrate on the equipment where a breach would reach the most customers.

That focus leaves a gap. Smaller distribution utilities face fewer mandatory requirements. Distribution utilities deliver power over local lines to homes and businesses. Distribution is also the level where an outage reaches customers directly.

Fewer requirements do not mean none. A smaller utility still runs systems that keep power flowing to its customers. The difference lies in how much of its protection is set by a mandatory standard and how much depends on choices the utility makes for itself.

The result is uneven coverage. The CIP standards set a mandatory floor for the parts of the grid with the widest reach. They do not apply to every utility to the same degree. For anyone weighing grid cyber risk, the question is not only whether rules exist but which parts of the system they cover.

Colonial Pipeline shut down its pipeline operations in May 2021 after a DarkSide ransomware attack. The case shows that a breach on the business side of a company can stop physical operations.

Colonial Pipeline is a fuel pipeline company, not an electric utility. The attack did not cause a power outage, and it was not a strike on the grid. It still belongs in a discussion of grid cyber risk. It shows how critical infrastructure can stop working without an attacker ever taking control of physical equipment.

Ransomware is malicious software that encrypts a victim's files or systems and then demands payment to restore access. A typical infection locks up data. It does not send commands to valves, pumps or circuit breakers.

Industrial companies generally run two kinds of networks. Information technology, or IT, covers the business side: email, accounting, billing and corporate records. Operational technology, or OT, covers the systems that monitor and control physical equipment. In a pipeline, OT controls the equipment that moves fuel. In a utility, it controls the equipment that generates and delivers electricity.

Colonial's breach was on the IT side, yet the operational side still stopped. The company halted pipeline operations itself, as a precaution. A company that cannot yet confirm how far an intrusion has spread may choose to halt operations on its own.

That distinction bears directly on whether hackers could take down the U.S. power grid. Electric utilities run IT and OT networks, as pipeline operators do. Ransomware confined to a utility's business systems would not, by itself, send commands to grid equipment. It could still lead operators to disconnect systems or pause some operations while they assess the damage.

The attacks on Ukraine's grid in 2015 and 2016 took a different route. In those cases, attackers acted on grid equipment directly. Colonial shows a second route to disruption — one in which the stoppage comes from a defensive choice rather than an attacker's command.

The case moves the question of electric grid cyber risk beyond control rooms. Protecting OT networks covers one path to an interruption. Keeping ransomware out of corporate networks covers another. A precautionary shutdown still halts service, whatever triggered it.

Volt Typhoon is a China-linked hacking group that U.S. agencies said in 2023 and 2024 had pre-positioned itself inside U.S. critical infrastructure networks. Some of those networks belong to the energy sector.

The warnings came from several federal agencies. CISA, the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, issued them alongside the FBI and other agencies. The alerts covered critical infrastructure broadly rather than the power grid alone.

The energy sector's place on that list carries extra weight. Electricity supports most other critical services, from water treatment to communications.

The U.S. government also described the group's purpose. It said the aim was the ability to disrupt operations in a future crisis. That stated goal separates the campaign from intrusions built only to steal data. The concern is less about what intruders copy out of a network. It is about what they could do later from inside it.

Pre-positioning means gaining access to a network in advance and keeping it. An intruder who already holds a foothold does not need to break in when a crisis begins. The access stays in place until someone decides to use it — or until defenders find and remove it. Access that sits unused also produces little activity for defenders to notice.

Access is not an outage. The public warnings described intrusions and intent. They did not report any U.S. blackout caused by the group. Holding a position inside a network and cutting power to customers are separate steps.

That distinction shapes where the Volt Typhoon warnings fit in the question of grid risk. By the U.S. government's own description, the group was preparing an ability to disrupt. The warnings did not say that ability had been used against the power grid. For grid operators, the gap between access and action is the window in which an intrusion can still be found and removed.

The 2003 Northeast blackout cut power to about 50 million people in the U.S. and Canada, and no hacker was involved. A software bug in an alarm system at FirstEnergy $FE in Ohio, along with overgrown trees touching power lines, helped set off a cascade that spread across a wide area.

The blackout matters for grid cyber risk because of how it spread. It shows the scale an outage can reach once a local problem moves through connected parts of the system.

A cascading failure follows the physics of a connected network. Electricity flows along every available path between generators and customers. When a line trips offline, the power it was carrying shifts onto neighboring lines. If those lines become overloaded, protective equipment can disconnect them too. Each loss pushes more load onto what remains.

Both contributing causes were common kinds of failure. Trees that had grown too close to transmission lines made contact with them, and that took lines out of service. The alarm software problem at FirstEnergy meant operators did not get the warnings that would normally flag that kind of trouble. Missing alarms make it harder for a control room to act while a problem is still small.

The pairing is what links 2003 to the cyber question. A failure in software, not in steel or wire, played a part in turning local line trips into a regional blackout. In 2003 that software failure was a bug. Operators losing an accurate view of their own system is also an effect that an intrusion into utility control systems could produce.

Fragmentation does not seal every part of the grid off from its neighbors. Sections that are connected share power, so they can also share failures.

The blackout marks a limit on what the comparison proves. It was an accident with physical triggers, not a coordinated attack. It does not show that a hacker could reproduce it. It shows that the grid's interconnected design can carry a local fault far beyond where it started, with or without anyone intending it.

U.S. utilities prepare to recover from a major outage with black start procedures, which restart power plants without any electricity from outside. They also rehearse responses to cyber and physical attacks in GridEx, an exercise that the North American Electric Reliability Corporation, or NERC, runs every two years.

A grid that has gone dark cannot simply be switched back on. If an attack or a failure leaves a region without power, operators need a way to get generation running again with no outside supply to draw on. Black start procedures provide that path.

The procedures are worked out before any emergency. Utilities do not have to invent a restart plan in the middle of a blackout. The plan already exists, and operators know which steps come first.

This bears directly on the question of whether hackers could take down the grid. Causing an outage is one problem for an attacker. Keeping power off is a separate one. A restart plan prepared in advance is aimed at the second problem. It gives operators a known route back.

GridEx covers the human side of recovery. The exercise puts utilities through simulated attacks and tests how their staff respond.

The exercise treats digital and physical threats as part of the same planning problem. A utility facing an intrusion in its networks may also face damage to equipment in the field. GridEx asks participants to handle both kinds of attack rather than one in isolation.

The two tools address different parts of recovery. Black start is about the machines and how generation comes back from zero. GridEx is about the people and organizations that have to make decisions under pressure.

Neither one stops an attack from happening. Both are built for what comes after an attack succeeds. They are designed to shorten an outage rather than to prevent the intrusion that caused it.

Ukraine's power companies restored service faster after their grid hacks partly because operators could switch to manual control. In the U.S., utilities pursue a layered set of defenses — network segmentation, multifactor authentication and monitoring of operational technology — with support from federal agencies.

Manual control matters because the Ukrainian attacks worked through remote systems. An intruder who controls the software that opens and closes breakers makes that software untrustworthy. The fallback is to run the equipment without the compromised network. A utility with trained staff and ready procedures for hands-on operation can restore power while its digital tools are still in doubt.

Network segmentation addresses a different problem. Utilities run two broad kinds of networks. Information technology, or IT, covers email, billing and business software. Operational technology, or OT, covers the systems that monitor and control physical equipment such as substations and generating units.

Segmentation divides these networks and limits the connections between them. The goal is to keep a breach on the business side from becoming a direct path to the machines that move electricity. An attacker who lands in an accounting system should still face barriers before reaching anything that controls power flow.

Multifactor authentication targets the risk of stolen login credentials. It requires a user to confirm their identity with something beyond a password, such as a code from a separate device or a physical security key. A password captured through a phishing email is then not enough on its own to open an account.

Monitoring OT networks means watching the traffic inside control systems for activity that does not match normal operations. Control networks generally carry routine, repetitive commands. An unexpected instruction to a breaker, or an unfamiliar device communicating on the network, can signal an intruder.

The federal government also backs utilities on this front. CISA, the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, works with utilities on cyber defense. The Department of Energy supports them through CESER, its Office of Cybersecurity, Energy Security, and Emergency Response.

None of these measures makes a grid immune to intrusion. Together, they aim to make an intrusion harder to achieve, quicker to detect and less likely to reach the equipment that keeps power flowing.
