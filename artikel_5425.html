Bill Ackman is asking the wrong question about Nvidia

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Ted Glover is a former senior executive at several large multinational companies.

When an executive leaves suddenly, people watch to see whose calls still get returned. If they believe the old boss might be back in the foreseeable future, they have a reason to wait him out. At Nvidia, where Jensen Huang famously had dozens of direct reports, that ambiguity could reach deep into the business. Bill Ackman thinks Nvidia’s board made a mistake by saying it does not intend to reinstate Huang. I think that statement was one of the more defensible things the board did.

Ackman’s criticism of the original decision deserves a hearing. On Nov. 8, Nvidia announced an independent inquiry and expressed confidence in Huang. On Nov. 11, according to its SEC filing, the board accepted his resignation, effective immediately, and announced his departure the next day. Ackman argues that the directors moved too quickly. Chairman Harvey Jones says Huang resigned voluntarily after disclosing conduct inconsistent with company standards. The board has offered shareholders limited detail about what changed. The speed warrants scrutiny, although the public timeline alone cannot tell us what the directors knew or when they learned it.

But the question of whether the board handled Huang’s departure properly is different from the question of what should happen next. Once he was out, the directors could give interim CEO Colette Kress clear authority or leave employees, customers and prospective successors wondering whether Huang would soon return. Ackman sees the board’s rejection of reinstatement as another failure. A serious and highly qualified candidate for the permanent job might see it as a reason to accept the assignment.

When I ran large subsidiaries of major corporations, I would have found it impossible to imagine managing anything close to 60 direct reports. Bloomberg reported roughly that number at Nvidia. Huang’s extraordinary span gave him direct information and influence across the enterprise. Nvidia’s achievements offer a powerful defense of his approach, but they also reveal a succession problem. Access to one exceptional founder by so many direct reports is not an organizational structure that can easily be handed to someone else. Executives accustomed to relying on Huang for decisions almost certainly had fewer opportunities to exercise independent judgment.

The strongest case for Huang is not sentimental. He helped build one of the most consequential companies in modern technology, and his strategic and technical instincts may be unusually difficult to replace. But irreplaceability is a dangerous principle on which to govern a public corporation. The talents that made him an extraordinary founder do not necessarily make him the right leader during a prolonged investigation, legal uncertainty and a wholesale transition in authority.

Kress, who remains chief financial officer while serving as interim CEO, must keep the company moving. The permanent successor will face a different task: determining where authority belongs and making the new arrangement durable. I would allow roughly a year after that person takes charge before judging the transition. Reporting lines can change overnight; confidence in independent decision-making, customer relationships and accountability cannot.

Meanwhile, there is a practical reason to keep Huang away from day-to-day operations. A shareholder derivative lawsuit has named him alongside numerous directors and executives. Investigations and possible additional legal proceedings create uncertainty about the demands on his time; no one can responsibly predict their ultimate scope or outcome. But even intermittent diversions could be consequential at a company whose decision-making has depended so heavily on one person. An adverse finding after reinstatement could force a second abrupt transition. Berkeley law professor Stavros Gadinis has warned that reinstating Huang before the investigation concludes could expose directors to fiduciary risk.

Nvidia is not a distressed company desperately searching for a rescuer. On Nov. 17, it reported quarterly revenue of approximately $115.8 billion, more than double the year-earlier figure. Strong results do not prove the transition will succeed: most of that performance reflects work undertaken before Huang’s departure. They do, however, underscore what is at stake. The board’s task is to protect an exceptionally successful business from a leadership struggle that could unsettle it. Jones has reiterated that the board does not intend to reinstate Huang, and the company has engaged Heidrick & Struggles to help find a permanent CEO.

That stance need not mean throwing away Huang’s expertise forever. If the investigations end favorably, and if the new CEO wants his help, Huang might advise on a particular technical question or customer relationship under clearly defined terms. A board role or limited consulting arrangement might eventually be considered, subject to governance and conflict concerns. Even a return as CEO need not be metaphysically impossible if a successor fails and the circumstances change. But none of those possibilities should become a standing invitation to undermine the person now in charge.

Consider the alternative: a year after the permanent CEO arrives, Nvidia continues to prosper. Customers remain loyal, the leadership team has earned confidence, and executives are making decisions within their own authority. Bringing Huang back then could have more downside than upside, reviving uncertainty and displacing a system that had proved itself. If the transition falters, the board will retain options. What it cannot afford is to deny a successor the opportunity to succeed in the first place.

The board cannot undo the uncertainty surrounding Huang’s departure. But it can prevent that uncertainty from paralyzing Nvidia’s future. Giving a successor the authority to succeed, while preserving the option of drawing on Huang’s extraordinary talents later, is not a repudiation of his legacy. It may be the best way to protect it.
