Why Mike Flanagan Just Couldn’t Kill Carrie White

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Flipping the script from Stephen King's novel and Brian De Palma's film, the 'Carrie' showrunner always knew his version of the iconic character would meet a different fate — even if there was no season two.

Mike Flanagan‘s Carrie, released on Prime Video last month, has been an all-around success. It scored a second season, bringing the story past what those might remember from the Stephen King novel and the 1976 Brian De Palma film. Flanagan’s most excited for the show’s lead, Summer H. Howell, and the rest of the cast.

“Watching the ascension of Summer Howell in real time has been incredible. The showrunner is already gearing up for more, sitting for a conversation in his office earlier this month. He’s back in the writers room and ready to make more of the show after spending some time “unofficially” working on it before it was picked up for the second season. “We’re right into season two, so now the time to enjoy it is over,” Flanagan says. “I’m back to work.”

Below, the writer digs into what it was like to make his version of the iconic prom scene, whether he’s changed his mind on the direction of the story and why he just couldn’t kill off Carrie White.

I spoke with Summer for our Next Big Thing series, and one thing she said to me that stuck out was that you told her Carrie was as much hers as it was yours or Stephen [King]’s. How did you get that across?

It’s a daunting thing for anyone to step into a legacy of any size, but especially one as monolithic as Carrie White. It’s an intimidation that I certainly felt very acutely going into it, and I really didn’t want Summer in particular, but any of our actors to have to shoulder. There’s so much pressure when you know you are inhabiting a character that’s been portrayed as iconically as Carrie White. It’s unavoidable. It was very fortunate that Summer had never seen DePalma’s film and had never seen Sissy Spacek’s incredible performance. I loved that. I really didn’t want to change that because I think [it’s] the only way to get through a situation that can be so fraught with pressure and anxiety and insecurity and second-guessing yourself and constantly trying to toe the line between invention and the courage we ask of actors in roles like this against the history of the part and the public perception and everything else. The only way she could make it through that was if she truly had an enormous amount of agency within the character and if it became hers. We didn’t want to see Summer’s interpretation of or homage to Sissy’s performance. We wanted to see Summer’s Carrie. That was something that was really important to me, and I think ultimately to Summer as well.

To that point, she mentioned that you made it a point to hire several women to help shape this narrative. Why was that important to you?

It’s a story about what it means to be a young woman in a world that fears and misunderstands them. [It’s] about the journey between girlhood and adolescence and young womanhood. I don’t know what the hell I’m talking about, as much as I want to. I want to understand that. But I think a big thing for me was just being able to say, “I don’t know this world. I can’t speak about it with any authority, and the only way this is going to work is if I surround myself with people who do know what they’re talking about and hand them the keys to the show.” It’s something we do for all of our shows, whatever the show happens to be about. There may be, at times, a temptation for creatives to feel like because we’ve been entrusted with a project, we must be an expert on it. Time and time again, in my experience, that hasn’t been true. It’s an inherently and undeniably female story, and it needed inherently and undeniably brilliant women to tell it and to bring it to life. It was a great honor for me to be able to empower them as much as I possibly could.

How’s everything going with season two? Where are you in the process?

I turned in the first episode script earlier this morning, which is very exciting. We’ve been working on it unofficially for a while because I had always hoped that it could go this way and wanted to be prepared in case it did. We’re gearing up to hopefully go into production next year, and we’ll see how it all shapes up. The beautiful thing is that Carrie’s journey has always seemed to me to have a clear trajectory. And that was something, even though we were never taking for granted that it would happen, it was something that was always in the back of my mind. I know it was in conversations that I had with Summer about where she could go and with Steve too — King was very aware and excited about the potential for that. It’s always been in the room with us, but we didn’t really have permission to fully explore it until it was real because you don’t want to jinx it. But we are fully immersed in it. The writers room is firing on all cylinders.

How long into developing the take did you make the conscious decision to change the ending and decide you wanted it to continue? Was that always the intention, or did it happen organically?

It happened organically in breaking that first season, in contemplating this very different version of who Carrie White was. She’s a very different character than she’s ever been. That character — starting her from a place of such kind of wide-eyed innocence and this core of her that’s so earnest and eager and kind and curious about the world. It broke my heart to imagine a version of the story that she didn’t survive. The first time I came back and said, “Well, here’s what I see for a season of Carrie — if it were going to be a limited series, here’s how I would see it.” Even then, it felt right to me that she survived the story and go out into the world. I thought she’d earned it. Whether we’d ever follow her into that world was the open question, but we’d see her go. That was always part of the pitch for it. It’s nice to say there’s potential in a story for the audience to get to continue on the road with the character, but it felt to me that this particular incarnation of the character — I didn’t want to live in a world where she died at the end of it.

This is a very different version of Carrie White than we’ve seen before.

The point of the story was different and killing her at the end, or even just reducing her to a killer at the end, really worked against the purpose of the story for us, and the purpose of the story that Steve was excited about too. The revenge tragedy version of it, we thought, had already been told beautifully and exists in amber forever. What excited me, Steve and Summer, as we got into it, was [that] we’re telling a different story. As we’re talking about cruelty and school violence and reconciling that tug of war in each of us between empathy and kindness and cruelty, what is the message we’re leaving people with about this? The message of kill your tormentors and then be dead. It just doesn’t feel like the story we’re telling. It feels like a horrible message to put into the world, today especially. All of us, Stephen King included, were very committed to a different road for this. It had a different heart to it by the end of it.

You’ve previously said you had envisioned this as a three-part story, so presumably three seasons. Do you still feel that way now that you’re into season two?

I do. I do. There’s a very clean arc. I can see it as a beginning, a middle and an end. The danger’s in the temptation to do more, which is very presumptuous because right now we only know we’re doing two. That hasn’t changed for me at all. It feels like a really clean arc.

There is, understandably, always temptation when things are doing well to do more, but there is a beauty in being able to know when it’s OK to stop.

Television is rife with examples of people who fell victim to that temptation and then had to tread water and do jazz hands beyond the expiration point or to stretch it. There’s so many clear examples of that. They’re wonderful cautionary tales for us.

I can’t think of a moment more etched in pop culture stone than the Carrie prom scene. How did you approach your take on the sequence?

One of the most daunting sequences of my career. Twofold. One was I knew when I agreed to do the project, and when we were going to move forward, I was like, well, I have to do the prom. I have to do that myself. If we’re going to do Carrie, you got to do the prom. The logistics of the scene, it is the most complex sequence of my career.

The shot list for the prom, which starts in episode seven and doesn’t end until halfway through episode eight — just the shot list is so much more voluminous than the script. It’s this huge document, and it’s logistically enormously complicated. We’re tying up the arcs of so many principal characters and so many perspectives. There’s extras and stunts and fire and water and blood and effects and everything. We’re destroying a set as we go through it, so we have to make sure we’re very deliberate about that. We’re stepping into the shadow of one of the most iconic sequences in genre filmmaking history. DePalma’s aesthetic approach to the prom is legendary and full of things that are so, like you said, burned into pop culture that we have to actively avoid them in something like this.

People asked if we were going to do split screens. [I said], “Hell no. We’re not going to do anything that’s going to come anywhere near the aesthetic approach that has come before it. There’s no point. It’s done perfectly.” In the middle of all of that, which was incredibly stressful and huge, I do remember on the second day that we were shooting it… It took us 10 days to film it. It was a lot of work. I was sitting on the stage in the gym and looking out — Summer was over there in her dress and everybody was in place and everything was going on. It occurred to me that I was at the prom with Carrie White, and that’s another feeling that comes out of the fact that this is so ingrained in our consciousness.

I remember grabbing the AD’s microphone and encouraging everybody to put the work down. Just for a minute, put everything down, take a breath, forget about the rush we’re under, take a breath and look around at each other. Everybody take a moment and realize we’re all at the prom with Carrie and just mark the moment a little. Then, from there, [we] went into days and days [of shooting]. We were shooting on a stage, [so] there’s no daylight. We don’t know what time it is. It’s just an endless prom that starts with music and dancing and joy and Summer’s up there beaming. By the end of it, we’re all covered with blood and smoke and flames and water and all of it’s happening to us as a crew while we dismantle and destroy this world that we’ve spent six months building. It was an incredible experience to shoot it. It’s one of my favorite sequences of my career and absolutely the most difficult.
