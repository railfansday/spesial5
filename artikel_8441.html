From Icy Morning to Dead Phone — John Muir Trail Day 22

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

The night before I dropped down steeply from Donohue Pass.  In addition to the extra mileage, I also figured it would be warmer at that lower elevation.  The camping spot I selected, looking out at a huge golden, grassy meadow, was lovely.

Probably it was warmer there, but that did not mean it was warm!  It was already chilly when I went to sleep, and as the night went on it just got colder and colder.  I gradually added items to my sleeping bag — sleeping bag liner, long pants, long shirt, puffy hat, gloves — until I used up the entirety of my anti-cold arsenal.

Fortunately, that did the trick.  But had it been just a little colder, I would have been overwhelmed.  It created a cognitive conflict during my pee errands in the night.  My temperature sensors urged me to return to the tent as quickly as possible.  My spiritual-aesthetic soul urged me to spend just a little more time under the glorious blanket of moon and stars.

Towards morning, I had a bizarre dream.  It this alternate reality, I was having a hot affair with a woman.  Actually, it was a cold affair.  Why?  Because she was really an alien, part of an extraterrestrial race that was thinking of settling on earth, but who didn’t like our climate.  It was too hot for them.  Having superior technology, however, they were able to manipulate Earth climate and make it much colder to their liking.  So, I was freezing cold, despite having this torrid affair.

Another capability of the alien was that she could make you mostly forget what you had done the night before.  So, I kind of knew I was having the affair, but also wasn’t completely sure that I was.  The hilarious part was that the alien in the dream was my wife’s friend DG from middle school!  When I told Lee about the dream, I had to swear that I had never, ever contemplated having an affair with DG!

Did this moonlight view up through the tent inspire my strange dream?

When it began to get light, I gave myself a little bit of a break because it had been so cold.  My last trip outside the tent in the early dawn I looked out on that golden meadow and saw that it was all white, coated with icy rime.

By the time I took this picture, the rime was already melting, but some is still visible

I also retrieved my bear cannister; it too had a layer of ice on the top cover.

In close-up, the ice on the bear cannister is very visible

By this I knew it had gone below freezing during the night.  So, I allowed myself some extra minutes of warmth in the sleeping bag while I waited for the sun to rise above the lip of the mountains.

I finally got up at 6:30.  When the sun came up the temperature rose quickly, but the problem then was that the ice on my tent fly became moisture.  Also, the floor was pitched on a sort of pine needle-covered mud.  This was very comfortable, but it ended up sticking to my ground sheet.  It took some time and effort to get everything dry and clean.

After all the morning errands, I did not get on the road until – wait for it – ten a.m.!  On a short day, that might not have mattered, but I was planning another 17+ miles for this day.  The good news was, that I was camped in the Lyell Valley, and the mostly flat trail followed the Lyell Fork river for almost nine miles, all the way to Tuolumne Meadows.

Sometimes the river and trail were close like this, sometimes farther apart

With this cruisy route (and my late departure) I turned on the jets as I headed out.  With my unfortunate record of falling on easy trails, I was also trying to be careful.

A good portion of the trail, especially in the meadows, was indented into the earth.  My feet were below the ground level in a narrow track.  In this configuration, I find that hiking poles are more hazard than help.  So, for most of the first nine miles, I was carrying my poles rather than using them.

The statistics tell the tale.  Over those nine miles, I was only over 20 minutes (3 mph) twice.  My best mile I did in 17:49.  That is no record speed, but I don’t think I was even close to that pace anywhere else on the trail.

The scenery was interesting, but not spectacular.  The main thing, with this wide-open valley, was that I could measure my progress visually.  I could see an unusual-shaped mountain lining the valley up ahead; then after a time it was closer; then I was opposite to it; and then it was starting to fall behind me.  In a slower progression, I could see the distant mountains at the foot of the valley ahead (where the river changes direction).  At first, they were far distant, then middle distant, and eventually they were quite close.

The wide glacial valley, filled with meadows, and the mountains at the far end that grew larger as I got closer

At faster than 3 mph, I was in Tuolumne Meadows in under three hours.  As I got closer it was apparent there was a tourist center nearby.  Suddenly many more persons were on the trail and most of them were geared out for just a day of hiking or fishing.

The design of Tuolumne was a little weird, with a “downtown” road (Tioga Road) on the north side of the river and a hiking trail parallel on the south side.   I generally don’t love road-walking.  However, because the Wilderness Center (for permits) was in the middle of downtown, and because I wanted a permit to climb Half Dome the next day, I intended to go via the road.

NPS map of Tuolumne Meadows (in the lower right, I should have turned onto the “National Scenic Trail” but instead stayed on the John Muir Trail)

I missed the turn.  Maybe my feet just wanted to stay in the woods?  As I realized my error, I ran into a really nice couple who were staying at the fancy lodge nearby.  They had a map, and they confirmed, “Yeah, you just have to go back to that last turn and then turn to go across the river, instead of going straight.”

I had a sudden aversion to that plan, particularly because of the miles I wanted to hike that day.  The woman must have seen my expression, because she said, “My husband is just like you; he hates to backtrack!”

It was true.  So, I said, “Maybe I’ll go to the Visitor Center instead, and they can help me there.”  I continued on.

Now I was surrounded by roads and parking lots.  As I passed one lot, I saw a uniformed ranger manning the booth and I walked up to him.  When I explained what I wanted, he said, “First of all, to get a permit, you have to go to the Wilderness Center.  Second, they will not give you a permit to climb Half Dome.  The only way to get a Half Dome permit is to get a whole different multi-day permit that includes climbing Half Dome within it.”

Now that had not been my understanding, but he seemed very confident.  At this point I was even further away from the Wilderness Center and even less inclined to backtrack.  I could see my Half Dome ambitions evaporating.  I figured I could still go to the Visitor Center and give it one last try.

En route I saw that I would pass the market that hosted the famous Tuolumne Grill, and I realized that it was just about lunchtime.  I left my pack outside and went in to order.  It seemed a simple layout with some picnic tables outside to eat what you ordered.  I got a regular hamburger that came with orange juice and some wedge potatoes.  The juice tasted delicious on this now-hot day, and the wedges, slathered with ketchup, were good too.  But the hamburger?  It was an ordinary sort of dry hockey puck, not terrible, but not special at all.  I concluded the Grill was famous because of where it was, and not for what it served.

As I ate it all, I took my phone off of Airplane Mode and called my wife, Lee.  (I always hike with it in Airplane Mode to preserve my battery charge.)  When I told her the likely new plan, without Half Dome, she sounded excited that I was almost done.  I was looking forward to seeing her soon as well.

The Visitor Center was a mile-long road walk from there.  I had two animal encounters (of a sort) along the way.

First, I saw wild turkeys!  How funny to spend all that time in the wilderness without seeing any (though I did see grouse).  Then in congested Tuolumne I saw them.  I even got some pictures.

If not for the legs, the turkey blended in…

Second, as I was walking, a car slowed way down opposite me and the driver shouted out his window, “A bear just walked across the road up ahead less than five minutes ago!”

I shouted back, “Good!  I hope I see him, because I am getting really hungry!”

Whether he was pulling my leg, or he really saw the bear, I did not see any bear.  I was definitely looking out for it though.

After pine forest and silence, it was disconcerting to walk on asphalt with diesel engines roaring past me.  I was glad it was only a mile.  On arrival, I went up to the very nice woman in front of the Visitor Center and asked about permits.  No surprise, she directed me to the Wilderness Center.  I concluded the fates did not intend me to climb Half Dome.

Then I asked about getting out of Yosemite when I reached Happy Isles.  She explained about the free shuttle within the park that could get me to Curry Village from Happy Isles if I didn’t want to walk there.  From Curry Village, the YARTS (Yosemite Area Regional Transportation System) could transport me for a fee.  The end of their line was Merced, about three hours away.  I figured I could take a train or bus from Merced, or maybe Gabriel could come from Santa Rosa and pick me up there.

This lady pointed to the YARTS timetable on their bulletin board and suggested I take a picture of it.  I replied, “That’s a great idea!  You’re a lot smarter than I am to think of it!”

She also gave me a paper map of the Yosemite Valley to include Happy Isles and Curry Village, saying, “I know you hikers don’t like to add weight, but I think you might find this useful.”

I told her I was sure I would and stashed it away.

With that, I aimed for the trail, which passed a ways behind the Visitor Center.  It was a relief to be walking on pine needles again!

It is funny how quickly the crowds taper off as you get even a short distance away from the tourist centers.

The other big change was in elevation profile.  In the morning, the trail had ranged from flat to slightly downhill.  Now I would face climbing up past Cathedral Lakes to Cathedral Pass.  This was the last pass on the John Muir Trail.  It was about 9,700 feet which was a lot lower than the passes in the south, but still more than 1,000 feet above Tuolumne Meadows.

Perhaps it was my energy expenditure in the morning, but now I was puffing and panting as I ascended.  “Where is my juice?” I asked myself.  “This is as bad as climbing Cottonwood Pass on the first day of the JMT!”

I was breathing hard, but enjoying the enormous granite rock

The inspiration was the views.  There was more than one rocky shape that could qualify as a “cathedral.”  Imagine Notre Dame.  Imagine that the roof is one thousand feet high, the spires soar over 1,500 feet, and the nave stretch two miles.  Then imagine a landscape dotted with two or three of these behemoths.  It was magical.

About the time my legs finally got warmed up, I came across a couple that were on the first day of their John Muir Trail hike.  The reversal I had imagined, when encountering near-finishers at the start of my own hike, was coming true.  In chatting, he warned me, “Oh, there’s no water except at Upper Cathedral.”

I appreciated that intel and was on the lookout for a spot near Upper Cathedral Creek.  As I ascended, coming above the tree line, I was surprised not to find it. Then, as I passed a quarter mile from Upper Cathedral Lake, I saw some people emerge on a trail from the lake onto the main trail.  Suddenly it dawned on me that there was no creek.  He meant the lake!

It was late afternoon and I wanted to press on and find a campsite.  But if this was really “last water” it would be foolish not to take the detour for it.

The lake seemed to have mostly marshy and muddy access points.  These would entail wet feet and dirty water to filter.  However, I saw a rocky promontory jutting into the lake where a bunch of guys were hanging out.  I headed there to refill my water.

I began chatting with them and learned they were from Kansas City.  I asked them whether they lived in Kansas or Missouri and they said that only someone who knows Kansas City would ask that question.  I explained that I had a cousin who lived in K.C. and they told me they lived on the Kansas side.

Overall, they were nice enough, but the banter occasionally felt a little mocking.  For example, they asked me, “Did you go up Whitney?”   I replied that I had, but that I had been fighting a respiratory infection, making it very difficult.

They responded, “Oh, that just adds to your story about climbing Mount Whitney.  Like, ‘everybody else just climbed it, but I climbed it with a respiratory infection, coughing every step of the way!’”

They were actually not wrong, but the tone felt a little snarky.

After I filled both my water bottles and my Katadyn BeFree to have maximum water available, I headed back toward the trail.  One of the guys, maybe the leader, followed me a short distance and called me over.  He was asking me about the trail, and it turned out he had done the trail himself a few years before.  It seemed a little odd, but I took him as just wanting to reminisce a bit about his own experience.

In a quarter mile I was back at the junction with the JMT.  The beauty of the scenery around Cathedral Lakes is so inspiring, I wanted to take some more pictures.  John Muir himself said, on summiting Cathedral Peak, “This I may say is the first time I have been at church in California, led here at last, every door graciously opened for the poor lonely worshiper.”  (Quoted from My First Summer in the Sierra, Chapter 10, entry for September 7, 1869.)

So, this “lonely worshiper” reached for his smart phone to capture the majestic giants and discovered… nothing.  Nothing at all, a black screen.  Now my eyesight is not great without glasses, but I had thought my phone showed 65% of charge when last I checked.

In case it had just shut off for some reason, I tried to restart it, but still nothing.  I was crestfallen, thinking, “Oh no!  My phone died here on the trail with barely one day left on my hike!!!”

I could not fathom how it happened.  I had not dropped it that I could recall.  Deeply disappointed, I began having paranoid thoughts.  “If I had charge, and now suddenly I don’t, could that guy who followed me, to talk to me, have had some technology that fried my phone?  Like some super magnet or something?”

It seemed wildly implausible, but the coincidence – that it happened right after our exchange – seemed strange.

Then I calmed down somewhat.  I reminded myself of what was truly important,  “This trip is about the experience itself.  It is not ultimately about telling the story or capturing the pictures, it is about your lived experience.”

Seen through that prism my loss was minor and I settled down.   My wife and I always try to distinguish “disappointments” from “tragedies,” and this was clearly the former.

With the light turning to a mellow glow in the sky, I continued hiking.  Cathedral Pass was not much further.   It was unusual, in that the trail reached the pass, but did not descend from there down the other side.  Instead, it continued on, ascending further up the ridge of one of the mountainsides bordering the pass.

Now my lack of a phone was frustrating because I had lost my navigation device.  I was not sure how far I should ideally go before selecting a camp, remembering that I wanted to make miles to ensure I could finish on the following day.

The mountains answered that question for me.  As I continued higher, I came to a turn on the outstretched flank of the range.  Extending from the trail was a large flat spot, with visibility in all directions.  The ground was ideal for pitching a tent.  The views were magical in the waning light.  I thought, “I can go further, and shorten my day tomorrow.  Or I can camp tonight in the most beautiful spot in the Sierras.”  That decision was an easy one.

In Tuolumne I heard we were in for another icy night, and I was now at a higher elevation than I had been the night before.  So, I was expecting cold.  In fact, as the sun dropped behind the peaks, I could feel the chill almost immediately.

I started to pitch my tent, and maybe I was in too much of a hurry?  I love my Big Agnes TigerWall, but on this night, as I tried to get a pole into the slot, the pole snapped.  After the dead phone, this might have been a devastating blow.  But in this stunning location, on the last night I needed a tent, it did not phase me.  Besides, I had been carrying a seemingly useless stupid little tube for miles and miles, wondering why I bothered, and now I had my answer.  I slid the tube over the broken spot, taped it in place with surgical tape (though that might have been unnecessary), and reseated the pole in the slot.  Good as new?  Maybe not, but close enough.

Having pitched my tent, I just basked in the beauty of the location for a few minutes.  Then I soaked my food and found a spot for a cat hole and dug what was likely to be my last cat hole on the trail.  The cold soak did not take long, but the result was the opposite of the previous night’s dinner.  That food had not rehydrated fully but was wonderfully seasoned and tasted delicious.  This night the mashed potatoes and chicken hydrated easily, but they were totally bland.  They didn’t taste bad really, they just didn’t taste like anything at all!

Of course, mindful as always that this is fuel first and flavor second, I wolfed it all down.

With no phone, I was communicating with my family by Garmin.  That was so onerous, typing out everything, one letter at a time.  (On the trail I learned of an app that makes this easier, but I didn’t have either the app or a working phone.)  Gabriel seemed amenable to picking me up in Merced, and so I realized I needed to make big speed the next day to get to Happy Isles.  The 4:50 p.m. YARTS departure to Merced arrived by 8:30 p.m.  Even that would mean getting back to Santa Rosa by midnight, after all.  Our back and forth about this on the Garmin was practically in code, but I thought the plan was set.

Wrapping up my chores in deep twilight, I stored everything in my bear cannister and – why not? — I hooked my phone to the charger.

Before going to sleep, I decided to just take a peek at the phone.  What??  Yes!  Yes!!  My phone was charging!!!

I was elated and still baffled as to how it happened.  I could not be sure.  The best explanation I came up with was this:  First, without my glasses I had misread the charge level on the phone.  It did not have 65%, it must have had much less.  Second, after speaking with my wife from the restaurant at lunch, I must not have turned Airplane Mode back on, so that the phone drained far more quickly in the afternoon than it did in the morning.  That is my best guess.  Operator error.

The most beautiful campsite — taken under the stars, after my phone revived

Snuggled in my tent, I got gussied up, wearing all of my clothing in preparation for a second icy cold night.

My tent in the moonlight of the last night — repaired and welcoming (except for the mess inside!)

It boggled my mind to think that – most probably – I was wrapped in my sleeping bag for the last time this year.  The excitement of (hopefully) completing the trail the next day would normally have kept me wide awake.  But no.  I was just too tired and slept right away.
