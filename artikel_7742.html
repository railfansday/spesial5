A combination square’s usefulness depends on the relationship between its blade and head. The blade should be straight, the graduations correctly spaced, and the working faces accurately set at 90 and 45 degrees. Fine scale divisions alone don’t establish accuracy; a detailed ruler cannot compensate for an out-of-square head.

Check a new square before relying on it. Register the head against a known straight edge, draw a fine line, then flip the square and draw again from the same point. Diverging lines indicate a problem. Keep contact surfaces free of debris, and repeat the check after a drop or impact that could affect alignment.

Look for a blade with crisp, easy-to-read markings in the units and increments you use most. For standard woodworking, 1/16-inch graduations are often used for general layouts, while 1/32-inch divisions are crucial for making tight fits in trim and moldings. If you’re more familiar with metrics, you’ll want to choose millimeter markings.

Squares with etched markings tend to be more expensive, but they also hold up better than printed markings that can wear off with repeated handling. Dark markings on satin-finished blades can be easier to read under bright shop lights.

For reliable combination square use, the blade should slide smoothly when the lock is loosened and remain fixed when tightened. Binding makes small adjustments frustrating, while excessive play can prevent the blade from seating consistently. Check that the head sits firmly against the workpiece without rocking and that the adjustment knob is easy to grip.

A combination square is a measuring and layout tool with a ruler that slides through an adjustable head. The standard head provides 90- and 45-degree working faces, allowing the tool to check corners, mark miters, and measure depths or offsets.

Rest the head firmly against a workpiece’s edge to mark or check 90- and 45-degree angles. Slide and lock the blade to measure depths, check heights, or mark consistent offsets. The removable blade also serves as a ruler. If included, the bubble vial checks level, and the scribe scratches fine layout lines.For checking level over longer distances, use a dedicated level suited to the job. Accuracy varies by model and manufacturing tolerances. A straight blade, accurately machined head, and secure lock all contribute to reliable results. Fine graduations don’t guarantee accuracy, and price alone doesn’t establish it. Check any new square before use and recheck it after a drop or impact.

Use a board with a known straight edge. Hold the square’s head firmly against that edge and draw a fine line along the blade. Flip the square, register the head against the same edge, and align the blade with the line’s starting point. Draw again. The lines should coincide; any divergence indicates a squareness error, assuming the board’s edge is straight and the tool is seated correctly.

To set the blade, loosen the locking knob, slide it to the desired measurement, and retighten. If the square appears inaccurate, remove the blade, clean its groove and the head’s contact surfaces, then reassemble and repeat the accuracy check.Calibration procedures vary by design. Some squares permit correction at the blade’s seating surfaces, but filing or sanding removes material permanently and can worsen the error. Follow the manufacturer’s instructions or seek service or replacement if cleaning doesn’t resolve the problem.

Wipe away dust, moisture, and debris after use, paying particular attention to the blade groove and locking mechanism. Dry the tool before storing it, and apply any lubricant or rust protection according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Avoid abrasives on the graduations and measuring faces. Store the square where its blade won’t bend, and protect the head from drops and impacts.
