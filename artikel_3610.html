The Surprising History of the Royal Walkabout

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

“I have to be seen to be believed,” the late Queen Elizabeth II once said. And on Christmas Day, the royal family will be seen en masse during their annual walkabout on their way to services at St. Mary Magdalene Church on the Sandringham estate. For years, the holiday stroll, which features major and minor royals mingling with the public, has been a milestone event on the royal calendar.

But three controversial royals, the former Prince Andrew, Prince Harry and Meghan Markle, Duchess of Sussex, almost certainly will not be there. While Andrew has disappeared into his new life at Marsh Farm in Norfolk, Harry and Meghan have continued the tradition of the walkabout, reportedly infuriating the palace with a planned meet-and-greet with a small group of well-wishers in Nottingham. Although no longer working members of the royal family, it seems the couple knows the value and the power of this famous tradition, whose origins are far more recent than many believe.

For centuries, commoners were lucky to get a glimpse of royals riding a horse on a march across the country or nodding from a carriage on the way to the opening of parliament. The first Buckingham Palace balcony appearance was not until 1851, when Prince Albert came upon the idea of Queen Victoria appearing to celebrate the Great Exhibition of London.

The leading figure behind the idea of making the monarchy more visible to the public was a surprising one: the dour, but savvy George V, who ascended the throne in 1910. “I may be uninspiring,” he admitted, “but I’m damned if I’m an alien.”

Queen Elizabeth II and Prince William waving from the balcony of Buckingham Palace during Trooping the Colour on June 16, 1990. Pictured: Prince Edward, Duchess Of York, Queen Mother, Princess Margaret, Queen Elizabeth II, Prince Philip, Prince William, The Grand Duchess Josephine Charlotte Of Luxembourg, Prince Harry, Prince Charles, Lord Frederick Windsor, Princess Diana and Lady Rose Windsor.

These royal appearances ramped up significantly during the first World War, when the royal family came under scrutiny for their German lineage. George visited everyday soldiers on the Front, and Queen Mary and her children spent hours visiting the wounded in hospitals.

“George took ‘getting out and about’ to new levels. He visited industrial areas and poor housing, went down a coal mine and went on a relentless round of opening hospitals and inspecting regiments,” royal news website Royal Central notes. “Queen Mary once retorted to a princess complaining about yet another hospital visit: ‘We are the royal family—and we love hospitals.’”

However, these events were still carefully curated, with a distance maintained between the royal family and those they were visiting. It was not until 1939, that the first unofficial walkabout occurred, when King George VI and Queen Elizabeth (better known as the Queen Mother) went to tour Canada on a goodwill tour in the midst of the looming war. In May, the couple laid a wreath at Ottawa National War Memorial while an estimated 10,000 veterans looked on.

Overcome with emotion, the royal couple decided to go into the crowd to speak with onlookers. A CBC radio journalist watching the scene could hardly have sounded more in awe. “They all look most happy, most excited,” the reporter said. “One had the hand of the queen in one hand and the hand of the king in another. A delighted expression.”

George and Elizabeth were equally touched, and continued these meet-and-greets throughout the tour. “I lost my heart to Canada and to Canadians, and my feelings have not changed with the passage of time,” the Queen Mother recalled.

Known for her effusive personality and warmth, the Queen Mother became a pro at speaking with everyday subjects. During World War II, George and Elizabeth were praised for going among the people in bombed out areas devastated by the Blitz, listening to their woes and offering encouragement and strength.

Queen Elizabeth II on walkabout in Launceston, Tasmania, during her tour of Australia, 1970.

But it was not until Queen Elizabeth II and Prince Philip toured Australia and New Zealand in 1970 that the modern walkabout was truly introduced into the British royal calendar. There are different origin stories about how the modern walkabout came to be. One version asserts that it was a spontaneous decision of the Queen to walk to her next stop in order to chat, shake hands and accept flowers from schoolchildren in New Zealand.

Others say that first walk was the brainchild of the modernizing Australian courtier William Heseltine, known as the “mastermind behind the monarchy.” A firm believer in making the family seem more approachable and normal, he was also behind the 1969 Royal Family documentary. But if that project proved to be controversial, the walkabout (a term coined as a result of the tour) was a smash hit.

According to Kitty Kelley, the first royal walkabout did not occur in London until 1972, during the celebration of Elizabeth and Philip’s 25th wedding anniversary. “People were agog; their sovereign had never been known to speak to ordinary people,” Kelley writes in The Royals. “This was the first royal walkabout London had ever seen, and flag-waving Britons cheered as the Queen, the Duke of Edinburgh, Prince Charles, and Princess Anne melted into the crowd, greeting people and trying to make the royal family appear less remote.”

While the Queen reportedly enjoyed the intimacy of the walkabout, not every royal family member was quite as thrilled. “At nineteen years old suddenly being dropped in the middle of the street, suddenly being told to pick someone and talk to them,” Princess Anne said. “Fun? No, I don’t think so. A challenge.”

Queen Elizabeth II visits the Town Hall in Sydney with Emmet McDermott, Lord Mayor of Sydney, during her tour of Australia, May 1970.

Soon, the walkabout was a major part of every royal’s schedule, and family members had to learn how to do them—like it or not. “I learnt the way a monkey learns—by watching its parents,” King Charles once said.

During international tours, the walkabouts made a packed schedule even more tiring, but Queen Elizabeth II never faltered. “On a tour of Canada, visiting Saskatchewan, the Canadian minister Alvin Hamilton was concerned that the Queen had been on her feet all day and had not even requested a ‘health break,’” Karen Dolby writes in The Wicked Wit of Queen Elizabeth II. “Her private secretary breezily replied that the Minister for Northern Affairs and National Resources need not worry, ‘Her Majesty is trained for eight hours.’ It’s impossible not to wonder about the training sessions.”

She also leaned upon her sense of humor when meeting folks of all different stripes. “On a walkabout in Scotland, a helpful observer commented that she looked just like the Queen, to which she quickly replied, ‘How reassuring,’” Dolby notes.

According to Dolby, the Queen also developed a system to communicate with her staff while talking with the public. If things were going well, she held her handbag in the crook of her left arm. If she wanted someone to relieve her so she could move on, the handbag was dropped to her side.

But the person who truly transformed the modern walkabout was Princess Diana, much to her husband Prince Charles’s chagrin. “Charles was smart enough to see what a stunning political asset Diana had become—but he was also deeply disturbed by all the adoration coming his young wife’s way,” Tina Brown writes in The Diana Chronicles.

From the start it was clear the young Princess had a connection with everyday people unlike any other royal. During a walkabout while touring Wales in 1981, Charles joked, “I seem to do nothing but collect flowers these days, I know my role.”

As Dianamania swept the world, the Princess became more and more comfortable with connecting with her often-rabid fans. Far from a stiff gloved handshake, she would clasp hands gloveless, hug well-wishers, kneel to speak with children. Frustrated that Diana constantly pulled focus, Charles delivered pithy one-liners with a forced smile. At one walkabout, the crowd openly groaned when they realized Charles was coming to their side, not Diana. “It’s not fair is it?” Charles quipped to the crowd. “You’d better ask for your money back.”

But despite Charles’s jealousy, he could not deny the impact Diana had during these walkabouts. “Everyone was really buoyed up to see her and she was making an effort to go along and speak to people and people appreciated the effort she put into meeting them,” one person she spoke with remembered. “I know it was her job but she gave that little bit extra to people that made her different and stand out.”

“She made people happy,” he noted. “It’s a rare gift.”

Catherine, Princess of Wales, has followed in her mother-in-law’s footsteps as a walkabout all-star. According to historian Robert Jobson, Catherine was initially scared during the walkabout, offering up the stiff refrain, “Thank you so much. We are so delighted to be here,” over and over. But she quickly acclimated and has become known for her chatty and warm ways. “There’s a real art to walkabouts,” she has said. “Everybody teases me in the family that I spend far too long chatting. I still have to learn a little bit more, and to pick up a few more tips, I suppose.”

In fact, it has become a sport for the press to catch Prince William subtly attempting to move Catherine along, often eventually giving up. During the Christmas Day walkabout in 2024, she was the last royal to leave the receiving line, quipping “I think I’ve lost my family!”

According to royal author Robert Hardman, King Charles can also be unusually chatty. No one quite wants to go up and go, ‘Sir, can you get a move on?’ he says. “Instead, Queen Camilla will nudge him in the small of his back with the corner of her handbag.”

But the predilection is one Camilla shares as well. On a walkabout in Wrexham, an irritated Charles was overheard telling an aide “Can we try to get [Camilla] back again? Please? We need to go! I was trying to wait for her, but she goes on!”

Prince Harry and Meghan, Duchess of Sussex, attend University of the South Pacific on October 24, 2018 in Suva, Fiji.

Learning the art of the walkabout—what to say, what not to say—is a lesson every royal must figure out, often on the job. When Meghan Markle married Prince Harry in 2018, she admitted her ignorance. “I never saw pictures or videos of a walkabout,” she claimed.

And with the advent of cell phones, the job has become much more difficult. “I’m glad that I’m not starting now because at least you had people to talk to. Now you don’t really. Phones are bad enough, but the iPads—you can’t even see their heads,” Princess Anne said.

And these engagements can occasionally go south. In 2018, the Duchess of Sussex was rushed out of a market in Fiji after the walkabout got out of hand. There are also the occasional overzealous fans, including recently when a woman attempted to kiss Prince William on the cheek. And who can forget the supremely uncomfortable walkabout at Windsor after the Queen’s death in 2022, when a warring William, Catherine, Harry and Meghan greeted public mourners. It was, Catherine reportedly said, “one of the hardest things she’d ever had to do.”

Walkabouts are also a logistical headache for royal protection officers. “You are always aware of who is in the crowd,” former royal bodyguard Simon Morgan explained to Hello! “That is one of the positives of our royal family, they always want to go and meet people and as a protection officer, you have to accept what their wishes are. The current trend of hugs, high fives and selfies, that’s going to continue, so the protection officer’s job is really brought to the front then, because the protection officer is constantly looking for what could go wrong, you are constantly working things through your mind for the ‘what if, what if, what if.’

But the walkabout shows no sign of disappearing anytime soon. And come Christmas Day, there will be cheers, hugs, and an enormous amount of quick, polite chats about the weather that citizens who wait and brave the winter cold will never forget.
