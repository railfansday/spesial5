Nail Discoloration Can Be a Mystery. Dermatologists Are Here to Solve It.

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

Research is uncovering all sorts of new causes—and solutions. Here’s your ultimate guide to both.

NAIL DISCOLORATION AND yellowing isn’t exactly the easiest thing to talk about—and, turns out, pinpointing the cause of it isn’t any easier. With a fairly wide range of visual cues to consider, board-certified dermatologist Dana Stern, MD, explains that ‘discoloration’ is a descriptive catch-all for a nail that has “some kind of color variation from what is considered the normal nail appearance. It’s associated with many different nail issues.”

That makes it particularly difficult to treat. After all, you’ve got to know what’s behind your issue before you can tackle it appropriately. What we can tell you for sure: Whether you’re currently experiencing some unwanted color in your nails or are just curious about future indicators, Dr. Stern says that nail discoloration is an under-discussed topic.

Meet the experts: Dana Stern, MD, is a board-certified dermatologist in New York City. Jennifer Hanway is a board-certified holistic nutritionist.

Well, no longer. Ahead, we break down the specifics on nail discoloration, how to treat and prevent it, and a recently discovered connection between nail discoloration and GLP-1 use.

The spectrum of colors that fall under the umbrella term of nail discoloration are plentiful. “It could be a wide variety of colors including brown, black, white, green, red, purple, and yellow,” says Dr. Stern. Below are common associations with each discoloration shade.

The answer: it depends. It’s actually quite common to have discoloration but it’s hard to nail down (pun intended) the rate of occurrence because it’s a symptom of underlying conditions and not considered a disease within itself.

Nail changes and discoloration frequently occur due to age, normal changes in growth rate, and yellowing are extremely common in older adults. Fungal and bacterial infections, causing greenish nails make up a large percentage of nail discoloration or abnormalities.

Despite overall prevalence and with no single culprit at times, it’s important to understand that nail discoloration can be an indicator of your overall health. “Your nails can reflect aspects of your nutritional status, but they don’t tell you exactly what you’re missing,” explains Jennifer Hanway, a board-certified holistic nutritionist. Though she says that certain changes in nail color have been associated with specific deficiencies, “none is diagnostic on its own,” making it difficult at times to pinpoint the cause and treat it. Dr. Stern mirrors that sentiment, saying that since there is such a wide variety of scenarios for discoloration, it’s important to see a dermatologist.

Hanway says that in some cases, vitamin and mineral deficiencies can cause discoloration. “Vitamin B12 can sometimes cause nail pigmentation; iron-deficiency anemia can cause pale nail beds, and in some cases, a condition known as koilonychia, which is when the nail becomes centrally concave.” Other deficiencies, such as zinc, can affect overall nail growth and appearance.

Though your first step should be to speak to your doctor, it’s also important to take clear photos of your nails for reference and to keep notes. Make reference to which nails are affected, when the changes started, and whether you recently changed your diet, lost weight, started taking supplements, or have begun a new medication. “Speak to your doctor about whether testing for nutritional deficiencies or other factors may be appropriate,” says Hanway.

Depending on your symptoms, medical history, and diet, Hanway says that you may need full bloodwork to test for ferritin, vitamin B12, or other targeted tests.

Dr. Stern says that you can prevent white patches or keratin granulations by using a base coat when you have your nails done or by avoiding prolonged polish wear. If you’re someone who has their nails done regularly, Dr. Stern recommends seeing an experienced nail tech to avoid any chance of encountering discoloration. “Pseudomonas tend to occur in moist spaces on the nail, classically in a lifted acrylic or other type of enhancement, which is why having them done by a professional nail tech can lessen the chances of this occurring.

If you hurt a fingernail where significant blood accumulates under the nail, your dermatologist can evacuate the blood by drilling a small hole in the nail can help to remove the pigment, while also helping to save the nail from falling off.

In terms of nutrition, Hanway says that adequate protein and overall nutrient intake matter. “Nails are made primarily of keratin, a structural protein, so getting enough dietary protein supports overall nail health.” Protein options include eggs, fish, greek yogurt, and nutrient dense foods such as red meat, shellfish, which provide you with iron and zinc. “Supplements should be targeted rather than taken on a hunch, and should ideally be with your healthcare professional’s guidance,” she says.

A multivitamin may help to fill the nutritional gaps when food intake is limited, but it won’t exactly replace a balanced diet or guarantee any nail changes won’t occur. Hanway cautions against taking biotin supplements, even though they are often marketed for stronger nails. “The evidence of benefit in people without a deficiency is limited,” she says. Higher doses of biotin can also interfere with certain laboratory tests, potentially producing misleading results, so be sure to let your healthcare professional know if you are taking it before having bloodwork.

If you’re one of the millions of American women on a GLP-1 medication, know that a recent study suggests there’s a relationship between nail discoloration and these drugs (although it’s not entirely clear on the connection or causality). The 2026 European Academy of Dermatology and Venereology study reported that nail discoloration occurred in 46 percent of GLP-1 users, compared with 17 percent of adults with obesity or type 2 diabetes who had never taken these medications. To add insult to discoloration, nail detachment was also reported by 39 percent of GLP-1 users, compared with 9 percent of the comparison group. Though these are noticeable differences, it’s safe to conclude that there may be an association, but it does not prove that the medication caused these changes.

Dr. Stern points out that the full study was not released to the public, and did not report that about half of the GLP-1 users had nail problems prior to treatment. “This would mean that this study population had a high rate of nail disorders prior to treatment and therefore these nail issues were unrelated to GLP-1 therapy,” she explains. “When the investigators looked at nail changes that began after starting GLP-1, the previously reported associations disappeared entirely. This is a situation where it does not seem that association is equal to causation, but it would be great to have access to the whole study.” Until then, these findings should be considered preliminarily until fully evaluated through peer review.
