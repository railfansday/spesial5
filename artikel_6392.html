The saying ‘blind as a bat’ is completely wrong, according to a new study

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

New research shows that bats have tiny eyes to stabilize vision and combine sight with echolocation to navigate and hunt.

Bats are not blind. They have small eyes with very sensitive vision that lets them see in conditions people might call pitch black, according to the U.S. Geological Survey (USGS).

Their eyesight is not as sharp or as colorful as ours, but it does not need to be. The agency likens bat vision to that of “a dark-adapted Mr. Magoo,” the cartoon character known for his poor eyesight.

Recent studies show how much bats use that vision. A 2026 study from Johns Hopkins University found that bats move their eyes to steady their view, overturning an assumption that dates to 1942.

Optometrist Gordon Walls made the claim in his 1942 book The Vertebrate Eye and Its Adaptive Radiation. He wrote that the eyes of small nocturnal mammals, bats among them, “never move even reflexively.”

The idea went untested for more than 80 years. It fit a view of bats as animals that rely on echolocation for nearly all of their navigation.

Kathleen Cullen is the Raj and Neera Singh Professor of Biomedical Engineering at Johns Hopkins. Her team tested the assumption in Seba’s short-tailed bats, Carollia perspicillata.

The team included Grace Capshaw, Dimitri Skandalis and Cynthia F. Moss of Johns Hopkins, along with Hui Ho Vanessa Chang of Inha University. Chang carried out the work as a Ph.D. student in Cullen’s lab.

The researchers moved visual patterns in front of the bats and recorded the animals’ eye responses.

The bats tracked the moving patterns across a range of about 10 degrees in each direction. The eyes slowly followed the motion, then quickly snapped back to center.

That response, called the optokinetic reflex, helps keep an image steady on the retina. It resembled the response of mice and other mammals.

The team also passively rotated the bats’ heads from side to side. Unlike in most mammals, their eyes barely moved in response to that rotation.

The bats did show strong eye responses driven by the otolith organs, which sense gravity and linear motion, when rotated on a tilted axis.

Micro-CT scans showed that their inner-ear balance organs are nearly identical to those of mice, which responded strongly to head rotation. The difference appears to lie in how the brain uses those signals.

The authors propose that the system is specialized for active flight. It stays relatively quiet when a bat is at rest but likely engages during rapid, acrobatic maneuvers.

The eye-movement experiments compared four bats with six mice.

“This study shows that the brain’s solutions to navigation are more flexible than we assumed,” Cullen said.

“Even in an animal as specialized as the bat, multiple senses are integrated in ways that depend on behavior and environment,” she added.

“In bats, we see that this integration is dynamically tuned depending on context,” she explained. “The same is true for humans.”

Understanding how the brain combines these senses could help in treating balance disorders and in designing better rehabilitation and assistive technologies, according to Cullen.

A 2025 study of wild common noctules, Nyctalus noctula, showed vision at work during hunting. Researchers from the Leibniz Institute for Zoo and Wildlife Research (Leibniz-IZW) and Aarhus University tagged 21 bats with miniature sensors.

The tags recorded light levels, echolocation calls and movements. When the bats hunted in brighter conditions, they cut their calling rate in half and called up to 7 decibels louder.

They also flapped harder and flew faster. Average foraging speed rose from about 11.6 mph (5.2 meters per second) in the dark to about 17.7 mph (7.9 meters per second) in light.

Prey capture was about 50 percent faster in lit conditions. When the bats were simply commuting, their calls and flight did not change with brightness.

Laura Stidsholt, the study’s first author, was a postdoctoral researcher at the Leibniz-IZW and is now at Aarhus University.

“We show that the bats reduce the acoustic tracking of their prey when enough light is available as complementary sensory information,” Stidsholt said.

Calling less did not make the bats worse hunters. “At the same time, we were able to reveal that this happens not at the expense of hunting efficiency,” Stidsholt pointed out.

Insect-eating bats are very active in the early hours of the night, at dusk. They can presumably combine echolocation and vision most efficiently then, in the view of Christian Voigt, head of the Leibniz-IZW Department of Evolutionary Ecology.

“Our study provides the first direct evidence that bats rely on multisensory information when foraging, highlighting the remarkable flexibility of their sensory systems,” Voigt noted.

The Johns Hopkins team reaches a similar conclusion. Its results challenge the view that bats rely almost entirely on sound, showing instead that they combine vision and sonar to make split-second decisions.

The study was published in the journal Current Biology.
