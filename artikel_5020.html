Claude Claims Historic Math Breakthrough One Day After Anthropic IPO

An Anthropic mathematician says Claude helped him crack the Riemann hypothesis, a 167-year-old puzzle with a $1 million prize attached.

His claim surfaced Friday, one day after the AI company’s stock-market debut, and has not yet been independently verified.

“I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis,” Levent Alpöge wrote in a four-page note. He says the method also resolves the broader Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, which extends the question to other mathematical functions.

Alpöge says he and Claude encoded their argument in Lean, software used to check mathematical proofs step by step. His note links to 14 downloadable parts of the proof archive, but its availability does not amount to acceptance by the mathematical community.

Alpöge says he messaged Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei about a possible announcement “late Sunday night,” November 15. He then consulted company leadership and said there was a constraint to respect, without explaining precisely what it was.

“The precise timing is horrible,” he acknowledged. Alpöge rejected a cynical interpretation of the timing, but did not explicitly say the company’s IPO caused the delay.

Anthropic began trading on Nasdaq Thursday, November 19, after raising $81.34 billion in its offering.

Alpöge figured in September’s AI math wars. OpenAI claimed it had solved the Navier-Stokes Millennium Prize problem as he and NYU mathematician Tristan Buckmaster pursued related fluid-equation work, sparking a credit dispute over research priority.

OpenAI followed in October with 722 manuscripts generated by an unreleased model, grouped into 372 families of results. Only 162 included Lean-checked main results, and mathematicians demanded stronger evidence rather than accepting the company’s flood of claims at face value.

Some mathematicians fear a “mathocalypse,” with AI solving coveted problems before researchers can learn from pursuing them. Toronto mathematician Daniel Litt warned that, without adaptation, “there’s just no more math in 50 years.” At an MIT panel this week, Buckmaster argued mathematics still requires human understanding.

First posed by Bernhard Riemann in 1859, the hypothesis predicts where certain zeros of the Riemann zeta function fall. The question matters because those zeros are linked to the distribution of prime numbers, whose patterns underlie extensive mathematical research.

A proof would be a historic mathematical result, since experts have been trying to solve that for over a century.

Lean can verify that formal deductions obey its rules. Mathematicians still need to reproduce the archive, check the formalized theorem matches the original conjecture, and examine the claimed result before considering it settled.

Anthropic previously reported progress on the zeta function without claiming to solve the full hypothesis. Alpöge’s new assertion goes far beyond that earlier work.

The Clay Mathematics Institute lists the Riemann hypothesis among its Millennium Prize Problems, each carrying a $1 million award. Under the institute’s prize rules, a proposed solution must be published in a qualifying outlet, survive at least two years of scrutiny, and earn general acceptance before the prize can be considered.
