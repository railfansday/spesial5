Welcome back for a dive into the Miami Mindset, a methodology for shaping a mental approach to competition trained by Travis Steele, the head basketball coach of the Miami RedHawks, a basketball team that has compiled a 36-0 regular-season record, extending back to the 2024-25 season.

These are the four core values of the Miami Mindset:

An explanation of each value follows, shared by Coach Steele in an interview for these two posts.

“I need guys that live for competition,” explained Steele. “Competition brings out the best in each other. It’s how we practice. It’s how we lift. It’s how we do academics. Every day’s an audition.”

“We always talk about being the hunter, and not the hunted in everything we do, and it starts with having that competitive mindset,” Steele continued. “We recruit guys that have that gene, but we make sure that comes to life with how we do practice. We create a very, very competitive practice structure that makes that come to life.”

“I use those words because I want it to be a family environment and I want these guys to be great teammates both on and off the court,” detailed Steele. “Elite teammates have a common characteristic, amongst them, that they can make (optimize) others around them. ‘One plus one equals three.’ I can make you better than you are by yourself."

“We get to three if we’re truly invested in each other,” Steele shared. “Pushing each other, holding each other accountable. Accountability is a really important piece, being very direct with teammates.”

“We want guys that are giving energy to others,” Steele said. “How do we make this come to life? If a teammate falls down on the floor, everybody on the floor sprints over and picks him up. We acknowledge the passer. The guy that scores the basket is the one that gets all the credit, so we point to the passer, which I think is important to show connectivity. Another way (for brotherhood) is tight huddles during timeouts and stoppage of play.”

“You’ve got to embrace the process,” Steele explained. “Everybody’s going to go through tough times. Even if you’re a great shooter, you’re going to miss three or four in a row, and the next time you’re open, you’ve got to be able to shoot.”

“We talk a lot about mental toughness,” he said. “We train our guys about how to respond (to adversity) in different ways. ‘What is your response to adversity?’ because we’re all going to face it. It’s a lot easier to talk about it, but you have to apply it. So, we do a lot of adversity training.”

“We coach the response piece to adversity harder than we do our schematic stuff because, ultimately, our team is going to go through tough times in the middle of the game,” shared Steele. “How can we reset our minds back to neutral and be able to continue to do our jobs at a very high level?”

“I put them in adverse situations every day,” he explained. “I want practice to be harder than the game. You have to work on your response to adversity every single day, and that’s been critically important to our success.”

“Embodying undeniable confidence is done by embracing the process and being mentally tough and understand that your response (to adversity) has to be bigger than the event that just happened,” Steele summarized.

“We talked about adversity,” said Steele as he introduced this final core value. “Handling prosperity is just as important. Sometimes guys take their foot off the gas pedal when they’ve won 10 games in a row, or maybe the guy’s playing really well for three or four games in a row, and he takes his foot off the pedal. That’s a pattern for a lot of people, and we have to fight that urge.”

He fights that urge with a picture of a guy driving a car from an inside-the-car view. "Fellas," he explains, "the rearview mirror, there’s a reason why they’re so small. All the games that have happened are in the past ... There’s a reason why the front windshield’s so gosh darned big, because we’ve got to keep our eyes forward. We got to learn from the past, but we’ve got to keep our eyes ahead to see what’s in front of us."

One of the players added, “If we’re listening to everybody telling us how good we are, it distracts us from keeping in front of us.”

“That’s what happens if everybody starts listening to all the people around,” Steele elaborated. “They’re telling them how good they are and then you take the foot off the pedal. You gotta keep your eyes forward. Keep your eye on the ball, or you’re going to strike out. Our guys really understood that."

“My job as one of the leaders of the team, along with our captains, is to make sure that if we sense just an ounce of complacency," Steel explained. "We have different things we can do within practice to ratchet it up quick."

“I try to always gauge the temperature of the room,” he continued. “Where’s their mindset at? Before practice I go up to the players, talk to them, look them in the eyes. I may have to make adjustments. I’ve got a few drills that will raise the competitive spirit in the practice."

“I think the 'Strive for Magis' got really tested last year, more than any year,” Steele reflected, "just because of the run we were on, and I give our guys a lot of credit. We had great player leadership, and we did a great job staying focused on the task at hand and through the whole run that we had.”

As NBA legend Michael Jordan shared with golfer Tiger Woods: “No matter how good they say you are, always keep working on your game.”

Those four core values closely parallel an Acceptance and Commitment Training (ACT) concept called psychological flexibility, defined as:

The ability to stay present with difficult circumstances, thoughts, and feelings, without letting them dictate your behavior, so you can respond effectively.

Coach Steele and his Miami RedHawks basketball team have embraced and empowered all that!
