Don't Let Future You Lead Present You Astray

Posted November 8, 2026 | Reviewed by Jessica Schrader

I have a friend who pays $99.99 a month for an unlimited gym membership he never uses. When his annual renewal came up back in October, I asked him if he was going to finally let it drop and give himself a $1,200 raise, and his reply was as predictable as it was perplexing. “Are you kidding?” he said. “Cold weather is right around the corner.” Refraining from pointing out that he didn’t darken the door of the gym during the short cold days of last winter, I let the matter drop.

Truth be told, as irrational (not to mention expensive) as my friend’s fruitless gym membership is, his seemingly capricious behavior is by no means unique to him. Most of us occasionally make plans for our future that our behavior in the past would suggest we ought to reconsider. The easy explanation for such poor planning is that it’s easier to make plans than to follow through with them, but a series of studies recently published in Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin suggests that there may be an even more interesting explanation for the behavior. According to the studies, we often ignore our past track record when making future plans because we identify less with our “self” in the past that we have already lived than we do with our self in the future that has not yet arrived.

In a total of 12 online studies conducted in nine different countries including the U.S., participants were asked to rate the level of connectedness of their current self with their past self, and their equidistant future self. Randomly assigned to one of three conditions—future-to-current, current-to-future, and current-to-past—participants were presented with seven pairs of circles that ranged from barely touching to almost completely overlapping. They were then asked to select the pair of circles that most accurately represented how “similar and connected” their current self was to their self of several years in the past (five years in some of the studies, 10 in others), or to their self of the same number of years in the future (depending on which condition they were assigned to).

Across all of the studies, participants perceived their current self to be more similar to their future self than to their past self. Regardless of the time period separating the current self from the past or future self (five years or 10), or whether they were thinking forward from their current to future self, or looking backward from their future to their current self, it was the future self with which participants felt more of a connection. The finding also held true across age groups and cultural contexts, as represented by the nine different countries involved in the studies.

The sense of connection with our future self, the researchers found, stems from an underestimation of how much we will change in the future relative to how much we have changed in the past. Because our future selves are more abstract than our past selves, being based on imagined rather than remembered details, we have a hard time imagining the sort of changes we have undergone in the past also occurring in our future. And this belief that our future self is more stable than our past self leads us to identify more closely with, and feel a stronger emotional connection to, our future self than to our past self.

Interestingly, the researchers found that this sense of connection with our future self extends to the goals and preferences that guide our decision-making. The things that we view as important right now, we believe, will remain similarly important to us in the future, regardless of how much such priorities might have changed for us in the past. And since the decisions we make for our future are based on how satisfied we believe they will make us, this overestimation of how stable our goals are can lead us to disregard our concrete past experience in favor of an abstract sense of future satisfaction. In other words, a weaker sense of connection with our past self, as compared with our future self, can limit our ability to learn from past experience.

As abstract as the concept of a “future self” may seem, our tendency to identify with it at the expense of our past self could potentially have some very concrete consequences by causing us to “apply different evaluative criteria for decisions made by different selves across time.” Contemplating a career change, for example, or speculating on a risky but lucrative financial investment—or, on a lesser note, considering whether or not to renew a gym membership we never use-- we can easily overestimate our future satisfaction with a given decision because we don’t give sufficient weight to our dissatisfaction with similar decisions we have made in the past.

It’s certainly useful to consult our imaginary future selves in such decisions, but we would be well-advised to also pay serious heed to the advice of our more experienced past selves. We might not like what they have to tell us, but we disregard the advice at our own expense.
