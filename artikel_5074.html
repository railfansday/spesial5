US Supreme Court Turns Down Custodia Bank Appeal Over Federal Reserve Master Account

Custodia Bank has reached the end of a significant route through the US federal courts in its effort to obtain direct access to Federal Reserve payment services. On November 16, the Supreme Court denied the Wyoming-chartered institution’s petition to review the dispute. The decision leaves a lower-court ruling in place. It is not a fresh opinion deciding every question about crypto-focused banks or an announcement that all such institutions must be kept out of the payments system.

Supreme Court docket 26-62 records a straightforward entry: the petition was denied on November 16, 2026. The justices did not agree to hear oral arguments and issue a merits opinion on the dispute. In practical terms, the lower-court position remains operative, but the denial should not be presented as the Supreme Court affirmatively endorsing each part of the earlier reasoning. This procedural distinction is important. It marks the end of this review attempt while leaving future policy and account-design questions to regulators, lawmakers and separate legal processes.

A Federal Reserve master account gives a qualified institution access to central-bank payment services without depending on another bank for the same connection. Custodia applied through the Kansas City Fed in 2020, arguing that its state bank charter supported its request. The application was denied in January 2023 following disputes over the institution’s business model and the risks the Fed identified. Custodia challenged the outcome, contending that federal law limited the Fed’s discretion. The appellate ruling instead recognised discretion to approve or deny applications in the relevant circumstances.

The banking system is already debating whether some new financial firms should receive narrower forms of direct payments access. A proposed limited or “skinny” account would not necessarily provide the same services or rights as a full master account. That difference matters to institutions building business models around settlement speed, access costs and interest treatment. Even if a different account structure emerges, a future limited product should not be confused with the full access Custodia originally sought. The exact eligibility conditions and supervision requirements will determine whether such designs change the industry materially.

Crypto companies operating as banks or partnering with banks have a commercial interest in dependable dollar settlement, but charter status and payment-system access are not interchangeable. Firms may still use intermediary banking arrangements, with associated concentration and operational risks. The Custodia decision removes one possible path to overturn a prior denial; it does not prohibit new applications or resolve every question about emerging financial infrastructure. The next informative developments will be formal Fed rules, account approvals and the requirements attached to them.

The practical consequence for businesses is continuing uncertainty rather than an abrupt change to payment access. A firm can be licensed at the state level and still face a separate federal assessment of whether it may hold a Reserve Bank account. Policymakers who want to expand access must resolve supervision, settlement risk and eligibility in rules that Reserve Banks can actually apply. Meanwhile, fintech firms may keep using correspondent banks and other intermediaries, with the associated costs and dependencies those arrangements create.

The clearest fact is in the court record: review was denied. Whether the broader US payment system opens new pathways for digital-asset banks depends on future decisions, not on an expansive reading of this one-line order.
