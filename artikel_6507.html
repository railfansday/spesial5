Dragonfly wings inspired a new way to build materials that can take a hit

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Engineers took cues from dragonfly wings to 3D-print materials that spread damage and absorb energy when compressed.

A neat grid or honeycomb looks like the smartest way to build something light and strong, and engineers have borrowed both for decades. Squeeze a printed grid hard enough, though, and it fails the way a stack of cans does.

One row buckles, then the next, in a band that runs across the whole piece.

A leaf’s veins, a dragonfly’s wing and a spider’s web are built the opposite way, from struts of wildly different sizes tangled together.

Materials printed on that plan spread the damage out instead of concentrating it. They also absorbed as much energy as the tidy lattices, or more.

Engineers at MIT and network scientists at Northeastern University’s Network Science Institute designed the new materials, which they call scale-rich metamaterials.

So far the team has tested them as flat, credit-card-sized polymer plates in a lab, not as helmets or packaging.

“This is a completely new way of thinking for metamaterials,” said study author Carlos Portela, associate professor of mechanical engineering at MIT.

Metamaterials get their properties from their internal architecture rather than their chemistry. Engineers usually design one small building block, a unit cell, and repeat it over and over.

So every strut inside is about the same length and thickness.

Albert-László Barabási, a physicist at Northeastern, has spent his career mapping networks from the internet to the brain. Those networks don’t look like a grid at all.

The veins in a dragonfly’s wing run from about four thousandths of an inch (0.1 millimeters) to about two inches (50 millimeters) long.

Leaf veins and spider silk span a range at least as wide, and the struts inside bone vary tenfold in thickness.

Barabási asked whether that spread of sizes could be built into a solid material on purpose. He took the question to Portela’s lab, with a mathematician at Utrecht University in the Netherlands joining the team.

The team’s recipe is short. A computer picks a random point inside a square and draws a straight beam through it at a random angle, until the beam hits the edge.

The computer picks another point and draws another beam, which stops when it meets the first.

Each new beam is thinner than the last, by a rate the designer sets. Only two settings matter: how thick the first beam is and how fast the thickness shrinks.

The program repeats the step hundreds of times, so the last beams are short and fine while the first few stay long and thick.

The finished design has no typical strut. Lengths, thicknesses and the number of crossings per beam all run across orders of magnitude inside one sample, the same pattern network scientists find in road maps and food webs.

Changing those two settings also changes how stiff the material is in different directions. Across 2,600 computer-generated designs, the directional stiffness varied 24-fold.

That is far more range than a square grid, a honeycomb or a random foam-like Voronoi pattern can offer.

The researchers 3D-printed the designs in a stiff polymer. Each sample was a few inches across, with beams from a few thousandths of an inch up to an inch or so long.

Then they squeezed the samples in a press, alongside square, honeycomb and Voronoi samples of the same density, about 42% solid, three of each.

The square grid buckled first in a single row, and the collapse spread sideways across the whole sample before the next row went. The honeycomb formed wide crushed bands.

Even the random Voronoi foam compacted from the bottom up while its top stayed untouched.

The scale-rich samples didn’t do that. Their stress rose smoothly as the press pushed down, with no sudden drops, and the crushing stayed spread across the whole piece.

The team scored that spread on a scale where zero means perfectly even deformation and one means all the damage in one spot.

The new samples stayed at or below 0.2 even after being compressed by 25% of their original height.

The energy they soaked up before packing down matched or beat the three conventional designs.

“Because there is such a richness of length scales in these networked materials, if one part fails, there is a strong web of structures around it that keeps the entire material from failing catastrophically,” Portela said.

“It’s more efficient at distributing damage and absorbing energy.”

For a final test, the team borrowed from the thick, graded rind of a pomelo, denser toward the outside.

They generated a network whose beams crowded the edges and steered load around a disc of clear acrylic embedded in the middle.

Under compression, the acrylic disc inside a square or honeycomb lattice showed bands of stress under polarized light. Inside the scale-rich network, the bands were far fewer.

Computer simulations put the stress on the disc at as little as about one-fifth of the stress in the conventional lattices.

The same two settings can also tune how fast vibrations travel through the material. The team used that to design a lens that focuses sound waves, so far only in simulation.

Everything the team crushed was a flat plate of one polymer, printed at a resolution of about four thousandths of an inch.

The authors showed on paper that the recipe extends to three dimensions, with plates or beams instead of lines. The stiffness trends carry over, but those 3D versions were simulated rather than squeezed.

The study didn’t test the materials as any product. Helmets, armor, packaging and crash parts are the uses the authors named, and each would need its own tests at full size.

The group has posted its generator code publicly. The authors wrote that a design needing only two inputs is well suited to AI-driven searches for structures with a target stiffness or energy absorption.

Future studies could run those searches, and print the 3D versions to see whether the even crushing holds in a solid block.

The full study was published in the journal Nature.
