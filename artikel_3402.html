‘I Play Rocky’ Star P.J. Byrne Isn’t a Scene-Stealer — He’s Something Better

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

You might not always remember his name, but you know P.J. Byrne. A master of “hey, I love that guy!” roles, the actor is often classified as a scene-stealer, thanks to standout turns in everything from “The Wolf of Wall Street” and “Babylon” to “Coyote vs. Acme” and “The Rainmaker.”

Byrne is funny, yes, but also energetic and an eager scene partner. He improves his projects not by simply showing up and grabbing some quick guffaws, but by really doing the work, learning his characters inside and out, and making it a priority to truly understand his directors. In his own words, he’s not stealing scenes; he’s elevating them. He doesn’t ever want to be the best thing in a film or show — he wants his process to help everything be better. He brings that same approach and ethic to his latest turn as iconic super-producer Irwin Winkler in Peter Farrelly’s crowd-pleasing semi-biopic “I Play Rocky.” Farrelly’s film tracks the “only in Hollywood!” making of Sylvester Stallone’s smash hit “Rocky,” a true underdog story that hinges on the constant rejection of the then-unknown star, who was intent on starring in the film he had written. Stallone’s quest is aided by the support of Winkler and his partner Robert Chartoff (also an excellent Toby Kebbell), who went so far as to mortgage their own homes to make “Rocky” a reality.

Well, it paid off — for both the eventual Best Picture winner “Rocky” and Farrelly’s own film, which also features a massive star turn from Anthony Ippolito as Sly. Sure, there’s lots of drama in the film (Stallone’s physical toll stands out), but Byrne consistently brings his signature good humor and big laughs to Winkler, without ever making the Hollywood icon feel too big or crazy for this particular story.

“For me, it’s actually the most grounded performance I’ve ever had, but — wildly! — getting some of the biggest laughs of my career,” Byrne told IndieWire during a recent interview. That’s the product of a process Byrne has worked on for decades. But as seems to be the case with an actor who is just as gregarious and charming in an interview as you’d expect, it’s also because of the collaborations that really get him excited. That’s better than scene-stealing.

The following interview has been edited and condensed for clarity and length.

IndieWire: You’re the kind of performer that, when you show up in a project, I’m like, “Oh, my God, I’m so excited to see him!” You steal scenes by making them better. You’re not chewing the scenery, you’re not stomping in, but you are adding this extra oomph to everything.

P.J. Byrne: I really believe all ships rise with the tide. If someone hogs the scene, or they want to get all the hits, it just goes south. I’ve just been doing this so long now, and it’s been my 10,000 hours. It started when Mike Nichols saw me and was like, “You need to go do whatever you want” [in “Charlie Wilson’s War”]. That gave me the fearlessness with Marty to make up my own monologue for [“The Wolf of Wall Street”] and just go crazy. I think all these great directors see, “You know what? He does the work.”

Before I start filming anything — and this sounds nuts — I try to show up two weeks before, and I literally just live in a hotel, almost to the point, I’m in my underwear, I’m getting food delivered, and I’m [looking at the script] like, “Where can I raise moments and how can I elevate this character and make this moment truly pop?” It’s not to win it from anyone else. I have to make sure it’s not trite, and it potentially could be zeitgeist-y, and it potentially is a tee-up for everybody else to do it. But I also know, if you’re going to improvise and add stuff, it has to be a nuclear weapon.

I always make the joke, my bio will be, “P.J. Byrne, Over the Shoulder, Out of Focus.” I know when the camera is coming to me. I know I get one shot. It better be a nuclear weapon, because then that gives me the freedom. Directors, like the Mike Nicholses or the Martys or the Damien Chazelles, they’re like, “Just go.” Peter was like, “Get the handcuffs off him. We’ve trust that he’s done the work and passed us knowing that character.” But it’s so important to me to take their vision, because after a while you realize what they really want, and then you can imbue your character perfectly into that and then share that with the other cast members. But that is a lot of work. It’s a crazy amount of work.

You cannot just show up on the day and be like, “Oh, here’s a line that I think that I can really put my signature on.”

Look, some people might have that gift. My gift is coming up with 10 amazing ones, calling friends, “Anything like that, whatever, how’s this one, whatever.” But don’t get me wrong, when the scenes happen, if you get one shot, I have my nuclear weapon. If we’re doing it a couple times, I’ve got four more to throw in the mix, and maybe the fourth wins. But that, to me, is the most fun on set. I’m so crazily prepared. I could still do probably all the lines of the movie now, or some of the [other movies] that I’ve done.

I did a small part in “Green Book,” and really, there are no small parts. I so believe that. Doing these small parts gets me into the world of all these people, and then that grows within that project, or I get a ton of repeat business. But that’s the best interview to ever do. It’s like, see me in action, see how I’m on the set, see what I can bring and try to elevate everybody. I’m never down, like a Friday night at 4:00 a.m., I’m still the Energizer Bunny. That’s a big part of me, because I just know it’s like we’re so lucky to be doing this. Then, I always say, “ABC,” always be classy. It’s so easy to be nice.

I know, it really is. And it makes you feel better. It doesn’t feel good to be a jerk or to be angry or to hold onto things.

It makes no sense to me. But if I see anyone sort of slacking, it makes me crazy, because I’m probably friends with the writer or whoever’s involved. They’ve put years of their lives into this. We don’t need any cancers. This is such a team effort. That’s why this movie in particular was so special, because everybody was there to crush.

The fact that Peter gave me this chance, and Rick Montgomery, I did a commercial with him years ago. They’re always like, “He needs a bigger part in a movie. P.J. needs a bigger shot.” Toby Emmerich, I did “Horrible Bosses” and “Final Destination 5” with him. They all were sort of rooting for me to finally get the shot. And because I had that, I saw it in Anthony. Anthony was getting his shot. It’s about giving Rocky a shot.

This film doesn’t work without you and Toby being an incredibly believable duo. How did you two build that bond?

Me, in this movie, is nothing without Toby. Anything positive is because of that beautiful, insanely good actor, the great Toby Kebbell. There’s so much truth. It’s an interesting thing when you show up at set like, “We have to have a 20-year relationship. I’ve just met you nine minutes ago, and action.” You know when you meet people, you’re like, “Oh, we’re going to be homies. We’re going to be friends forever”? We had a conscious effort of just looking at each other. I think it comes through. We’re having whole conversations just looking at each other, because that’s really what producers are. They don’t talk a lot. They just observe the world.

What did you know about this real story before you came on board?

All I really knew was that he demanded to play the part. I knew that what he was doing in his life, that he had no money. Those are the fundamentals. The crazy part to me is the fact that the studio wanted him to fail. We all know how hard it is to make movies, and when the studio is purposely waiting for you to die, essentially, that was an extraordinary moment. The fact that it turned out this way, with the insane low budget, and they’re doing one takes, and we find the Steadicam, thank God.

Did you know that the real guy showed up when we were doing the steps scene? The man that made that camera, Garrett Brown. When you watch football games and you see the cameras drop, he’s the camera master of making the Steadicam, and everything else. The shot you see in the movie of the girl running up the stairs, that is actual footage, that’s real footage of his wife from 50 years ago. And he’s from “Sesame Street!” It’s like, what a cute place for it to come from.

OK, so this is crazy. Let’s just say, I knew of him, because he produced “The Wolf of Wall Street.” I know the power of Irwin Winkler, because when we were doing scenes, and there’s a thousand people in a room, and it’s just craziness, Irwin Winkler walks on with Marty Scorsese and 1,000 people shut up. You’re just like, “Who’s this? What’s happened?” People are very quiet for Marty, but when those two were chatting on set, it was like 1,000 people are quiet. It’s like, “OK, that’s an interesting asset that this man has.”

When I knew this was coming, I was like, “I have to get a shot at this. I must.” Thank God, Peter and Rick, they’re like, “Put it down. Let’s go. Let’s see what you got, baby.” So then when I did get the part, Irwin said, “Come over to my house, and we’ll chat.” It’s like, “Oh, fuck.” I go over, and he lives in a mansion north of Sunset Boulevard, and he calls it “the house that Rocky built.” It is every magical thing that you can imagine. He’s so gracious, his wife’s lovely.

You go into this amazing old school office, and you’re just, “Can I write every script and take meetings and calls in this room?” What’s so intimidating is, everybody famous from 1960 to present day, there’s a picture of them on his wall with him staring at you while you’re like, “Hi, can I ask about…?” And then he has a film crew there doing a documentary, so they’re filming me asking him questions. It’s an intimidating thing, because he remembers 50 years ago like it’s 15 minutes ago.

I learned from our own interview with Anthony that, while Sly said for a while that he wasn’t involved, he actually met with Peter before the film was made. But Peter was like, “Can you not talk about this? We don’t want you to feel too involved.” And then he went too far to the loop.

Yes. Crazy. Crazy. Crazy. No, yeah, they both blessed it. They both blessed the script. It’s like they blessed us playing those parts separately.

When you are on set with someone like Anthony, who is about to have this massive breakout, what does it feel like for you?

This is super meta, it’s life imitating art. He’s just a kid from Long Island. You’ve got to remember, what is he, 24 when he’s auditioning? We’re shooting, he’s 25. Sylvester was 29, 30, doing this movie. He’s also a younger kid playing this part. He’s number one on the call sheet. This is his first gigantic movie. He’s got Tracy Letts and Matt Dillon, he’s got Peter Farrelly. So it’s very intimidating. This is the greatest part ever, where you have to do the thing of a real thing that happened in a movie.

But the fact that we are living, in the theme of Sylvester Stallone, let’s give somebody a chance, it played out through all of us. I say it all time, I’m so lucky to have been around extraordinary performances, like Leo in “Wolf of Wall Street.” Margot Robbie in “Babylon,” when they do the scene where she comes on the set, and she has to cry, and she does one tear and then two tears, that was real every time. You become an audience member. Even though you’re there and you’re saying your lines, a part of your brain is like, “We’re in the zone, it’s happening.” And that happened with Anthony.

At times, I felt like, “Did Sylvester Stallone travel through space and time and possess a 25-year-old kid’s body?” It’s so natural, and subtle, and soulful, and heartfelt. I would literally look at Peter, and I was like, “He is the greatest actor ever?” It can be an “SNL” performance; it could be a caricature; everybody does a version of him. And it wasn’t just that. It was soulful.

At what point did you realize this was going to become such a crowd-pleaser? I wasn’t at the TIFF premiere, but I went the next morning, and that screening was like being at a Super Bowl or something. Everyone was constantly clapping and cheering and yelling.

None of us thought it would be that. [The TIFF premiere] is the greatest viewing experience of watching a movie that I’m in that I’ve ever had. People are jumping up during the movie, screaming and missing lines because they’re clapping. That was the greatest thing. I was like, “They’ve got to watch it again!” The standing ovation at the end, they stayed up as we were walking on the stage. It was almost an out-of-body experience. It was pure and beautiful. And then, thank God, there were so many moments where I added stuff, but the moment that I didn’t know was going to get that big of a laugh was, you know when the boxing choreographer comes on?

I do. The best boxing choreographer in Hollywood!

That guy’s incredible, too, that guy is so sick. In the original scene, though, I didn’t come on where he gives me his jacket, we added that little thing: “Oh, I’ll take your jacket. … That was interesting.” Also, in that bit, my character, all he said is, “It’s a boxing movie.” I went to Peter, I was like, “There has to be something more, because at this point, he’s never boxed, he can’t box, Stephan [James, who plays Carl Weathers] can’t box.” I’m like, “Let me get the world’s greatest boxing choreographer,” because the job as a producer is to put out fires and lower risk.

I was so nervous about that night because it was the night before Thanksgiving. I know it was late shooting, so I’m like, “Am I going to add a whole bit here at the end of this goddamn thing?” But to Peter’s cred, he’s like, “Well, let’s see what you got.” All those little things that you add, it just shows, if you do the work, if you’re true to what the character is, you’re true to the essence of what the film is, moments like that happen.

If you prepare enough, you can make character choices that are informed. I hear this from a lot of performers: “If you know it enough, you have more freedom.”

But you have to have a director — and this is the big part — who is fearless. Meaning, again, Peter, Chazelle, Marty, Clint Eastwood. They’re just like, “This is what I’m doing. I know what I’m doing, and then I’m going to pass the keys to everybody.” When you have other people who are operating out of fear … they’re not wanting to be creative, everyone’s tight and tense, and you feel it on set, and there’s no room to grow or play. You’re just hitting speed bumps the whole way.

You are also in another movie this year that I love, “Coyote vs. Acme.”

We’re starting to see a push to have it recognized as an animated film, including at the Oscars.

Apparently, they’re technically allowed. It’s over 75 percent animation, so they are allowed to be put into that category.

What does that possibility feel like for you, after everything this film had to go through to even get released?

The director, Dave Green, is a legend. I lived this process with this beautiful human. He’s another one of the kindest, classiest people, the way he handled the whole process, always being calm in the storm. We all knew, and as we’re doing it, something special is happening. We can all feel it. He’s another guy who’s like, “Let P.J. run and [John] Cena go, let Will [Forte] go.” That was another big, important thing, just that this movie is out there.

But also the biggest part truly, in my heart, is Eric Bauza. He just passed away, and he was the nicest, warmest, loveliest human being you’ve ever met. I really believe he’s our generational talent for voiceovers. He is the guy of our moment. He was presenting an award at the 2024 Annie Awards — and the bulk of his income came through Warner Bros., that’s the bulk of this man’s income — and he was the first person, art over commerce, going, “Release ‘Coyote vs. Acme!’” Against the people that pay him!

Please, God, let this movie be nominated. The work, the music, the animation, there’s so many extraordinary things. To think that that was not going to see the light of day! The fact that the world loved it, it’s got 96 percent on Rotten Tomatoes. It’s just like, the world’s right. Every once in a while, the world is right.

Do you get recognized on the street? And if so, what do you get recognized for?

It’s like anything and everything. Sometimes, they’ll just be like, “You’re a great stand-up comic.” I’m like, “I don’t even know what that means. I’ve never done that before.” Sometimes people hear my voice, and they’re like, “Are you from that cartoon?” That stuff, God, it goes back years, too. I did a show 15 years ago called “The Game,” and if I’m in Atlanta, they’re like, “Let’s go. I hate you, but I love you!” People screaming at me. Sometimes it’s “Wolf.” Sometimes super cinephiles go crazy for “Babylon.” They go absolutely nuts, corner you. Anybody talking about me? I’m always happy. That means I’m working or doing something.

My last question is my favorite to ask of people who I feel like have already done so many different things: What have you not done yet that you really want to do?

God, I would love to do a “Hoosiers” and be a coach. No one’s putting me in a war movie, but if you look at it now, I’m kind of growing into the general role, or some higher officer role. I want to be a bad guy in “Star Wars.” I want to be like, “Let me see how someone spends $340 million.” I want to be the man in “Mission: Impossible” standing next to Tom Cruise, or being the other guy on a computer. All of the big massive movies, because I miss doing that stuff, and they don’t shoot in LA as much, those big monster, masterful movies.

Ultimately, I want to work with great directors who are fearless. That’s the real thing. The directors that go, “P.J., take the handcuffs off and go out there and play, let them throw the touchdown pass to you.” The quarterback’s the star, but I promise I will make a great play as the wide receiver. Whoever that person is, whoever’s doing that, I will show up, and I promise I will score for you.

An Amazon MGM Studios release, “I Play Rocky” will be in theaters everywhere on Friday, November 20.
