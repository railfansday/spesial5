‘Cliff Booth’ Brings Back a Fan-Favorite ‘Once Upon a Time in Hollywood’ Face

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

”The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth” stands on its own as a beautifully written (by Quentin Tarantino) and directed (by David Fincher) Los Angeles noir that both invites and earns comparison with 1970s classics like “Night Moves” and “The Long Goodbye,” but its rewards increase exponentially the more familiar you are with its predecessor, “Once Upon a Time in Hollywood.” Although Fincher and Tarantino have both argued that the movie is less a sequel than simply another Cliff Booth adventure, the movie has quite a few fun Easter eggs to reward fans of Brad Pitt’s first time playing the laid-back stuntman.

Perhaps the most delightful callback to “Once Upon a Time” is the reappearance of two of cowboy actor Rick Dalton’s (Leonardo DiCaprio) collaborators on the “Lancer” pilot that took up a good section of the middle third of Tarantino’s earlier film. Co-star James Stacy (Timothy Olyphant) makes a brief appearance alongside “Lancer” director Sam Wanamaker, played once again by Nicholas Hammond, whose exuberant turn as the real-life actor-director in “Once Upon a Time in Hollywood” was one of that film’s greatest pleasures. Hammond’s part is, like the other returning faces from “Once Upon a Time” (aside from Pitt, of course), essentially a blink-and-you’ll-miss-it cameo, but he was delighted to return to Tarantino’s world and to share the screen with Olyphant once again. He first heard rumblings that Wanamaker might return for “Cliff Booth” well over a year ago, when the assumption was that Tarantino would direct the script.

“Quentin has his team, and it’s the same crew that works on all his films,” Hammond told IndieWire. “I’d gotten to know them on ‘Once Upon a Time in Hollywood,’ and a couple of the guys from the crew sent me emails saying, ‘Hey, it’s great we’re going to be working together again.’ Everyone had an ironclad NDA, so no one could tell me anything about the film, but that was my one little clue. Then, of course, when Quentin dropped out, and it looked like the movie was not happening, I thought, ‘There goes that chance.'”

Hammond was elated, therefore, when Pitt resurrected the movie by giving the script to his friend Fincher. “Timothy and I knew that we were coming in basically as an Easter egg,” Hammond said. “It was a tiny scene, and we knew that, but we were just thrilled that we were asked to come back, that Quentin thought it would be cool to have those characters back in the movie again. Let’s face it, he can get any actor he wants in the world to do anything, so it was an honor and a joy even though it’s a tiny little moment in the film.”

When Hammond played Wanamaker in the first film, he saw an opportunity to pay tribute to all the talented, hardworking directors and actors who work in the industry without necessarily becoming A-list names — a category that includes himself. “Your career ebbs and flows,” Hammond said. “You do a lot of small roles on television or small roles in movies, and then you get your own series, or then you get a big movie, and that’s the life of most actors in Hollywood. Even people like Leonardo and Brad say they have tons of friends who were as good as them when they were all starting out, but so much of it depends on if the right role comes along at the right moment.”

Hammond noted that for a lot of actors that moment never comes, but Tarantino has always recognized that that doesn’t make someone any less of an actor. “Most people think that if you’re good, you’re going to become a star. Quentin realizes there are a hell of a lot of very good actors out there who don’t become stars — that there are so many stars that have to align in the universe for that to happen. He admires and celebrates actors like Michael Madsen and Bruce Dern who have been journeyman actors their entire lives with moments of success, or flares of global recognition.”

Hammond experienced one of those flares as a teenager, when he played one of the Von Trapp kids in Robert Wise’s classic musical “The Sound of Music,” and again several years later when he played Peter Parker on the CBS television series “The Amazing Spider-Man” — the role that made Tarantino a lifelong Hammond fan. “I’m fortunate in that way,” Hammond said. “I feel luckier than most actors in my situation, because I’ve had a few goalpost roles that the world is aware of.”

60 years after he made it, Hammond still runs into “Sound of Music” fans all the time. “I was on a plane yesterday flying to Sydney, and a stewardess crouched down next to my seat and said, ‘Nicholas, I just have to let you know that my family watches ‘Sound of Music’ every year,'” Hammond said. “‘When my grandmother was ready to pass away, she said the last thing she wanted was for all of us to gather around the TV together as a family and watch that movie.’ I don’t care how rich you are or how many movies you’ve been in, how many actors get a chance to be in a movie that has that kind of effect on people?”

Hammond says that, having lived through the eras “Once Upon a Time in Hollywood” and “Cliff Booth” recreate, he knows Tarantino got that period of L.A. history completely right—something he attributes to the writer’s encyclopedic knowledge of his own work and that of all his peers. “There are very few people who could capture the 1970s as accurately as Quentin does,” Hammond said. “It’s his era. He grew up watching me as Peter Parker in ‘Spider-Man,’ and he watched all the episodes of ‘Gunsmoke’ I did, and all the episodes of ‘The Waltons’ and ‘The Brady Bunch.’ There’s not a show I was on that he didn’t see and know about.”

In “Once Upon a Time in Hollywood,” Hammond honored the directors of those episodic series whose names the public has forgotten, though Wanamaker, as an actor, was probably better known than many. When he got the part, Hammond was intent on playing Wanamaker with respect while still delivering on all the comic potential of Tarantino’s conception. “One of my fears when we were doing the film is I did not want Zoe Wanamaker to think we were making fun of her father, because we definitely weren’t,” Hammond said. “He was a very serious actor and a serious theater person.”

Hammond took a deep dive into Wanamaker’s life and career for the part. “Quentin’s office sent me a stack of DVDs of movies that Wanamaker had directed, and movies that he had appeared in,” Hammond said. “I got quite familiar with him. The Globe Theatre exists in England today solely because of him. If it weren’t for Sam Wanamaker petitioning and raising money and basically forcing the British government to authorize the building, the Globe wouldn’t be there. They wanted to build public housing there, and he managed to get a groundswell of support. And now, of course, it’s a huge tourist attraction, and in his honor the smaller studio theater within the building is called the Sam Wanamaker Theater.”

Hammond says he knew he was a part of something special when he saw “Once Upon a Time in Hollywood” at its Cannes world premiere. “I’ve been lucky enough to be in three really significant films: [Peter Brook’s] ‘Lord of the Flies,’ ‘The Sound of Music,’ and ‘Once Upon a Time in Hollywood,'” Hammond said. “With three very significant directors. So I’ve had a very fortunate career, and I still get excited just seeing my name on the call sheet. And when you’re working with people like Leo or Timothy Olyphant who are right at the top of their game, they’re the best of the best. I’m on the New York Yankees now, and when I get up to the plate, I better deliver.”

Despite starring in several classics, Hammond is less interested in looking back than in whatever he’s working on now. “I absolutely love being an actor, and I love being on stage. I’ve done a great deal of stage work, and I’m always asked, ‘Which do you like better?’ And the answer always is, whatever I’m doing right then. When I walk into the stage door of a theater, I get exactly the same charge of excitement that I do walking onto a sound stage at a studio. I just feel like I’m home. I feel these are my people. These are the people that I’ve always been the happiest being with since I was 10 years old, and I that will never change.”

Hammond looks to his “Sound of Music” costar Christopher Plummer, who did some of his best work late in life, as a role model. “I’ll do this as long as I possibly can. I’m of the Christopher Plummer philosophy that way,” Hammond said. “The word retirement doesn’t really exist. What would I do? Give up the thing I love the most in the world to play golf? I’m not doing that.”
