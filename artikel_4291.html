World’s largest trial of robotic knee replacement reveals surprising results

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Robotic technology can help surgeons perform knee replacement operations with greater precision, but that accuracy does not necessarily translate into better results for patients. The RACER-Knee trial, the world's largest clinical study of its kind, found that patients who underwent robot-assisted knee replacement experienced similar levels of pain, mobility, and recovery to those who received conventional surgery. The findings provide important evidence about the use of robotics in health care and highlight where future technological developments and research could make a difference. Coordinated by the University of Warwick and University Hospitals Coventry and Warwickshire (UHCW) NHS Trust, the trial was published in The Lancet, the world's oldest and one of its best-known general medical journals.

Robotic technology is becoming increasingly common in knee replacement surgery around the world. It is now used in 6% of procedures in the UK, 16% in the US, and 42% in Australia. The technology is designed to help surgeons position artificial knee joints more accurately and customize procedures to suit each patient's anatomy.

The UK-wide RACER-Knee trial was funded by the National Institute for Health and Care Research (NIHR) in partnership with Stryker, the manufacturer of the robotic equipment evaluated in the study. The Warwick Clinical Trials Unit, UHCW NHS Trust, and the Royal Orthopaedic Hospital in Birmingham led the research. It represents the world's largest double-blinded, randomized controlled trial comparing traditional total knee replacement with procedures performed using the Stryker Mako robotic system, the most widely used system of its kind worldwide.

Greater Precision Does Not Improve Recovery

Twelve months after surgery, patients in both groups reported comparable recovery, mobility, and pain levels. Although the robotic system enabled surgeons to operate with greater precision, patients did not experience measurable improvements in their overall outcomes during the first year. The researchers also found no increased risk of serious adverse events associated with robotic assistance, suggesting that it was as safe as conventional surgery.

The trial was jointly led by two chief investigators in the UK.

Professor Andrew Metcalfe, of Warwick Medical School at the University of Warwick and a consultant orthopedic surgeon at UHCW NHS Trust, said: "We are delighted to have completed this world-leading randomized trial of an exciting technology that is now being used globally and at scale. It is an important step in the process of innovation and will stimulate lots more high-quality studies. We are very grateful for the support of the many patients who took part which is so important, but also the NIHR and Stryker for working so well together to support this critical study. Technology such as this could really help the care we give for patients, but for robotic-assisted knee replacements, there is still work to do before we see benefits such as less pain or better movement."

Professor Edward Davis, a consultant orthopedic surgeon at The Royal Orthopaedic Hospital NHS Foundation Trust and honorary professor at the University of Birmingham, said: "The trial demonstrates robotic-arm-assistance allows for higher precision, and in theory the system should allow surgeons to customize the procedure according to a patient's anatomy. These results are by no means a condemnation of the robotic systems, but more understanding of the ideal position for a knee replacement and personalization for each patient is needed. We'll then be able to truly take advantage of the advanced precision of robotic systems."

Professor Anthony Gordon, director of the NIHR Health Technology Assessment (HTA) Programme, said: "Innovation in healthcare depends on evaluating promising technologies rigorously and at scale. While this study shows robotic-assisted knee replacement is not yet improving patient outcomes, it highlights opportunities to refine these technologies and harness their precision to deliver better care in the future. The NIHR continues to fund robust studies, such as RACER, ensuring we learn how to deliver the latest technologies most effectively within the NHS."

Total knee replacement is among the most frequently performed elective surgical procedures worldwide for people with knee arthritis. During traditional surgery, orthopedic surgeons use standard surgical instruments and rely on their training and experience to position the artificial joint.

Robotic technology offers an alternative approach in which the surgeon controls a robotic arm during the operation. The system helps position surgical instruments with exceptional accuracy and allows surgeons to adjust the angles and depths of their cuts with greater flexibility. These capabilities make it possible to customize procedures to individual anatomy in ways that are not achievable with conventional techniques.

Landmark Trial Finds Similar Patient Outcomes

The study involved 339 patients treated by 33 surgeons at 10 hospitals across the UK. Despite the potential advantages of robotic technology, researchers found no meaningful difference between the two groups in the trial's primary outcome measure, the Forgotten Joint Score, one year after surgery.

This assessment measures how conscious patients are of their artificial joint while carrying out everyday activities. Ideally, a successful knee replacement allows patients to go about their daily lives without frequently noticing the artificial joint. Patients achieved similar scores regardless of which surgical technique was used.

Other measures also showed comparable results. These included walking ability, pain during hospitalization, pain during the first three months of recovery and at 12 months, and the likelihood of requiring another operation.

Researchers plan to monitor participants for ten years to determine whether differences between the two surgical approaches emerge over a longer period. One particularly important question is whether robotic assistance reduces the number of patients who eventually need additional knee surgery.

Robotic Knee Surgery Takes Longer and Costs More

Operations performed with robotic assistance required additional time (on average 10.5 minutes) and cost approximately £950 more per procedure.

Although the robotic system improved surgical precision, it was not associated with a higher risk of serious adverse events, indicating that the procedure appeared to be as safe as traditional knee replacement.

However, when assessed against current NHS spending thresholds, the robotic technology evaluated in the trial was not considered cost-effective during the first year following surgery.

The Future of Personalized Knee Replacement

The researchers believe that robotic technology could become more valuable if surgeons learn how to use its enhanced precision to better accommodate differences in individual patients' anatomy.

Determining the ideal placement of an artificial knee joint for each patient could eventually allow surgeons to turn greater technical accuracy into meaningful improvements in recovery, pain relief, and mobility. Achieving those benefits will require further technological development and additional research, making personalized robotic surgery a promising area for future investigation.

The findings of the RACER-Knee trial were published in The Lancet.

The research was organized by University Hospitals Coventry and Warwickshire NHS Trust and the University of Warwick. Funding was provided by the UK NHS research organization, the National Institute for Health Research, through its Health Technology Assessment Programme (NIHR128768).

Stryker, the company that manufactures the robotic system, covered the additional expenses associated with performing robotic surgery during the trial. The company did not participate in analyzing or interpreting the study results.
