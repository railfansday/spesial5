In 1958, the Hope Diamond went to the Smithsonian by mail for $2.44 in postage, and the museum says a jeweler likely embellished its curse to sell it

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

A jeweler's employee paid $145.29 to mail the 45.52-carat blue gem from New York, nearly all of it to insure it for $1 million.

On November 8, 1958, an employee of Harry Winston’s jewelry store in New York City mailed an ordinary-looking package. Inside was the Hope Diamond.

The employee paid $145.29 to send it to the Smithsonian Institution in Washington, D.C., according to the Smithsonian’s National Postal Museum. Only $2.44 of that was postage. The rest paid for $1 million in insurance.

The 45.52-carat blue diamond traveled by registered mail in a plain brown wrapper. It is still on display at the Smithsonian’s National Museum of Natural History, and the museum has a short answer for anyone who asks whether it carries a curse.

“Is the Hope Diamond cursed? Of course not!” the museum says on its history of the gem.

In Washington, local letter carrier James G. Todd picked up the package at the city post office, a building that now houses the National Postal Museum.

Leonard Carmichael, the Secretary of the Smithsonian, brought Todd into the Gem Hall, where reporters and cameras watched him pull the package from his mail satchel and hand it over, the Postal Museum recounts.

The Natural History Museum dates the arrival to November 10, 1958. Harry Winston’s wife presented the diamond to Carmichael and to George S. Switzer, the museum’s curator of mineralogy. It “almost immediately became its premier attraction,” according to the museum.

The diamond’s history is far older. The Smithsonian traces it to a blue diamond of about 115 carats known as Tavernier’s Diamond, after the French merchant Jean-Baptiste Tavernier, which King Louis XIV had recut.

It must have come from India, the museum says, since India was the only commercial source of diamonds in Tavernier’s time.

The curse is much younger. “The story of a curse is a recent part of the diamond’s history, apparently having its origins in the early 20th century,” the museum says.

One early account ran in the Washington Post in January 1908. According to the Smithsonian, it offered “a selective history” of the gem, listing the bad luck of earlier owners. Later versions of the curse followed the same pattern.

A November 1909 newspaper report that a former owner, the Turkish diamond merchant Selim Habib, had drowned with the gem when a steamer sank at Singapore added to the legend.

The museum calls that report “wildly inaccurate.” Neither Habib nor the diamond was on the ship, and he had already sold the stone in June 1909.

The Smithsonian says some of the people who spread the curse stories may have done so for their own gain.

The French jewelry house Cartier bought the Hope Diamond in 1910, and Pierre Cartier set out to sell it. Intriguing histories helped sell gems in those years.

“Cartier thus began to fabricate a fanciful story around the Hope Diamond that included a curse, which he would pitch to potential buyers,” the museum writes.

He pitched it to Ned and Evalyn Walsh McLean, and the sale took two years. In the Smithsonian’s words, Cartier “likely embellished the stories as a sales tactic.”

The tale did not scare Evalyn McLean off. She told Cartier she believed objects that brought bad luck to others would bring good luck to her.

After he had the stone reset and left it with the couple for a weekend, she agreed to buy it in 1912 for $180,000 plus the return of a pendant she no longer wanted.

May Yohé, who was once married to Lord Francis Hope, an earlier owner, helped make the curse a popular modern legend.

In a book and a film released in 1921, she blamed the diamond for her failed marriage and the loss of the Hope family fortune, according to the Smithsonian.

After Evalyn McLean died in 1947, Harry Winston bought her jewelry, including the Hope Diamond, in 1949. He showed it across the United States in a traveling exhibition called the Court of Jewels before giving it to the Smithsonian.

At the donation ceremony, Winston’s wife scoffed at the idea of a curse and noted that her family had held the gem without any ill effects.

Todd, the letter carrier, had a harder time. Within a year of the delivery, he was hurt in two separate car accidents, his wife died of a heart attack, his dog strangled on its leash and his home in Seat Pleasant, Maryland, was partly destroyed by fire, according to the Postal Museum.

He still did not blame the diamond. “I don’t believe any of that stuff,” he told a reporter.

“If the hex is supposed to affect the owners, then the public should be having the bad luck,” he added.

The diamond stayed at the Natural History Museum, and the brown paper wrapper it came in is now part of the National Postal Museum’s collection.

The Hope’s deep blue color has its own science story. Earth.com has reported that some blue diamonds formed hundreds of miles below Earth’s surface.

The Smithsonian has hidden history of its own, including a secret chamber from 1847 found beneath the Smithsonian Castle.

The information was obtained from the Smithsonian National Museum of Natural History’s History of the Hope Diamond and the National Postal Museum’s Delivering the Hope Diamond.
