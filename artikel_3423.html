What Are Local Movie Theaters Showing for Thanksgiving 2026?

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

One of the great things about going to the movies by yourself is that, technically speaking, you’re not alone. Even if you’re the only person in the auditorium, somebody thought about what you might like to watch, put it on the calendar, and made sure there was a place for you to sit.

That’s something worth being grateful for this Thanksgiving.

Earlier this month, IndieWire surveyed the programming at independent movie theaters across the United States on Election Night. Continuing that exercise over Thanksgiving weekend reveals another delightful cross-section of American film culture — albeit with considerably more cannibalism. At the Music Box Theatre in Chicago, where audiences have spent more than two decades celebrating Thanksgiving by singing along to “The Sound of Music,” moviegoers can also catch Ruggero Deodato’s “Cannibal Holocaust” late Friday night. It’s hard to imagine the Von Trapps sticking around for that one, but there’s something lovely about a neighborhood institution accommodating both crowds.

In Seattle, the Beacon is observing its own Thanksgiving tradition with “Blood Rage,” the gloriously ridiculous slasher that made cranberry sauce an enduring point of reference for a certain subset of horror fans. For a somewhat more realistic depiction of the horrors of going home for Thanksgiving, SALA Event Center in Los Alamos, New Mexico, is showing Jodie Foster’s “Home for the Holidays.”

In Cambridge, Massachusetts, the Brattle Theatre is celebrating Black Friday with a double feature of “Chopping Mall” and “Night of the Comet.” If you’d rather watch people die in a shopping mall than go to one yourself, that’s certainly an economical alternative. Meanwhile, Minneapolis’ Trylon Cinema is spending the biggest shopping weekend of the year celebrating the work of documentary filmmaker Barbara Kopple, screening her Oscar-winning “Harlan County, U.S.A.” and “American Dream” as part of a series dedicated to class struggle.

Thanksgiving also marks the 50th anniversary of the Band’s legendary farewell concert, held at San Francisco’s Winterland Ballroom in 1976. At Film Streams in Omaha, Nebraska, audiences can spend Friday morning watching Martin Scorsese’s “The Last Waltz.” That half a century later, people are still getting together to experience a concert most of them weren’t alive to attend speaks to one of the more miraculous things movies can do.

And in Portland, Oregon, stop-motion animation studio LAIKA is expressing its gratitude to its hometown audience by offering free screenings of “Wildwood“ on Sunday at theaters across the area, including the historic Hollywood Theatre. Considering how many thousands of hours of human labor go into making a stop-motion animated feature, there’s something especially sweet about giving people whose real home served as the setting a chance to enjoy the outcome together.

Of course, what Americans celebrate on Thanksgiving — and whether they celebrate it at all — varies considerably. On Wednesday, California Indian Nations College is presenting a free screening of “People of the West,” a documentary series examining the history of California’s Indigenous peoples from Indigenous perspectives, in Palm Desert. At the historic Raymond Theatre in Raymond, Washington, a free Saturday screening of a documentary about the Qwayat Canoe Society’s journey to Nisqually offers an opportunity to learn more about a living Indigenous tradition.

And for anyone navigating a particularly painful holiday season, a film festival in Magnolia, Texas, is making a different kind of space. Presented by the nonprofit My Grief Angels, Grief Fest is hosting an in-person community screening on Black Friday, with films also available to watch virtually throughout Thanksgiving weekend. Dedicated to exploring loss and healing through cinema, the festival recognizes just how complicated an invitation to celebrate can be.

There are innumerable ways to spend a long weekend like this, and movies aren’t a substitute for the people we wish we could spend it with. But the wonderful thing about indie exhibition is just how much of it depends on random people thinking about other random people. Someone has to remember that a certain movie exists. Then someone else has to think it might mean something to you. And finally a different person has to care enough to bring it all together.

Whether you’re somebody who spends every Thanksgiving with the same people or a cinephile whose traditions are still taking shape, consider making a little time for your local movie theater this weekend. Thank the person who takes your ticket, and tell the programmer when you love something they booked. Maybe even invite that friend you’ve been meaning to catch up with.

There are so many extraordinary works of art to see in this lifetime and never quite enough time to see them all. How lucky we are to get to watch some of them together.
