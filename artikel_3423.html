How ‘1 Train Cypher’ Became New York’s Hottest Open Mic

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

S tand clear of the closing doors, please.” That phrase — and the accompanying “bing bong” chime — is familiar to anyone who has ever ridden the New York City subway. The announcement is a sign for most people to find their spot, but for the city’s pool of entertainers and performers, it’s another drop of the beat.

For straphangers lucky enough to stumble upon a 1 Train Cypher, the scene is more reminiscent of one you’d expect to find at a music venue, not on mass transit. Every few weeks, a herd of teens, spirited Generation X holdovers, and all demos in between, gather at the intersection of West 242nd Street and Broadway in the Bronx, before descending to the downtown 1 train heading toward Manhattan.

The space inside the train is tight with people, the air thick, everything on the precipice of potential chaos. Suddenly someone starts beatboxing, or an MC begins freestyling, and the crowd joins the sonic fracas. Once rhymes are being spat, it’s impossible not to be transfixed by the passion, energy, and artistry manifesting before your eyes. Music fills the car, and now the commute has become a stage. Strangers are now an audience. There are no audio engineers, vocal booths, nor mulligans. There is only an iron horse and unbridled creative expression.

For the lyricists of the 1 Train Cypher, the subway is more than a means for transporting citizens and tourists. It is a meeting place, melting pot, showcase, and mobile party driven by community, camaraderie, performance, and culture.

“What drew me to the platform is the idea of a cypher where we all get together and test our skills,” Wyclef Jean says. Clef collaborated with 1 Train Cypher in November 2025 for a special-edition performance of his single “Back From Abu Dhabi,” originally featuring Rick Ross and French Montana.

“It’s like when I used to watch martial-arts movies when I was younger.” he continues. “It’s the best martial artists getting together. With a cypher, you’ve gotta show up with your best pen game, right? And for those that know, when it comes to the train and the hood, it goes together. The 1 Train Cypher movement grew from one New Yorker’s conviction that the city’s subway system already contained everything a living hip-hop platform needed: an audience, unpredictability, history, and talent. More importantly, the connectivity to the neighborhoods that have always given New York its creative and cultural identity.

“The 1 train was native to me,” Steven Muelz, creator of 1 Train Cypher, says of the Interborough Rapid Transit Co.’s (IRT) Broadway–Seventh Avenue Line. Running through the west side of the Bronx and Manhattan, the 1’s route travels through a diverse array of ethnic enclaves, filled with Hispanics, Africans, Latinos, African Americans, and other minorities. “I took that shit through middle school, high school, and college. For going to parties and going to play sports. I was always on that 1 line. It just made sense to bring the cyphers there.”

That instinct — to bring the 1 Train Cypher to a place that already belonged to everybody — has become the foundation of a burgeoning grassroots creative community. What began as a relatively modest experiment by a core group of friends has become a platform that has drawn thousands of participants and millions of viewers. With co-signs from mainstream darlings like Grammy Award winner Doechii and lyricists like Bronx rapper Cory Gunz appearing alongside emerging artists, the 1 Train Cypher has created a sense of communal anticipation around a viral event that still retains the rough edges of its beginnings. “It’s dope,” Gunz, who’s collaborated with Lil Wayne, Rihanna, Nicki Minaj, and Meek Mill, says. “It’s like passing the torch because we’re all still in the Olympics. Like rubbing shoulders with the gladiators in the Coliseum and the legends you see and hear about, and then being able to be in the same rooms with them, exposing you to their audience and vice versa.”

Muelz’s own trajectory mirrors that philosophy. Born in the Dominican Republic, Muelz immigrated to America as an infant with his mother, settling in Harlem. Raised by his mother and stepfather, Muelz also spent time at his grandmother’s home in the Bronx, both areas shaping his understanding of identity, ambition, family, and community. The city’s cultural richness was visible everywhere, even amid the poverty and crime plaguing upper Manhattan and the Bronx at that time.

“It was just growing up in that environment, and then noticing as I got older, there has to be something more than just this,” he says. “There are so many beautiful things happening in the hood. It’s covered by the violence and what the media portrays, but there’s so much talent, unity, and culture that gets overlooked.”

After graduating from high school in Midtown Manhattan, he went north to college in Riverdale, and the 1 train became an informal part of his education. “When I run the cyphers, it’s just taking me back to my college days,” Muelz says.

After earning a degree in finance, Muelz prepared to follow the conventional trajectory of a Wall Street gig. Instead, an internship convinced him that he was headed in the wrong direction. “I was bored. I was falling asleep on the desk. I’m like, ‘I can’t do this for the rest of my life.’ I was lost. And in the midst of being depressed, I started trying out all types of art. It was rapping, trying to make beats, to write poems. In the midst of me trying out a thousand different things, I picked up the camera, and that was the only thing that gave me a spark.”

Enrolling in YouTube University, Muelz immersed himself in photography and videography tutorials, gradually transforming curiosity into a working skill set. By 2018, that self-directed education had begun translating into professional momentum. At that point, he decided to create and launch his platform, which ultimately evolved into 1 Train Cypher.

The name derived from his roots uptown. Defined as a gathering or circle of improvisational dancers, beatboxers, or rappers performing, the term “cypher” comes from the Arabic word for “zero” (“sifr”), which evolved into “cipher” when the Arabic numerical system was adopted in Europe. For New York’s influential Five Percent Nation of Gods and Earths, an uptown- and Bronx-based community that was pioneering in the early days of hip-hop, the word “cipher” came to mean a person, circle, or completed cycle of knowledge, or a group’s artistry and rhyme skills.

“I was already gaining some notoriety in the city,” Muelz recalls. “I was like, ‘I want to do a cypher; there’s no cyphers right now.’” Inspired by the BET Hip Hop Awards Cyphers, Colors, and other performances, Muelz became intent on following suit, albeit while molding his showcase into one that reflects the artistry and improvisation he’d witnessed on train rides growing up. “I was like, ‘I want something more raw and something more authentic to New York.’ I was like, ‘Fuck it, I take the 1 train every day. People already perform on the [train]. I’m going to ask four or five artists that I’ve already worked with to bring them on the train,’ and that was that.”

NEW YORK’S SUBWAY SYSTEM has long been one of the city’s most visible stages for musicians and performers. However, many of those talents are only exposed to a few New Yorkers and tourists lucky enough to catch them in passing during their commute. On the other hand, 1 Train Cypher documents that energy and distributes it online, beyond the confines of the train car itself.

“There are so many talented people, that’s the beauty of the train,” Muelz shares. “The train is more symbolic than just a place to go from point A to point B. It’s also a canvas of talent from people in these neighborhoods, from all backgrounds. The trains were a huge element of people coming together and showcasing their art back in the day. There weren’t cameras and social media, so people couldn’t see it, but it was happening long before us. It’s just cool that we get to modernize it.”

The first 1 Train Cyphers were intimate, with Muelz, his brother, a friend handling the camera, music, and logistics, and just a handful of artists. Then something happened that could not be engineered in advance: The passengers started participating. “It was people coming home from work,” Muelz recalls. “People started recording it, people were cheering, and I was like, ‘Damn, we’ve got something real special here.’”

Gunz praises the platform’s street-level innovation: “You’re actually on a locomotive while you’re going loco type shit. It’s gutter, it’s straight hip-hop from the ground.”

“That raw nature of it is why people fuck with it,” says Muelz. “There’s no gimmicks; it’s people talking about real shit. You get to hear struggles we’re dealing with at different levels. It might be somebody who’s a millionaire talking about their problems, but then you’ve got somebody that’s maybe in poverty. You’ve got mother issues, father issues, family issues, siblings. You have a place to express yourself, and people resonate with that.”

The cypher, in this sense, becomes more than a performance. It becomes a moment of truth. Status matters less when everyone is standing in the same train car and rhyming about their lives in front of an audience. A 1 Train Cypher team member, named, simply, Mike, sees the event as an equalizer. “Forget your name, we don’t care how many followers you have — in that moment, everybody is the same,” he says. “You feel the hunger from the underground scene. You feel the hunger from the legends that step into the circle. It just brings it back to the essence of hip-hop.”

THE 1 TRAIN CYPHER’S ASCENDANCE and popularity have been heightened by the increasing number of legends and rising stars aligning with the platform. Rapper Styles P, who was introduced to Muelz through a mutual friend, deems the 1 Train Cypher essential to the culture. “It’s about repping where you’re from, repping your style, but also having enough ammo to keep going,” Styles P says. “The cypher shows the essence of New York hip-hop, MC’ing, and what it’s like to be an MC when you’re not in the studio. I think 1 Train Cypher is fucking necessary.”

In fact, he’s such a fan that he’s brought the show with him. “We had it at my dispensary, Strains for Life,” the Lox member continues. “We also took it to the state park. It was a beautiful day, highlighting what MCs are about, and giving people who don’t always get a shot or a look. That’s what makes the platform beautiful: People with bars get highlighted for having bars.”

Muelz points to that embrace of the movement when detailing his hope to bridge the gaps between fame, notoriety, and skill.

“Now that we’re getting the bigger names coming, our job and our focus is we want to be the first platform to showcase what it looks like when we have two or three legends combined with aspiring artists in the same cypher and creating those moments,” he explains. “How can we put Griselda and have Benny the Butcher, but also have a fire spitter from uptown in the same bubble?”

Although rooted in displays of lyricism, Muelz’s ambitions for 1 Train Cypher extend beyond hip-hop. The team plans to incorporate R&B, Latin music, DJs, and other genre-specific experiences as the brand grows without abandoning its base. “I’m excited about that chapter,” Muelz says. “We’re always going to do hip-hop. That’s forever, but we’re also going to invite other genres as well to showcase different types of talent from the other side.”

The expansion is also connected to a broader philosophy of representation. In October, 1 Train Cypher staged an event tied to breast-cancer awareness and fundraising, a cause with personal significance for its founder.

“Breast cancer runs deep in my family, so that’s something that I want to make sure we can raise money [for] and donate,” Muelz says. “We sell shirts, and we make sure everybody wears pink.” In March, an all-women cypher gives female artists a dedicated platform, evidence of Muelz’s value of inclusion and philanthropy.

The movement’s definition of hip-hop is expansive, too. It is music, certainly, but it is also a language for documenting people, neighborhoods, struggles, aspirations, and relationships. The cypher offers a format in which those things can coexist without being packaged into a single commercial identity. “It means everything. It means life. It means culture,” Muelz says. “It means perspective. It gives us a new angle of ‘This is who we are. This is what we stand for. This is what we believe in.’”

Muelz sees in community his vision for the platform’s legacy. “In five years, I want everyone to look at us and be like, ‘They brought back an energy of hip-hop that was missing. Something that we didn’t see since the early 2000s.’ We’re showing a different light to the hood and the city because New York is special. Hopefully, they could look back and be like, ‘Damn, they birthed a whole bunch of new acts, new talent, and they did it the right way.’”

At its best, the 1 Train Cypher feels like a reminder of what culture can look like when people decide to participate. The train’s motion is essential and metaphorical. Nothing about the 1 Train Cypher suggests that the culture it celebrates is frozen in a nostalgic version of New York. Instead, it treats the city’s history as raw material— something to be carried forward, constantly remixed, and made contemporary.

Muelz teases that it may become a touring platform, as he shares visions of bringing the 1 Train Cypher to Madison Square Garden and Yankee Stadium. To him, the possibilities are limitless. But its most compelling quality is freedom, that somewhere between 242nd Street and South Ferry, the commute becomes a cypher again.

“In New York, the subway is culture,” says Gunz. “It’s going through the whole city, from hood to hood. It’s getting a taste of every style. You’re getting a chance to see a gumbo of the city’s best in one spot. It shows the significance of the journey. That’s what it correlates to.
