3 AI agents value a home similarly, could they be wrong?

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Three AI valuation systems can produce similar home values while relying on the same market definition and comparable selection assumptions. Agreement does not validate accuracy if the competitive market area is misidentified.  AI Summary

The similarities in the estimations made by three independent systems might be perceived as confirmation of accuracy. However, when AI-powered systems operate under the same assumptions regarding a property’s competitive market, the agreement might bring no additional assurance.

Imagine three different AI agents estimating the value of your house. The first system offers a $1.59 million valuation. The second – $1.61 million. And the third – $1.60 million.

It looks like three independent systems agree on the price. This information can be quite reassuring for the homeowner who plans to sell. Moreover, the similarity in the numbers can be influential for the asking price.

However, this question comes to mind: Have three different systems produced the same number because they analyzed the data independently, or is the result just similar due to the same initial assumptions?

Let’s talk about this case in more details and figure out whether agreement can increase the probability of a mistaken valuation.

The issue can arise long before any of the numbers is calculated by the systems.

The real estate sector accumulates huge volumes of property data. Closed transactions, MLS records, active listings, public records and other sources provide rich information about the prices, property features and market activity.

Modern valuation models use highly advanced statistical techniques and machine learning algorithms to make sense of this information. AI agents can extract and analyze property records, use third-party valuations or generate an accurate price explanation based on the market data.

However, the three independent systems can be based on the same underlying sources, analytical methods or similar understanding of the competitive market. And, in case the initial assumptions are incorrect, the similarity of results cannot provide any additional assurance.

Let’s imagine a condominium in Miami on the 24th floor of a high-rise building.

AI generates valuation based on the recent transactions of condominiums with the same square footage, number of bedrooms and geographical proximity. It uses the information to make calculations and produce a detailed valuation.

Everything looks good. Properties exist. Transaction histories are true. Property characteristics match the search criteria. The calculations are accurate. But do these properties compete in the same market?

In dense residential markets like Miami, geographical proximity and similar property characteristics can hide significant differences in competitive positioning.

Two condominiums may have the same number of bedrooms, the same square footage, and even be located right across the street from each other. But one may be located in the entry-level residential community while another – in the waterfront development.

They may have identical property types, but different price ranges, ownership costs, amenities, views, and other characteristics that are relevant for the market analysis.

On paper, the properties look comparable. But in reality, they may attract different buyers and be in competition with different properties.

This concept is well understood by established organizations like Fannie Mae. According to the Appraiser Update of December 2024, two properties that are located side by side can be in the same neighborhood, but occupy different market areas since they appeal to different buyers.

According to the guidelines on the comparable sales, the comparable properties should compete for the same buyers as the subject property.

This is an important difference between searching similar property records and identifying relevant market evidence.

Competitive market area may begin with the condominium building where the subject property is located, include similar units within the same development, or some competing residential communities in the area. Price range classification and property characteristics can help with the organization of this analysis, but they cannot substitute the process itself.

The market area should be defined with the help of buyer alternatives, not the geographical proximity.

I have worked with residential property data and analyzed the range of automated valuation models and AI-generated valuations, and the distinction between preparing property records and organizing the market analysis keeps coming back.

Property records can contain accurate addresses, standardized property types, verified square footage, complete transaction history, and precise geographic coordinates. All of the duplicate records are deleted, inconsistencies are corrected, and missing information is added.

These are important steps in data preparation. But clean data does not tell anything about the competition between the residential communities, the price range segments they occupy, and the relevant properties for the market analysis.

This requires one more step: organizing residential communities, analyzing their characteristics, establishing the relations between the properties, and validating those relations on the market evidence.

These processes can be automated with the help of machine learning. But the underlying definitions and inferences require testing and maintenance as well. It does not mean that current valuation models are based on radius searches. Many of them use the much more complicated methods and can find the complex relationships between property characteristics, locations, and market activity.

The challenge here is more fundamental: can we make sure that the information used to support the valuation is the appropriate representation of the competitive market area of the subject property?

Larger dataset, more advanced model, or a better explanation of the results cannot guarantee this. More property data does not mean more market evidence automatically.

Defining market areas has its consequences not only for comparable sales, but for the statistics describing the supply, demand, and the current competitive conditions as well.

Inventory, speed of selling, days on market and months of supply depend on which properties are included in the calculation. Let’s take a simplified, hypothetical example.

Imagine a competitive market area that consists of a group of competing condominium communities with 12 active listings. This market area has been experiencing three sales per month. So, the inventory represents four months of supply.

Now, let’s imagine an AI system combining these properties with another residential area with 48 active listings and three sales per month.

Combined market area will include 60 active listings and six monthly sales, resulting in 10 months of supply.

Both calculations are mathematically correct. But the second number describes a different set of properties, not necessarily the competitive environment of the first market area.

For the homeowner, the difference is important. Four months of supply and 10 months of supply can indicate very different competitive conditions. Depending on the way of interpretation, this statistic may have impact on the pricing expectations and the decision whether to change the asking price.

Of course, the actual market analysis requires consistency of the measurement periods, sufficient transaction activity, and the consideration of changing conditions. Small samples can lead to uncertainties, even in case of correct market definition.

But the example shows an important point. Calculation can be mathematically correct, but the population of properties that is included in the calculation can be wrong for the particular decision.

The same concerns median prices, selling speed, and other market indicators. If the dataset includes communities from different competitive segments, its summary statistics may cover the completely irrelevant competitive conditions.

That is why market definition and maintenance of relevant residential communities is not just an improvement of comparable selection. This is an essential part of market analysis.

Research in other industries provides one more argument to be skeptical about the opinion agreement of several AI systems.

The 2025 study Correlated Errors in Large Language Models published at the International Conference on Machine Learning has examined more than 350 language models and has found out substantial correlations in their mistakes.

On one benchmark, when both models produced a mistake, they chose the same wrong answer in 60% of cases. Correlations were also found between models of different architecture and provided by different organizations.

However, the study was not concerned about the home valuations, and its results do not prove that AI pricing agents routinely make the same mistakes. But it reveals an important limitation of apparent model independence: different models can make correlated mistakes.

In case of property valuations, the risk of correlated mistakes is associated with shared assumptions.

If several AI systems analyze overlapping market information, use similar property-selection criteria, or rely on the same initial valuation source, their conclusions may be less independent than they seem.

And when the underlying market is improperly defined, these systems can reinforce misleading interpretation without generating a single false record.

It is important to remember that this situation differs from the usual problem of AI hallucinations. Hallucination provides some unsupported or invented information. Market definition mistake can produce misleading conclusion on the basis of the correct data.

Verifying the existence of transactions is necessary. But it does not guarantee that these transactions are really relevant for the analysis.

The most valuable AI pricing advice should do more than producing a number. It should help homeowners to understand the market evidence behind the number, assumptions and uncertainties associated with it.

First of all, it is the market itself. Which residential communities and competing properties have been considered as relevant, and why? Does the analysis distinguish between the properties that are geographically close and actually compete with the same buyers?

Then it is the evidence itself. Which closed transactions serve as the base for the recommended price? How the characteristics of the subject property compare to these sales? Which active listings show the current competition? Were the relevant transactions excluded, or less comparable properties were included just due to the geographical proximity?

Understanding of the limitations of the system is also important.

Model can have limited transaction activity in the subject’s competitive market. The particular unit may have some unique characteristics. Comparable properties may require adjustments, or the market conditions have already changed after the closing of the most relevant transactions.

In these circumstances, admitting the uncertainty is more valuable than producing an artificial precise conclusion.

Also, homeowners should be able to distinguish between explanation and validation. System can provide a good explanation of its estimation, but this fact does not confirm the predictive accuracy of the method.

Where possible, evidence of how a valuation method performs against actual sales prices for comparable properties and market segments can be more valuable than the agreement of several AI-generated estimates.

Not every automated valuation model operates with a conventional list of comparable sales, and not every proprietary system allows checking its inner workings. This is the reason why transparency of available evidence, limitations, and performance metrics is especially important.

For the homeowner receiving three similar estimates, the next step should be to check whether they are based on the different market evidence or they just reflect the same initial data.

Agreement may be reassuring. Understanding of the reasons for it is more valuable.

As AI becomes more and more accessible, the ability to retrieve property records, calculate statistics, and generate the polished price explanations will become commonplace.

The meaningful differentiation will consist in the quality of the market intelligence. It includes reliable property data, well-organized residential communities, relevant relations between the competing properties, consistent market definitions, and methods that can be validated.

Neither proprietary dataset nor publicly available records are superior. What matters is how this information is organized, validated, maintained, and applied to solve the particular problem.

Technological sophistication of models will continue to grow, but it should not be mistaken for independent verification.

For the homeowners preparing to sell, the consequences are practical. Pricing decision can impact the way of entering the market, the competition of the property with alternatives, its listing period, and the negotiating position of the seller.

The purpose is not replacing the professional judgment with the impressive number. It is providing homeowners and real estate professionals with the better evidence and the more precise market context.

Three AI agents valuing your home similarly sound convincing. However, before trusting their conclusion, one more question is important to ask.

Is the market they are analyzing the right one?

Jake Miakota is the CEO of Subdivisions.com, a residential community-based real estate market intelligence platform.
