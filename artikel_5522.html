VantageScore 4.0 can create more homebuyers. Now America needs more homes.

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

VantageScore 4.0 adds trended credit data and can score more consumers, potentially expanding mortgage eligibility. The article argues added credit access must be paired with zoning and permitting reforms to address a 3.7M to 4.7M housing shortfall.   AI Summary

A credit score should tell lenders not just where a borrower is, but where that borrower is headed.

For example, consider two mortgage applicants who each carry a $5,000 revolving balance today. One paid that balance down from $15,000. The other ran it up from $500. Their current balances are identical, but their trajectory plainly is not.

For decades, mortgages sold to Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac relied almost exclusively on a scoring model built largely around a single point-in-time view of credit. That is changing. FHFA approved VantageScore 4.0 and FICO 10T for use by the GSEs in 2022, and in July 2025 announced the interim transition policy allowing VantageScore 4.0 to be used for Enterprise loans. After a limited rollout this spring and summer, Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac opened VantageScore 4.0 to every approved lender in September.

The change arrives in a market that remains stubbornly out of reach for many Americans. Rates are well above where they were last decade, prices are near record highs in many markets and inventories are tight. VantageScore 4.0 can help lenders identify creditworthy borrowers who were previously overlooked. But more qualified buyers are only half the equation. Credit availability is not the same as housing availability, and the policy response has to address both.

VantageScore 4.0 incorporates trended credit data: how balances, utilization and repayment behavior have changed over time, rather than a single snapshot. Credit scores have traditionally been very good at taking photographs, but VantageScore 4.0 is instead trying to give lenders a short movie. That is the difference between the two applicants above. Today, VantageScore 4.0 is the only newer trended-data model currently eligible for delivery to the GSEs.

It can also use rent and utility payments when they appear in a consumer’s credit file. For thin-file borrowers with years of on-time payments but little traditional credit, that can be the difference between being scorable and being invisible. The caveat is that on-time rent is still often unreported. VantageScore’s analysis estimates that nearly four million renters could reach a 620 with positive rental data. Wider reporting would unlock more of that.

VantageScore says its model can score up to 33 million more adults than traditional models, and a VantageScore-sponsored analysis identifies nearly five million consumers it considers mortgage-ready.

An Urban Institute analysis of roughly 27.5 million Fannie Mae loans found all three models effective at ranking risk. Both VantageScore 4.0 and FICO 10T captured more defaults in the riskiest decile than Classic FICO, with FICO 10T capturing the most.

That is not a knock on either model. It is the argument for competition. Two modern models pushing each other to become more predictive leave lenders and borrowers better off than a one-model world did.

The market is already moving. In September, Rocket Mortgage said it would make VantageScore 4.0 its preferred model for eligible retail loans starting in the fourth quarter. It made that decision after about four months of testing across 1.4 million credit reports.

Rocket reported that VantageScore helped more clients qualify, and that borrowers who saved money saved an average of about $1,600 at closing. Adoption is still early and concentrated among a few large lenders, but the direction is clear.

VantageScore 4.0 does not replace underwriting. Income, debt-to-income, loan-to-value, reserves, employment stability and collateral still decide who gets a loan. What the model can do is give lenders richer information on a wider pool of borrowers, without asking them to abandon risk discipline.

Implementation matters, too. The two models can assign different scores to the same borrower, and independent analysts have warned that lenders choosing whichever score is higher could introduce bias into default rates. The GSEs have responded with guardrails, including requiring one model across all borrowers on a loan and publishing pricing for each model.

Competition should be judged on predictive accuracy, innovation and cost, not on which model produces the friendliest number on a given file. That is an argument for careful implementation, not for preserving a single-model system.

More qualified buyers means stronger effective demand. Where supply can respond, that demand is a revenue signal that helps marginal projects pencil out. Where zoning, permitting, land and construction costs keep supply frozen, the same purchasing power mostly chases the same homes and shows up in higher prices rather than an inducement for more housing supply.

The scale of the gap is large. Freddie Mac estimates the national housing supply shortfall at about 3.7 million homes, while Zillow puts it at roughly 4.7 million. A separate White House analysis this spring estimated that the United States would have roughly 10 million more single-family homes today if post-2008 construction had continued at historical rates. These measures are not identical, but they point in the same direction: America is not building enough housing.

Supply can respond to better rules. From 2017 to 2022, Minneapolis grew its housing stock 12% while rents rose 1%, according to Pew Charitable Trusts research. Over the same period, the rest of Minnesota grew 4% and saw rents rise 14%. Pew points to reforms including eliminating parking mandates, which made more apartments financially feasible to build.

VantageScore 4.0 can widen the path to homeownership for responsible borrowers, but that path ends at a front door only if there is a home behind it. State legislators and local zoning boards should legalize more housing types, cut parking mandates and shorten permitting timelines. Lenders, scoring companies and policymakers all have a stake in that outcome.

VantageScore 4.0 is a welcome step toward a more competitive, more predictive mortgage credit market. It will hopefully promote America’s needed next step to build more homes for the buyers it helps create.
