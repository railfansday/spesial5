What's on the Ethereum Roadmap: Glamsterdam, Hegotá and Beyond

Like all blockchain projects, Ethereum is under active development, with upgrades designed to make it faster, cheaper, and easier to use.

Instead of a single "Ethereum 2.0" event, the network upgrades through coordinated changes called hard forks that introduce new features or modify how the protocol operates.

Since the Merge in September 2022, developers have focused on scaling, lowering transaction costs, improving wallets, and making it easier to run nodes and validators. The Ethereum community is also aiming for roughly two major upgrades per year when research and testing are ready.

Ethereum's scaling plan relies on layer-2 networks. These are separate blockchains built on top of Ethereum that process transactions off-chain and send results back to Ethereum for security and settlement.

Many layer-2 systems use rollups, which bundle multiple transactions together and post them to Ethereum as a single batch, allowing Ethereum to support more activity without the base chain processing every transaction.

As a result, much of Ethereum's development has focused on making it cheaper and easier for rollups to use the network. More recent upgrades have also aimed to scale Ethereum's base layer directly, by raising the amount of activity each block can hold.

In July 2022, Ethereum co-founder Vitalik Buterin described the network's six roadmap phases as the Merge, the Surge, the Scourge, the Verge, the Purge, and the Splurge.

These phases are not single upgrades but broad goals, and several progress in parallel.

Ethereum's roadmap is implemented through a series of hard forks.

Glamsterdam also raises the gas cost of creating new state, such as accounts and contracts, to target database growth of about 120 GiB a year, and reprices reading stored data, to keep hardware requirements manageable as capacity rises. Under one proposal, EIP-8037, creating a new account would cost about seven times as much gas as before.

Other scheduled changes would make a standard ETH transfer between existing accounts up to 71% cheaper, let developers deploy contracts to the same address across chains, make ETH transfers emit a log that apps can track, raise the maximum contract size from about 24KiB to 64KiB, and speed up validator exits and consolidations.

Node operators and stakers will need to update their clients to support the fork; ETH holders do not need to do anything.

Developers originally targeted the first half of 2026. Glamsterdam went live on the Sepolia testnet in October 2026, but a fork on the Hoodi testnet planned for October 26, 2026, was postponed after security firm ChainSecurity warned that the upgrade's gas changes could weaken safeguards in existing smart contracts.

In November 2026, developers rescheduled the Hoodi fork for December 15, 2026, and agreed to ship the gas changes unaltered. As of November 2026, no mainnet date had been set.

Hegotá is the upgrade planned to follow Glamsterdam. Its name combines "Bogotá," the execution layer upgrade named after a previous Devcon location, and "Heze," the consensus layer upgrade named after a star.

As of its October 2026 update, ethereum.org expected Hegotá on mainnet in the second quarter of 2027, with a fork on the Sepolia testnet to come first, though no dates had been confirmed. Two changes are scheduled for inclusion.

The headliner, Fork-choice Enforced Inclusion Lists (FOCIL, EIP-7805), aims to strengthen censorship resistance by having a committee of validators propose lists of transactions that block builders must include, so that no single party can keep a valid transaction out of a block. Frame Transactions (EIP-8141) would let each account set its own rules for what counts as a valid transaction, rather than relying on a single fixed signature scheme. That would bring features such as social recovery, spending limits, multiple approvals and gas sponsorship directly into the protocol, and let accounts adopt signature schemes designed to resist quantum computers.

Dozens of other proposals are being considered, but none are settled, and the rest of Hegotá's scope is still being decided. In November 2026, some developers argued that FOCIL should be dropped to free up resources for work to protect Ethereum against future quantum computers, with a decision on Hegotá's full scope expected in December 2026. Forkcast tracks the live status of each proposal.

Upgrade names and scopes can change during development as proposals are refined before each hard fork.

Ethereum's roadmap continues to evolve as research progresses and upgrades are tested on devnets and testnets before mainnet deployment.
