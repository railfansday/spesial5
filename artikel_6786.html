Are We in a Sugar Baby Economy?

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Dating is expensive, so why not get paid for it?

Ana, 37, has a rule: she doesn't spend her own money on men. Not on dinner, not on an Uber home, and definitely not on any emotional labor of a situationship that she knows isn’t going to go anywhere. She’s had this rule for three years now.

“I realized over the years that dating men came with a lot of risk,” she tells Cosmopolitan. Ana is bisexual, and she says she kept running into the same thing with men that she never felt with women: a lack of transparency, and the creeping sense of being used.

“At the end of the day, it seemed like the majority of the men I had dated wanted an embellished sex worker, who also provided care and mothering, without necessarily providing something equivalent back.” So she decided to make sure she was getting something back. “Because money, I can count,” she says. “Fake promises? Not really.”

Dating in 2026 is already an expensive extra-curricular on its own. BMO's research puts the average Gen Z date at around $205 once you add up the outfit, the ride, and the actual dinner (millennials clock in even higher, at $252). The average all-in cost of a date climbed 12.5 percent to $189, outpacing the cost of living itself. Bank of America found that more than half of Gen Z, 53 percent of men and 54 percent of women, spend literally $0 a month on dating—because they’re just not doing it.

But even when people are willing to pay the price, it’s hard to find someone to splurge on. Dating app sessions dropped 7 percent year over year in 2025. Swiping is tiring, and paying for premium tiers to keep seeing the same guy you’ve swiped left on on four other apps doesn’t seem worth it. So when “sugar baby” discourse started taking over TikTok and the timeline, it wasn't random.

Clu, 29, remembers exactly how it started. She was working a day job, hustling for what seemed like every dollar, and watching friends who were sugar babies get everything paid for.

“It started off as a financial pressure,” she says. “I'm looking around, I'm seeing these girls get everything paid for, and a lot of my friends are also sugar babies. They're like, ‘Girl, just do it.’”

The tipping point was dating guys her own age and not getting the same treatment. Men who were older and financially stable, on the other hand, knew exactly what they wanted—and were willing to pay for it.

What she's selling is mostly time. “It's really just companionship,” she says, “Going on dates and pretending, most of the time.” Some men want a hand to hold at a lake after a rough day at work while others want a full-on girlfriend experience and promise to cover everything from her bills to a new car. The pay varies wildly: a couple hundred dollars for a date, or, if you get lucky, a designer bag. Twice, Clu says, married men have told her their wives knew and had given their blessing, because they couldn't give their husbands what they were looking for.

“You always want to think of it as a financial transaction because this is a service,” she says. “I'm providing you a service of having a good time and escaping from your reality." Catch feelings, she warns, and things crumble. And never let yourself believe you're the prize: "There's always someone younger. There's always gonna be another sugar baby."

And at first, Clu says the money felt like freedom, but eventually that feeling faded.

“After a while, it does take a toll, because you're constantly depending on these people,” she says. “They can stop paying your bills tomorrow. So it does become financial survival.”

A 2024 study of 77 people in the U.S. and Canada found that money was the primary driver for sugar babies, but companionship mattered to everyone involved, and plenty of arrangements looked a lot like regular dating. It also found that more than a third of the sugar babies had done other kinds of transactional sex work, and that they worried about physical safety and coercion, especially early on.

Sugar dating itself isn't illegal in most places, according to legal explainers on the subject. The line tends to come down to whether money is being exchanged specifically for sex. Which is exactly why so many arrangements stay carefully, deliberately vague.

Ana's current setup doesn't fit the stereotype at all. There's no monthly allowance. Her partner covers things like groceries and Ubers and generally makes her life easier. They live separately and see each other once a week: date night on Saturday, an overnight, brunch on Sunday. They text daily. They aren't exclusive. And all of it was spelled out from the start. “Those were the conditions I set,” she says. “I no longer engage with men without some kind of arrangement being set from the start.”

Before this, she had two more traditional sugar-daddy relationships, which worked more like pay-per-meet. Roughly twice a month, she'd ask for a specific bill to be paid: a shopping spree, repairs at her house, a doctor's bill. She has never liked the idea of a monthly allowance, she explains, because she worried it would make her feel obligated to see people even when she didn't want to.

That autonomy is the point. Ana doesn't frame this as freedom (”I already had that before,” she says) so much as respect. “I have never been treated this nice by men whom I gave my time and love and care for free, so that says a lot.”

“All relationship types come with compromises. But most relationships between men and women come with sometimes feeling pressured to do stuff. And I have learned not to bow.”

But Ana says the sites themselves have changed. "Most sugar arrangement sites have become a place for medium income men to underpay for sex work," she says. In her experience, a lot of them aren't looking for anything complex. “They do want sex by the hour but say they don't want to use escorts as they feel bad about themselves.”

James Foley, LCSW-R, a psychotherapist, treats men who engage in what he calls sugaring behaviors, and he says the economy and the internet both play a role. He explains that social media has given everyone a 24/7, high-resolution view of the rich: their cars, homes, and vacations. Technology, meanwhile, lets people at opposite ends of the economic spectrum connect discreetly and make deals.

But both people—sugar daddies and babies alike–—are walking into an emotionally powerful situation they sometimes can't manage.

“It is a tremendous struggle to maintain your reality testing when something is real and fake at the same time,” he says.

“If you come across the opportunity, why not take it?" Clu says. “Go on that date and come back with that Cartier.” She encourages women to take the chance, as long as they build real relationships instead of treating each man as a one-time payout.

Ana, on the other hand, isn't going back to traditional dating in any form.

In 2026, a lot of people feel like they're paying more and getting less, for dinner, for rent, for love. Sugar dating promises to flip that equation, whether it actually does depends on the contract, the person across the table, and how long you can keep the line between transaction and relationship from blurring.

As Clu puts it, you can be confident in what these men will do for you, and still know the balance can change overnight. Anyone considering it should go in knowing that.
