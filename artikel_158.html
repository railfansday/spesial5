Saying it was necessary to prevent the possible spontaneous explosion of a stockpile of material used in igniters and blasting caps, the US Army conducted a controlled detonation of over 9,700 pounds of lead azide today at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant (IAAAP) in Middletown, Iowa. Videos of the detonation showed plumes of smoke several hundred feet high drifting south before quickly dissipating.

Following the explosion, the Army confirmed that all of the barrels at the plant had been destroyed without any secondary explosions or infrastructure damage. “I believe, absolutely, that we reduced, as close to zero as it’s absolutely humanly possible, the risk to the health and safety of the people of Middletown and the extended communities throughout Iowa,” said Maj. Gen. Eric Shirley during a press conference held two hours after the blast, according to the Associated Press.

The US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) has been monitoring the area around the ammunition plant leading up to today’s event. According to a statement from the EPA following the detonation, “real-time air monitors are reporting zero detection of airborne lead throughout the region” an hour after the detonation. The data collected by EPA employees, contractors, and monitoring equipment is shared here.

The Army has used lead azide in munitions for nearly a century but began transitioning away from the material around 15 years ago because it can be difficult to transport and store. Over time, the material can degrade and crystallize and eventually become unstable enough to detonate at ambient temperatures. The Iowa ammunition plant housed 68 barrels containing about 9,700 pounds of lead azide, representing the country’s remaining stockpile. For safety reasons, the material was stored in liquid form embedded in sawdust and burlap bags while water and ethanol were added to prevent freezing. However, the decision was made to detonate the stockpile after an inspection by the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms (ATF) discovered “significant crystallization on the exterior of the barrels” last March.

Ahead of today’s detonation, the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) supported a recommended, but voluntary, temporary relocation of all the residents of Middletown and immediate surrounding areas that began yesterday. A cone-shaped evacuation zone extends about five miles southwest of the ammunition plant to account for the post-explosion dispersion and deposition path of dust. Displaced residents will be lodged until Friday, November 27th, and can return home once the all-clear has been given.

To reduce the amount of lead dust released into surrounding areas and the environment, the Army said it took several additional precautions as part of today’s detonation. Walls made of four 16,000-gallon water tanks were installed to capture lead particulate, while the aging earth-covered magazines holding the barrels were covered with tarps and lead containment screens and flooded with foam.
