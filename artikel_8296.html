PCT Week 19: Choose Your Own Adventure

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

📍 Pratt River Backcountry Campsite | 🥾 37.71 km | ⛰️ +902m / -1,527m

This morning we got vortexed. It really wasn’t our fault. We did laundry, and it took forever. When it was finally done we trekked back into the village, picking up a few last minute resupply items from the store and gas station.

Then it was time to head off on a new kind of adventure. The next 70 miles of trail from here were closed thanks to nearby wildfires. But the PCT Association had published an alternate route we could take using other trails. There was a problem, though. Our navigation app was showing that there was a fire on the alternate. We called the Forest Service to check, and they didn’t seem to have any reliable information about it. Deciding we’d rather not accidentally end up doing an out-and-back, we instead mapped out an alternate route to the alternate.

It began with a road walk. Thankfully, it was a pretty quiet road in terms of traffic. The trail proper began with a climb. It was well maintained, and a decent grade – not too steep. But I was dragging. I just couldn’t make my feet move fast, and the group soon got ahead of me. I didn’t mind too much, I knew I’d run across them eventually. The climb ended, but there was no time to enjoy the elevation: the trail sent me straight down the other side of the mountain.

I arrived at a beautiful lake, where the group had decided to take a lunch break and a swim. It was too chilly for me by far, so I just filled up on water and chatted to them for a little while. We agreed on a campsite, and I set off knowing they’d all catch me soon enough.

I twisted and turned down what felt like an endless series of switchbacks through a dark forest, and soon enough the group had overtaken me. But I caught up to them again at a river crossing. I was dismayed to see there was no chance of rockhopping: I hate having wet feet, so shoes off it was. When I got to the other side Shakedown reported that he’d tried to balance on a log, but it had broken into bits beneath him and he was soaked.

From there, there were more water crossings although thankfully none that required shoe removal – they were small enough, or had good bridges or rock hops. The trail from there was through more dark forest, this time lined with ferns that reminded me of hiking in New Zealand. But even though the going was pretty easy, our paces weren’t very high for some reason. Darkness rapidly approached, and we were still miles from camp. The headlamps came out for the last hour of the day, and it grew pretty chilly.

📍 Mile 2,467.4 | 🥾 47.83 km | ⛰️ +1,302m / -1,313m

This morning we had two choices: ford the river, close to our campsite; or hike three miles up to a bridge. I chose the bridge, as did everyone except Catch. He missed out: the bridge ended up being pretty epic, arching above the clear blue waters of the Pratt River. We met up with him at the campground on the other side, then headed down the road for a few miles until we found a picnic table where Shakedown, Sabretooth and I had a late breakfast.

Then it was more road walking up to the next trailhead, where we ran into a large hiking group. They asked where we were heading and when we said “Canada”, looked confused. Normally we get quite excited reactions when we say that, but to be fair this trail isn’t where the PCT usually goes.

The next few miles were nice and flat, and passed by quickly. Everyone got ahead of me again, and I battled up the climb to Snoqualmie Lake alone. I ran into Sass, Sabretooth and Shakedown shortly afterwards at Bear Lake, where we luxuriated in the sun for a little while before hitting the trail once more.

I took my time on the descent to Dorothy Lake. The trail didn’t get much sun, and a lot of it was reinforced with rocks that were slippery with moss. When I arrived at the lakeshore I thought the going would get faster, but it didn’t. Boulders were everywhere, keeping my progress slow. But at the end of it I arrived at a dirt road, and from there I was flying. Soon I’d overtaken Sass, then Sabretooth and Shakedown – who’d all passed me at Dorothy. Not Catch, though. None of us had seen him in a while, so we assumed he’d raced ahead to town.

Towards the end of the road everything began to hurt, and my speed dropped once more, especially when we hit asphalt. The others caught up to me easily as I hobbled through the last mile. Arriving at the highway that would lead us to the trail town of Skykomish, we were dismayed to see a tunnel. We didn’t want to risk walking through it on a busy road, so instead started trying for a hitch. Luckily, we didn’t have to wait long.

We were dropped in front of the only place still serving food past 7pm: the Whistling Post Saloon. We headed inside and began hunting for a table. A local came up, asked if we were PCT hikers, and insisted on buying us a drink when we confirmed we were. Meanwhile, Sabretooth had made friends with some other locals, who invited us to join their table since there were no others free. Then we were offered a free campsite on a local business’s property, and showers at another local’s house. What was going on?? There was SO MUCH trail magic here, it was incredible. “I love this town,” Shakedown kept repeating as we gratefully accepted all the offers.

📍 Mile 2,485.8 | 🥾 30.62 km | ⛰️ +1,361m / -1,119m

By 9.01am we’d grabbed a table at Cascadia and were helping ourselves to huge plates of fried eggs, bacon, fresh fruit and other breakfast goodies. Surprisingly, it didn’t take long for us to eat our fill. Though we did all at least go for seconds, and in some cases thirds. Then it was time to resupply. Our only options were a deli and a gas station; the town didn’t have a supermarket. The locals had all individually been warning us how disgusting the gas station was, so I was pretty nervous about venturing inside. But it turned out to be perfectly fine, and I’d soon picked up everything I needed for the next couple of days.

Getting back to trail required a little bit of tetris in the back of a small hatchback. I climbed in the boot with most of the packs, and the other four all squeezed into the middle seat with a pack or two on top. We were dropped at Stevens Pass which was chock full of cars for a mountain biking event and, we’d soon discover, plenty of day hikers. It felt good to be back on the PCT proper. The trail felt nice and gentle even as it steadily climbed away from Stevens Pass past a series of lakes. We stopped for a late lunch at Lake Janus, where both Shakedown and Sass confessed that when they’d arrived at the lake they’d thought Catch, with his shoulder-length hair, taking a swim in the lake, had been a topless woman. Sabretooth and I cackled.

The afternoon was spent climbing up to Grizzly Peak. I spent most of it doing mental maths. We’d checked the weather in town and seen that rain, and potentially snow, was forecast in a few days. Considering how cold it’s getting, I’d rather be nice and dry in town if it rains. What we hadn’t checked, though, was the forecast at the terminus. I played through potential scenarios in my head, trying to optimise our time on trail.

At the top of Grizzly Peak I ran into three other PCT hikers, and had a brief chat with them. They were planning to cowboy camp up there. Mad respect, it was way too cold in my opinion. After a few minutes I continued on and was met with the kind of view only the PCT provides. The brown ribbon of trail was lined with scarlet bushes, leading me to a wide open meadow of long, faded yellow grass with views of tall pines framing snow-capped peaks in the far distance.

I arrived at the junction to Pear Lake, and found Sass sitting on a rock looking morose. “What’s up?” I asked. He just pointed to the ground near my feet. I was instantly on alert – was it a snake?! No, it was a huge arrow that someone had drawn in the dirt. Sass thought Catch had drawn it, to let us know he’d pushed on past the lake. We discussed what to do. I was pretty set on staying at the lake – I didn’t want to night hike. We decided to wait a little while for Shakedown and Sabretooth. But after only 5 minutes of waiting and with daylight rapidly fading I gave up and headed down to the lake. Sass scuffed at the ground to get rid of the arrow, drawing a new one towards the lake for Shakedown and Sabretooth before following me. But when I made it down to the campsites I couldn’t help but crow with laughter. Catch was there, tent already pitched and dinner well underway. He hadn’t even seen the arrow. Sass’s jaw dropped when he arrived, and we laughed again as we told the story to Shakedown and Sabretooth who arrived not long afterwards.

📍 North Fork Sauk Trailhead | 🥾 48.03 km | ⛰️ +1,593m / -2,467m

By 7am we were trickling out of camp one-by-one, into a beautiful morning full of blue skies and golden sunlight that danced on the changing leaves. Then, after about an hour of twisting and turning through the forest, I arrived on a wide open hillside. It was carpeted in bushes whose leaves were turning vermillion and amber, contrasting beautifully with the proud green trees which pointed towards tall glacier-topped mountains in the distance. It was perfect. Today was the kind of day that leaves you feeling truly awe-struck. It was not a day for listening to audiobooks – sorry, book club. Every sense needed to be available to take it all in. I was practically floating.

At Lake Sally Ann I came across a large group of hunters. A couple of them were pretty chatty, and wanted to know if I was hiking the PCT. They seemed nice, but I was cautious: “Yeah, I am. I’m with a couple of people – you might’ve already seen two of them?” They had. They asked me a few questions about the trail and how to prepare, then admitted they thought they were too old to attempt a thru-hike. “Not at all! There are people in their 80s doing this,” I told them. They seemed intrigued, like maybe they would actually look into giving it a go. I hope they do.

I continued on, making very slow progress as I made the most of the scenery, stopping frequently not just for photos, but to take it all in and commit it to memory. At the border of Glacier Peaks Wilderness I reached a milestone: 2,500 miles on the redline. Wow. Even though I’d had to skip some miles thanks to closures, since I’d tacked on the Tahoe Rim Trail and other side quests I was still well above that number in terms of miles completed while on the PCT. Arriving there alone was a little anticlimactic, though. I’d hoped Catch and Sass would’ve stopped for lunch. They hadn’t, but I decided to stop anyway and enjoy, hoping Shakedown and Sabretooth would turn up while I ate. They didn’t, so after half an hour I pushed on.

A few miles later I came across Catch and Sass lying in a meadow with a spectacular view of Glacier Peak. I threw my pack down and joined them for a little while, savoring this last little stretch of official PCT miles before we once again had to take an alternate – this time, around the Miner’s and Sisi fires. After today’s scenery, being forced to take this alternate hurt. Emotionally and literally. We descended the North Fork Sauk Trail along a series of sharp switchbacks which were filled with golf ball sized stones. By the end of it, my knees were protesting loudly. Thankfully, from there it was flat trail through a gorgeous forest to the campground at the trailhead.

Over dinner, we discussed tactics. Rain was meant to start on day 134. But we were facing around 70 miles of road walking around to the Cascade Pass trailhead, via the town of Darrington. We had no service, so couldn’t check for an updated forecast, but agreed that if rain was still coming in then we’d skip the road from Darrington to the trailhead. We thought it would be pretty pointless to waste lovely, sunny weather road walking on an alternate when we could spend the sunny weather on trail and arrive in town just as the rain started.

📍 Johannesburg Campground | 🥾 28.78 km | ⛰️ +168m / -604m

Although all of today would be road walking, the five of us set out together, spirits high. We walked in a line straight across the dirt road, chatting happily, darting over to the side any time a car approached. Thankfully, they were few and far between. The scenery wasn’t much to write home about, with tall, dark forest on all sides, but the road helped us make good time. We were hitting over 3 miles an hour without even trying.

A few hours in, we found a couple of bars of phone service and checked the forecast. No change: rain was still meant to start on day 134. That was it, then; we’d try and hitch this afternoon from Darrington up to the Cascade Pass trailhead.

Eventually the road changed from dirt to asphalt. It didn’t take long for my body to complain. My feet and knees ached, and I struggled to keep pace with the group. Then it hit me: if we’re already skipping the road from Darrington onwards, why don’t we just skip this road, too? Everyone agreed, and the next time I heard a car coming I turned around and stuck out my thumb. It didn’t take us long to get hitches, and soon we were seated for lunch at Moe’s Cafe, where I demolished one of the best sandwiches I’ve had in a long while. Then we resupplied at the supermarket next door before splitting up to try and hitch to the trailhead.

Catch, Sass and I got our first ride from the first car that passed us, up to the casino on the edge of town. Then the casino courtesy bus immediately offered to drive us as far as the town of Concrete. Win! We were dropped off outside an old gas station. But our luck seemed to have run out. Cars streamed past for half an hour. We moved down towards an intersection, hoping to catch someone coming from another direction. And soon enough we were in yet another hitch. And maybe our luck hadn’t run out after all: he ended up picking up Sabretooth and Shakedown when we drove past them in Marblemount as well, and then he agreed to take us all the way to the trailhead, even though he hadn’t been heading that way. What a legend. Not to mention, he had two very friendly dogs who we enjoyed petting for the entire ride.

We arrived at the trailhead just after the sun disappeared behind the mountains. There was an outrageously beautiful view of the sheer, jagged peaks and their long, shimmering glaciers in the dying light. Unfortunately, the campsite was amongst the trees, so we didn’t get to enjoy it for long. And when we got to the campsite, we discovered that space was very limited. Shakedown and Sabretooth took one spot for their massive 2P Durston, while Catch, Sass and I decided to cowboy camp in the only other spot. There was no way all our tents would fit, and it was surprisingly warm beneath the trees. It felt right to squeeze in a final cowboy on the PCT, too.

📍 Dan’s Camp | 🥾 43.32 km | ⛰️ +1,577m / -1,510m

I’d had a brainwave yesterday. Since we now had an extra day up our sleeves thanks to skipping the roadwalk, why not add on a side quest? My side quest of choice was the Rainbow Lake Trail down to Stehekin, which is a PCT town with a very famous bakery. I’d been a little sad about having to miss it thanks to the fires, but now it looked like I could have the best of both worlds if I did a couple of big days. I couldn’t convince the rest of the group to join me, though. They liked the sound of two shorter days better. So I set off on trail first, and alone. We’d meet up again in Mazama the day after tomorrow.

I was soon speeding past day hikers, and keeping well ahead of a pair of trail runners, feeling very satisfied with myself as I ascended a series of switchbacks up to Cascade Pass. The views up there were showstopping. I was up amongst the peaks we’d admired from the trailhead yesterday afternoon, enjoying the autumn colours that spilled over the landscape below. I was accompanied by a fat chipmunk who had no fear of me whatsoever as he sat atop a stone bench at the pass, clearly preparing for a big day of crumb stealing. The descent, however, was full of rocks, and so it was slower than I’d have liked. But at least there were plenty of gorgeous views of the valley to keep me occupied.

Then I was in the valley, back into the tunnel of trees. I suppose it’s not accurate to call it a green tunnel anymore; there was plenty of yellow, orange and red as well. I paused for lunch at a campsite, and not long afterwards ran into Sass, who’d gotten ahead of me. He told me he was waiting out a bear. Although I’d seen some bear scat on the trail earlier, I didn’t believe him and kept moving forward. He seemed committed, though, and it was several minutes before I heard him steadily gaining on me. Eventually he overtook, and not long afterwards stopped in his tracks, claiming the bear was back. I walked up to where he was, and was a little surprised to see a large, dark brown rear end meandering up the trail. Sass tried yelling “hey bear”, although you couldn’t really call it a yell. I could barely hear him and I was right behind him. So instead we sat down on nearby rocks and waited for the bear to move on.

When we got going again, there was no sign of the bear. Not even any more scat, so we assumed he’d left the trail. Soon afterwards we were rejoining the PCT. This time, though, it didn’t feel so great to be back on the redline. We’d been spoiled by the beauty of Cascade Pass, and now we were just tramping through forest, catching only small glimpses of mountaintops through the canopy.

After only 7 miles, though, we were leaving the PCT once again. Sass had decided to join my side quest to Stehekin after all. We arrived at camp just as night fell, pitching our tents and gathering water in pitch black.

📍 Mile 2,589.4 | 🥾 52.58 km | ⛰️ +2,647m / -2,706m

I’d done the maths, and my Stehekin side quest was meant to be 24 miles, plus another 5 miles to Rainy Pass once we rejoined the redline. Easy. We set off at 7am into a very chilly morning. Our campsite had been down in a valley and the cold air seemed to have pooled there. We wound beneath the treetops, trying to warm up. Unfortunately, the sun didn’t seem to be on our side. As far as we could see, the sky was grey and overcast.

We emerged from the trees into a meadow at the base of a horseshoe of scree-covered mountainsides. We approached and began to climb what turned out to be an excellent trail with fantastic views of the mountains. The wind didn’t let us linger at the top of the pass for too long, though.

We descended quickly, overlooking the namesake Rainbow Lake. What we saw from above made the short side-trip to the lakeshore mandatory. When we arrived we gazed, entranced, for several minutes. The lake’s surface was a perfect mirror, reflecting the scree-filled mountains on its far side that also boasted a collage of autumn colours. When we finally got going again, we congratulated ourselves on making excellent choices.

We continued our descent, catching glimpses of what we thought was a cloud inversion in the valley ahead. As the day progressed the blue sky disappeared, but the cloud inversion stayed where it was. Then we caught a whiff of it and realised we were idiots: it was smoke haze from the Sisi fire. The blanket of it thickened as we descended further and further down the valley, eventually becoming strong enough to scratch at my throat a little. We ended up walking through an old burn scar, too; the combination of it and the smoke was a little sobering.

We made it to the road into Stehekin and started charging towards the bakery. We stepped inside and up to the counter, and my eyes almost popped. The pastries were HUGE, and so cheap. After some deliberation I decided to try the sticky bun, and grabbed a coffee to help wash it down.

After two hours enjoying the comfort of the bakery, we grabbed some cinnamon rolls to pack out for the group and set out on trail once more. It began with backtracking over some of the trail we’d used to get to town, but after a few miles we took the McAlester Trail which would rejoin the PCT seven miles ahead of where we’d left it. It was nice to be able to enjoy some new scenery.

But I was growing tired, and ready for camp. I began compulsively checking Gaia, trying to work out how much further I had to go. It should only have been just over 11 miles from Stehekin back to the PCT, according to my maths. I’d used the official distance from a North Cascades National Parks map and everything! But then I realised: I’d miscalculated. The map showed the distances between campsites, and I’d added those together. But the distances didn’t always appear on the same side of the trail line, and I’d missed a 5 mile section when I was doing the maths. Damn. It looked like it’d be a long night.

We timed it perfectly, though, arriving at McAlester Pass just as dusk began to fall, and entering treeline once more as it grew dark. We walked for an hour or so in the dark until we finally made it to camp, where we set up and ate dinner quickly, exhausted after a mammoth day of mileage and our biggest-ever day of elevation gain.
