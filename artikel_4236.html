Loneliness is making us sick, but we may be treating it all wrong

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Research has established that loneliness can have serious consequences for physical health. However, a new University of Michigan study warns that treating loneliness primarily as a health problem could shift responsibility away from society and place it on a healthcare system that lacks the tools to restore meaningful social connections.

Published in Social Problems, the study examines how chronic loneliness and social isolation became recognized as major public health concerns after researchers uncovered links to illness, premature death, and increased healthcare spending.

Previous studies highlighted in the research illustrate the severity of these consequences. One estimated that lacking social connections could be as harmful to health as smoking 15 cigarettes a day. Another found that loneliness, social isolation, and living alone were each associated with an approximately 30% higher risk of premature death. The financial burden is also substantial, with separate research estimating that social isolation costs Medicare $6.7 billion each year in care for older adults.

"Once people saw these findings, they began to treat loneliness as urgent," said University of Michigan doctoral student Sofia Hiltner. "But it made me wonder: Why do we only consider problems important once they are tied to health?"

To understand how loneliness became a medical concern, Hiltner conducted interviews with experts and reviewed more than a decade of media coverage, medical journal articles, and academic literature.

Her analysis revealed that establishing a connection between loneliness and poor health helped the issue gain credibility. However, it also encouraged what she describes as a "logical leap": the belief that any problem affecting human health should become the responsibility of the healthcare system.

"Not everything that relates to health belongs in healthcare," she said. "Doctors can screen patients and connect them to services, but they cannot rebuild communities or shorten work hours."

Hiltner, a predoctoral trainee in social demography at the Population Studies Center within the University of Michigan's Institute for Social Research, was inspired to investigate loneliness after witnessing her grandmother become increasingly isolated as she aged.

When Hiltner entered graduate school to research social isolation among older adults, she learned that clinical trials were investigating a "loneliness pill." The discovery prompted her to question why loneliness was being approached primarily as a medical condition rather than a social problem.

"After my grandfather died, my grandmother lived alone and there was little structure in her life," Hiltner said. "She didn't seem to have many friends or activities. It made me think about everything that happened throughout her life that led to that point. I wondered about the social policies, in addition to dynamics at the family level, that could have helped prevent that situation."

Finding Solutions Beyond the Healthcare System

Interestingly, Hiltner found that the medical community was initially reluctant to take responsibility for addressing loneliness. Much of the momentum behind incorporating loneliness into healthcare instead came from academic researchers, government health officials, and insurance companies.

Although recognizing loneliness as a medical concern can generate greater awareness and encourage intervention, it may also divert valuable resources from efforts to address the social circumstances that leave people isolated.

"There is the risk that defining loneliness as medical crowds out other ways of responding to the issue," she said. "Attention, time and resources are limited. On the other hand, framing it as medical may inspire more action than would have otherwise happened."

Hiltner hopes her findings will encourage policymakers to reconsider how they define social problems and explore preventive strategies that address their underlying causes before they develop into serious health concerns.

"I would like them to reflect on the strengths and limitations of addressing problems through the healthcare system, and look farther upstream to understand how a problem emerges, considering ways to intervene before it occurs," she said. "Healthcare can help, but it can only go so far."
