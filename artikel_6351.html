Paying down debt can leave a country more exposed when the next hurricane hits

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

After Hurricane Melissa, Jamaica borrowed about 18% of its economy to recover, and researchers say lenders reward debt cuts but not seawalls.

Paying down debt is supposed to make a country safer. Lenders reward it with better credit ratings and lower borrowing costs. Jamaica spent more than a decade doing exactly that, but one hurricane undid much of the progress in a matter of weeks.

The reason, four researchers argue, is that the rules rewarding a country for paying off loans offer nothing comparable for building seawalls and drains.

Hurricane Melissa hit Jamaica as a Category 5 storm in October 2025. It caused an estimated $12.2 billion in damage, losses, and costs.

This represents about 57 percent of the country’s entire economic output for 2024. Insurance covered about 2 percent of that, and Jamaica borrowed roughly $3.6 billion to recover.

Aayushi Mishra, a doctoral candidate at Northeastern University, traced what happened next. Her co-authors are Advait Arun of the Center for Public Enterprise and Northeastern’s Evan Kodra and Auroop Ganguly.

The paper is a policy analysis built on Jamaica’s public finance and disaster-loss figures, not a new data study. It argues for changes at the International Monetary Fund (IMF), the development banks, and the rating agencies.

In an interview with Earth.com, Mishra said the people running those institutions are not the villains of the story. “No one here is acting in bad faith: every institution is doing exactly what its own rules ask of it,” she said.

“The problem is that those rules, once taken together, leave resilience underfunded.”

In 2013, Jamaica owed about 144 percent of its yearly economic output in debt. By 2023 it had cut that to about 72 percent, and it was on track for 60 percent by the 2027 to 2028 budget year.

The country did this through years of tight budgets and strict spending rules that it adopted on its own, not as a condition set by the IMF.

Lenders noticed. In September 2025, the rating agency S&P Global upgraded Jamaica’s credit rating. A few weeks later, Melissa made landfall.

Asked by Earth.com what surprised her most, Mishra pointed to the speed of the reversal. “Honestly, how little time it took for Jamaica’s hard-won progress to get diluted by just one storm, albeit a massive one,” she said.

The team projects Jamaica’s debt will now peak at about 68.5 percent of its economy, roughly 8.5 points above the path it was on. New borrowing makes up most of that increase, and the storm’s damage to output accounts for the rest, shrinking the economy against which analysts measure the debt.

Jamaica had done the things disaster experts recommend. It held a catastrophe bond through the World Bank, which pays investors’ money to the country if a storm crosses set thresholds for wind speed, track, and pressure.

It also bought insurance from a regional pool, the Caribbean Catastrophe Risk Insurance Facility (CCRIF), that pays on the same measurements rather than on assessed damage.

Both paid quickly. The bond paid its full $150 million, and with the insurance and other aid the total reached about $242 million, within 3 to 14 days of landfall. Jamaica waited about three months for its IMF money to be approved.

But $242 million against $12.2 billion in losses is 2 percent. Speed was never the problem; scale was.

The year before, Hurricane Beryl had caused about $1 billion in damage, and the bond paid nothing because the storm’s measured strength fell short of its trigger. The insurance pool covered $26.6 million of Beryl’s damage, under 3 percent.

The instruments worked exactly as specified, the team wrote. Insurers have a name for the gap between what a storm measures and what it costs: basis risk. For a whole country, that gap was about 98 cents of every dollar lost.

The country is paying for the rest of the recovery with roughly $3.6 billion in new loans, about 18 percent of Jamaica’s pre-storm output in a single year. That is above the level the IMF treats as a warning sign for emerging economies.

The loans come from the IMF, the World Bank, and three regional development banks, mostly on terms that have not been made public.

So the team modeled a range, from low-cost development lending to near-market rates. The team put total repayments, including interest, at $4.9 billion to $5.7 billion.

What matters to Jamaica’s budget is the yearly cash payment. At peak repayment, the team estimates, servicing the new debt alone would approach or exceed Jamaica’s entire annual budget for public investment.

Jamaica’s investment budget was already small. In the 2024 to 2025 budget year, Jamaica spent about $3.15 billion servicing debt and about $510 million on public capital projects.

That second budget is the one that pays for coastal defenses and drainage. “So for every $1 the country spent on things like public infrastructure, it paid $6 in debt service,” Mishra told Earth.com.

How much would it have taken to avoid the borrowing? Using cost-benefit ratios from disaster research, the team estimates that spending $360 million to $900 million a year on protection before the storm could have offset the new debt entirely.

The IMF’s own 2025 review of Jamaica’s economy found the country could afford only $40 million to $80 million a year on climate adaptation without straining its debt path. That is 4 to 22 times less than the estimated need.

The gap narrows if private investors cover most of the cost, as the researchers assume they would.

The government’s share would then be about $120 million to $300 million a year, still 1.5 to 7.5 times what the IMF considered sustainable.

The team wrote that these figures can’t say which investments would have worked best, or how long they would have taken to build.

Researchers have modeled this pattern before and call it a climate-debt doom loop.

A disaster hits an underprepared country, and the country borrows to recover. The repayments then crowd out spending on protection, so the country is just as exposed when the next disaster arrives.

The authors wrote that no one had documented the loop unfolding in real time inside a single country before.

Rating agencies grade countries on budget surpluses, how fast debt falls, and how strictly budget rules are followed. Some have begun adding climate risk to side reports, but the authors wrote that it stays peripheral to the core decision. Nothing in that system changes when a country builds a seawall.

The IMF’s health check on a country’s debt added climate risk guidance for some programs in 2024. The authors wrote that it is still not part of the core method.

Jamaica’s 2025 review asked how much adaptation the country could afford without hurting its debt path. It didn’t ask the reverse: what happens to the debt when adaptation falls short and a catastrophe follows.

The paper’s four proposals aim at the lenders, not at Jamaica. Resilience spending should count in the IMF’s debt analysis as something that lowers the odds of a catastrophic loss.

An IMF trust fund created in 2022 for low-cost, long-term climate lending should be scaled up for the most indebted small island states.

Storm insurance triggers should use rainfall and surge as well as wind, and development banks should back the pools so they can pay more.

The fourth is a clause in loan contracts. Mishra said countries can add these now, while their finances are strong, as Barbados has.

“Simply put, these clauses let debt payments pause for up to two years after a disaster, so the country doesn’t have to take on new debt to cover them,” she said. Seventy-three countries and the major development banks have committed to them.

Ganguly, Mishra’s doctoral advisor, said much of the underlying data in this field is kept behind institutional walls or never disclosed.

“We would like to see funders and multilateral institutions to treat disclosure of these terms as standard practice (and not a courtesy), since the public and researchers alike are left to trust conclusions that they can’t necessarily check independently,” he said.

Nearly a year after Melissa, many of the terms of Jamaica’s recovery loans have still not been disclosed, Mishra said. Until they are, the cost of one storm to Jamaica is a range, not a number.

The next test is whether pause clauses get written into loans before the next hurricane arrives, rather than after.

The full study was published in the journal Science.
