Water sinking near Antarctica carries nutrients on a decades-long journey

Tanggal: 2026-11-11

Southern Ocean nutrients ride north for decades in two sinking water layers, and a new study finds each layer recycles a different mix.

The Southern Ocean looks remote, about as far from the Gulf of Maine or the North Pacific as water gets. Yet the water that sinks there each winter spends decades sliding north beneath the world’s oceans.

It carries nutrients that support plankton growth across much of the ocean.

A new study finds that two of those sinking layers recycle their nutrients in different ways. Each one arrives decades later with a different mix.

Barney Balch, a senior research scientist emeritus at Bigelow Laboratory for Ocean Sciences in Maine, led the work.

He collaborated with colleagues at the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution, the Bermuda Institute of Ocean Sciences, Texas A&M University and the University of Miami.

The team sampled water from a ship along a single line south of Hawaii, so the findings describe one slice of the Pacific.

“When you integrate this water around the globe, the nutrient regeneration in Subantarctic Mode Water and Antarctic Intermediate Water supports about three-quarters of the primary production on Earth north of the 30th parallel south,” Balch said.

Powerful winds and currents pull deep water up to the surface around Antarctica.

Winter cooling mixes it into thick, uniform layers, and some of that water sinks again and heads north.

Two layers matter here. Subantarctic Mode Water forms farther north and rides higher.

Antarctic Intermediate Water forms closer to the ice, slides beneath the mode water, and ends up 2,000 to 3,600 feet (600 to 1,100 meters) down.

Both layers carry the remains of dead plankton sinking from above. Microbes break those remains down, and nitrogen and phosphorus return to the water in forms plankton can use again.

The glass-like shells of diatoms dissolve more slowly and release silicate, which diatoms need to build new shells.

The research vessel Roger Revelle left Honolulu on December 26, 2020. It worked its way south along the 150th meridian west to 60 degrees south and returned in February 2021.

The hard part was knowing how old each parcel of water was.

The team used chlorofluorocarbons, the refrigerant gases that leaked into the air through much of the 20th century.

The mix of those gases changed year by year as factories switched compounds, so water that last touched the air carries a blend that can be matched to a year.

By that clock, the mode water was mostly 35 to 40 years old.

The intermediate water ran older, up to about 70 years. Pairing the ages with nutrient levels let the team turn a snapshot into rates of change.

“What makes this paper unique is that we are combining the biological or chemical measurements with the time clock, the CFC-derived age of the water, which allows you to talk about rates,” Balch said.

In the mode water, nitrate built up about seven times faster than in the intermediate water, and phosphate about six times faster.

Both are essential, and nitrogen often limits how much plankton can grow, so the shallower layer is the one getting richer on its journey.

The mode water also gained dissolved carbon about twice as fast, and it appeared to use up oxygen faster too, which fits more microbial activity there.

The team wrote that mixing with neighboring water can inflate rates like these, by as much as double.

Silicate was the exception. It accumulated almost twice as fast in the deeper intermediate water as in the mode water, at about 2.6 against 1.4 micromoles per kilogram of seawater per year.

The split could matter at the surface. When the mode water finally mixes back up into sunlit water, its nitrogen-rich recipe would favor plankton that don’t need silica.

One example is the coccolithophores, which armor themselves in chalk plates.

Deeper, stronger mixing that brings up intermediate water would favor diatoms, the glass-shelled algae of cold waters.

One result surprised the authors. Silicate accumulated in the intermediate water at least 20 times faster than the slow dissolution of suspended shells could explain.

That points to fast-sinking clumps of diatom debris as a major source.

Temperature and salinity shifted with age too. In the mode water, the youngest samples were warmer and saltier than the oldest.

Across the samples, temperature decreased by about 0.3°C for each additional year of estimated water age.

This relationship was much steeper than warming trends reported in other Southern Ocean records.

In the intermediate water, the youngest water was fresher, the opposite trend.

The authors wrote that those patterns could record changing conditions where the layers form, or mixing with water above and below along the way. The data can’t tell which.

Older samples also occurred at greater depths, with each additional year of estimated age corresponding to about 240 feet (73 meters) in the mode water and 128 feet (39 meters) in the intermediate water.

The study covers one north-south line in one season, and the chlorofluorocarbon clock itself is uncertain by roughly 30%.

The authors wrote that they could not fully separate recycling inside each layer from year-to-year changes in the water when it formed, or from mixing along the way.

Whether the two recipes actually change which plankton bloom when the water resurfaces, decades after it sank, has not been measured.

The test would be to follow the same layers into the North Pacific, where they finally return to the light.

The full study was published in the journal Journal of Geophysical Research: Oceans.
