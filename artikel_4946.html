Fera Fruit Oil Claims It’s Healthier Than Even Avocado Oil. Should You Make the Switch?

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

EREWHON, EVERYONE’S FAVORITE love-to-hate overpriced supermarket chain made news recently for switching up their oils. Yes, the certified organic grocer announced that it's no longer using avocado oil to fry its cafe food. Instead, all 15 locations are switching to certified organic fera fruit oil.

Erewhon isn’t the only company hawking fera fruit oil as a way to further health-ify their vegan buffalo cauliflower and turmeric chicken tenders. Since launching in April 2026, the oil is now in more than 800 grocers, restaurants, wellness clubs, and luxury hotels like Mezeh Mediterranean Grill and Wynn Las Vegas.

Fera fruit oil may sound exotic and healthy (it’s made from fruit?), but as a registered dietitian, I'm not one for assumptions when it comes to food. Especially when it coincides with the peak of seed-oil fear-messaging. Keep reading to learn what this new-to-the-scene frying oil is and if it's really worth the hype.

FERA FRUIT OIL is basically palm oil. The company behind it, Zero Acre Farms, trademarked the name “fera fruit oil” for high oleic palm oil, a type of palm oil that's produced by pressing the fruit of a hybrid palm tree called Oleifera × Guineensis (OxG for short).

The hybrid plant was originally planted by farmers to reduce bud rot, a disease that notoriously destroys palm crops. A happy side effect was that the oil also ended up having higher oleic acid content, the same heart-healthy monounsaturated fat found in avocado and olive oil.

TRADITIONALLY GROWN PALM has a problematic history of deforestation, but according to Zero Acre Farms, fera fruit oil is a perennial crop that requires just 0.3 acres to produce a ton of oil, using 15 to 20 times less land than soybean or canola oil.

Of course, Zero Acre Farms’ website doesn't cite sources for these numbers, so I did my own digging. Independent research suggests traditional palm yields 3.3 tons per hectare, which is impressive compared to soybean, canola, and sunflower, which collectively produce 0.6 tons per hectare. But OxG palm yields about 1 ton less than traditional palm, which means the demonized seed oils use 3 to 4 times more land, not 15 to 20 times. So the claims seem a bit sensationalized at best?

COMPARED WITH REGULAR palm oil, high oleic palm oil—including the trademarked fera fruit oil—offers a significant nutritional upgrade.

Traditional palm oil contains about 50 percent saturated fat, including about 40 percent oleic acid, and 10 percent polyunsaturated fatty acids. OxG, the hybrid that produces fera fruit, contains 50 percent oleic acid, 33 percent saturated fatty acids, and 12 percent polyunsaturated fatty acids.

As a dietitian, I can get behind these improvements, but the benefits don't translate to real-world benefits quite as well.

The only way you'll eat fera fruit oil is if you're enjoying pre-made food, most likely fried food, from an establishment using it, as it's not currently available for consumer purchase. And while food fried in fera fruit oil may be slightly nutritionally better than traditional palm oil (which most establishments in the U.S. aren't using anyway), it's still fried food.

This ratio not only contains less saturated fat, but also helps maintain stability over multiple rounds of deep frying compared to other oils. A study presented at the 21st International Oil Palm Conference found that high oleic palm oil lasted more rounds of frying than palm, sunflower, soy, or canola. Because it has less polyunsaturated fat and more monounsaturated and saturated fat, it resists degradation better. This adds up to cost savings, because the oil can be used multiple times, but there's unlikely much of a health advantage. Deep-frying meat and starchy foods in any oil can create harmful compounds, but the link between reusing oil, oil stability, and human health is less clear.

Unrefined high oleic palm oil is rich in antioxidants, including carotenoids and vitamin E. Fera fruit oil is marketed as clean, neutral-tasting, and expeller-pressed, but it offers no guarantee or information on what antioxidants are in the bottle at use or how much.

So while this all sounds great on paper, it's hard to tell how it actually translates into real-world benefits. Is it worth the hype? I'd personally like to see tangible evidence that it does before shelling out extra money for it, and that evidence doesn't seem to exist yet.

Prior to October 15, Erewhon used avocado oil in its fryers. So while there's no arguing that Fera fruit oil has a healthier fatty acid profile than regular palm oil, how does it compare to other fruit oils, like avocado and olive oil, in terms of both health and sustainability?

While olive oil has strong evidence supporting its positive effects on human health, especially heart health, clinical research on the health effects of high-oleic palm oil, like Fera fruit oil, is limited. In one study, 160 participants had either 25 mL of high oleic palm oil or 25 mL of extra virgin olive oil daily for three months. Both groups had similar reductions in total cholesterol and LDL-cholesterol. While these results are promising, the study was small, and more clinical trials are needed to confidently say high oleic palm oil is as beneficial as olive oil.

Avocado oil has a favorable fatty acid profile of 71 percent monounsaturated fatty acids, 13 percent polyunsaturated fatty acids, and 16 percent saturated fatty acids. Fera fruit oil has more saturated fat and less monounsaturated fat, but no head-to-head studies have tested their health effects.

When an elite grocery store like Erewhon switches to a new fryer oil, it's easy to assume it's the healthiest choice. But I wouldn't be so quick to jump on board.

After reviewing the research and understanding the food climate we're currently in, where seed oils are viewed as the culprit of all disease and beef tallow is a cure-all, this honestly seems like just another misguided trend. In the context of reality, when you eat an overall healthy and balanced diet, the occasional fried food, whether it's fried in avocado oil, canola oil, or fera fruit oil, will likely have very little impact on your overall health.
