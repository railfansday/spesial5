Here Be Dragons: Why AI Is Not The Thing To Fear

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Adrian Carr is CEO of global master data management provider Stibo Systems, which empowers companies through trustworthy intelligence.

​For me, the phrase “here be dragons” conjures images of a cartographer from times of old, reaching the edge of what he knew. With a quill pen, he fills the empty spaces of his maps with sea serpents and other beasts. Warnings, to keep sailors from venturing too far into the unknown.

So, even the dragons were a story told to fill a gap.

I find that fitting, because we are doing exactly the same with AI.

Every generation reaches the edge of its map. Ours just happens to involve AI, and the margins are filling up quickly. Some people are drawing utopias there, others monsters. Most of us draw a bit of both.

The people who move a map’s edge are rarely the ones who draw dragons. They are the explorers, the innovators and they sailed on regardless of them. For AI to be created, it needed many things in place first: the microchip, which made processing power small and cheap enough to be everywhere. It also needed the internet, which connected us all and filled the world with the text, images and records that models learn from.

Each of those innovations was somebody’s edge of the map once. Ours is simply the next.

The trouble is that most of us are frightened of this one. Humans respond to uncertainty in different ways, but most of us feel fear. With AI, we are also confused, anxious about missing out. Yet fear has never stopped all of us. There are always people who overcome it and sail on, and they are the ones who change the trajectory of the human race.

For 20 years, search engines taught us to speak to machines in fragments. We would type “restaurants near me open now” or “weather tomorrow afternoon.” We adapted to the machine. AI has turned that around—the machine now adapts to us, answering (even badly phrased) questions in plain language and with context.

That is a real shift. But it also creates a trust paradox. A global study of more than 48,000 people by the University of Melbourne and KPMG found that 66% use AI regularly, yet only 46% trust it. More telling, 66% said they rely on AI output without evaluating its accuracy, and 56% said they had made mistakes in their work because of it.

We use AI, we doubt AI and we act on AI anyway. That is precisely the kind of gap where dragons get drawn.

When you began reading this, you probably assumed the dragon I was talking about was AI. If you are still with me, I believe the dragon on our map is the unexamined ground underneath AI.

Earlier this year, I had my first journey in a car capable of autonomous driving. I confess to many nervous minutes watching every move, my head spinning to check for traffic and cyclists. And plenty of people share that unease; AAA’s 2025 survey found that 6 in 10 U.S. drivers are still afraid to ride in a self-driving vehicle.

But this feeling faded as I reasoned that the car had more sensors than me, with greater capability. The car could react faster. It was unlikely to get distracted or even sneeze. I concluded the car was likely a safer driver than I could ever be.

But on that same journey, the car got stuck at a traffic light. The lights were not in the standard positions, and the car became confused. The car was capable, but the underlying infrastructure had gaps.

Most organizations are in the same position with AI. We spend a great deal of time asking what models can do, and far less asking whether the information under them is fit for a machine to act on.

For more than a century, charts of the Coral Sea showed Sandy Island, a sizable strip of land between Australia and New Caledonia. It was copied from map to map, eventually to digital maps. In 2012, scientists on an Australian research voyage noticed it was missing from the ship’s own nautical charts and went to have a look.

They found only an expanse of open water. No island.

Every large organization has phantom islands of its own, and customer data is where I would look first. The same customer’s information can exist across sales, service, billing and marketing systems as several slightly different people, each version confident it is the right one. Something changes, and only some of those records get updated.

For years, we smoothed over those gaps with human intervention. But an AI agent acting fast will just act on whichever version of the customer it finds first, and it will repeat the mistake as faithfully as the mapmakers copied Sandy Island.

In a time when we are likely to jump ship due to anything less than a great customer experience, that is not good.

Every dragon dissolves as the map expands. Someone asks, “Are you sure?” and goes to find out. Curiosity gets us close enough to look. Trust is what makes the dragon disappear. And trust comes from evidence, not reassurance.

AI will follow the same path. The organizations that go furthest will be the ones that get their foundations ready for machines to trust. For leaders, that means asking a different set of questions: Do we have one version of each customer that we would trust an AI agent to act on? Where is our information still designed only for human eyes? Do we know where our most important data comes from, who owns it, how current it is and whether it is governed?

We should stop wasting energy on our fear of the unknown. We must assess the risks logically, mitigate them and step forward cautiously, because step forward we must.

Trusted data is what turns artificial intelligence into trustworthy intelligence.
