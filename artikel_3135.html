AI Is The Accommodation Nobody Has To Approve

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

Dave Goyal | CEO, Think AI | Author | Speaker | Harvard AI Certified | 30+ yrs in Data & AI | Inc5000 Honoree.

​When I roll into a conference on my electric scooter, I see it on people’s faces: sympathy, then distance. When I reach out, some people pull back. When the room starts to mingle, I often get left out.

My first thought used to be, “I shouldn’t have this problem.” My second thought is the one that built my career: “Let me shine through with my knowledge and connect with a few people.”

Founders are expected to move city to city, state to state, country to country. Physical limits hit hardest right when you want to grow fastest.

Mindset is the bigger fight. You spend years trying to show you are not different.

Then there is money. Entrepreneurs with disabilities average about $61,000 a year in revenue, less than half the $135,000 of other entrepreneurs, according to a 2025 fact sheet from AEO based on NORC data.

Yet we choose this path more often. In 2025, 9.1% of employed people with a disability were self-employed, compared with 5.9% of everyone else, according to the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics.

The ones who make it have serious grit. But grit is tied to mindset, and it runs low. What keeps mine going is a life mission. Find your one superpower and a mission for life. Without them, it is very hard to survive, let alone grow.

An employee with a disability asks HR for an accommodation and waits for a yes. A founder has no HR. AI is the accommodation you can give yourself.

This summer I drove 3,200 miles: Los Angeles to Seattle to Yellowstone, back through Las Vegas and home. My car’s supervised self-driving system let me drive without my braces, using only my left leg. An AI assistant connected to a mapping service planned every day: miles between stops, where to eat and which hotels had the access I need. Not every hotel does, so I packed a folding shower chair.

Founders like me are already leaning in. Twenty-two percent of entrepreneurs with disabilities use generative AI, compared with 10% of other entrepreneurs, per the same AEO fact sheet.

Franklin D. Roosevelt had polio, like me, and founded the March of Dimes. Richard Branson and Ingvar Kamprad built Virgin and IKEA with dyslexia. Stevie Wonder, blind since infancy, has owned a Los Angeles radio station since 1979. Tony Robbins grew up poor and turned it into a lifelong mission to feed families.

Music was my own calmer. Learning to sing, write songs and play guitar built my confidence and songwriting taught me to write.

I have started nine companies, and five failed. AI helped me learn the full process of building a business, end to end, check it against what those nine taught me and turn it into a repeatable system. AI can keep the notes, so you do not have to carry it all in your head.

Preparing my first podcast episode took 24 to 30 hours: guest research, follow-ups, questions and how to bring my own thinking into the conversation. Today an automated process does that work, and I plan an entire episode, including recording, in two to three hours.

Start by tracking which tasks drain your energy for a week. Hand off the worst ones first.

I earned an MBA in international business with a finance major and studied toward the CFA, yet dyslexia meant I swapped numbers and never fully trusted them.

Now my AI works from a read-only copy of my accounting data. Nothing goes back without my approval, and client names are stored apart from the amounts, so the AI never sees who paid what. It sorts every transaction and catches my slips. It also catches the email where I meant one name and typed another. I finally trust my numbers.

I run an agentic operating system: an AI C-suite that holds daily stand-ups and works through a now, next, later task list with time for reflection. Several AI models share one knowledge base, so when something drops, it gets picked back up quickly.

Every agent has hard stops. Never delete anything. Never touch the financial system. Never send anything to clients or anyone outside the business. It all runs on a separate computer, cut off from the internet, that takes input only through a firewall.

My belief system: Practice active and reflective listening, offer solutions on the spot and never judge. Give without expecting money or work in return. If you give someone one thing in value, you get 10 times back in the long run. Just do not give because you hope for it.

To founders with disabilities: Find your superpower and get out in public. Do not worry about how you look, how you speak or what you do. Be open about your story, build in your niche and, once you succeed, give back. I have helped build tools that have reached a few thousand children with learning differences, most of them ages 6 to 13.

To technology leaders: Build communities and systems that teach people with disabilities to start and run businesses, and help them grow.

AI will not fix access to capital, health insurance costs or other people’s bias. But it can give back the energy, the hours and the confidence those barriers take.

Forbes Technology Council is an invitation-only community for world-class CIOs, CTOs and technology executives. Do I qualify?
