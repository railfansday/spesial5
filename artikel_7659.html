Journal of Free Speech Law: "An Unsettling Agreement: Meta, Jawboning, and the First Amendment," by Evelyn Douek

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

The first in a series of articles we'll be publishing over the next few months from our Free Speech and the Meta Settlement symposium.

The settlement agreement that Meta reached with state Attorneys General in August 2026 is constitutionally unsound. The changes that the AGs pressured Meta to make to its platforms as part of the settlement burden the rights of third parties—Meta's users—in various ways (some content-neutral, some content-based, and some even viewpoint-based), and yet these terms have so far escaped First Amendment scrutiny.

Because these changes are being done under government pressure, the agreement violates the rule against jawboning; Meta's users should therefore be able to bring a First Amendment challenge seeking to enjoin government actors from enforcing the agreement's speech-restrictive terms, or seeking to have a court declare that those terms are unenforceable. Settlement agreements should not be allowed to become a loophole in the First Amendment's protections.

According to the settlement agreement that Meta reached with 52 Attorneys General in August 2026, it is the parties' belief that "the public interest is best served when platforms and regulators work as partners." This may well be true in some, or perhaps even many, cases. But the settlement creates a system of pervasive and invasive speech regulation, and in that context such cooperation might be said to be "startling and dangerous."

First Amendment doctrine stands for the idea that we should generally be suspicious of government power over speech, and specifically ensures that government actors cannot pressure, or partner with, private actors to "do indirectly what [they are] barred from doing directly." That is: the government cannot evade constitutional constraints by enlisting private actors to impose its favored speech rules on its behalf. A settlement agreement is not a First Amendment-free zone, and the terms of Meta's deal with the Attorneys General at the very least need to be subjected to First Amendment scrutiny. This Article argues that because the Settlement Agreement attempts to evade such scrutiny, it is constitutionally unsound.

To make the stakes of this argument clear, imagine an alternate settlement agreement in which Meta, instead of promising changes like age assurance measures and time limits on kids' usage of its platforms, agreed to prohibit all users from seeing political content or (to make the point even more acute) all pro-Democrat content. The dangers of allowing such an agreement to escape constitutional scrutiny seem obvious. But because concern about kids' use of social media is bipartisan—and no doubt in part because kids themselves are often excluded from conversations about their rights—the constitutional infirmities of the Settlement Agreement have generally flown under the radar. But kids have speech rights too, as do the adult users of Meta's platforms who will also be affected by this Agreement, and politicians and platforms cannot constitutionally agree to forgo those rights on others' behalf.

I will argue that the third parties whose speech rights are burdened by the Settlement Agreement can bring a First Amendment challenge seeking to enjoin government actors from enforcing the Agreement's speech-restrictive terms, or seeking to have a court declare that those terms are unenforceable. This is true for settlement agreements that create such burdens on third-party rights in general, because any other conclusion would allow settlement agreements to become a loophole officials could use to regulate speech without facing constitutional scrutiny. And, as applied to this Settlement Agreement in particular, a large number of its provisions may well fail to survive constitutional scrutiny. Many of the content-neutral requirements in the Agreement mirror mandates that states have attempted to impose through legislation in recent years, and while these laws are still working their way through the courts and the case outcomes are mixed, the majority of decisions have enjoined similar requirements as violating the First Amendment. There are also content- and viewpoint-based requirements in the Agreement that are aimed at influencing what kids see online that would almost certainly fail First Amendment scrutiny if enacted as law.

Considering the Settlement Agreement against this backdrop throws into sharp relief exactly why the First Amendment has rules to ensure that the government cannot avoid constitutional scrutiny by regulating speech through means other than legislation. This fact may be deeply frustrating to some. We are now around a decade into the "techlash" against social media platforms. Despite all these years of concern and debate, the law and politics of platform regulation have made legislative solutions elusive. Attempts to reimagine First Amendment doctrine to give states more leeway to regulate the internet have failed.

There might be a temptation, therefore, to think of the Agreement as an overdue reckoning that cleverly overcomes these obstacles and finally brings Big Tech's worst offender to heel. Indeed, it may only be the start of the reckoning: A month after Meta agreed to settle, TikTok entered into a settlement with Alabama that is clearly modeled on the Meta Settlement Agreement. States are bringing lawsuits against artificial intelligence companies using the same theories as in the Meta case that led to the Settlement. States may think they have found a new way of achieving their regulatory objectives that does not require passing any laws at all.

But the First Amendment does not permit the government to evade review by simply changing the instrument of regulation. Otherwise, its protections would be paper-thin. And as frustrating as the government may find the commands of the First Amendment, they are a feature and not a bug of the American constitutional system. It is imperative that courts make clear that settlement agreements are not one neat trick to evade these constraints, lest states conclude they can abandon the hard and important work of building political will and legislative capacity for more comprehensive legal solutions, and instead regulate through one constitutionally infirm settlement at a time.

This Article proceeds as follows. Part I shows how the Settlement Agreement creates burdens on the speech rights of third parties: namely, Meta's users. Part II, the crux of the Article, argues that given these burdens, the Settlement Agreement violates the broad rule against what is known as jawboning—governmental pressure on private actors to restrict the speech of others. Part III takes a step back from the doctrinal landscape and considers what the Settlement Agreement suggests about the reduced ambitions and disappointments of debates about platform regulation.

How to effectively regulate new and fast-changing technologies continues to be one of the most pressing challenges of our time. Our regulatory solutions must respect the rights of society at large and not merely reflect the interests of the tech companies and the politicians that regulate them. The First Amendment is one essential safeguard in ensuring this is the case.
