Arlo’s Third Quarter: A Good Story, Priced on 1 Launch

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

Arlo's near-two-thirds recurring mix and 49% non-GAAP gross margin show why the subscription engine matters more than hardware sales.

On Nov. 5, Arlo Technologies (ARLO -2.50%) told investors that its security cameras are becoming a subscription business. That is mostly true. The harder question is whether the next leg of growth is as certain as management made it sound.

Arlo sells cameras at a loss on purpose, then collects monthly fees at high margins. Subscription and services revenue hit a record $96.0 million, up 20%, and made up 64.4% of revenue, with non-GAAP services gross margin of 83.9%. GAAP, or Generally Accepted Accounting Principles, is the official accounting standard. Non-GAAP is management's adjusted version. Paid accounts reached 6.6 million, up 23%.

That is a real, durable machine. The call's three claims, though, each come with a footnote.

ARR, or Annual Recurring Revenue, is subscription revenue annualized. It is $369.9 million, growing 14.5%. The 2027 target requires doubling that growth rate.

The bridge is Comcast, ADT, and a new elder-care business. Comcast is the big one. Management has framed its 31 million broadband households as a potential opportunity comparable to Arlo's European alarm partner, Verisure. But the launch has already slipped toward early 2027, subject to field testing.

The filings add a wrinkle. Paid accounts are growing faster than ARR, so revenue per account has fallen roughly 7% in a year, by this analysis. More customers are paying less each. Quarterly ARR additions also shrank, from about $8 million in Q2 to about $5 million in Q3.

The share is accurate, but part of the reason is that hardware shrank. Product revenue fell from $59.6 million to $53.1 million while total revenue grew about 7%. Services gross margin also slipped, from 85.1% to 83.9% on the non-GAAP basis. The call offered no explanation.

Profit. GAAP operating income was $1.0 million on $149 million of revenue. Gross profit rose $12.9 million year over year, but operating expenses rose $12.7 million. The gain was absorbed.

The main culprit is stock-based compensation, or stock paid to employees instead of cash. It was $21.7 million in the quarter versus $13.1 million a year ago. Over nine months, the company repurchased $44.4 million of stock and still saw shares outstanding rise from 105.0 million to 107.5 million. A new $100 million buyback was authorized anyway. Free cash flow of $50.6 million over nine months falls below zero once $63.2 million of stock comp is subtracted.

One more item to watch. Channel inventory at U.S. retailers rose to 14.6 weeks from 9.6, and at U.S. distributors to 14.9 weeks from 5.9. Q4 revenue guidance of $137 million to $147 million sits below Q3.

The investment thesis survives, but it narrows. Arlo has no debt and $144.0 million in cash and short-term investments, so the downside is cushioned. The upside, however, rests largely on one partner launch arriving on time and on revenue per account stabilizing.

At roughly $13 before the print, about 3.4 times ARR on enterprise value, the stock is fairly priced if growth stays in the mid-teens and attractive only if the 30% target proves real. Three signals matter most: the Comcast launch date, ARR per account, and whether buybacks finally shrink the share count.
