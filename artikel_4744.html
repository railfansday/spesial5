Why Gratitude Sometimes Backfires

You can appreciate the people in your life and still dread being asked to announce it over dinner.

The turkey is carved. The cranberry sauce has made its annual appearance in the shape of the can it came from. Then someone says: “Let’s go around the table and say what we’re grateful for.”

Forks pause midair. Eyes drift toward the mashed potatoes like they might offer an escape.

A cousin says something heartfelt and beautiful, and now the emotional bar has been raised to Olympic height.

Am I grateful enough? Should I say something profound?

Gratitude rituals are meant to create warmth. And often they do. But psychologically, gratitude is a little more complicated than the greeting cards suggest.

The benefits are real. A 2023 meta-analysis of 25 randomized controlled trials involving 6,745 participants found that expressing gratitude reliably improved happiness, life satisfaction, and psychological well-being compared with neutral activities.

But that doesn’t tell us everything about how gratitude feels in the moment.

In three experiments, psychologists examined people’s feelings after exercises such as recalling another person’s kindness or writing a gratitude letter they never sent. Alongside feeling moved and uplifted, the gratitude participants tended to report more indebtedness (and sometimes more feelings of guilt) than participants who did a neutral task.

It's like unwrapping a gift and realizing you didn’t bring one.

Gratitude can feel uplifting and slightly uncomfortable at the same time. It can carry a kind of social math: They gave + I received = Now I owe them

That discomfort doesn’t necessarily mean something has gone wrong. Wanting to give something back can be part of caring for a relationship.

But if gratitude can make us feel both good and uncomfortable, what happens when the discomfort arrives from somewhere else entirely?

In a randomized study of 156 undergraduates, participants completed about 12 minutes of gratitude practice, mindful breath awareness, loving-kindness, or a neutral visualization exercise. Those who practiced gratitude did report more positive emotion.

But then researchers introduced a stressor: Participants had to submerge their hand in a bucket of ice-cold water while a researcher in a lab coat stood nearby, observing with the emotional warmth of a marble statue — which is exactly as pleasant as it sounds.

Under that stress, the gratitude group actually became the most emotionally reactive, reporting more negative emotion than any of the other groups. And of the four groups, they were also the least likely to donate time to help others afterward.

Does this mean we should cancel giving thanks? Of course not.

Gratitude strengthens relationships and reinforces others’ kindness. But there's a difference between privately reflecting on gratitude and sharing it.

Privately reflecting on someone's kindness can leave us with an emotional mix: appreciation, plus obligation. Expressing gratitude may not make those complicated feelings disappear. But it can offer something more: connection.

A study of 916 students compared four approaches: writing gratitude letters they never sent, sending personal thank-you texts, posting gratitude publicly on social media, or just tracking daily activities. Participants repeated their assigned activity four times over about a week. Those who sent personal thank-you texts showed the strongest gains in feeling connected to and supported by others.

The takeaway isn't that texting is magical. It's that gratitude may do more to strengthen feelings of being connected and supported when we share it directly with the person we're grateful for rather than keeping it to ourselves.

And it may mean more to that person than we realize. In experiments by Amit Kumar and Nicholas Epley, people expressing gratitude underestimated how positive recipients would feel and overestimated how awkward they would feel.

The thank-you you’re second-guessing may land more warmly than you expect.

In my book, Micropractice: A Science-Backed Approach for Calm, Clarity, and Joy in 30 Seconds or Less, I explore in greater depth how to practice gratitude without getting caught in feelings of indebtedness, and how to weave more appreciation, connection, and joy into the moments you already have.

This Thanksgiving, when you feel gratitude for someone, try expressing it to them. Here’s a simple trick: Put a “you” in the sentence.

Instead of, “I’m grateful for my family," try, “Mom, you’ve always made people feel at home, even when the turkey’s running late.”

Instead of, “I’m grateful for my friends," try, "Noor, you’re the person I call when life gets messy, and somehow you always have a way of making it lighter.”

One describes what matters to you. The other lets someone know they matter.

Gratitude isn’t just noticing goodness. It’s letting someone know they helped create it.

This Thanksgiving, don’t just count your blessings. Let your blessings know they’re counted.
