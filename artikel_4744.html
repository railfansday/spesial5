After many surprising elections these past few months, many are bewildered that people's values could seemingly shift so easily. However, our moral values—what we believe is right and wrong—aren’t random and rarely change. They’re shaped by experience, genetics, and upbringing.

According to moral foundations theory, most people's values fall into six key categories:

Most people care about all of these. We just rank them differently. Researchers found that libertarians rank liberty highest, liberals rank care and fairness highest, and conservatives rank all six about equally.

When we assume others don’t value what we do, we miss the point. Often, they simply prioritize different moral foundations. Instead we could be asking: What do they value most?

You are unlikely to shift people's political positions, but you can still find ways to see eye-to-eye.

Look for one value you both rank high. A shared value works like a doorway. Arguing builds a wall.

Begin by demonstrating a true curiosity, a chance to learn. Swap questions like “Why do you think that?”—which sounds like a challenge—for “Oh, interesting. So would you say...?” This helps establish you as someone who is interested and trying to understand rather than someone who is trying to win an argument.

Once you have an understanding of their point of view, name a value you share. Say you rank care first and your uncle ranks liberty first. You both want people to thrive. He believes they thrive when they are free to choose. You believe they thrive when someone looks out for them. Same goal, different road. Start there.

This is also an anchor point. If the conversation starts feeling like a debate, you can always fall back to this: "Hey, we are all here to make sure people are able to do what they want in life."

After you build a connection, keep it alive. Become a person they feel safe talking to, the exception in their life. Maybe they say, “Oh, not all liberals are like that.” Maybe you hear, “Not all conservatives are like that.” Either way, you have opened a door rather than built a wall.

If each of us becomes another person’s exception, we can keep our conversations constructive.

Pick one person this week. Ask what matters most to them.
