Can AI Companions Really Solve Loneliness?

Engagement with artificial intelligence seems to be everywhere today, from education to medicine to business to making day-to-day life easier. Undoubtedly, some uses will prove beneficial, and others harmful. Evaluating the effect of different forms of AI engagement on human flourishing will thus be critical, and we believe our flourishing measure provides a helpful framework by which to do so. The effects of AI on flourishing may also differ across domains of flourishing.

There may be incredible potential for AI to advance human happiness, health, and financial security. It is not guaranteed to do so, and indeed, as per Pope Leo XIV’s recent encyclical Magnifica Humanitas (Magnificent Humanity), much of what AI does or does not achieve will depend upon whether the human person and human dignity are made central in the use and diffusion of AI. However, if employed well and equitably, the potential for AI to advance these aspects of human well-being may be substantial.

The effects, however, of AI technologies on more humanistic aspects of well-being, such as meaning, character, or relationships, seem more ambiguous. Rich, developed countries already seem to be struggling with these more humanistic aspects of well-being, possibly in part due to technology. Will AI help or hinder our sense of meaning, our character, our relationships? As always, so much depends on how we use it.

AI technologies with various relational elements have effectively been used for purposes ranging from helping autistic children develop social skills to building the capacity of university students for civil discourse. While such uses arguably always ought to point back to real relationships, these systems may indeed have the potential to truly enhance human relationships.

However, there is another class of chatbots that aims not at enhancing human relationships, but at replacing them. These are chatbots whose principal aim and purpose is friendship and romance. In a recent article, we have argued that such friendship and romance chatbots have no place in a flourishing life. This may seem like an extreme position. After all, what harm is there in having loneliness somewhat relieved if one’s friends or family are proving disappointing?

The problem is that the temporary alleviation of loneliness is not a solution to loneliness. Loneliness, as painful as it can be, is experienced precisely to try to direct our attention, and our longings, towards deeper human connection. That can take a lot of work and time, and may not be fully within our grasp. But that thirst for human connection has a fundamental role in our life, in guiding us towards others.

When AI chatbots have, as their goal, not helping us with human relationships, but replacing them, they will do damage—damage to ourselves, damage to those we might have interacted with, and damage to the social fabric of society. If they satisfy our loneliness, they do so without another person. A chatbot that always seems to understand, that never has needs of its own, that treats neglect with a delight to see us, ultimately makes us weaker in our capacity for real human relationships. We come to have unrealistic expectations of what to hope for in real human relationships. We lose the capacity to grow through the very difficulties and frictions that real human relationships entail. We come to focus on ourselves rather than on providing for the needs of others. Indeed, with AI friendship and romance, there is no one on the other side to whose life we have contributed.

While AI may have some capacity to enhance our relationships (pointing us towards helpful books or resources for particular problems we are wrestling with) and while certain AI chatbot applications may benefit from relational elements (like the training programs above), this is very different from AI chatbots aimed at replacing real relationships. The effects of these are universally and comprehensively detrimental. They harm us, and they harm those around us. Even if we do not feel the negative effects now, or if they seem small, the increasing incapacity for social relationships will over time gradually erode human connection, human community, and the fabric of society. Human relationships are critical for our health and well-being.

In a remarkable turn of events, China, which has been very pro-AI, in July put into place comprehensive legislation restricting the development and marketing of AI tools “that provide emotional care, companionship, support, and other emotional services,” and including a strict ban on tools “excessively pandering to users, inducing emotional reliance or addiction, [or] harming users' relationships with real people.” What does China know that we don’t? They seem to have almost presciently anticipated the likely effects of AI romance and relationships on human society, human relationships, human reproduction, and human community.

There have also recently been some helpful legislative efforts here in the United States, especially for minors. However, we are naïve to think children are the only ones at risk.

While we do think a ban on AI “relationship chatbots” for friendship and romance may be reasonable here (after all, we ban heroin in the United States), some might view such measures as too extreme. Even if so, we nevertheless believe every effort should be made in practice to purge AI friendship and romance from our lives as individuals and as communities. A technology designed to replace human connection is not a neutral technology.

Love—including love of neighbor and even love of enemy—is crucial for the flourishing of our society. In the words of Pope Leo (and others before him), if we are to seek a flourishing society (and to do so in the age of AI), we need to build “a civilization of love.” We need to love and care for one another. We need to be with one another. We need to have the capacity to work through the difficulties and frictions that real relationships entail, and to grow through those struggles. Therein lies our flourishing, for ourselves, for others, and for society. Real human love requires real human people.
