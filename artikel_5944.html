Obama judge's Omar warrant unsealing directly contradicts his own decision in Trump case, DOJ says

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Former Acting ICE Director Jonathan Fahey joins 'Fox & Friends' to discuss the newly unsealed search warrant records revealing a decades-long federal investigation into Rep. Ilhan Omar, D-Minn.

Government lawyers argue unsealing documents related to the warrant used to search the Washington, D.C., home of Rep. Ilhan Omar, D-Minn., is at odds with a ruling made by the same Obama-appointed federal judge in a high-profile case against President Donald Trump.

Lawyers for the Justice Department pleaded in the filings with Judge James Boasberg, who has frequently found himself at odds with the Trump administration, not to unseal search warrant materials in the Omar case due to the ongoing nature of the investigation,.

The DOJ also argued it has not yet finished executing that search warrant.

Still, Boasberg ordered last week the redacted documents to be released. The DOJ submitted their redacted and the materials were made public Monday night. The only document Boasberg ordered to remain sealed was the government's affidavit presented to a magistrate court judge in order to secure the search warrant.

"Just a few years ago, Chief Judge Boasberg ruled that search warrant proceedings and related documents should not be made open to the public while an investigation is ongoing," the DOJ said in an emergency ex parte brief in opposition to the unsealing.

"The Chief Judge was merely echoing many other courts—like the D.C. Circuit, the Ninth Circuit, the Fourth Circuit, and others when he said that," the brief continued.

The case to which the DOJ referred — where Boasberg ruled that search warrant documents should remain under seal during active investigations — was none other than Special Counsel Jack Smith's probe into Trump for alleged election interference after his 2020 presidential loss, and for the alleged retention of classified documents.

Trump was indicted in both of those investigations. All the charges were eventually dropped.

In that case, like the Omar case, media organizations sought to unseal a warrant related to the search. Specifically, the press wanted to view a warrant obtained by Smith related to Twitter's records associated with Trump's account, @realDonaldTrump.

Boasberg's tune was much different in that case, when, like in the Omar case, the government asked him to keep the warrant under seal due to the ongoing nature of the case.

Notably, Trump had already been indicted when Boasberg ruled that the records needed to remain sealed while Omar has not been indicted.

"Ultimately, the undeniable need to protect an ongoing criminal investigation tips the balance toward the Government’s request to continue sealing," Boasberg wrote in his Nov. 29, 2023 ruling.

The White House did not immediately respond to Fox News Digital's request for comment on the apparent u-turn from the judge.

In the Omar case, government lawyers said they weren't opposed to releasing the documents at an appropriate future date, but did not want them unsealed while the case is ongoing. Boasberg didn't find that argument compelling.

"There may yet come a time when press access becomes appropriate, but at present the Application is a premature bid for sensitive information pertaining to an active investigation," he wrote in 2023.

Throughout his 2023 ruling in favor of the government's bid to keep the warrant in the Trump case sealed, Boasberg continually noted the government's ability to keep warrants under seal.

He said in a second instance: "When it comes to an active investigation, the Government 'has always been able to restrict access to warrant materials by requesting a sealing order, which courts have granted freely upon a showing that a given criminal investigation requires secrecy,'" Boasberg's ruling said.

In response to the media's claim that the First Amendment should be a deciding factor in unsealing the Trump warrant, Boasberg disagreed.

"The historical practice is somewhat muddled, but it suggests that the First Amendment does not open search warrants and related documents to public examination when an investigation is ongoing. Search-warrant applications traditionally have been filed ex parte and thus out of the public’s view."

The DOJ confirmed in a brief statement to Fox News Digital on Tuesday that the characterization that Boasberg contradicted himself in unsealing the Omar documents "is accurate and a point we have made in our court filings."

One issue distinguishing Boasberg's ruling in the 2023 Trump case from the Omar case is the Constitution's Speech or Debate Clause, which shields lawmakers from certain government intrusions into their legislative work.

In the Omar case, the Justice Department argued that searching her home did not involve those protections. Magistrate Judge Matthew Sharbaugh disagreed and required safeguards for potentially privileged materials.

Boasberg later questioned why the legal arguments over those constitutional protections should remain sealed when sensitive investigative details could be redacted. He nevertheless agreed to keep the government's warrant affidavit sealed to protect the ongoing investigation.

Boasberg has also faced scrutiny over investigative secrecy during the Biden-era Arctic Frost probe, when he signed nondisclosure orders tied to subpoenas for Republican lawmakers' phone records. Unlike the Omar and Trump rulings, those orders restricted telecommunications companies from revealing the subpoenas rather than deciding whether court documents should be made public. Court officials have said Boasberg may not have known lawmakers' records were involved.

The District Court for the District of Columbia declined to comment. The U.S. Attorney’s Office for the District of Columbia did not return a request for comment.

Omar's home was raided in the early morning hours of Oct. 12, 2026. Fox News Digital confirmed that the investigators are probing whether Omar or her father fraudulently filled out her citizenship paperwork, though the full scope of the investigation remains under wraps.

The unsealing of last night's documents revealed that investigators are seeking evidence dating back as far as 1980, and that investigators believe Omar committed "crimes over decades— beginning long before she was ever in Congress."
