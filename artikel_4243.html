Exterminauts Preview: A Second Shift On Planet Tian Proves The Loop Works

Tanggal: 2026-11-11

My second shift on planet Tian came with a new account and no shortcuts. Exterminauts’ loop still delivered, solo or with friends at your back.

The last time I played Exterminauts, I was setting my editor-in-chief and one of the game’s developers on fire at gamescom. That was a curated session alongside people who knew exactly what they were doing, and it was glorious chaos from start to finish. This time, I started the way most players will when the game launches: on a brand-new account, with nothing unlocked and nobody from the studio at my shoulder.

Weaponized power tools, alien pest control, and the most friendly fire I've committed in a single session. Exterminauts is already feeling special.

Full disclosure: the Exterminauts team very kindly gave me beta access and a key, and answered some of our questions, too. I’ve played around six hours, which isn’t nearly as much as I would have liked, so I haven’t seen the deep end of the build system or the toughest contracts. What I did get was a proper look at the first few days on the job, and those days were very good.

The biggest difference for me from gamescom is the tutorial, and it’s a genuinely good introduction that gives new players a proper footing before planet Tian starts throwing things at them. From there, the game is fully committed to the bit. You start and end each mission at the same point, so every run feels like a shift: clock in, head out, deal with whatever the planet has in store, and come back to get paid. That paycheque goes back into your work gear and sponsorships.

Promotions follow the same logic. Each rank asks something different of you, from simply completing two jobs early on to finishing three of the extra tasks that pop up mid-job, or unlocking a new specification. Every promotion brings more dangerous work with it, and you can feel yourself genuinely getting better at your job. You’re not a chosen one, you’re a field worker who earned the next rung on the ladder.

If anything, the game sometimes leans a little too hard into that fantasy. At times, the whole thing has a very “we’re the lads, and we’re getting the job done” energy, that sometimes has the opposite effect of taking me out of the setting because it feels forced. Still, it does feel like Exterminauts knows very specifically who it’s trying to win over.

The jobs themselves have some nice touches. Every so often, you’ll need to weld something back together, and the game rates your accuracy while gently guiding your aim. Other tasks, like hauling a fuel tank while a teammate waits at a terminal to relaunch a device, are designed to split the work. In practice, nobody stands still at a computer while their friend gets swarmed, so it mostly turns into everyone protecting everyone.

The extra jobs that pop up mid-run are the best of the bunch, because they stop you sprinting from objective to objective and pull you into corners of the map you’d otherwise never see. Some are modest, like having to kill some monsters after activating a mysterious looking device, to get rewarded with additional max health for the run. Others are much bigger, like stumbling across an abandoned mech that only one person can pilot until its ammo runs dry. It’s a lovely way of rewarding curiosity.

From there, the game is fully committed to the bit. You start and end each mission at the same point, so every run feels like a shift: clock in, head out, deal with whatever the planet has in store, and come back to get paid.

All that wandering means meeting a lot of wildlife, and the enemy variety stood out to me immediately. My favourite is the Hot Potato, a little fire monster that attacks you by rolling into you, and I can’t explain why, but the name just does it for me. It’s in wonderfully strange company, from pests that are mostly mouth and launch themselves at you, to long-necked ones firing lasers from a single giant eye, to floating balloon-like creatures that release swarms of flyers to shock you. And the planet they all call home is still a big wow, with a colour palette so dynamic that even rolling fire potatoes look lovely.

My primary weapon was, well, just a gun. What I’ll remember is the reloading: the magazines are small, which makes sense when you’ve only just started the job, but when a tide of pests rolls in and you just want to clear it and move on, constant reloads get frustrating fast. Your weapons will get stronger, but so will the enemies, and I do wonder whether they’ll still feel a little spongy for the size of your magazine.

The special weapons are a different story. I mostly used the plasma sludge launcher, which coats enemies in corrosive gunk that keeps eating away at them, and it’s brilliant against groups and huge enemies alike – though for the really big ones, grenades usually did the job faster. The Overdrive boost makes your special fire quicker and hit harder at the cost of some battery power, and watching an entire pack melt, ringleader included, is one of the best feelings in the game. I didn’t get to try it, but the CrossCut fires comically oversized saw blades that latch onto enemies and keep spinning, and charging a shot keeps them going for longer.

Those weapons come from sponsorships, which work a little like classes, each with its own passive. I went with Bullet Crown, which boosts critical hit damage and gives you a rapid-fire SMG, because mostly playing solo, Vardhan’s shared healing didn’t make much sense. I’ll admit I was a little sad about it, though. The Vardhan gear is pink, and I would have looked very cute.

Solo play didn’t feel worse at all, which I didn’t expect. The difficulty seems to scale down appropriately when you’re alone, and it encourages a slower, more methodical style, so I spent a lot of time scoping out high ground and picking enemies off before they got close. Even on missions where perching up high wasn’t an option, it was still a blast. Difficulty runs across eight levels, adding tougher pests, extra repairs and hazards like extreme weather as you climb, but as a new hire, I didn’t get anywhere near the top.

And the planet they all call home is still a big wow, with a colour palette so dynamic that even rolling fire potatoes look lovely.

My other gripe is the extraction phase, which takes so long. Before you can leave, you have to hold off enemies while earning rewards based on your performance against your own party. Solo, when you just want a few quick runs, it drags, even with support items like sentry turrets and a device that gives you a short burst of unlimited ammo. In a group, though, being scored against your teammates is practically an invitation to friendly fire, and having already incinerated a developer, I have no qualms about doing it again.

That’s really where Exterminauts shines. I share the developers’ view that it’s best with a small group of friends, all yelling at each other while the battlefield falls apart around you. The chaos I loved at gamescom is still there; it just needs a crew to get going.

Six hours in, there’s still a real sense of discovery. The builds already look varied, the pests keep surprising me, and the loop keeps pulling me back for one more shift. There’s plenty to work towards, too, from gear upgrades and trinkets with extra passives to a season pass you can either grind through or pay to unlock. If you enjoy the tutorial and the core loop, I think the rest – the feel, the controls, that gorgeous planet – will win you over.

The bigger challenge is everything around it. There are a lot of games like this out there, and Exterminauts will have to work hard to pull people away from their current favourite. At gamescom, the team outlined six-week seasons with no progression resets, and that cadence will need to give players a reason to stay.

For now, the job is a good one. Whether it turns into a career is up to the seasons that follow.
