Warren Buffett's Successor, Greg Abel, Just Dropped a $426 Million Bombshell on Wall Street

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

Berkshire Hathaway’s bosses have been net sellers of stocks in 15 of the last 16 quarters.

This has been a year of immense change for Berkshire Hathaway (BRKA +0.47%)(BRKB +0.53%), the trillion-dollar conglomerate that Warren Buffett built. Following the Oracle of Omaha's retirement as CEO on Dec. 31, oversight of the company's day-to-day operations and its closely monitored $368 billion portfolio fell to his successor, Greg Abel.

During the first quarter, Abel wasted little time making his mark. He sent 16 stocks in Berkshire's portfolio to the chopping block, reduced six others, and absolutely piled into Google parent Alphabet (GOOGL -0.98%)(GOOG -0.96%). It set the stage for Berkshire to be more tech-focused than it's ever been.

Berkshire's new boss, Greg Abel, was a net seller of stocks in the third quarter. Image source: Getty Images.

But as several things change, one trait persists: the net selling of securities.

During the third quarter, Abel oversaw $2.366 billion in purchases of equity securities and $2.792 billion in sales, resulting in net sales of $426 million. It's the 15th time over the last 16 quarters (four years) that Berkshire's bosses have sold more stocks than they've purchased -- and there's a clear reason why.

Even though Warren Buffett is no longer overseeing Berkshire Hathaway's portfolio, Abel's investment philosophy is very similar. They both favor companies with well-defined competitive advantages, stable capital-return programs, and strong management teams.

Most importantly, "value" is the one trait neither Buffett nor Abel will ever waver on. Even if all the other factors are met, Buffett and Abel have consistently shown a willingness to sit on their proverbial hands if the valuation doesn't make sense.

BRK.A Price to Book Value data by YCharts

We've witnessed this focus on value play out in Berkshire's share repurchase program. There was a 21-month stretch (June 2024 – February 2026) in which Berkshire's stock traded at a 55% to 80% premium to its book value. During this period, neither Buffett nor Abel spent a dime buying back their company's shares.

But in instances when Berkshire's stock traded below a 50% premium to book value, Buffett and Abel have collectively spent around $92 billion repurchasing their company's stock since July 2018.

Value is everything to Berkshire's current and former bosses, and a historically pricey stock market offers little in the way of bargains.

In a 2001 interview with Fortune magazine, Buffett referred to the market-cap-to-GDP ratio as "probably the best single measure of where valuations stand at any given moment." This valuation tool has since become known as the Buffett indicator.

Over the last 56 years, the Buffett indicator has averaged 89% -- i.e., the cumulative value of all public stocks has equaled 89% of U.S. gross domestic product (GDP). In August, the Buffett indicator reached a record high of 236.42%.

Buffett's and Abel's persistent net selling suggests there isn't much to entice fundamentally focused value investors.

While investors would assuredly prefer to see Berkshire's $377.4 billion treasure chest put to work, the company's bosses have phenomenal track records of pouncing during inevitable periods of panic on Wall Street. For example, Warren Buffett's timely $5 billion purchase of Bank of America's (BAC -0.46%) preferred stock in August 2011 eventually led to a windfall profit for his company.

Although Greg Abel's $426 million bombshell throws cold water on the stock market for the foreseeable future, Berkshire's treasure trove of cash gives its new boss plenty of dry powder to deploy when the situation warrants it.
