Australia passes bill that would let people sue AI chatbot providers

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

The right to sue would start 12 months after Royal Assent and apply when a covered provider’s safety breach causes loss or damage.

Australia’s Parliament passed the Digital Duty of Care bill on Nov. 25, giving people a future route to sue covered AI chatbot providers over loss or damage caused by a breach of the new online safety duty. Both the duty and the right to sue are due to take effect 12 months after Royal Assent.

The Nov. 25 vote completed the bill’s passage through Parliament. Schedule 2 of Australia’s Digital Duty of Care bill sets out the safety duty and the private right of action. The Senate committee’s report sets out the transition period for that schedule. The communications department confirmed on Nov. 27 that the duty would start 12 months after Royal Assent.

A different part of the bill follows an earlier timetable. Schedule 1, including expanded powers for the eSafety Commissioner over apps and websites used to generate fake nude material, is due to take effect the day after Royal Assent.

Expanded eSafety powers start sooner than the digital duty and the court remedy.

The court remedy follows the 12-month timetable.

Section 28D covers providers of services that use an internet carriage service to generate material with AI. That brings covered AI chatbots within the scope of the private court remedy. The provision also names social media services, relevant electronic services and designated internet services.

A claimant would need to show that the provider failed to comply with its safety duty and that the failure caused their loss or damage. The bill treats that connection as established if the breach substantially contributed to the loss or damage.

The duty requires providers to take appropriate safety measures, including effective steps to address the risks they identify in their assessments. The explanatory memorandum, quoted by the Senate committee, says a harmful interaction does not necessarily establish a breach. The provider’s response to reasonably foreseeable risks matters, including whether it put effective safeguards in place and kept them working.

If a claim succeeds, the court could award damages, including for emotional distress, issue an injunction or order the provider to apologize. The bill gives courts discretion to choose a remedy suited to the circumstances.

The time limit for bringing a claim is tied to the claimant’s age and the date they suffered the loss or damage. Under section 28E, proceedings must begin before the later of their 21st birthday or six years after the day they suffered the loss or damage.

For someone who is already 21 when they suffer the loss, the six-year period gives the later date. For someone who suffers loss or damage as a child, the 21st birthday can be the later date, depending on their age when they suffered the loss.
