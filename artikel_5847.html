Ilhan Omar records show judge, DOJ officials debating before raid of home

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Newly unsealed search warrant documents reveal a decades-long federal investigation into Rep. Ilhan Omar, D-Minn., going back to 1980. Former federal prosecutor Jonathan Fahey joins 'Fox & Friends' to analyze the records and the Department of Justice's search for evidence of redacted crimes. Fahey discusses the legal implications of the unsealed warrant for her Washington, D.C. home.

Court documents released Monday night showed U.S. Department of Justice prosecutors battling with U.S. Magistrate Judge Matthew J. Sharbaugh to obtain a search warrant for the Washington, D.C., home of Rep. Ilhan Omar, D-Minn.

The DOJ applied to the magistrate for a search warrant on Oct. 2, eventually receiving the warrant which led to a Homeland Security Investigations search of the home on Oct. 12.

However, the newly released petition for the warrant reveals the court raised concerns that the DOJ could be violating the Constitution’s Speech and Debate Clause.

"The search warrant here is directed to a sitting member of the United States House of Representatives. This means that, even after ensuring that there exists probable cause to issue the warrant, see Fed. R. Crim. P. 41(d)(1), the Court must separately balance any necessary constitutional considerations under governing precedent," the court wrote in a memorandum.

The DOJ, for its part, argued that the requested warrant did not relate to any speech Omar had made as a Congressperson or to her legislative duties at all.

The memorandum, according to the document, affirms a previous oral ruling from the court which questioned the government’s assertions that the search would be independent of Omar’s Congressional business.

"The issue that remains and the issue this Court independently raised with the government after its initial review of the warrant application is whether the search of residence implicates the Speech or Debate Clause. The government says no. This Court sees it differently. It concludes that the proposed search of the residence of a sitting Member of Congress, at least on the facts here, requires the balancing of interests that the D.C. Circuit explicated in Rayburn," the memorandum read.

The court specifically cited the warrant’s request to search Omar’s cell phone, arguing that most adults now bring some portion of work home with them, particularly on their electronic devices.

Federal agents with Homeland Security Investigations raided the Washington, D.C., home of Rep. Ilhan Omar, D-Minn., early Monday morning, a source familiar with the operation told Fox News Digital. (Andrew Murray/Fox News Digital)

"The Court has no trouble concluding that the presence of at least some legislative materials along those lines is 'inevitable' in the residence of a sitting member of Congress, whether in a formal home office setting, a work bag inside the home that travels back and forth to Capitol Hill, or otherwise," the memorandum read.

Despite the pushback, Sharbaugh ultimately granted the DOJ the search warrant but with the caveat that Omar could invoke Congressional privilege over any seized items that could relate to legislative activity and require prosecutors to obtain a separate warrant to search the content of electronic devices.

The documents also reveal that U.S. Attorney for the District of Columbia Jeanine Pirro is searching for documents related to any crimes going back to the 1980s.

Though parts of the document released Monday were heavily redacted, it revealed that prosecutors believed Omar committed "[redacted] crimes over decades."

According to Fox News legal analyst Jonathan Turley, the scope and time frame "confirms the broad sweep of the investigation back to her birth."

The documents were released by Judge James Boasberg, the chief judge for the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia, against DOJ wishes.

Boasberg denied a federal prosecutor's request to keep the records sealed.

The DOJ had previously argued in the documents that unsealing the records could prompt potential suspects into fleeing or witness tampering. Sharbaugh apparently agreed, signing off on the documents' sealing on Oct. 9.

However, Boasberg signed off on the unsealing and ordered the release of the heavily redacted 170-page documents. He also indicated that a further release could be coming in the future with significant reductions in redactions.

Boasberg excluded the 67-page search warrant affidavit, the search warrant itself, and a list of seized items from the release, citing an ongoing investigation.

Fox News Digital contacted the DOJ and Rep. Omar's office for further comment.
