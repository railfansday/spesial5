You might know New Balance as the preeminent source of dad shoes—those chunky, overlay-clad sneakers ubiquitous among lawn-mowing, beer-drinking guys of a certain age. Yes, New Balance still makes these kicks (and they’re actually back in style), but that’s just a small sliver of this company’s story.

New Balance is a highly respected brand with a long history: The company was founded in 1906 in Boston and originally sold arch supports. Among runners, New Balance has a reputation for creating reliable training shoes with a strong emphasis on comfort. The Ellipse, which earned a 2026 Shoe Award, is a great example: It’s a cushioned, stable, bouncy trainer that works well for a wide swath of runners.

In recent years, the brand has broadened its lineup beyond trusty workhorse shoes with models like the SuperComp Elite, a carbon-plated speed shoe designed for racing. Today, New Balance is on the cutting edge—no lawn mower required.

This guide highlights the top pairs in the New Balance stable. The brand makes something for everyone, and there are well-regarded models here to suit all kinds of runners, including supportive trainers, max-cushion sneakers, racing shoes, and even trail shoes. Read on to learn more about the key tech NB uses in its shoes and dive into our favorite pairs below.

To write this guide, I talked with a New Balance brand rep and reviewed the company’s lineup to learn more about its latest models and shoe tech. I also combed through Runner’s World reviews of New Balance shoes and consulted with RW Running Reviews Editor Amanda Furrer, who provided testing data on New Balance models included in the Runner’s World wear-test program.

I chose the shoes you see below, making sure to include models that showcase the best New Balance tech and design approaches as well as shoes for different running disciplines.

The 880 is one of New Balance’s core training shoes, and the v15 is a real standout: RW testers called it “one shoe that can do it all,” and it earned a 2025 Shoe Award thanks to its versatility and stable ride.

The 880 is a neutral trainer that features an EVA-based Fresh Foam X midsole. New Balance bumped the stack height to more than 40 mm for v15, giving this shoe even more cushioning (at the cost of increased weight). But as Runner-in-Chief Jeff Dengate notes in his review, the shoe doesn’t feel pillowy soft. Instead, it’s surprisingly stable: The thick midsole takes the sting out of impacts and feels planted underfoot, and combined with the 880’s snug upper, the shoe serves up a comfortable, predictable ride that’s great for everyday training.

The 880 is classic New Balance: a trusty workhorse that’ll keep your feet happy through neighborhood jogs and long runs alike (one tester even liked it for speed workouts). If reliability and comfort are your top priorities for running shoes, you can’t go wrong with this model.

This below-$100 shoe doesn’t skimp on comfort despite the low price tag. The Fresh Foam 680v9 has soft, supportive cushioning that’s great for everyday training. It’s surprisingly durable for accruing miles, and you won’t feel like you’re slogging because it’s anything but beefy.

The shoe has an accommodating fit in the midfoot area, though some commented the toebox feels slightly narrow. You can go up a size—or up a width. The 680v9 comes in wide (men’s, women’s) and extra wide (men’s).

The Ellipse, included in our 2026 Shoe Awards, is the shoe version of a run friend. Meant to encapsulate the feeling of going on a group run, New Balance’s newest shoe has plush, supportive foam that makes your runs feel easier and bouncier.

The midsole, Fresh Foam X, gets tweaked with new geometry (look ma, no more hexagons!). The foam compresses and quickly springs back upon impact, making the ride feel trampoline-like. But the Ellipse isn’t for speedwork. Testers commented how it’s quite bulky to run in. However, they enjoyed how the shoe put pep in their step during easy runs and recovery runs.

“The shoe is SO springy,” said tester Laura Parks. “It’s like running with a strong wind at your back except the extra momentum is coming from the ground.”

We get it—you want to run in your carbon-plated, super foam shoes every day. Thing is, with the exception of a few test runs, you should save your super shoes for race day so they stay fresh. Here’s where the SC Rebel comes in. New Balance created a carbon-plated shoe specifically for training. It’s intended to make your runs feel easier as you reap the propulsion from the carbon plate and TPEE-based Infinion foam.

Furrer took the SC Rebel out on a long run over road and trail, and found the shoe’s traction reliable on both surfaces. “The shoe has a wider platform that helps with stability; it has more support than your standard super shoe,” she said. “I was impressed with the snap and slightly spongy but not aggressive rebound. I trusted the shoe over loose dirt and pavement pocked with potholes.”

Testers who initially said it was too bulky eventually deemed the shoe as their new daily trainer.

“The Infinion Foam tends to hit that balance I look for in a daily trainer—it has some pop in the cushioning without being too soft,” said tester and Director of Content Operations Brian Dalek. “And it lasts a long time. “I found these sneakers to be like ‘running on air.’ The springiness of the Infinion foam gave me a bouncy rebound each step,” she said. “I felt energized! A popular and award-winning trail shoe, the Hierro continues with the formula that has won it such a loyal following: a soft Fresh Foam X midsole paired with a rugged and grippy Vibram outsole, now upgraded for v9 with deeper lugs for better grip on challenging terrain. Although it lacks a rock plate, the plush midsole does a good job of soaking up shocks and bumps on the trail.

The Hierro v9 features a dual-density Fresh Foam X midsole: a soft top layer cushions impacts and creates a plush underfoot feel, and a firmer lower layer stabilizes the shoe and keeps sharp debris from poking up into your feet. The mesh upper is designed to breathe well, and overlays keep your feet protected from scrapes against rocks and brush. On the bottom, the Vibram outsole now features enlarged 4.5mm lugs for plenty of bite in the dirt.

RW testers gave the Hierro high marks overall, and they especially loved its cushy Fresh Foam X midsole: “For comfort over distance, it’s one of the best I’ve tried,” one tester said.

But a few testers did note a sloppy fit that caused their feet to slide around in the shoe on technical terrain and downhills. That means the Hierro isn’t the best choice for super gnarly trails, but for everyday trail running on light to moderate terrain, its cushioned ride is top-notch.

New Balance has been steadily tweaking the Rebel over the past couple iterations, and the latest edition offers significantly increased cushioning while still maintaining a feathery weight. This is a great shoe for speed training, although it serves up a comfy overall feel that works well at slower speeds, too.

Like the v4, the Rebel v5 features a blend of PEBA and EVA foams in its FuelCell midsole. The main difference? There’s more of it: The shoe’s stack height grew from 30mm in the v4 up to 35mm in the v5. The sole has a pronounced rocker shape, and despite the relatively tall wedge of cushioning, our testers said the shoe felt firm—but comfy.

“[The Rebel v5] is just a little firmer than other New Balance shoes I’ve run in, but it gets the job done,” said tester Sean O’Connor, who previously tested the cushier FuelCell SuperComp Trainer. Despite the shoe’s firmer platform, O’Connor described the ride as “super comfortable the entire run.”

The Rebel lacks the carbon-fiber plate found in its race-oriented sibling, the SuperComp Elite, so it can’t quite achieve super shoe levels of bounciness from its midsole. Even so, runners say it feels peppy, and its more streamlined sole design makes it very comfortable at a wide range of paces.

New Balance seems to revamp its super shoes every year, with no two models looking—or riding—alike. And we’re here for that ride. In lieu of FuelCell, the brand switched in a lighter and bouncier version of Infinion, the new foam introduced in the max-cushioned 1080v15. It’s a move we’re seeing lots of brands participate in, such as Saucony and Asics, swapping PEBA for A-TPU, or in this case, TPEE.

This makes the SuperComp Elite v6 even lighter than its predecessor (although, all things considered, the FuelCell SuperComp Elite v4 was a chonk). To shave off even more weight, the upper is replaced with translucent mesh, which gives the shoe a track flat vibe.

The shoe isn’t as aggressive as some of its competitors, and a couple testers found the cushioning firm. But the accommodating fit in the midfoot area provided comfort over long miles when testers’ feet began to swell. The plush cushioning in the heel was also a favorite feature, especially for heel strikers.

“Very comfy for long miles,” said shoe tester Josh Davis. “You can definitely wear these in a marathon and count on the comfort to last.”

Now on its 15th iteration, the 860 is a trusted workhorse trainer that offers a supportive feel and good cushioning for routine training miles. The latest version has the 14’s new last and rocker shape for smooth strides; it also includes the previous iteration’s Stability Plane, a thin, stiff EVA-based midsole plate that supports the foot.

The 860 is built with a relatively wide full-contact sole, which gives it some inherent stability. The midsole features two flavors of Fresh Foam X plus the embedded Stability Plane. There’s a denser, harder layer of foam at the bottom, and it works in concert with the rigid Stability Plane to resist overpronation and support the foot. The top layer of the midsole is a softer version of Fresh Foam X that absorbs impact forces for a plush feel.

With its somewhat heavy build and comfort-oriented midsole design, the 860 isn’t the smartest choice for tempo runs, but testers loved it for general training, and it’s a great pick for those who need some extra support under their feet.

Midsole foams are the main technology that differentiates New Balance shoes. Like other shoe brands, New Balance has entered the foam wars by engineering its own proprietary cushioning foams. There are two key ones to know: Fresh Foam and FuelCell.

The latest version of Fresh Foam is Fresh Foam X, and it’s used in the brand’s workhorse training shoes, including stalwart models like the 880, 1080, and 860. This is an EVA-based foam that’s formulated for softness, and it gives these shoes a well-padded feel underfoot. Fresh Foam X serves up a comfortable ride that’s ideal for casual running and day-to-day training.

FuelCell is used in New Balance’s speed-oriented models, like the Rebel and SuperComp Elite, and it’s optimized for energy return and responsiveness. New Balance tweaks the exact formula of this foam depending on the shoe and its intended use.

The training-oriented Rebel has a FuelCell midsole made from a blend of EVA and polyether block amide (PEBA)—a lightweight, soft, and very springy material that’s used in many brands’ speed shoes. This blend gives the Rebel a good balance of shock absorption and bounce.

The race-focused SuperComp Elite, however, has a FuelCell midsole made entirely from PEBA, which saves weight while still allowing for a very thick layer of cushioning. It’s paired with a carbon-fiber plate that stabilizes your foot as you run and helps you get maximum squish—and propulsive energy return—from the hefty slice of foam. The result is a bouncy, propulsive ride that helps you push the pace, and it’s a great choice for race day.

Introduced with the 1080v15, Infinion foam is lighter and bouncier than the brand’s Fresh Foam X. New Balance has also tweaked Infinion for its racing collection of shoes. The TPEE-based foam in the SuperComp Elite v6 and SC Rebel delivers higher rebound, working with the rocker profile to encourage fast turnover, and the Energy Arc carbon plate, which helps stabilize the foam and provides propulsion.

For decades, New Balance has used a number system to name its shoes—you’re probably familiar with classic models like the 990. The number system encompasses a broad range of shoes, including many casual pairs not designed for running. The numbered models intended for running (like the 1080) are training shoes that have been a part of the New Balance lineup for years.

By breaking up the model number, you can decipher the shoe’s intended use. Start at the end: The last two digits denote the model’s level of support. Shoes with names ending in 80 are neutral shoes, while shoes ending in 60 are stability shoes designed to counter overpronation. Shoes that end in 40, like the 1540, are old-school motion-control shoes with maximum support to bolster the foot.

Higher model numbers generally indicate the shoe has more midsole tech and more cushioning. The 1080, for example, is built with top-tier Fresh Foam X cushioning and has more overall padding than the 680, which uses an older version of Fresh Foam.

An important caveat: Many New Balance running shoes have unique names (like the Hierro and Rebel) and do not follow this number system at all.

What type of races should I choose the FuelCell SuperComp Pacer and SuperComp Elite for?

The Pacer is lighter and leaner than the Elite, making it a better choice for shorter, faster races like a 5K, 10K, or even a half marathon. The Elite has more midsole cushioning (its heel stack is 5mm taller than the Pacer), so it’s better suited for longer efforts, like a marathon, where the added cushioning can help reduce foot fatigue.

What are good budget shoes from New Balance if I don’t want to spend over $140?

Besides the 680v9 on this list, the Kaiha Road v2 is a solid pick for budget-conscious runners. You’ll get a well-padded ride that’ll feel comfortable for both running and walking. The Ellipse is also worth considering. Even though it’s priced at $150, it’s our best value shoe because of its incredible comfort and responsive bounce. Premium materials guarantee longer shelf life, so you truly get your money’s worth.
