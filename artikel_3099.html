Your Best Suppliers Are Losing Bookings They Deserve To Win

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Abhinav Sinha is Co-founder and CEO of ZentrumHub, a unified hotel API platform serving 90+ OTAs globally.

Earlier this year, my team audited the supplier routing of a midsize online travel agency. One wholesaler was confirming nearly every booking it received for hotels in Japan. It had deep direct contracts in the market, clean rates and almost no relocations. It sat fourth in the call sequence. Two of the suppliers ahead of it were failing roughly one booking in 10 on the same properties.

When we asked why the order looked the way it did, we got the most honest answer in hotel distribution: That’s the order the contracts were signed in.

For a decade, our industry has repeated the same line: Supplier loyalty is dead, wholesale inventory is a commodity and the only lever left is margin. I think the diagnosis is wrong. Loyalty isn’t dead. It’s invisible. Most distribution systems were never built to see which supplier is actually performing, so they have no way to reward it.

The commodity story collapses the moment you look at supplier behavior property by property. Wholesalers and aggregators invest in very specific strengths—direct contracts in a handful of markets, rate accuracy on particular chains, confirmation reliability, clean cancellation handling, content depth. These investments are real and expensive.

The channel they flow through isn’t a backwater, either. Expedia Group reported B2B gross bookings up 21% year over year in the second quarter of 2026, well ahead of its consumer business, marking its 20th consecutive quarter of double-digit B2B growth. The money moving through B2B hotel distribution keeps compounding. The sophistication with which buyers choose among suppliers for any single booking has barely moved.

At most booking platforms I’ve seen, supplier selection is static. A preference order is written when contracts are signed. It reflects commercial politics, integration chronology and whoever had the best rates during the pilot, and it’s rarely revisited.

From then on, every search walks the same waterfall. The supplier that’s genuinely best for this hotel, on these dates, for this traveler, often isn’t asked at all or is asked last after caches and timeouts have already shaped the answer.

Static routing also protects the wrong suppliers. When buyers can’t distinguish disciplined suppliers from leaky ones, the whole category gets judged together. The mid-year World Parity Monitor found at least one online travel agency undercutting the hotel’s own website in 75% of searches, and a recurring driver it identifies is wholesale rates resold with unauthorized discounts.

A supplier that keeps its rates clean ends up subsidizing the reputational damage caused by one that doesn’t because the routing layer can’t tell them apart. With performance invisible, the only lever anyone can pull is margin. The race to the bottom is a routing artifact.

The alternative is to route every request on observed performance for that property and market—availability accuracy, booking confirmation rate, cancellation and relocation rates, content quality and net price after failure costs rather than headline price. Each of these is measurable per supplier, per property, from data that platforms already generate.

When selection runs on that data, the supplier who invested in Japan wins Japan. The incentives flip. Investment becomes visible, visibility gets rewarded and suppliers start competing on reliability as well as price. In the migrations I’ve been close to, the first effect is usually not cheaper rooms but fewer failed bookings, which turns out to be worth more.

If you run distribution or supply at a travel platform, four moves matter most in my experience:

1. Measure per property, not per contract. Portfolio averages hide everything important.

2. Price in failure since a rate that’s 2% cheaper but fails far more often isn’t cheaper once you count rebooking costs and customer damage.

3. Treat routing as a living system, reviewed continuously rather than at contract renewal.

4. Share scorecards with your suppliers. The ones worth keeping will improve whatever they can finally see.

If you’re a supplier, ask every buyer one question: How do you decide which of us gets the booking? If the answer is contract order, then a configuration file is neutralizing the market strengths you spent years building.

I argued in July that this industry still prices shopping as if it were scarce. Allocation is the same story one layer down. My bet is that within two years, “how does your routing decide?” becomes a standard question in supplier negotiations ahead of another round of margin shaving. The suppliers that ask it, and the platforms that can answer it with data, will take share from everyone still routing by paperwork.

Loyalty was never dead. We just never gave it a scoreboard.​
