I hope Alan responds. In case not, my understanding is that:

For a living person, a "qualified" (completely tax-free) Roth distribution requires two things: the account must pass the 5-year clock, and the owner must be age 59½ or older. However, death is considered a qualifying event by the IRS. The moment you die, the age requirement is permanently waived for your beneficiary of the inherited Roth.

The 5-year clock does not reset when the account is inherited; the beneficiary inherits your exact timeline. The 5-year clock tracks back to January 1 of the tax year of your very first contribution or conversion to any Roth IRA. Recent contributions do not reset the clock; even if you opened the specific account or made contributions/conversions within 5 years of your death, since you funded another Roth IRA more than 5 years ago, the 5-year rule is completely satisfied for the entire inherited balance.

I'm not an expert, so please verify from an authoritative source.
Yes, you are correct.
I would add that disability has the same effect as death regarding Roth qualification, although in some limited cases a disability may be temporary.

Also, the 5 year period not only starts with your first Roth contribution, that year continues to apply even if you later close all your Roths and later resume contributions. However, the 5 year clock does not start if that contribution is returned as a corrective distribution with allocated earnings or is recharacterized as a TIRA contribution.
