Proteome diversity is generated through alternative mRNA splicing1. In tumours, mis-spliced transcripts that contain intronic sequences, skipped exons or noncanonical donor/acceptor splice junctions resulting in frameshifts are often translated into neoantigens that drive anti-tumour immunity2,3. Here we report that neutrophils from patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), unlike neutrophils from healthy donors or patients with rheumatoid arthritis, contain many aberrantly spliced gene transcripts, the translation of which was predicted to result in novel amino acid sequences that are absent in normal splice variants or any other proteins. Twenty-one peptides derived from these novel amino acid sequences were detected by mass spectrometry on class I major histocompatibility complex molecules in patient samples. Co-culture of T cells from patients with overlapping sets of 15-mer peptides from the novel sequences resulted in the expansion of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells that were rapidly reactivated to secrete interferon-γ. T cell antigen receptor sequencing and re-expression transferred neoantigen reactivity to a reporter T cell. Serum from patients with SLE also contained IgG autoantibodies that recognized epitopes within the novel amino acid sequences. Our findings demonstrate that dysregulated mRNA splicing in SLE neutrophils generates neoantigens that have caused T and B cell activation, clonal expansion, IgG class-switch and autoimmunity.

SLE is a debilitating autoimmune disorder of unclear aetiology4,5,6. SLE involves multiple organs and is characterized by the presence of autoantibodies against nuclear proteins, nucleic acid-associated proteins and double-stranded DNA7. Although antigen-specific T cell responses8 have a substantial role, it is largely unknown what antigens the T cells respond to9. Mounting evidence also suggests that innate immune cells have a central role in driving the underlying mechanisms of SLE10,11,12,13,14, including the elevated levels of type I interferons found in most patients with SLE15. Neutrophils are increasingly implicated11,12,13,16,17,18 as sources of SLE autoantigens, including both nucleic acids and proteins, that are released during inflammatory cell death via processes such as NETosis13 and ferroptosis19. Although neutropoiesis is accelerated in SLE15, many patients are neutropenic, indicating increased neutrophil consumption. Neutrophils in SLE display widespread genomic hypomethylation, leading, in part, to a distinctive type I interferon-weighted transcriptional profile20, which differs from that in other autoimmune diseases, but bears much resemblance to that in neutrophils from SARS-CoV2-infected individuals21.

Alternative RNA transcript splicing begins during gene transcription and results in the nuclear export of mature mRNA variants that profoundly contribute to proteome complexity and molecular diversity1. Proteins that are translated from alternatively spliced mRNA often display substantial differences in molecular functions, stability and interactions with other proteins or nucleic acids. Alternative splicing can also modulate mRNA disposition, including degradation through nonsense-mediated decay (NMD)22. Alternative splicing occurs more frequently in autoantigen transcripts20 than in randomly selected proteins, and escape from NMD has been shown to cause autoimmunity23. Neutrophils represent an abundant reservoir of intracellular proteins and nucleic acids that potentially function as autoantigenic sources.

To comprehensively analyse alternative mRNA splicing, we created the RiboSplitter24 pipeline, which yields the translated protein products of splice variants. With this tool, we characterized 65,730 high-confidence alternative splicing events in 5,697 protein-coding genes in neutrophils from patients with SLE, of which 521 events in 356 genes were significantly different from healthy donors25. Here we focus on the SLE-restricted mRNA splicing events, referred to as ‘cryptic’ splicing events, that are predicted to generate novel protein sequences.

Thirty-four patients with SLE, representing a typical academic medical centre population (Supplementary Table 1), consented to blood donation. Healthy donors and patients with rheumatoid arthritis served as normal and disease controls, respectively. Immune cells and neutrophils were isolated, and the patients’ sera were stored frozen until use. RNA was isolated from neutrophils and used for stranded paired-end RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) (Fig. 1a) to a sequencing depth of approximately 50 million reads with 101 bp read length (Fig. 1b). We applied a minimum threshold of five reads to exclude stochastic low-frequency splicing artefacts, detecting a total of 271 cryptic splicing events in 130 genes in SLE neutrophils (n = 34), all of which were absent in neutrophils from the healthy donors (n = 20) and patients with rheumatoid arthritis (n = 15). The most abundant events were abnormal exon skips, followed by multi-exon skips, atypical 5′ or 3′ splice sites and intron retention (Fig. 1c). Several cryptic events were combinations of these mechanisms. A pathway analysis of the aberrantly spliced genes showed a strong bias for type I interferon-inducible genes (Fig. 1d), including the most frequently mis-spliced genes IFI44L, MX1, OAS1, RSAD2, XAF1, IFI27, MX1 and IFIT1. However, cryptic splicing events were also observed in genes that are not considered interferon-inducible, such as ABCG1, PSMF1 and OGG1 (Supplementary Fig. 1), indicating that expression level alone cannot explain the generation of these cryptic splicing events, although it could be a factor. As expected, read support for cryptic splicing events was lower than for non-cryptic events (Fig. 1e), but read support for cryptic splicing events that generated novel peptide sequences varied from 283 reads down to our cut-off threshold of 5 (median = 10).

Cryptic splicing events were detected in 33 out of 34 patients with SLE (Fig. 1f), but the total number of events and the genes that they occurred in varied among patients, from 45 events in 18 genes down to 1 event in 1 gene (Fig. 1f). These data also revealed that the precise pattern of cryptic splicing is relatively unique to each patient, although several genes are mis-spliced in up to 15 patients. For comparison, we analysed RNA-seq data from SLE monocytes (Fig. 1i), interferon-treated healthy donor neutrophils (Fig. 1j), Sjögren’s disease neutrophils (Fig. 1j), and two publicly available RNA-seq datasets of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from patients with SLE (n = 36 total) (Fig. 1k) using our pipeline and found that only one of the cryptic splicing events in monocytes and two in one SLE PBMC dataset, suggesting that these patterns of cryptic splicing are unique to neutrophils in SLE. Similarly, the acute myeloid cell line AML14, which represents an immature myeloid phenotype, lacked any cryptic splicing observed in SLE neutrophils. These datasets had higher sequencing depth, providing greater sensitivity for detecting splicing events than our SLE neutrophils (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Cryptic splicing correlated with interferon-inducible genes (Supplementary Fig. 2b), but not with anti-nuclear autoantibodies (ribonucleoprotein, Sm antigen, double-stranded DNA, Sjögren's-syndrome-related antigen A (SSA)), SLE disease activity index (SLEDAI), or steroid use (Supplementary Fig. 2c). A subset of cryptic events, particularly in many interferon-inducible genes, correlated with higher expression in those individuals with the cryptic events. However, in more than half of the genes, there was no such correlation (Supplementary Fig. 3a–d). This was also observed by calculating the per cent-spliced in (PSI) for the cryptic splicing events: for many cryptic events, PSI showed minimal change, although for the majority of cryptic splicing events PSI was much altered (Supplementary Fig. 3e).

Using RiboSplitter, we translated the observed cryptic splicing events into peptide sequences. The total number of 9-mer and 15-mer cryptic peptides that were optimal for class I and class II major histocompatibility complex (MHC) binding, respectively, varied between patients (Fig. 1g). To evaluate whether these peptides could bind MHC and be presented to T cells, we determined MHC class I and II haplotypes for each patient using arcasHLA (Supplementary Table 2) and ranked peptides derived from their individual set of novel amino acid sequences for binding using NetMHCpan-4.1 and NetMHCIIpan-4.026. We only considered peptides that did not match any known human protein sequences in Ensemble and in secondary BLAST searches. Individual patients with SLE varied in the number of potential neoantigen peptides with predicted high-affinity binding to their own haplotype of class I or II molecules (Fig. 1h): 86 peptides from 46 genes ranked in the top 2nd percentile, 65 of them in the top 0.5th percentile.

To experimentally confirm cryptic mRNA splicing events, we designed PCR primers flanking the splicing event that were designed to yield an amplicon of diagnostic size. In a patient with SLE with the multi-exon skip in USP18, this variant was visualized by the appearance of an amplicon corresponding to the predicted 207 bp (Fig. 2a). Sanger sequencing of this band verified its identity as the joined exons 6 and 11. In addition, there were bands of approximately 300 bp and 450 bp, representing the skip of 3 or 2 exons (rather than 4). The canonical splice form was also prominently present at the predicted size of 653 bp. These data validate the in silico detection of this cryptic splice variant.

Similarly, the cryptic ABCG1 exon skip was validated by the flanking PCR primers yielding a 203-bp band (Fig. 2b), the sequencing of which verified the joining of exon 8 to exon 10, as well as the canonical 352-bp band. The skip of exons 4 and 5 in the cryptic IFI44L variant was seen as a 101-bp amplicon (Fig. 2c) by flanking PCR primers and its sequence verified the joining of exon 3 to exon 6. The cryptic splicing of OAS1 (Fig. 2d) and MX1 (Fig. 2e), were validated by PCR with reverse transcription (RT–PCR) and Sanger sequencing of the relevant band in the agarose gel. All these data also illustrate that the cryptic splice variants are less abundant than the canonical or physiological splice variants, in agreement with the RNA-seq read counts (Fig. 2f,g).

Next we used an immunopeptidomics27 approach to determine if peptides derived from cryptic splicing were indeed translated and proteolytically processed for presentation on MHC28. This is vital, as transcripts with premature stop codons are frequently degraded by NMD29,30,31 and because the proteolytic processing creates skewing in the presented peptide repertoire. We immunoprecipitated class I MHC molecules from SLE neutrophils using an anti-pan HLA antibody and analysed the eluted peptides by targeted liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS), using a custom reference database containing computationally predicted neoantigen peptides. These experiments yielded spectra matching 21 of the predicted novel peptides and their calculated daughter ions for a high confidence of identification (Fig. 3, Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 4). We refer to the novel peptides identified by LC–MS/MS in HLA immunoprecipitates as bona fide neoantigens. By NetMHCpan-4.1 ranking, the majority of them fell into the highest-affinity group (Supplementary Fig. 5), with only one notable exception: the 10-residue peptide AGRRKEEEIL derived from a cryptic splicing event in MX1 which was predicted to bind the HLA haplotype of the patient with modest affinity, perhaps reflecting some post-translational modification that is not recognized in the LC–MS/MS, a mutation or modification of the HLA molecule in this patient, or a false negative prediction by NetMHCpan-4.1. Figure 3 also illustrates cryptic splicing events that generated novel amino acid sequences from which peptides bound to HLA were detected. For example, a cryptic splicing event in USP18 (Fig. 3a) involving a multi-exon skip to splice exon 6 to exon 11 resulted in a frameshift of exon 11 and translation of 24 codons (purple box in Fig. 3a) until an in-frame stop codon (indicated by a red line). The translated USP18 protein has a novel C terminus, from which an 11-amino acid peptide was identified with very high confidence (dot product of experimental and theoretical isotopic distributions (idotp) 0.99) in anti-HLA immunoprecipitates. A similar event in the transcript from MYO15B (Fig. 3b) excised exons 41–43, resulting in a frameshift in the translation of exon 44 (purple box in Fig. 3b), from which a 9-residue peptide was detected by LC–MS/MS in an anti-HLA immunoprecipitate from the patient predicted to have this cryptic splice variant.

A cryptic splicing event in PHF11 in another patient with SLE (Fig. 3c) generated a novel amino acid sequence by a different mechanism: an aberrant skip of exon 11, in which a stop codon normally terminates translation, resulted in a continued translation of 53 codons in the normally untranslated exon 12. The junction of exon 10 and this novel amino acid sequence yields an HLA-bound peptide FQAGCHTQL (idotp 0.92) in which the first 4 residues are encoded by exon 10, but the following 5 represent neo-sequences that are unique to the cryptic splicing event and have no match in a BLAST search. In the canonical isoform, exon 10 is translated in its intended reading-frame resulting in a completely different amino acid sequence (orange box).

A similar mechanism generated a novel amino acid product of an OAS1 transcript (Fig. 3d), where a cryptic splicing event representing both a skip of exon 6 (which occurs physiologically in transcript variant 10) and an altered 3′ acceptor site for splicing located 3′ of the stop codon in exon 7, resulting in the translation of 26 codons of exon 7 (purple box in Fig. 3d) until an in-frame stop codon (red line). Thus, the resulting OAS1 protein has a novel C terminus, which can give rise to the FLLTGVDL peptide that we detected by LC–MS/MS on HLA-A*02:01 in the neutrophils from the patient that carries this cryptic splicing event with 15 read counts.

A different mechanism generated a 32-amino-acid residue novel sequence from the aberrant splicing of the FCGRT transcript, in which 96 nucleotides of an inverted 302-bp AluJb element located in the 10-kb intron between exons 4 and 5 was spliced in to generate the novel sequence (Fig. 3e). A peptide encoded by the junction of exon 4 and the Alu sequence (NLEWKETGF) was detected by LC–MS/MS in anti-HLA immunoprecipitates. Further illustrative examples of splicing events that were unique to SLE included a two-exon skip plus an aberrant 3′ splice acceptor site in MX1 (Fig. 3f), resulting in a frameshift in the translation of exon 16, from which a peptide was identified by LC–MS/MS.

Supplementary Fig. 2 shows additional cryptic splicing events in ABCG1, RSAD2, DAAM2, IFIT1, IFI44 and IFI44L. The event in RSAD2 includes part of an intron consisting of a long interspersed element-1 (LINE1)-derived HAL1 (half-L132) retrotransposon, whereas the cryptic event in IFIT1 includes part of the intron containing an inverted AluSx element. Some of the events shown generated peptides that we identified in anti-HLA immunoprecipitates, whereas others did not.

Most of the cryptic splicing events yielding detectable neoantigen peptides occurred near the 3′ end of the open reading frame and therefore do not introduce premature stop codons that would result in NMD. Another set of peptides was derived from events that spliced in a portion of an intron—for example, an inverted Alu element—but did not introduce a premature stop codon.

To test whether class-switched IgG autoantibodies exist in our patient cohort against these neo-sequences, we synthesized a series of 15-residue peptides to cover, in a tiled fashion, the novel amino acid sequences generated by a set of cryptic splicing events observed in the highest numbers of patients with SLE. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs) with these peptides revealed that many of the patients computationally predicted to express the selected neoantigens indeed had IgG that was reactive with the corresponding peptides (Fig. 4). Using the 95th percentile of the background reactivity of healthy donors as a cut-off, 17 out of 34 patients with SLE were positive for IgG recognizing the first IFI44L peptide, including 7 out of 9 patients who were observed to generate this neo-sequence at the time of blood draw for our RNA-seq. By contrast, peptides from the canonical amino acid sequence of IFI44L selected to have high affinity for the SLE risk allele HLA-DR3, did not show increased IgG reactivity in SLE compared with healthy donors (Fig. 4g). These results indicate that the IFI44L neo-sequence was generated chronically or episodically in these patients for a sufficiently long time to generate a B cell response leading to circulating IgG antibodies against this sequence. IgG autoantibodies also recognized peptides from the novel amino acid sequences in OAS1, MX1, IFI44 and RSAD2, with the fifth peptide generated by the frameshifted translation of MX1 exon 6 after the cryptic multi-exon skip and 3′ neojunction detected in 5 of 8 patients with SLE exhibiting this event.

Autoantibodies were also detected in some patients even when the cryptic splicing event generating the neoantigen was not observed. These either represent patients who had the cryptic splice form earlier, but not at the time of venipuncture, or events that were missed in our analysis because our RNA-seq depth of 50 million reads is estimated to give us approximately 60% coverage of mRNA splicing events33,34, indicating that there might be more splicing-generated neoantigens than we observed.

The presence of class-switched IgG autoantibodies that recognize the cryptic peptides suggests that neoantigen-specific T cells were involved and provided help for class switching and affinity maturation. To test this directly, we used a 14-day expansion and restimulation assay that was optimized for the detection of tumour antigen-specific T cells35. In this regimen, the antigen-specific T cells are activated and undergo several cell divisions, and then return to a resting state before restimulation on day 14 (Fig. 5a). PBMCs from patients including those in the RNA-seq cohort were incubated for 14 days in the presence of peptide pools consisting of 3–7 distinct 15-mer peptides covering the novel amino acid sequence of a cryptic splice variant, and restimulated on day 14 with each individual peptide separately, and assayed on day 15 for IFNγ and IL-17 production by ELISpot and analysed for activation using flow cytometry. T cells from one immunosuppressed patient with SLE (undergoing treatment with tacrolimus and prednisone) showed only weak reactivity, whereas T cells from nine other patients (Fig. 5b–e and Supplementary Figs. 9 and 11a), showed moderate to strong reactivity against several peptides, with T cells from each patient displaying a unique profile of reactivity. We also tested a set of control peptides. Three peptides from the canonical splice variant of IFI44L with high predicted affinity for class II MHC (DR3), and three peptides with random amino acid sequences lacking homology with any human proteins, did not activate any T cell response (Fig. 5e and Supplementary Fig. 9). IFNγ and IL-17 secretion were frequently detected, whereas IL-5 was rarely produced and was not increased by any of the peptides (data not shown). Both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells exhibited increased expression of the activation marker CD69 (Fig. 5g–i), but many individual peptides preferentially activated either CD4+ or CD8+ T cells. By contrast, T cells from healthy donors showed little response to any of the neoantigen peptides (Fig. 5j and Supplementary Fig. 10). In the patient represented in Fig. 5i, peptides derived from the cryptic variants of ABCG1, FCGRT, SERPING1, RSAD2, IFIT1, TBC1D15, MX1, IFI44L, and IFI44 stimulated CD8+ T cells, whereas peptides derived from cryptic variants of SERPING1, IFIT1, IFI44L and IFI44 stimulated CD4+ T cells. A statistical analysis based on T cell numbers in flow cytometry using χ2 test and adjusted for multiple comparisons, gave significant P values for many of these peptide responses (complete list in Supplementary Table 4).

Single-cell sequencing of the T cell antigen receptor (TCR) β chain gene (TCRB) revealed that 105 cells in the starting cultures had approximately 89,400 different TCR sequences with a Simpson clonality index of 0.006, whereas the control cultures without peptide had 22,000 TCRs, also with low clonality. By contrast, two neoantigen peptides from IFI44L resulted in the notable expansion of several specific TCRs (Supplementary Fig. 11b), with Simpson clonality indices of 0.072 and 0.185. The copy number after PCR amplification (template frequency) of TCRs from the IFI44L-1 culture was increased from <1 to 6,366; that of the TCRs from the IFI44L-2 culture was increased from <1 to 1,832. Additional TCRs were also expanded, but to a lesser extent. The amino acid sequences of the unique complementary determining regions (CDRs) 1, 2 and 3 of the top ten TCRs are shown in Supplementary Fig. 11c.

To validate the neoantigen specificity of expanded T cell clones, we used throughput-intensive rapid TCR library sequencing (TIRTL-seq)36 to sequence expanded TCR α and β chain (TCRA/TCRB) pairs from the same neoantigen-stimulated cultures and then expressed the TCR pairs in a reporter T cell line, termed T-FINDER37. Figure 5k shows a TCR (TCR14) identified among the 18 tested, which responded vigorously to the IFI44L-2 neoantigen peptides presented by the patient’s own B cells. The T-FINDER cells with this TCR did not respond to 11 other neoantigen peptides or the 3 canonical IFI44L peptides (Fig. 5l), demonstrating high selectivity for the IFI44L-2 peptide. In addition, a pool of all six possible 10-mer peptides covering the sequence of the 15-mer IFI44L-2 did not activate the TCR14-expressing cells, indicating that the 15-mer IFI44L-2 peptide was presented on class II MHC. Thus, this TCR was derived from a CD4+ T cell in the patient with SLE.

Given that SLE neutrophils with cryptic splice variants result in neoantigen peptides presented on their surface class I MHC, as evidenced by our immunopeptidomics experiments, we stained SLE neutrophils with a monoclonal antibody that recognizes the N-terminal knob that is exposed in 20–24 copies on the extracellular face of polymerized perforin pores38 generated by cytotoxic T cells. We previously showed that this antibody detects poly-perforin pores on the surface of 15–45% of neutrophils freshly isolated from patients with rheumatoid arthritis as well as neutrophils treated with monomeric perforin38. Indeed, 0–20% of SLE neutrophils contained dots (Fig. 5m) of the same size as those in rheumatoid arthritis neutrophils, whereas neutrophils from healthy donors did not (Fig. 5n), indicating that cytotoxic T cells have encountered antigen on their surface and attempted to kill them via perforin deposition. The proportion of SLE neutrophils bearing poly-perforin pores was lower than that observed in rheumatoid arthritis (Fig. 5o).

These experiments demonstrate that neoantigen-specific T cells exist in patients with SLE at frequencies that can be estimated to reach 1–15 per million T cells, which is comparable to the frequencies of tumour antigen-specific T cells35. The presence of IgG autoantibodies that recognize neo-sequences suggests that some of the antigen-specific CD4+ T cells provided help to B cells—that is, they are either follicular helper T cells (TFH), which are are known to be expanded in SLE, or peripheral helper T cells (TPH).

Our finding that neoantigens are generated from aberrant mRNA splicing in SLE neutrophils introduces a potential new model for the pathogenesis of this disease. We propose that the appearance of neoantigens, rather than loss of tolerance, is the driving factor behind the escalating immune activation that characterizes this disease. In this scenario, the immune system acts as it is programmed to do: making every effort to eliminate the source of ‘foreign’ neoantigens. This may explain many features of the disease, such as its waxing and waning course, the broad range of organ manifestations and symptoms, and the strong immunosuppression required for treating active disease. This paradigm is also in agreement with the notable similarity in neutrophil expression profiles in SLE and SARS-CoV2 infected individuals21.

The importance of neoantigens is well-recognized in anti-tumour immunity, which is pivotal for cancer immunotherapy39,40, and in which altered self-peptides are recognized as foreign antigen by CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, ultimately resulting in tumour killing by CD8+ cytotoxic T cells. These tumour-specific peptides can be generated from point mutations in proteins41, cancer-specific post-translational modifications, intergenic translation products or aberrant mRNA splicing3,42,43, with aberrant mRNA splicing yielding the largest repertoire of neoantigens. Our findings in SLE are similar in that aberrant transcript splicing is widespread (detected in 33 out of 34 patients) and results in novel amino acid sequences with the capacity to yield neoantigens presented on class I and II MHC molecules. At a sequencing depth of 50 million, our estimated coverage of mRNA splicing events is about 60%. Thus, it is likely that many aberrant slicing events remained undetected in our analysis. Our mRNA splicing data also represents a single time point, while the presence of IgG autoantibodies against the novel amino acid sequences indicates that patients had been exposed to them over an extended time before our sample acquisition. Thus, it is possible that aberrant mRNA splicing is more widespread than our findings show. It may also vary over time, perhaps related to exacerbations interspersed with periods of low disease activity.

Neoantigens presented on class I MHC on the surface of SLE neutrophils would make them vulnerable to killing by cytotoxic CD8+ T cells, which are activated44 but decreased in number45 in SLE. Indeed, we detect poly-perforin pores on the surface of SLE neutrophils, which are known to be consumed at higher rates in SLE15. Perforin-mediated killing is more inflammatory than neutrophil apoptosis and may include other programmed cell death pathways11,13,46. In SLE, cytotoxic effector T cells have a CD38hiCD127− phenotype similar to that of CD8+ T cells in checkpoint inhibitor-induced arthritis and viral infections47, a phenotype that can be induced in vitro by type I interferons47.

The processing of nascent transcripts begins during transcription and involves a large number of splicing factors, RNA molecules, enzymes and RNA modifications. Thus, a multitude of different disturbances could underpin our findings. Whereas expression of genes encoding several core mRNA splicing factors and RNA-binding proteins was either decreased (SF3B148, SF3B5, SF3A2, U2AF2, SRSF7 and DDX4649) or increased (SNRNP27) in neutrophils from patients with SLE compared with healthy and rheumatoid arthritis neutrophils (Supplementary Fig. 6), a more specific set of RNA-binding proteins were found to be associated with sequences at cryptic splice sites in the ENCODE dataset (Supplementary Fig. 7). Downmodulation by short-hairpin RNA interference of a set of individual splice factors in the ENCODE data resulted in some of the cryptic events that we found in SLE neutrophils (Supplementary Fig. 8). Two of these splice factors, DDX24 and SF3B4, were present in both results. Another notable candidate is Ro60 (also known as TROVE2), a known target of autoantibodies in SLE. Out of the four patients with cryptic splicing events correlated with Ro60, three had SSA autoantibodies (recognizing Ro52 and Ro60). DDX24 was expressed at relatively low levels in the patient with cryptic splicing of TTC14 compared with all SLE samples (Supplementary Fig. 7c) and SF4B4 expression was very low in the patient with cryptic splicing of MON2 (Supplementary Fig. 7c). Supplementary Fig. 7d illustrates where these two latter proteins bound in the transcript in the eCLIP data. These data suggest that dysfunction of individual splice factors may be responsible for the cryptic RNA splicing in SLE neutrophils.

Aberrant mRNA splicing is common in haematological malignancies50 and solid tumours51, but the exact molecular mechanisms underlying these phenomena remain unclear except in cases of specific splicing factor mutations, such as SF3B1K700E (refs. 52,53,54) in myeloid leukaemias. Of note, SF3B1 expression was somewhat reduced (log2 fold enrichment = −0.24, adjusted P value (Padj) = 0.045) in SLE neutrophils, as were four other splicing factors (SF3A2, U2AF2, DDX41 and DDX46). However, alternative splicing is a complex process involving more than 200 proteins and U RNA components, making it challenging to identify the cause of aberrant splicing, which may not be the same in every patient with SLE.

Neutropoiesis includes a burst of proliferation in the bone marrow, during which deficiencies in epigenetic regulation could be amplified to significant levels in the mature neutrophils. Decreased DNA methylation and epigenetic gene silencing are hallmarks of SLE55,56, leading to increased transcription of many loci and genes, such as endogenous retroviruses, L1 retrotransposons, Alu elements and other transposable elements. In healthy individuals, intronic transposable elements in nascent transcripts are prevented from acting as splice donor or acceptor sites by specific RNA binding proteins encoded by HNRNPC, ELAVL157, TIA and PTBP158. SLE neutrophils express reduced levels of PTBP1 (log2 fold enrichment = −0.44, Padj = 1.75 × 10−5) and ELAVL1 (log2 fold enrichment = −0.27, Padj = 0.010). This may explain the aberrant splicing in of an inverted Alu in FCGRT (Fig. 3e), an inverted Alu in IFIT1 (Supplementary Fig. 2d), a HAL1 element in RSAD2 (Supplementary Fig. 2b) and other cases of cryptic exonization.

The importance of these cryptic-splicing-derived amino acid sequences in SLE pathogenesis can ultimately be determined only through clinical trials using agents that block their production. In support of an important role, recent work with anti-CD19 chimeric antigen receptor T cells has demonstrated that B cell depletion needs to be complete59 for an apparent cure of SLE to be achieved60. The B cells that re-emerge after treatment are newly developed naive B cells, which, consequently, are tolerant of self-epitopes, including any neoantigens that are present in the bone marrow where B cell tolerance develops and maturing neutrophils are abundant. These trials highlight the central role of B cells (rather than only dendritic cells) in neoantigen presentation to T cells and subsequent immunity against neoantigens derived from altered mRNA splicing in neutrophils.

We obtained freshly drawn blood from adult (n = 34) patients with SLE, healthy individuals (n = 20), and patients with rheumatoid arthritis (n = 15) to serve as disease controls. Study participants were recruited through the University of Washington, Division of Rheumatology Biorepository to participate in research studies at the University of Washington Medical Center, Seattle, WA. The study was approved by the institutional ethics board (STUDY00006196), the research was performed in accordance with all relevant guidelines and regulations, and informed written consent was obtained from all participants according to the Declaration of Helsinki.

The 34 patients with SLE included in our study represent the typical spectrum of SLE disease seen in an academic medical centre with standard-of-care medication (Supplementary Table 1). Eleven patients had active disease, defined as having SLEDAI ≥ 6, while 24 had inactive disease with a SLEDAI < 6 at the time of venipuncture. Based on the 95th percentile of the expression of 111 interferon-inducible genes in healthy donors, 26 were considered interferon signature positive. All healthy donors and patients with rheumatoid arthritis were interferon signature negative.

Polymorphonuclear (PMN) cells were isolated from freshly drawn venous blood (within 1 h of venipuncture) by gradient centrifugation on PolymorphPrep according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The PMN fraction was >95% CD66b+ and >85% CD15+. The cells were washed and suspended in Hanks’ buffered salt solution at 107 per ml.

Extraction of RNA was performed using Quick-RNA Whole Blood kit (Zymo Research, R1201). Cells were lysed in DNA/RNA Shield and RNAs were isolated following manufacturer’s instruction and eluted in RNase-free water. RNA was sent to the UW Northwest Genomics core for strand-specific paired-end RNA-seq at 50 million 101-bp reads per sample.

After quality control of sequencing quality, FASTQ files were aligned to the reference genome (GRCh38) using STAR (v2.7.9a)61 with two-pass mode which improves detection of reads mapping to novel splicing junctions. After post-alignment quality control, sorted and indexed BAM files were used to identify alternative splicing events by SplAdder (v3.0.3)62 which uses alignment and annotation data to create splicing graphs that are enriched with splicing events based on the provided RNA-seq data, followed by quantification of splicing events. The software filters input alignment reads based on their quality and allows four levels of confidence; we used the highest confidence setting. Additionally, this tool uses event-specific criteria to confirm splicing events based on genomic coordinates and coverage; we only included confirmed events in our analysis. Both annotated and novel splicing events were detected including six types of events: exon skip, alternative 3′ or 5′ splice site, intron retention, mutually exclusive exons, and multiple exon skip.

We detected cryptic alternative splicing in SLE, defined as splicing events with read support in SLE neutrophils and either absence or rare read support in healthy and rheumatoid arthritis disease controls. We used our established methodology (RiboSplitter24) to translate splicing events into peptide sequences to allow downstream analysis and validation. Splicing events with valid unambiguous translations were used for downstream analyses.

Because an alternative splicing event includes two isoforms, we identified cryptic splicing events (both isoforms are cryptic), as well as cryptic splicing isoforms (one of the event’s isoforms is cryptic). We used strict filtering criteria as follows: first, we required cryptic events and isoforms to have ≥10 supporting reads in at least one SLE sample and no more than 2 supporting reads in each healthy or rheumatoid arthritis sample; or ≥5 supporting reads in at least 1 SLE sample and zero supporting reads in each healthy or rheumatoid arthritis sample. Second, we excluded annotated splicing events and isoforms based on the GRCh38 reference. Third, cryptic peptides were excluded if a match existed among known annotated proteins of the gene. These filtering criteria add specificity in detecting events that are cryptic and unique to SLE neutrophils.

We consistently calculated PSI values for isoform 2, so that a higher PSI always indicates higher proportion of isoform 2, which makes it easier to interpret which group has more of which isoform.

We identified each class I and class II MHC haplotypes for each patient with SLE from RNA-seq data using arcasHLA (v0.5.0), which was shown to be highly accurate (100% accurate for class I MHC and >99.7% accurate for class II MHC)63. MHC affinity binding was predicted for cryptic SLE peptides using NetMHCpan-4.1 (mers length = 8–11) and NetMHCIIpan-4.026 (mers length = 14–16) that use neural networks trained on binding affinity as well as mass spectrometry data. Strong affinity binding was defined as percent rank of predicted affinity ≤2% (compared to a set of 100,000 random natural peptides) with predicted binding affinity <500.

Peptides that bind to class I MHC were immunoprecipitated using a method described previously64. In brief, HLA-A, HLA-B and HLA-C (W6/32) or isotype antibody was conjugated to protein A magnetic beads (Millipore-Sigma) at 1 mg ml−1. Cell lysate from 10 million cells was mixed with 100 µl of anti-HLA beads (or isotype control beads). After washing, bound peptides were eluted with 0.2% trifluoroacetic acid followed by a C-18 column clean-up and further analysed by LC–MS.

Mass spectrometry was performed at the University of Washington’s Proteomics Resource (UWPR). Peptide separation was performed on a Thermo EASY-nLC system using a 120-min gradient at 300 nl min−1. Mobile phase B was increased from 6% to 45% over 89 min, followed by a ramp to 100% B and column re-equilibration. Samples were loaded at 2.5 µl min−1. Mass spectrometric analysis was carried out on an Orbitrap Exploris 480 operated in positive ion mode. The full mass spectrometry scans were acquired at 120,000 resolution (at m/z 200) across an m/z range of 375–1575, with a normalized AGC target of 200%. Targeted parallel reaction monitoring MS/MS scans were acquired at 30,000 resolution using high-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) fragmentation with a normalized collision energy of 30%. The isolation window was set to 1.6 m/z. Scheduled acquisition windows of 0–120 min were used for targeted precursor ions listed in the inclusion list. Individual peptide was further analysed in Skyline65.

The Skyline chromatogram data were imported into and visualized with Skyline (64-bit) 24.1.0.199. Data were filtered to include only precursors with an idotp ≥0.85 and mass error <10 ppm. Then the match of peak shape and retention time of the spectra from the daughter ions to those from precursors were visually inspected and confirmed.

Selected neoantigen peptides synthesized by Shanghai RoyoBiotechnology were coated onto medium-binding EIA plates at 1 ng μl−1 in 0.1 M bicarbonate coating buffer, pH 9.5. Plates were then blocked in 2% BSA in 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Sera from patients with SLE and healthy donors were added to the blocked wells followed by anti-human IgG–horseradish peroxidase. TMB substrates were added to the wells, and the reaction was stopped by 2N sulfuric acid solution. Absorbance at 450 nm and 630 nm were measured with a BioTek Synergy 2 plate reader.

We quantified gene expression using Rsubread featureCounts66 (v2.12.3). The average expression of 111 interferon-induced genes (ISGs)67 was calculated per sample, and SLE samples were considered having high interferon signatures when the average expression of ISGs was higher than the 95% percentile of ISG expression in healthy controls.

To test whether cryptic splicing events that we discovered in SLE neutrophils were also seen in PBMCs, we used two publicly available RNA-seq datasets of PBMCs from patients with SLE. One study (GSE122459)68 included 20 patients with SLE and 6 healthy controls, and the other (GSE211700)69 included 20 patients with SLE and 10 healthy controls. We ran the same analysis pipeline as described above to align the data and detect alternative splicing events. Then we searched for neutrophil SLE cryptic splicing in the PBMC datasets.

PBMCs (2 × 106 per well) from patients with SLE were cultured in 24-well plates in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, 11-875-093) supplemented with 5% autologous patient serum that was syringe-filtered (0.22 μm), heat-inactivated at 56 °C for 20 min, and re-filtered (0.22 μm). Cells were stimulated with 5 μg ml−1 peptide pools containing three to six neopeptides and supplemented with IL-2 (10 U ml−1; R&D, BT-002-050) on days 4 and 9. Positive controls consisted of stimulation with phytohaemagglutinin-M (10 μg ml−1; Sigma, NC2034236) on day 1, followed by restimulation with CD3/CD28 Dynabeads (Gibco, 11131D) on day 14. Tetanus toxoid (Fisher Scientific/List Biological Labs, NC0603970) served as an additional positive control in a subset of experiments. On day 14, PBMCs were restimulated with individual peptides from each pool, and ELISPOT plates were coated with capture antibody for overnight incubation. Following 18–24 h coculture, triplicate aliquots of 3 × 105 cells were resuspended in CTL medium supplemented with 1% L-glutamine (Gibco, 2503008), plated into coated ELISPOT wells, and incubated for 4 h at 37 °C. The remaining cells were collected for flow cytometry analysis.

Following 14 days of stimulation, cells were transferred to a 96-well round bottom plate and.washed with 1× PBS. Neutrophils from healthy donors, and patients with rheumatoid arthritis or SLE were seeded onto glass-bottom 96-well plates and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min to adhere to the plate. Cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, followed by 3× PBS washes and blocked with 10% normal goat serum overnight at 4 °C. Neutrophils were incubated with perforin 4G10 antibody (1:20 dilution) overnight, washed three times with PBS, and then stained with goat anti-mouse IgG AF647-conjugated secondary antibody (ThermoFisher Scientific, A-21236; 1:1000 dilution) and DAPI (ThermoFisher Scientific, 62248) for 45 min. ENCODE short hairpin RNA (shRNA)-seq datasets generated in K562 cells were used to assess potential effects of RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) on cryptic splicing72. Only paired-end, strand-specific polyadenylated mRNA libraries were included. Reads were aligned and alternative splicing events were quantified as described above. SLE-defined cryptic events were matched to ENCODE-derived splicing events using genomic coordinates. Events with at least one supporting read were retained for analysis. Each shRNA knockdown (KD) sample was paired with its matched non-targeting control samples. Control matching was performed at the sample level, and only explicitly matched control samples were used for each KD replicate. For each cryptic splicing event and RBP, knockdown effects were evaluated using a replicate-aware, read-based framework. Let KDi denote the cryptic-associated read metric for the ith KD biological replicate and let Ci denote the set of matched control samples for that replicate. A replicate-level effect size was defined as

Each biological replicate contributed one independent estimate of knockdown-induced cryptic splicing change. For each event–RBP pair, replicate-level effects were summarized by the mean effect size and assessed for directional consistency across replicates. Candidate regulatory effects were defined as those supported by at least two KD biological replicates, with consistent directionality across replicates and a mean effect size >2 reads. Event-level results were subsequently aggregated at the RBP level to summarize the number of cryptic splicing events observed following RBP knockdown.

ENCODE eCLIP peak data generated in K562 cells were used to evaluate the physical association between RBPs and cryptic splicing events73. For each RBP, biological replicates were merged to obtain a single reproducible peak set. Peaks were filtered using stringent criteria, requiring both high statistical significance and binding enrichment (−log10 P value ≥3 and log2 fold enrichment ≥3), and only peaks with valid genomic coordinates were retained.

For each alternative splicing event, event-specific binding regions were defined to capture the genomic window where RBPs are most likely to influence splice-site choice. These regions were centred on the relevant splice junctions or exon bodies depending on event type (for example, donor or acceptor sites for alternative splice sites, exon boundaries for exon skipping, intron boundaries for intron retention), using a uniform ±300 bp window.

For each RBP, the observed number of target cryptic event regions overlapping at least one filtered eCLIP peak was calculated using bedtools intersect74, counting each target region only once. To assess whether the observed overlap exceeded random expectation, a shuffle-based null model was constructed. Cryptic event regions were randomly shuffled across the genome 1,000 times using bedtools shuffle. For each shuffle iteration, the number of overlaps between shuffled target regions and the RBP’s filtered peaks was computed, generating a null distribution of overlap counts. From this null distribution, the mean and standard deviation of expected overlaps were estimated. An empirical p-value was calculated as the proportion of shuffled overlap counts greater than or equal to the observed overlap count, with a pseudocount added to avoid zero values. A z-score was computed to quantify the deviation of the observed overlap from the null expectation. To account for differences in overall binding abundance across RBPs, the total number of filtered peaks per RBP was recorded, and additional normalization metrics were derived, including peak density and a normalized enrichment score obtained by scaling the z-score by the square root of the peak count.

Empirical P values were adjusted for multiple hypothesis testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. RBPs were prioritized based on a combination of empirical significance, enrichment strength, and normalized enrichment. For enriched RBPs, event-level binding was further examined by intersecting filtered peaks with individual cryptic event regions. When multiple peaks overlapped the same event, peak-level statistics were summarized using the maximum log2 fold enrichment and maximum −log10 P value. These summaries were used for downstream visualization, event–RBP matrix construction, and network-based analyses.

We quantified differentially expressed genes and calculated gene expression normalized counts using DESeq2 (v1.36.0)75 with Wald test with correction for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Independent categorical data were tested with the χ2 test, and nonparametric data were tested using the Wilcoxon rank sum test. Continuous data were correlated with Pearson’s test. All P values are two-sided and a cut-off of 0.05 was used for statistical significance.
