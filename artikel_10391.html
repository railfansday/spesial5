AI companies are missing a key reason people hate data centers: They’re ugly

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

The U.S. is experiencing a huge boom in AI investment, and data centers are at the epicenter. But amid power outages, criticisms around water usage, and moral quandaries, the infrastructure is facing intense backlash from communities earmarked for site development.

The new AI Infrastructure Coalition (AIIC)—a trade organization whose members include Google, Meta, Microsoft, and other tech giants—hopes that pledges to limit resource use, prioritize local hiring, and invest in community projects will ease the mounting friction. But all this forgets one crucial thing: People don’t want to live next to eyesores.

Of all of the millions of dollars that data center developers are spending on these structures, barely anything is allocated to their visual design. And while I would expect the largest expenses to be what they are—AI servers, electrical and power infrastructure, cooling systems, and labor—the buildings’ aesthetics mustn’t be sidelined. Far more time and money must be spent on this part of the equation.

Otherwise, AI companies risk simply adding to the growing list of reasons to resist construction. Neighborhoods are already resisting development and the backlash is reaching the leading data center locations. In Texas, for example, Governor Greg Abbott has gone so far as to implement a statewide moratorium requiring an energy audit for AI companies, halting grid approvals in the meantime. And the governor-elect, Gina Hinojosa, has said she’ll go farther, with a moratorium until the legislature passes new data center laws “to protect Texans.”

AI companies and developers will need to get creative to deal with the scale of the challenge, and while I accept that improving how centers look may not solve everything, investing more in their visual appeal would certainly help.

Because, fundamentally, people tend to view nicer-looking things more favorably. We are superficial creatures, and psychological researchers have even found that people attribute greater moral standing to animals, objects, and buildings that they perceive as more beautiful. Data centers could certainly do with that boost.

Current designs tend to have the opposite effect. Huge gray commercial units are simply not nice to look at, and economically speaking, can spark homeowner concerns about whether they’ll be able to sell their property if they choose to. Owners are already on edge, given that home sales are down, and property delistings—sellers pulling homes off the market—are hitting near-record rates. At a time when the residential market is volatile, the arrival of a massive gray industrial unit is even more unlikely to be welcomed.

It matters to everyone in the local area because Americans have a deep-rooted sense of pride in their communities, especially the way they look. The look and feel of a town really makes a difference to the people who live there, and I regularly see just how much pride a homeowner takes in having a pristine, clean-cut lawn or an aesthetically pleasing home exterior.

It is why Homeowners Associations (HOAs) keep popping up. Nationwide there are more than 370,000 communities managed by HOAs, encompassing more than 77 million residents. These HOAs set guidelines for home appearance and waste management, among other matters.

The associations take maintaining standards very seriously. Nearly half of all HOA violations are based on landscaping or architectural concerns, and they are met with penalties and fines. It could not be clearer to me that residents care deeply about these kinds of design elements—and that data center developers are missing an easy win with local communities by overlooking sites’ aesthetics.

Putting more thought into the building’s shape, ensuring the entrance has a well-maintained lawn and foliage, or making architectural modifications that match other local buildings can make a meaningful difference in how a site looks. And it could go a long way toward making residents more amenable to them.

Don’t get me wrong. I’m not saying that enhancing visual design will lead to a U-turn in public opinion. There are still plenty of resource-driven concerns that I know will worry communities far more than the building’s exterior. But, by and large, the AIIC is trying to addressing these concerns in its recent pledges.

What developers, and the AIIC for that matter, are missing is the aesthetic consideration. It carries a surprising amount of weight, and given that the leading states for data center development—Virginia and Texas—are also home to some of the largest numbers of HOA communities, it cannot be left to fly under the radar any longer.

Beautifying data centers is the low-hanging fruit that could help shift the dial back in developers’ favor. Without it, the appearance of these ugly buildings will steadily add more fuel to the flames, with communities continuing to resist construction in their backyards.
