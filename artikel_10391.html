Avoid these 5 common open enrollment pitfalls

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

Whether you’re among the 53.5% of Americans who get their health insurance through work, the 20.1% enrolled in Medicare, or the 10.5% purchasing ACA coverage, mid-October or early November typically marks the start of the open enrollment period for next year’s insurance coverage.

Life would be easier if your health insurance coverage stayed the same if you liked it or adapted to your life automatically if you didn’t. Unfortunately, we mere mortals have to make decisions about our upcoming healthcare plan each year, in part because employers and other providers make regular changes to their insurance offerings—in addition to any legal changes that might affect federal benefits and ACA coverage.

The good news is that employers, Medicare providers, and the ACA marketplace typically time their open enrollment period to coincide, giving you November to make decisions about next year’s benefits.

But just because you know when to expect open enrollment season—who can miss all the open enrollment carols on the radio?—doesn’t mean you’re prepared for the important decisions you need to make. In fact, there are several common mistakes that could cost you big bucks in 2027 if you don’t know how to avoid them.

Here’s what you need to know about the most common open enrollment pitfalls.

The pitfall: missing a deadline

We all know how fast the fourth quarter tends to go. One minute you’re passing out candy to adorable Spidermen/Gwens and the next you’re eating Christmas cookies while making resolutions for 2027. The open enrollment deadline can get lost in the holiday shuffle.

For reference, these are the important deadlines for enrolling in a health insurance program during open enrollment:

  • ACA Marketplace has two enrollment deadlines:
    • December 15, 2026 for coverage beginning January 1, 2027
    • January 15, 2027 for coverage beginning February 1, 2027
  • Medicare: Enroll by December 7, 2026; coverage changes take effect January 1, 2027
  • Employer-sponsored health insurance coverage: Your deadline will vary depending on your employer. Your HR department will have the necessary information.

There are two possible problems you may face after missing an open enrollment deadline:

  1. You might have to go without insurance. If you miss the ACA open enrollment period, you can’t get insurance through the health insurance marketplace this year—unless you qualify for a special enrollment period. To qualify, you’d need to have gotten married or divorced, had a baby, moved, or lost your previous healthcare coverage.
  2. You might be reenrolled in the same plan. Medicare beneficiaries who miss the open enrollment deadline are typically reenrolled in the same plan if it’s still offered. But it may not be the best option for your needs.

Workers with employer-sponsored insurance plans may still be able to enroll after missing a deadline, depending on how draconian the HR department is—but it’s not a good idea to count on it.

The pitfall: procrastination

As busy as this time of year can be, it’s rare that we miss an open enrollment deadline because it slips our minds. Instead, it’s much more likely that we procrastinate until the last minute. Humans tend to put off decision-making that feels overwhelming, confusing, or deeply boring—which is precisely the kind of choices you need to make during open enrollment.

Luckily, we know how to beat procrastination. If you know you have open enrollment decisions to make but have been putting them off, follow these steps to keep from procrastinating yourself sick:

  1. Make it an appointment on your calendar. Set a specific time to deal with open enrollment paperwork. Schedule it in your calendar the same way you would a work meeting or doctor’s appointment.
  2. Gather necessary information beforehand. After you make your “open enrollment appointment” on your calendar, generate a list of the things you think you’ll need to make decisions. Start gathering this information a little at a time. Some of the data you might need includes the names, birthdates, and Social Security numbers of every member of your household, pay stubs or tax returns to show your household income, how much you paid out of pocket in 2026, and your preferred healthcare providers and medications.
  3. Spend no more than an hour on open enrollment. The reason we avoid tasks like open enrollment is that our brains convince us they’ll take forever. So give yourself permission to work for only 60 minutes. Stop at the end of the hour, even if you’re not completely done. Just schedule the next appointment before you quit for the day.
  4. Don’t let perfectionism thwart your decisions: Part of what makes open enrollment nerve-racking is the fact that it’s impossible to know ahead of time if you’ve made the best decisions for next year’s healthcare needs. Trying to find the perfect plan option that will maximize your healthcare coverage while minimizing your out-of-pocket costs can drive you nuts, since a year of robust health may follow a year of multiple ER visits. Instead of aiming for a perfect plan, base your decision on what’s probable for 2027.

The pitfall: making assumptions

We often try to reduce the complexity of tough decisions by making assumptions. Unfortunately, open enrollment is not a time when assumptions are your friends. It’s very common for healthcare plans to change their coverage and options from one year to the next, even when the plan itself keeps the same name or offered coverage level.

Here are some of the common changes that could catch you flat-footed if you’re not looking for them:

  • A revised formulary: Your plan may no longer cover the medication you’ve been taking for years—or it may start covering a medication that you’ve previously had to pay for out-of-pocket.
  • Increased annual deductible: Next year, you may have to pay more out of pocket before your coverage kicks in.
  • Revised coinsurance amount: Coinsurance is the percentage of the cost of a covered service that you’re responsible for paying, after meeting the annual deductible. Don’t assume that the coinsurance amount will stay the same from year to year.
  • Increased premium amounts for dependents: The monthly premium required to cover your spouse and children can often go up from one year to the next.
  • Increased out-of-pocket maximum: The maximum amount you might pay during 2027 may be higher than the limit set for 2026.

The pitfall: choosing based on price

Expecting a current plan will stay the same is not the only way assumptions could steer you wrong. It’s also easy to make assumptions about how well a plan will fit you based on cost.

For example, rather than compare options one by one, you might decide to enroll in the most expensive plan, assuming the higher cost means it’s better coverage. But the best health insurance plan for your needs depends on your specific situation. Springing for the high-premium/higher-coverage plan may be a waste of money for a marathon runner who sees the doctor only for an annual checkup. (Unless they tear an ACL, of course . . .)

But you might make a similar mistake in the other direction by signing up for the plan with the lowest premiums. It’s easy to assume lower monthly premiums will cost you less overall if you don’t consider the other costs you’ll have to pay. But lower premiums typically means lower coverage levels.

This price catch-22 can contribute to your procrastination, since it may feel like you’re going to overpay no matter what. (As a cynical veteran of open enrollment, I can honestly say that feeling is correct. You are probably going to pay more than you could have if you’d had a crystal ball.)

However, there are some questions you can ask yourself to help reduce the likelihood of making a decision-by-price mistake during open enrollment:

  1. Did my 2026 plan cover my healthcare needs? This gives you a baseline for determining if a new plan will suit your situation.
  2. How much did I spend out of pocket this year? If you spent more than you expected or more than you budgeted for, that can help you identify where you need more robust coverage.
  3. What confused me about my current coverage? Now’s an excellent time to clear up that confusion by contacting your HR representative, the ACA helpline at 1-800-318-2596, or Medicare at 1-800-MEDICARE (1-800-633-4227).

The pitfall: not checking your work

A jaw-dropping 90% of employers have open enrollment errors. These errors may include relatively minor problems like billing mistakes, as well as more major issues like eligible employees not getting enrolled despite opting in—leaving them without coverage.

Before you turn in your enrollment paperwork (or, more likely, hit the SUBMIT button), go back through the entire process to double-check that all the information is correct. Then, when you receive the documents, read through them for accuracy, as well. It’s much easier to fix a clerical error during open enrollment than after the new plan year begins.

Set yourself up for a healthy 2027

Open enrollment may rival filing taxes for the worst annual adulting requirement—but taking some time to make well-thought-out decisions for next year’s health insurance plans will always be something you’re glad you did.
