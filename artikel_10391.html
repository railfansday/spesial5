Making 6 figures? You might still be turning to ‘buy now, pay later,’ according to new research

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

We are a nation united in our desire to pay tomorrow for what we’re buying today.

In the past decade, various deferred-payment options have grown in popularity, and new data from Block might buck the notion of who the “typical” customer is. Once favored by younger shoppers and those consumers with limited access to credit, these options are actually more likely to be used today by households earning more than $100,000 per year, according to the Oakland, California-based fintech company.

“Buy now, pay later” (BNPL), or “checkout financing,” is most popular among American adults ages 18 to 59, with 66% reporting they used a deferred-payment option in the past year, according to a survey by Block. The study also found that BNPL use climbs with income: 69% of households earning over $100,000 used it in the past 12 months, compared with 65% earning $50,000 to $100,000 and 61% earning less than $50,000.

But it’s not just traditional BNPL that’s popular among high-earning Americans. Block’s study found that four of seven different deferred-payment options were more commonly used by the highest earners, including post-purchase payment splitting and installment plans offered directly by a store, service provider, or lender.

Though Block has a dog in the race—it owns Afterpay and Cash App, both of which offer deferred-payment options—the company contends that the growing demand for flexible payments isn’t necessarily a sign that people are spending beyond their means. Rather, Block found that 72% of users financed at least one expense in the past year they technically had the cash to cover, but that paying up front would have felt like a strain. Another 47% opted to spread out the payments to keep money set aside for savings, investments, or other priorities.

The report shows how people weigh the trade-offs of the cost of financing versus keeping cash in their accounts. 

“People don’t look at their checking balance and think of it all as money they ⁠can spend,” Juan Hernandez, head of credit and underwriting at Block told Reuters. “Some of it is already set aside ​for rent, some is for the next bill, and some they want to keep in case something ​goes wrong.”

FINANCING SHOWS STRAIN

Even so, some of the Block data underscores the strain that many Americans are feeling amid the current affordability crisis that’s seen people struggle to pay for everyday expenses. That’s important, as consumer spending accounts for roughly two-thirds of gross domestic product. And while Americans have largely weathered the current affordability crisis, one measure of consumer confidence recently fell to the lowest level since 2014. 

The Block study found that people are using deferred-payment methods to finance everyday purchases, like buying groceries, filling up at the gas station, or paying utility bills. To cover these basics, cash advances, earned wage access, and short-term loans accounted for 58% of financed transactions.

Even though a vast majority of consumers—82% for essentials and 73% for bills—reported repayment timelines within six weeks, the data still shows that many people are struggling to keep pace with their day-to-day expenses.

Such data underscores the findings of a recent survey of 5,166 U.S. adults by the Pew Research Center: 27% said they can’t pay some of their monthly bills—or can only afford to make partial payments.

The so-called blue tsunami that saw Democrats take control of both the Senate and House of Representatives in the midterm elections also gave these politicians a mandate to address affordability. And New York Rep. Hakeem Jeffries, the current speaker-designate of the House, indicated last week that this will be among the priorities for the new Congress come January.

“We’re going to relentlessly fight for an affordable America,” Jeffries said in a press conference. “We’re going to work hard to drive down the high cost of groceries and gas and housing.”
