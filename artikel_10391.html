The probable future is bleak. It’s time for improbable futures

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Massed protesters, enraged by hunger and joblessness, loot shops along the Boulevard Saint-Germain. A crowd marches on the Interior Ministry and is driven back with bayonets. Students tear their school apart and barricade the doors; after hours of fighting with police, the army is called in.

This is Paris in March 1883, and it bears striking similarities to Paris in October 2026, when grievance became a national movement. Then, it was about bread and work; today, the cost of living and declining public services. Then, the industrial revolution added a backdrop of anxiety; today, another revolution in technology. Then, as now, students rebelled against the schools meant to form them. 

France is an extreme case of a global mood. Across the 30 countries tracked by Ipsos, the public opinion research firm, 62% say their country is on the wrong track. In the U.S., the figure is 73%; in France, it’s 89%. Edelman, a communications consultancy, finds in its annual trust survey that only 32% of people across 28 countries expect the next generation to be better off. In the U.S., it’s 21%, and in France it’s just 6%. These numbers are so bad that the social contract, that bedrock of civil society, is at risk. 

The problem isn’t a lack of change. It’s that the change underway isn’t the change people want.

If the probable future is bleak, the most pragmatic thing to do is to take seriously the quest for improbable futures. Unfortunately, that is not a muscle most political or business leaders have built, trained as they are in playing the present game, not inventing entirely new forms of social and financial value. The case study method of business schools entrenches lessons from the past, not from the future; being trained in the logic of the law and finance does not naturally foster the kind of empathetic creativity that designs for what people want next. 

You can see it at this time of year, as companies draw up their annual plans: new columns in spreadsheets unwittingly determining a future that’s very much like the past, plus a bit. This year many companies will bake artificial intelligence into their forecasts to drive efficiency: a future that’s very much like the past, minus a bit. Is that really our highest aspiration for what people claim to be the most powerful technology ever created? And there is growing evidence that AI may deepen the problem. It favors the incremental over the radically new, making everyone’s ideas more alike.

The probable future is not destiny

The probable future is just one scenario among many. If we make different choices, we can design for something different: a plausible future, or more ambitiously, a possible future—or even, if we really allow ourselves to dream—a preposterous future. The field of strategic foresight describes this as the “futures cone.” Designed and built well, ambitious ideas start in the outer bounds, moving towards the center with diligent work. 

In 1956, Britain’s Astronomer Royal called space travel “utter bilge.” A moon landing became possible when the Soviet cosmonaut Yuri Gagarin orbited the Earth in 1961, plausible when John F. Kennedy put the weight of the U.S. government behind it, and probable when Apollo 10’s dress rehearsal succeeded in May 1969. One of humanity’s defining acts went from “bilge” to history in 13 years.

History does not give us a road map to what’s next, but it does suggest some principles as to how to pursue the improbable.

1: Respond to a crisis with creativity

While good times encourage incrementalism, the toughest moments open receptivity for the bold. The economist Milton Friedman went so far as to say that only a crisis produces real change, and that when it comes, “the actions that are taken depend on the ideas that are lying around.” The fragility of these moments can lead to great damage, or great progress. The Red Cross was inspired by the ravages of war. So were the Bauhaus, the German architecture and design movement, and the Paralympics. Today’s pervasive discontent does not preclude ambitious ideas; it creates the context for creation. 

2: Take the first steps towards a preferable future

The Schuman Declaration of 1950 proposed cooperation between France and West Germany for coal and steel. Two countries, two products. The aim was to make war between them “not merely unthinkable, but materially impossible.” A united Europe was preposterous just five years after World War II, but as the declaration put it, “Europe will not be made all at once, or according to a single plan.” That first, discrete partnership began the process of bringing the preposterous towards the plausible.

3: Be willing to look ridiculous, together

The most important principle is psychological. By their nature, improbable ideas are early, may be wrong, and risk ridicule. A prominent professor of futures studies, Jim Dator, in his second law of the future, goes so far as to say that any useful idea about the future should appear to be ridiculous. Which means its champions must be willing to be ridiculed. This is not natural in the meeting rooms, PowerPoint presentations and three-year plans where the future is articulated. 

It helps to assemble a group of people to withstand this phase. The 12 men who met in a London print shop in 1787 to end Britain’s slave trade had each other, and soon took their cause across England with lecture tours and petitions. This is where language, symbols, and rituals come in: visible signs of belonging and strength in numbers. The abolitionists had their Wedgwood medallion, with its image of a Black man in chains and its unforgettable motto, “Am I not a man and a brother?” The Red Cross had its emblem. From chants to T-shirts to lapel pins, the champions of the improbable need to see and find each other. 

What happened after the 1883 Parisian protests?

While many of the French protestors of 1883 were expelled from school and others arrested, that same year, a 20-year-old of no great achievements, troubled by a nation still reeling from its defeat by Prussia, asked himself how he could help foster the character of a generation, such that civil society could thrive. He traveled across the Channel to England, home to the industrial revolution and an empire, and began researching its education system. He decided that playing sports was central to England’s development of citizens. It was where they learned respect, fair play, and collaboration within the context of rules. 

After nearly a decade of study and campaigning, this man stood up at the Sorbonne—just steps from the school the students had barricaded—and closed a speech by proposing the revival of the Olympic Games, dormant for some 1,500 years. The audience applauded, but almost nothing happened. 

The idea of uniting the world around sport was preposterous. So he took the first steps. In 1894, he slipped the Olympic revival onto the agenda of a congress on amateur sport. He convened 78 delegates, formed a 13-member International Olympic Committee, publicly declared their intent, invited others in, and championed the language and symbols of grand, shared endeavor. Within four years of his first sharing his preposterous, idealistic, pro-human idea, the first modern Olympics were held in Athens in 1896. 

The young man was Pierre de Coubertin, and he set in motion humanity’s greatest gathering, which draws billions of people into shared drama and values, from a moment of crisis much like our own. 

So for anyone drawing up next year’s plan, know that the gravitational pull towards the smart, expected future is strong. Ask yourself if there’s at least one possible, or even preposterous, idea on the table, how to take the first steps towards it, and who is going to hold the vision with you while it sounds absurd.

The world is looking for new ways forward. What’s our de Coubertin moment?

Sebastian Buck is a cofounder of ENSO, a future design company.
