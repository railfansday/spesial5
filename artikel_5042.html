Russia Lets Everyday Investors Buy Bitcoin Starting Today

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Everyday investors in Russia can legally buy bitcoin through regulated brokers and crypto exchange offices starting Tuesday, but two of the country’s biggest retail brokers, T-Bank and VTB, are not offering it yet.

The Bank of Russia ordinance that took effect Nov. 17 approves bitcoin, ether and Tether’s USDT for purchase by non-qualified investors, the legal term for ordinary retail buyers who do not meet Russia’s wealth or experience thresholds. From the same date, exchanges may admit those three assets to public trading.

Retail buyers can purchase through a broker, a trust manager or a registered crypto exchange office, on an exchange or over the counter. Purchases through a single intermediary are capped at 300,000 rubles a year, roughly $3,600 at the central bank’s official rate on Tuesday. The cap applies per intermediary, not per person.

Qualified investors face no cap and can access any crypto asset. Every buyer, regardless of status, must pass a knowledge test and read a risk disclosure before the first trade.

The legal door opened before the apps did. “The main reason T-Bank clients do not yet see cryptocurrencies in their banking app is the absence of a market standard for testing,” Alexander Kretov, head of blockchain technology development at T-Bank, told TASS on Tuesday. “As soon as the Bank of Russia publishes it on its official website, we are ready to launch external operations.”

Kretov said T-Bank’s crypto exchange office and digital depository are built and in the final stages of live testing, and that the service will roll out in several stages, “starting with the most active users.”

VTB’s brokerage arm told the agency it is testing the customer journey in its VTB My Investments app and is waiting for the remaining regulations. Once those land, it plans to open crypto trading first to qualified investors who pass the test. The central bank added VTB to its registries of digital depositories and crypto exchangers in October, and the broker ran its first crypto trades for corporate clients the same month.

The single test that brokers are waiting on has been drafted by two industry groups, NEXUS and NAUFOR. As of Nov. 10, the draft had been sent to the Bank of Russia for approval as part of a basic investor-protection standard, but it had not been approved. NAUFOR won self-regulatory status for crypto exchanges and digital depositories in October.

Tuesday’s rule is the first of several dates on Russia’s calendar. A separate central bank ordinance lets exchanges open organized crypto trading from Nov. 22, once the regulator has approved their trading rules. Moscow Exchange, the country’s largest venue, has said it plans to start bitcoin, ether and USDT trading on Dec. 1, initially as a pilot. The exchange and its clearing house are under U.S. sanctions.

The central bank said it limited the retail list to the most liquid assets, judged by market capitalization, average daily trading volume and at least five years of pricing history on foreign exchanges. “This approach protects non-qualified investors from significant losses during sharp and unpredictable price swings,” the regulator said when it announced the list on Nov. 6, as quoted by *Kommersant*.

Russian buyers are not new to bitcoin. They already trade heavily on offshore platforms, Dmitry Lesnov, Finam’s deputy CEO for brokerage, told *Vedomosti*, which reported his estimate that Russia accounts for 15% to 20% of traffic on the largest Asian crypto exchanges. Lesnov also said the 300,000-ruble cap will significantly limit retail interest, and that some buyers will likely stay on foreign exchanges, non-custodial wallets and decentralized platforms.

The framework rests on Federal Law No. 282-FZ, “On Digital Currencies and Digital Rights,” whose main provisions took effect Sept. 1. It licenses exchanges, brokers and depositories but still bans using crypto to pay for goods and services inside Russia.

The question now is less whether Russia allows retail bitcoin buying, and more how long the testing standard takes and whether the first apps can pull buyers home from the offshore desks they already use.
