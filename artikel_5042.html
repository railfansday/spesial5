Anthropic hits $1.5 trillion valuation in record IPO as ECB warns AI funding could dry up

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

The central bank says IPO booms have historically preceded weaker stock returns. It does not see the issuance surge as a systemic risk today.

Anthropic priced its $81 billion IPO at $83 a share Wednesday. The company behind Claude said it expected trading to begin on Nasdaq on Nov. 19. If the stock opens above $83, investors who got shares in the offering will have an immediate gain on paper. Anthropic won't get any extra cash from that move.

The net result is that Anthropic has effectively raised $81.3 billion from its public offering, with a further $26 billion private placement. This creates a $1.5 trillion fully diluted valuation despite growing concerns over AI infrastructure spending and financing risks.

The European Central Bank warned Wednesday that stock returns have tended to weaken after a rush of companies going public. Its concern is what happens when the rush ends: AI companies may still have data centers to finish and bills to pay, but could find it harder to raise cash if stock prices fall and lenders pull back. For Bitcoin and the broader crypto industry, the question is whether a pullback in AI shares becomes a wider selloff in risk assets, not whether the IPO itself takes money out of crypto.

US initial public offerings brought in about $44 billion in 2025. In its November financial stability review, the ECB cited a July analyst forecast of about $225 billion for all of 2026, alongside its staff's separate $212 billion estimate from early November. Both were made before Anthropic set its final price.

It's easier to see why offerings tend to bunch together from the company's side. If investors are willing to pay more for each share, a company can raise the same amount of cash by selling a smaller piece of itself. Other companies see those terms and have a reason to get their own deals done before demand cools.

The ECB compared the size of each quarter's IPOs relative to the economy with what the broader stock market returned over the next two years. In the US, higher issuance went with weaker returns, but the relationship accounted for only about a tenth of the variation. It tells us more about the conditions in which companies choose to sell shares than it does about where Anthropic will trade this afternoon.

Selling shares gives a company cash it doesn't have to repay. If the price drops, shareholders take the loss, and the company keeps the money it already raised. The trouble starts when a business still needs money to finish building and has fewer ways to raise it. Borrowers still owe interest, and AI companies can still owe rent for computing space they no longer need. When a data-center operator borrows against its tenants' payments, the people buying those data-center bonds are counting on the tenants to keep paying.

The ECB’s review of AI borrowing extends that risk beyond shareholders: if lenders start doubting projects will bring in enough cash, they can charge more or stop funding new ones, even as falling stock prices make raising money by selling shares more expensive. The ECB said the issuance surge and the rise of passive investing do not currently pose a systemic risk.

When a company goes public, it sells newly created shares to raise money, and people who already own shares can sell some of theirs in the same deal. Anthropic will be doing both.

Its final terms cover 980 million shares at $83 each. About $79 billion goes to Anthropic for the new shares and $2 billion goes to existing shareholders, before fees in both cases. That excludes an option for the banks running the deal to buy up to 147 million additional shares. Anthropic also arranged a separate $26 billion private placement with strategic partners, contingent on the IPO closing.

Those banks are the underwriters. They take indications of how many shares investors want and what they're willing to pay, then help the company set the price and decide who gets shares. Under the deal's underwriting terms, the banks agree to buy the shares from Anthropic and the selling shareholders and resell them to investors. They're paid through a discount: the banks pay the sellers less per share than the investors pay. If you got an allocation, you were assigned a number of shares at $83. If you buy after trading starts, you pay the market price, and the money goes to the investor selling you the shares.

The money Anthropic gets from selling new stock gives it more room to pay for the equipment and computing capacity it needs to run Claude. Its IPO filing puts expected payments on contractual commitments at $71 billion over the 12 months after Sept. 30, including $4 billion tied to arrangements with conditions or termination rights. Those are primarily commitments for computing and infrastructure, not a loan coming due or a bill the IPO was specifically set up to pay.

The company also reported $147 billion in liquidity as of Sept. 30, before the IPO and private placement, so the commitments do not by themselves indicate an immediate cash shortfall. They give a sense of why access to funding counts long after the first trade. The stock is due to start trading Thursday, while Anthropic expects the offering itself to close Friday if the usual conditions are met.

Anthropic is due to trade under the ticker ANT. Nasdaq's regular session starts at 9:30 a.m. New York time, but the stock doesn't have to open at that hour. The exchange runs an opening auction for IPOs, collecting buy and sell orders and finding the price at which the most shares can change hands. The first trade tells you how that initial batch of orders matched. The closing price will show whether buyers were still willing to pay as much after a session of trading.

Index funds can bring another set of buyers later. These funds aim to match a specified list of stocks, so when an index adds a company, the funds that follow it generally adjust their holdings. MSCI says Anthropic would enter its broad investable market indexes at the Nov. 30 close, effective Dec. 1, if it qualifies for early entry. Which funds would buy depends on the exact index each one tracks.

The ECB found that companies already on the market tended to sell more stock when index funds owned a larger share of them. Its researchers think the prospect of those buyers arriving soon after listing could help some companies raise more at their IPO, too. They didn't estimate how much of Anthropic's $83 price came from that expectation.

Crypto traders already have contracts tied to Anthropic's share price. The Nasdaq debut gives them an actual share price to compare those contracts with. Bitcoin‘s 30-day correlation with the Nasdaq-100 stood at 0.7 in Talos's Nov. 12 report, pointing to sensitivity to the same shifts in investor risk appetite. But a move in both on Thursday wouldn't establish that investors sold Bitcoin to buy ANT.

Even a strong first day would tell us only what buyers were willing to pay for Anthropic now. The ECB's warning is about whether AI companies can still raise cash on reasonable terms when investors are less eager, and the bills are still coming due.
