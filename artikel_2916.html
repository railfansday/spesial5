Nearing Retirement? 2 Key Portfolio Moves You Don’t Want to Skip

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

It's important to set your portfolio up for success when your career is almost over.

By Maurie Backman – Nov 23, 2026 at 11:09AM EST

The approach you take to building a retirement portfolio should hinge on where you are in your savings journey. When you're in your 30s, 40s, or 50s, and retirement is more than a decade away, it often makes sense to go heavy on stocks to grow your money efficiently. But as retirement nears, your strategy should shift.

If you're now within a few years of retirement, your portfolio may require a closer look. Here are two key moves to make.

Investing in stocks can be a great way to supercharge your IRA or 401(k). But stocks can be extremely volatile.

When you're not tapping your retirement savings for income, that's not a problem. Sure, it's not fun to watch your portfolio value swing wildly, but it's part of the process. But once you're at the point where your portfolio becomes an income stream, it's important to limit volatility so you're able to access funds when you need to.

To that end, think about a reduced stock allocation you're comfortable with. You may decide to keep 60% of your portfolio in stocks and put the rest in bonds and cash. Or, you may feel comfortable with a larger stock allocation.

One thing you don't want to do, though, is unload stocks entirely. You want your portfolio to continue growing in retirement so it can keep up with inflation.

If you're living off your savings in retirement and the market crashes, selling assets could mean locking in permanent portfolio losses. That's not what you want. To avoid that scenario, build a cash buffer so you can cover your expenses for a period without having to sell any assets whose value has dropped.

The amount of cash you keep on hand should depend on your income needs, how flexible your budget is, and how much non-portfolio income you have, such as Social Security. A good rule of thumb, though, is to keep one to three years' worth of living costs in cash in case of a prolonged market slump.

If you get large Social Security checks that cover most of your costs, you may be fine to stick to the lower end of that range. If your benefits only cover a small portion of your bills, the larger end may be more appropriate.

You no doubt worked hard to build your portfolio. Now, you need to make sure it's set up to serve you well in retirement. So if you're nearing that stage, these are two moves you don't want to gloss over.
