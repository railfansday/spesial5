It’s Time to Settle With Russia

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

MICHAEL C. DESCH is Packey J. Dee Professor of International Relations at the University of Notre Dame and founding Brian and Jeannelle Brady Family Director of the O’Brien Notre Dame International Security Center.

What America Should Give to Make Peace in Ukraine

In October, U.S. President Donald Trump sold his eleventh-hour deal to secure diesel from Russian President Vladimir Putin—pausing sanctions that his own administration had imposed—as a boon for the negotiations to end the war in Ukraine. The arrangement, he wrote on Truth Social, was intended to keep Russia at “the bargaining table.” Of course, it was always obvious to observers that the gambit had as much or more to do with bolstering the U.S. economy and stemming a massive victory for the Democrats in this month’s midterm elections, and in that, it failed.

Cynics have grounds for questioning all of Trump’s moves when it comes to Russia and Ukraine. He clearly dislikes Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky and shows a marked preference for illiberal leaders, especially those willing to give him easy wins. Still, a stopped clock can be right twice a day. And concessions such as the fuel deal are exactly what might move Russia and Ukraine closer to peace, even if they are not wholly motivated by public-spiritedness.

Ukraine and its backers in Europe and the United States have long maintained that Russia only responds to sticks. The problem with their argument is that since 2014, and especially since 2022, Moscow has been subjected to nothing but sticks. According to Kaja Kallas, the European Union’s foreign policy chief, the more than 25,000 sanctions imposed on Russia after its full-scale invasion of Ukraine had cost its economy almost $1.5 trillion as of June. And although Trump has backed Ukraine less consistently than U.S. President Joe Biden did, especially in his rhetoric, his administration has still provided significant financial, military, and intelligence support; his CIA has been more aggressive than Biden’s in assisting Ukraine’s offensive and defensive air operations. All of these coercive measures have proved insufficient to compel a change in Putin’s policy or turn the tide in Ukraine’s favor on the battlefield.

European leaders may fret that Trump, now a lame duck and focused on his legacy, will push for a deal on Ukraine that suits the Kremlin’s interests more than their own. But ultimately, this remains a war in which Russia has the advantage. Europe should recognize that its pressure tactics have not worked and welcome the U.S. president’s willingness to throw in a few carrots among the many sticks used to try to beat the Kremlin into submission. Washington, meanwhile, should continue to pursue a concessionary strategy by encouraging Ukraine to surrender territory, freezing NATO’s expansion, and offering sanctions relief to Moscow. Putin has shown a willingness to sign favorable deals in the past, including the Minsk agreements in 2014 and 2015, which promised an end to the civil conflict in Donbas only to collapse under the duress of Kyiv and European capitals. A more enduring peace is desperately needed in Ukraine, however unfair the price may seem.

Ukraine has had some recent success on the battlefield. Early in 2026, Kyiv launched offensives in western Zaporizhzhia that erased a year of Russian gains there. During the summer, the Ukrainian military embarked on a major counteroffensive, Operation Vivaldi, that made significant progress near Liman, delaying Russia’s campaign to take the Donbas fortress cities of Kramatorsk and Sloviansk. And after four years, Ukrainian forces have finally inflicted serious, albeit not irreparable, damage to the bridge connecting Crimea and Russia, disrupting a military transport route and tarnishing a potent symbol of Putin-era expansionism. Kyiv has also brought the war home to segments of the Russian public with its attacks on oil infrastructure and commercial targets such as warehouses for Wildberries, Russia’s largest online retailer. Supporters in Western capitals have trumpeted these achievements as evidence that Ukraine has turned the tide in a war that previously seemed to be moving in Moscow’s favor.

But tactical wins do not necessarily add up to strategic victory. And, as Undersecretary of Defense for Policy Elbridge Colby reminded the Ukraine Defense Contact Group at a meeting earlier this month, the war will be decided on the ground. Casualties remain a huge problem for Ukraine. The few times Zelensky has addressed them, he has offered implausibly low figures for his side and cited astronomically high losses on the other. A recent study by Alexander Grable, a King’s College London war studies researcher, dissects these figures and concludes that Ukraine is suffering far higher casualties than Russia in absolute numbers—findings that, if true, refute the canard that Russia is uniquely careless with the lives of its soldiers. These days, while Ukraine pursues costly offensives to temporarily recover territory, Russia is taking cities and densely fortified areas by encirclement rather than frontal assault, reducing the risk to its fighters.

Although Ukraine’s strikes on Russian oil infrastructure have disrupted refining and strained domestic fuel supplies, Moscow has managed to cushion the blow through emergency measures and rapid repairs. In May, the U.S. Defense Intelligence Agency concluded that Kyiv’s long-range drone attacks did not “substantially affect Russian energy production,” which may explain why the Russian civilian population has not felt the full bite of them. The average Russian seems to be weathering this pain with something between resigned stoicism and increased determination to see the war through to victory. If Ukraine’s deep strikes were designed to encourage the United States to support Zelensky’s proposal for an energy truce, its effects have been counterproductive: higher oil prices have refilled the Kremlin’s war chest and greater energy costs are undermining allied support for Ukraine, especially in the United States.

Many Western battlefield analyses suggest Russia has recently lost a bit of ground. In fact, the battle lines in Ukraine remain ambiguous. According to the widely followed Military Summary Telegram channel, which applies a nuanced metric of territorial control to geolocated videos from Russian and Ukrainian sources, Russia has continued to make inroads through October and November. In the north, its troops have carved out a buffer zone on the border, making gains around Sumy and Velykyi Burluk. The see-saw battle for Kupyiansk—a fiercely contested gateway to Kharkiv and northeastern Ukraine—once again favors Moscow. In Donetsk, Russian forces have largely taken the city of Dobropillia, which could enable them to creep north behind the Donbas fortress cities and encircle them. And Russia continues to gain ground in eastern Zaporizhzhia, where its units are now edging closer to the region’s administrative capital. Operation Vivaldi may have slowed the Russian advance north of Kramatorsk, but only at a significant cost to some of Ukraine’s best units. Much like the Kursk campaign in 2024, the counteroffensive seems more like a temporary media victory than a real change in the ground war.

In the air, Russia has commenced a deep strike campaign across Ukraine, using drones and ballistic missiles to turn the tables in key regions. Russian forces have severely disrupted Kyiv’s Black Sea operations, intensified strikes against data centers and electricity infrastructure, attacked locomotives, and hit targets ever closer to Ukraine’s western borders, and are now going after major bridges across the Dnieper, which could literally cut the country in two. Russia’s quantitative and qualitative advantage in jet-powered drones allows these weapons to function as effectively as cruise missiles, and recent air operations have been more sustained and systematic as a result. Ukraine’s dearth of American-made PAC-3 interceptor missiles—a result of the United States’ own dwindling defense stockpiles—means that Russian ballistic missiles are mostly unstoppable.

In October, Trump angrily wrote on social media that Zelensky “doesn’t want to end this war, and probably never did.” This was unfair: undoubtedly, Zelensky does want to end it, just on his own terms. The aid and support Europe and the United States have lavished on Ukraine—initially necessary for preventing a catastrophic defeat—now gives its president little incentive to make peace any other way. Early in the current war, when Ukraine was unexpectedly prevailing on the battlefield, it passed on two promising peace tracks that might have ended the war soon after it began.

But negotiations are now the only way forward for Ukraine, and they will not progress by targeting Russia exclusively with sticks. To overcome the current impasse, Ukraine and its supporters have to put their chips on the table. That means forgoing any hope of NATO accession—or of a sustained NATO military presence in Ukraine—and relinquishing territory to Russia. If Kyiv remains unwilling to make the concessions demanded of losing parties in war, then Washington can take steps to encourage better judgement. The United States could taper down or cut off certain services and munitions it provides the Ukrainian armed forces, including Starlink and Starshield satellite connectivity, Patriot missile defense systems, and intelligence support for air defense and deep strikes on Russian targets. Any of these deprivations would be felt immediately on the battlefield.

Washington should offer carrots of its own accord, too. President Trump can lift some sanctions, such as those banning Russian commercial flights from U.S. airspace, without much controversy. Lifting others, such as those restricting fossil fuel exports and financial transactions, will be less palatable but more attractive to Russia and thus more effective. Russia’s readmission to the SWIFT interbank messaging service and other global commercial networks must be pursued accordingly.

Pro-Ukrainian hardliners argue that no amount of carrots will be enough to satisfy Putin’s voracious appetite for territory. They warn that if Russia is not stopped in Ukraine, it will continue its campaign of conquest farther west through Poland, Lithuania, Latvia, Estonia, and beyond, with no end in sight. As Zelensky warned European leaders at the Berlin Security Conference in early November, the Russian military buildup is not just directed at his country, “it is directed against you.” But although Russia is making grinding progress on the fronts in eastern Ukraine, at the rate of its advances in 2025 and this year, it would take three decades just to capture the rest of Ukraine east of the Dnieper. And the idea that Russia, with a military of under 1.6 million men and women in uniform, could defeat a NATO alliance boasting 3.65 million soldiers is implausible, especially considering NATO’s overwhelming advantages in major weapons and military spending.

Even if Russia could take on NATO directly, it is far from clear that Putin wants to. The Kremlin’s war aims in Ukraine may not seem just, but they are narrower than many analysts allege. Since his 2007 speech in Munich excoriating the United States’ global hegemony—and after the 2008 NATO summit declaration that welcomed eventual membership for Georgia and Ukraine—NATO expansion has been Putin’s idée fixe. He has been similarly preoccupied with the status of the pro-Russia citizens of Eastern Ukraine and devoted substantial effort to ensuring protection of their linguistic rights and political influence through the Minsk agreements. Only when that failed did Moscow recognize the two regions of the Donbas, Luhansk and Donetsk as independent states eligible for membership in the Russian Federation, paving the way for direct Russian intervention in the region. Nonetheless, Putin continued to pursue a negotiated solution to the conflict through Turkish President Recep Tayyip Erdogan and Israel’s then prime minister Naftali Bennett. As with the Minsk process, it was Ukraine and its backers who walked away from these negotiations when their aims—the complete liberation of all Russian-occupied territory—still appeared achievable on the battlefield.

In a round of U.S.-led peace talks earlier this month in Abu Dhabi, Russia made maximalist demands, asking for the pullback of NATO forces from Poland and the Baltic states, limits on European nuclear deterrence, and restrictions on the size and capabilities of Ukraine’s military. But read in the broader context of Putin’s singular and consistent fixations, these demands look more like negotiating ploys than dealbreakers. It has long been clear what the Kremlin will settle for: control over Ukraine’s eastern border regions and a largely demilitarized Kyiv. Whatever the ultimate terms of this deal, some basic facts are clear. There is no going back to a Ukrainian Donbas, nor a Ukrainian Crimea or Novorossiya. And whatever security guarantees Europe and the United States give Ukraine in the future, they cannot include NATO membership.

The status quo in Ukraine unsustainable. Four years of war have lulled many of Kyiv’s supporters into complacency about the risk that continued pressure on Russia could create an escalatory spiral. There is increasingly cavalier talk in some newer NATO capitals, for example, about undertaking aggressive NATO exercises near Kaliningrad and overcoming the city’s air and missile defenses. But those steps may only further widen and deepen the war. The clear and present danger of vertical and horizontal escalation must not be dismissed.

Cracks are already showing in the West’s united front against Russia. France and other western European states are experiencing heightened social and political unrest, fueled in part by the costs of the war. In late October, Finnish President Alexander Stubb and Norwegian Prime Minister Jonas Gahr Store made a rare phone call to Putin to “convey messages directly regarding future negotiations” toward peace, according to a statement released by the two leaders. In the United States, a once bipartisan hard-line toward Russia is starting to fall out of favor, even among Democrats. In mid-October, Democratic Representative Jim Himes told the Warsaw Security Forum that a future Democratic president will have to acknowledge the “tectonic fact” that the United States can no longer afford to play its historic leading role in confronting Russia.

There is reason to think that the Trump administration’s more conciliatory approach has already opened the door to diplomacy. The Oleshky truce brokered by First Lady Melania Trump and the Vatican successfully evacuated civilians trapped in a Russian-occupied city south of Kherson. And the president’s comments in his 60 Minutes interview this month suggested that he is ready to ply Russia with the incentives necessary to get a deal over the line. These are steps in the right direction, but in addition to altering the balance of carrots offered to Russia, the Trump administration must consider providing Ukraine security guarantees outside of the NATO framework.

Now in the twilight of his political career, Trump has made clear his desire to leave a legacy in the form of Nobel Peace Prizes and triumphal arches. Ending a major war in Ukraine would be a crowning achievement, which is why he has waxed lyrical about a “big, beautiful agreement” since he entered his second term in office. And if Trump still believes that China is the pacing threat of the twenty-first century, reintegrating Russia into the global economy and weakening the axis between Putin and Chinese leader Xi Jinping will be paramount for advancing his long-term political vision.

No one has lost money betting against peace in Ukraine since 2014, and it would be foolhardy to expect Trump to pursue diplomacy solely for altruistic reasons. But perhaps if he sees peace as being in his own interest, as well as that of the United States, he might, despite himself, broker an end to a bloody and increasingly fruitless war. Doing so could be one of the few positive legacies of his second term, even if it does not get him to Oslo.
