Cummins Told Electrolyser Customers to Stop. Who Keeps the Hydrogen Plants Running?

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Cummins is withdrawing from new electrolyser sales, but its customers have a problem that cannot be handled by closing a sales office. In September, its Hydrogenics subsidiaries recommended that customers stop using HyLYZER electrolysers following a review of operating data. The company disclosed the recommendation in its November quarterly report, said that it had not launched a recall, and was still assessing what modifications might be needed for equipment to return to service. It had not established the cost of resolving the issue and warned that the eventual financial effect could be material.

Cummins had a substantial position in the PEM electrolyser business. Accelera reports more than 45 HyLYZER 500 systems and more than 20 HyLYZER 1000 systems rated at 5 MW each. Using those minimum reported system counts and equipment ratings, its historical deployments represent roughly 145–213 MW, equivalent to 11–16% of the IEA’s projected 1.3 GW global PEM installed base at the end of 2026. Cummins has not published the number of HyLYZER plants covered by its September recommendation. The company is leaving a sizeable equipment estate requiring expert maintenance, field engineers and potential safety modifications.

The timing makes the situation consequential. Cummins announced in February that it intended to cease new commercial electrolyser activity, while continuing existing customer commitments. Its 2025 Accelera restructuring and impairment charges reached $458 million across several actions, including the electrolyser retreat. It is reducing specialist capacity at the same time as installed equipment needs engineering attention. Revenue from new orders can disappear faster than warranties, service obligations and safety responsibilities.

My TFIE Strategy Briefing on Cummins’ hydrogen hangover examines the HyLYZER warning, the customer projects and the financial implications of manufacturers leaving shrinking markets. The underlying evidence and project comparisons are in the full analysis.

The problem now moves from selling hardware to keeping it safe and serviceable. Investigating field data, testing modifications, certifying repair procedures and maintaining proprietary controls requires specialist teams. Those teams must remain capable long after the sales pipeline and the business unit built around it have contracted.

Estonia’s Stargate Hydrogen has taken over Cummins’ legacy HySTAT alkaline electrolyser platform and supplies replacement stacks and service for a fleet of more than 400 installations. That handover shows how intellectual property, manufacturing capability and field-service expertise can survive an OEM exit. Cummins’ HyLYZER PEM fleet needs its own engineering and aftermarket solution.

For owners, the financial question also depends on the purpose of the hydrogen. Air Liquide’s 20 MW Bécancour installation and Linde’s 35 MW Niagara Falls project serve companies with industrial hydrogen customers and alternative supply networks. Interruptions can mean lost sales, replacement purchases and costs of maintaining customer commitments. An owner with contracted industrial demand has a commercial reason to restore production, provided the equipment can meet the required safety standards and the replacement supply costs justify it.

Florida Power & Light’s Cavendish project presents another decision. Its 25 MW HyLYZER installation converts electricity to hydrogen for limited blending into natural gas used in power generation. Even without a stop-use issue, that application has to compete with using electricity directly or obtaining reliable grid services by other means. At an illustrative 55 kWh/kg of electrolyser electricity and 60% combined-cycle generation efficiency, about 36% of the original electricity returns after converting it to hydrogen and burning it, before compression and storage. Input power at $50/MWh would cost roughly $138/MWh of recovered electricity before equipment and operating expenses. Repairing the electrolyser would not change that conversion arithmetic.

The economic difference appears again at Hysolar’s Nieuwegein installation. A 2025 KWR H-Flex evaluation documented project delays, higher costs and hydrogen demand expanding more slowly than planned. Its electrolyser, dedicated pipeline, refuelling station and vehicle customers are linked by a common demand forecast. Better equipment performance helps only when enough customers will buy the hydrogen at prices covering production and delivery. The same distinction applies to demonstration projects combining buses, heating, electricity and gas blending: several proposed uses do not guarantee several profitable markets.

Manufacturers face the cost of retaining the people and intellectual property to support complex equipment long after the expected growth market has contracted. Cummins’ separate exit from its low-pressure fuel-cell business illustrates the potential bill: in March 2026, it made a $175 million net payment as part of a sale, cancellation of commitments and claims settlement, producing a $199 million accounting charge. Those are fuel-cell transaction figures, not estimated HyLYZER repair costs. The PEM matter remains unquantified.

The customers’ cost can exceed the repair bill. Owners need a safe restart route, long-term supply of stacks and controls, a price for replacement hydrogen during outages, and buyers able to pay for the output afterward. Lenders face a thinner resale market for specialised plants when their OEM leaves. Public funders face grant, regulated cost-recovery and guarantee liabilities when projects stop operating.

The wider hydrogen market makes these questions urgent. The IEA’s Global Hydrogen Review 2026 finds that global demand still sits overwhelmingly in traditional industry and refining. Only about one-fifth of newly signed low-emissions hydrogen offtake volumes in 2025 had firm contractual commitments. A producer, refuelling-station operator and vehicle buyer can each invest on the assumption that the other two will grow. That is one demand risk distributed across three balance sheets, not three independent investments.

The next investment decision needs a safe route back to service, contracted customers, dependable parts supply and a costed exit alternative. Governments should direct support toward industrial customers who need hydrogen and can pay for it, rather than finance energy-carrier applications that lose to direct electricity. Cummins is exposing the long tail of earlier commitments. Other suppliers, buyers and lenders still control their next decisions.
