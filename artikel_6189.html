Situated halfway between San Francisco and the Oregon border, California's Mendocino Coast has managed to remain a relatively hidden gem, of sorts. While it's certainly not a secret, its remote location has kept it from getting overrun with tourists — but new direct flights and millions of dollars in roadwork funding are making it more accessible than ever. And that's just one reason the coastal town of Mendocino made our list of top destinations to visit in 2027.

Nestled between rugged cliffs, redwood forests and rich wine terroirs, Mendocino has it all. No matter what you get up to out here — and there’s more to do than you might expect — it’s virtually impossible not to feel your blood pressure drop. The area’s relaxed pace of life is infectious, and during a recent two-day trip, this type-A traveler found herself hopping on the slow travel train.

From how to get there and where to stay to the best things to do and eat, here’s how to spend a long weekend (or longer!) on the Mendocino Coast. I dare you not to fall in love.

Travelers have several airports to choose from when flying to Mendocino.

The closest, albeit smallest, option is Charles M. Schulz–Sonoma County Airport (STS), which puts you only a two-to-three-hour drive from Mendocino. Alaska Airlines, Delta and Southwest have all launched new routes to STS this year from Austin, Phoenix, Salt Lake City and more.

You’ll have more options if you fly into San Francisco International Airport (SFO), Oakland San Francisco Bay Airport (OAK) or Sacramento International Airport (SAC). All are roughly the same distance from Mendocino — three to four hours depending on traffic — and offer plenty of direct flights from around the country through various airlines. A local I spoke to recommended SAC, saying that the drive is less stressful because it doesn’t go through a city.

No matter where your flight lands, you’ll need to rent a car. The drive can be tiring, especially as the roads get windier approaching the coast. Thankfully, there are plenty of wineries and cideries along the way where you can stop for a restroom break (and maybe a drink or two, if you’re not the driver).

One of the many facets of Mendocino’s appeal is the charming, walkable downtown — complete with views of Mendocino Bay.

You’ll find no shortage of souvenirs here, from bookshops and toy stores to clothing and home decor. Art lovers will enjoy perusing the handful of galleries. Coffeeshops, bakeries and chocolate shops abound, so you needn't worry about finding caffeine or snacks to sustain you during your wanderings.

If you wear jewelry (or are in the market for a unique gift that will fit in your suitcase), you won’t want to miss Mendocino Sandpiper, which offers a wide variety of work by local artists.

And that’s just the town of Mendocino. Its slightly larger neighbor, Fort Bragg, located about a 15-minute drive north along the coast, has even more boutiques to browse.

You will need a car to get around the Mendocino Coast area, but you won’t have to spend much time in the car once you arrive. That’s because the area’s best outdoors activities are within easy reach of Mendocino and Fort Bragg.

The expansive Mendocino Coast Botanical Gardens were a highlight for me, and would probably be stunning in the spring and summer when most of the flowers are in bloom.

Mendocino Headlands State Park is literally steps from downtown, and there are several other state parks under 20 minutes away, including Jug Handle State Natural Reserve and Russian Gulch State Park. To commune with the area’s famous redwoods, head to Otis R. Johnson Wilderness Park, a glorious corner of green tucked away on the edge of Fort Bragg. Or head to MacKerricher State Park for a stroll along a black-sand beach and views of the aptly named Seal Rocks.

February to March is peak whale-watching season due to the gray whale migration, but this area gets visits from various whale species year-round, so keep your eyes peeled. I didn’t spot any whales, but I did see some seals and sea lions bobbing in the waves, as well as flocks of pelicans and other waterbirds.

You may also hear about a place called Glass Beach, which served as a community garbage dump at the beginning of the 20th century; over several decades, the waves tumbled some of the debris into colorful sea glass. Locals warned me that most of the glass is gone now, snatched up by tourists over the years. You’re better off going to the International Sea Glass Museum in Fort Bragg — with over 3,000 pieces on display, it boasts the world’s largest permanent sea glass exhibit.

Both Mendocino and Fort Bragg have surprisingly varied food scenes, from the casual organic Good Life Cafe & Bakery and elevated all-vegan Fog Eater to the Irish-inspired Patterson’s Pub and Asian fusion Gnar Bar. And foodies won’t want to miss the local farmers’ markets, held on summer Fridays in Mendocino and Wednesdays year-round in Fort Bragg.

However, the main draws for foodies are, of course, the seafood — I heard Fort Bragg’s Sea Pals described as “the best fish ‘n’ chips outside of the U.K.” — and the thriving beverage scene.

Oenophiles will want to take the Highway 128 Wine Road to sample the fruits of the nearby Anderson Valley and Yorkville Highlands wine regions. But you’ll get plenty of opportunities to sample local wines in town as well. My hotel even offered a lobby wine machine where you can get wine samples with the press a button and tap of a keycard.

If wine isn’t your thing, don’t worry, there are plenty more local beverages to try. Gowan’s Cider Garden, which brews both alcoholic and non-alcoholic varieties, bills itself as California's first-ever cider tasting orchard. Meanwhile, the zero-waste, solar-powered North Coast Brewing Co. has been brewing unique beers (and winning awards for them) since 1988.

The Mendocino Coast is also home to some fascinating history, and it has an array of museums and other attractions where visitors can learn more.

Fort Bragg’s Guest House Museum is a great starting point to learn about local history, from the logging industry to the Great 1906 Earthquake. Or visit during Labor Day weekend to experience lumberjack games at the annual Paul Bunyan Days festival.

If you have young travelers in tow, check out Fort Bragg’s Noyo Center for Marine Science, which offers interactive exhibits like tide pools and a full orca skeleton. Or climb to the top of a lighthouse and see a real Fresnel lens at either Point Cabrillo Light Station State Historic Park or the 156-year-old Point Arena Lighthouse.

Train enthusiasts won’t want to miss the historic Skunk Train, which started carrying timber in the late 1800s and now carries passengers through scenic redwood forests. While the train’s traditional 35-mile route has a reputation for jaw-dropping scenery, a tunnel collapse in recent years has shortened the route to a sliver of its former glory. However, I still enjoyed the nostalgic vibes of the Redwood Presidential Class, which made me feel like I had been dropped into one of my favorite Agatha Christie murder mysteries.

The Mendocino Coast has been leaning into slow and wellness travel, and for obvious reasons. With ancient redwoods on one side and windswept cliffs on the other — and blessedly few tourists crowding the trails — the landscape itself is therapeutic.

If you want something more structured, there are plenty of wellness activities if you know where to look. Many are offered by locals hotels but open to the public.

A few examples include yoga classes at Stanford Inn by the Sea, redwood hot tub and sauna sessions at Sweet Water Inn & Spa, sound healing in a geodesic dome at Mendocino Grove, and guided shinrin-yoku (forest bathing) walks at Brewery Gulch Inn & Spa.

Summer is Mendocino’s most popular season. It will also be the most expensive and busiest time to go — though I don’t expect the area to get too packed at any time of year, given its remoteness.

For adventure seekers looking to make the most of the area’s hiking, water sports and more, it’s probably worth planning your trip in summer for longer days and maximum sunshine. And with average highs topping out in the mid-70s Fahrenheit, Mendo could make a nice coolcation spot.

However, you can save money and all but guarantee a quiet vacation by traveling in the shoulder seasons from mid-October to November and April to early June. During these times, you can still expect warm enough weather for outdoor exploits, though temperatures will drop at night and more frequent rains may wash out hiking trails occasionally.

The hotel scene in Mendocino is characterized by quiet luxury. Its neighbor, Fort Bragg, has more variety and more budget-friendly options.

I spent three nights in the new Hill House at Trailborn Mendocino, a half-century-old property that was recently renovated before joining the Trailborn nature lodge collection and Marriott Bonvoy program. Other points options include Holiday Inn Express Fort Bragg by IHG and Baymont by Wyndham Fort Bragg.

If, like me, you love a good independent or boutique hotel, Mendocino is full of them. For instance, you could book a seaside cottage at Agate Cove Inn through Amex Travel or Capital One Travel, earning 5 points per dollar with your or 10 miles per dollar with your Capital One Venture X Rewards Credit Card, respectively.

Or consider staying at SCP Mendocino Inn and Farm, a 19th-century manor turned B&B where guests can enjoy complimentary breakfast sourced from the onsite farm. Even better, you can redeem your points for more than 1 cent apiece via Chase Points Boost.

Overall, one of the things I loved most about the Mendocino Coast was its manageable size. From the downtown areas to the state parks, everything I visited was small but high-quality.

So instead of running around a city trying to cram in as much as possible, you can spend a leisurely weekend (or longer) exploring charming boutiques, quirky museums, local dining or natural wonders. You won’t run out of things to do, but the close proximity between attractions and “island time” mentality will help you savor the activities you choose.

That’s what slow travel is all about, right?
