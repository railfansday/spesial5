These incoming members of Congress have been here before

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

Rep.-elect Chris Rabb, D-Pa., greets current members of Congress in the Capitol on Nov. 11. (Tom Williams/CQ Roll Call)

When Rep.-elect Chris Rabb first worked on Capitol Hill in the 1990s, it didn’t exactly inspire him to want to go into politics.

“I said, ‘Oh, this is how the sausage is made? I should be a vegan,’” the Pennsylvania Democrat recalled.

Working for Carol Moseley Braun, the first Black woman elected as a U.S. senator, Rabb saw threats and “just vile things” directed at her.

“My entrée to service behind the scenes was seeing voluminous amounts of violence, hate speech and death threats against my boss, and I thought to myself, ‘Why would anyone want to serve?’” he said.

During his time in the state legislature starting in 2017, Rabb “fell in love with public service, despite the vitriol.” Now, the democratic socialist describes coming to Congress as “surreal,” as he succeeds retiring Rep. Dwight Evans in a deep-blue district based in West Philadelphia.

Rabb is one of more than a dozen incoming members who have worked on the Hill before, ranging from interns to chiefs of staff and everything in between.

Unlike some in the 100-plus-strong freshman class, they already know their way around the hallways. And Rabb has some advice for his new colleagues: “Treat your staff well. Take care of your staff; they’ll take care of you.”

Here are some of the former staffers who will join the House in January.

A couple years ago, Killin was working as chief of staff to former second gentleman Doug Emhoff. But she started out on the Hill.

“It is helpful to know, as I joke, where the bathrooms are, but I still feel like I have a lot to learn,” said the Army veteran, who unseated Republican Rep. Jeff Crank in a Colorado Springs-area district.

She served as the top staffer for three House Democrats: Donna Shalala of Florida, Carolyn Maloney of New York and Marie Gluesenkamp Perez of Washington.

“I’ve actually opened up two freshman offices, so it’s helpful to have done that before,” Killin said.

White will be just the third person in modern times to hold the job of nonvoting D.C. delegate. He replaces retiring Del. Eleanor Holmes Norton — also his former boss.

Before his time on the D.C. Council, he worked as her legislative counsel, watching her navigate a role with limited power in her perennial fight for statehood.

“I need to make sure I’m developing a national profile for this seat,” he said, vowing to continue the quest for full representation even as President Donald Trump seeks to exert more control over the District.

One of just a few Republican women in the freshman class, Gruters said she “never in a million years” imagined coming to Congress herself, despite her years as a staffer.

She will replace retiring Rep. Vern Buchanan in Florida’s 16th District, after once serving as his operations director. She also worked as district director for Rep. Greg Steube, R-Fla.

“I never thought this would be the path, but I’m honored to do it,” said Gruters, whose husband leads the Republican National Committee.

Rogers added another title to his résumé last week: freshman class president.

His fellow incoming Republicans tapped him for the role. Not so long ago, he was working for the Senate Judiciary Committee, fresh off a stint at the white-shoe law firm White & Case.

In 2025 he served as general counsel for Sen. Rick Scott, R-Fla., and plans to keep pushing for a bill he helped draft that aims to crack down on foreign surrogacy contracts. “We are currently selling American children to China, our No. 1 adversary,” Rogers said, describing it as “deplorable.”

Rogers will succeed retiring GOP Rep. Neal Dunn in the Florida Panhandle.

García’s run for her boss’s seat drew controversy from the start. When Rep. Jesús “Chuy” García, D-Ill., waited to publicly announce his retirement until after the filing deadline in Illinois had already passed, critics blasted him for effectively boxing out other candidates and clearing the way for his chief of staff.

He faced a rare formal disapproval vote in the House over the move. But García has insisted she is more than a hand-picked successor, saying on her campaign website that she has “delivered solutions rooted in lived experience.”

The son of Ghanaian immigrants, Boafo is a state delegate who has lobbied on the Hill for tech company Oracle.

He also worked as campaign manager for Rep. Steny H. Hoyer, earning an endorsement from the retiring longtime Democrat and campaign cash from groups aligned with him.

Boafo prevailed in a crowded primary, even as Speaker Emerita Nancy Pelosi threw her weight behind a different candidate, former Capitol Police officer Harry Dunn.

Sell worked under then-House Agriculture Chair Larry Combest during the 2000 crop insurance overhaul and the fight over the 2002 farm bill. He later joined his former boss in launching an agricultural lobbying firm, Combest, Sell and Associates.

Once a precocious child magician who wrote his own book of tricks, Lasher also got an early start in politics.

“I feel supremely fortunate to have wandered into Jerry Nadler’s political orbit as a teenager 30 years ago, and then to have worked in his congressional office a decade later,” Lasher wrote in an opinion piece for City & State New York.

Backed by the retiring Nadler, the self-described “nerd” beat out a couple of big names in the primary, including a Kennedy family heir. Another former boss endorsed him, too: Gov. Kathy Hochul. Before serving as a New York state assemblyman, Lasher worked as her policy director.

Villegas ousted GOP Rep. David Valadao in a closely watched race in the San Joaquin Valley.

A decade ago, he was an intern for Rep. John Garamendi, D-Calif. And the progressive also interned for former California state Rep. Rudy Salas, who made unsuccessful bids to unseat Valadao in 2022 and 2024.

A San Diego City councilmember, Von Wilpert flipped the seat held by Republican Rep. Darrell Issa, who decided not to run for reelection under a map redrawn by Democrats.

When she worked for the National Labor Relations Board in 2016, she was detailed to the House Education and Labor Committee, working under ranking member Robert C. Scott, D-Va.

Jackie Wang and Nick Eskow contributed to this report.
