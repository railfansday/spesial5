Perfectionism Isn’t the Problem

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

Updated November 9, 2026 | Reviewed by Gary Drevitch

I speak to many adolescents and young adults who call themselves “perfectionists”. Often, their parents, if they are involved in the referral, want me to get them to change—by which they mean, lower their standards. Or at least lower their standards in some domains, the ones that parents perceive as less important, such as appearance, friendship, dating, Snapchat streaks and TikTok reels. As a psychotherapist, it’s not my job to “fix” behaviour or “correct” thought patterns, but it is my job to try to make sense of it. As something of a perfectionist myself, I don’t see aiming high as problematic and I admire young people with ambition, who strive to be their best selves. What I do see as a problem, however, is the pressure to be perfect. When the pressure is internal, it can be debilitating and counterproductive. When it comes from external influences it often feels contrary – “Aim high, anything less than the best is less than good enough, but also; stop being such a perfectionist!”

Young people in contemporary society are under more pressure than ever before, and the bar keeps getting higher. In the UK, GCSEs are graded 1–9, where a 7 is roughly equivalent to the old A grade, an 8 is an A* and a 9 is even higher than that.

An “A” isn’t enough at A* level either, since the introduction of A*. So, being an A-grade student means you’re less than perfect, and that’s perceived as less than good enough.

Most young people are now expected to apply to university, rather than an elite minority—even those who struggle academically, setting them up for more pressure and more experiences of being less than perfect. They’re under pressure to know what comes next and have their sights set on a “good career”, even though jobs are scarcer, because there’s so much competition, because there are so many more graduates. Meanwhile, the social pressure to be perfect involves an impossible checklist. Girls have to be slim and curvy and beautiful with perfectly aligned teeth and brows and lashes and not look like they tried. Boys have to be buff. All genders have to be partnered up and smashing their “relationship goals”. They need to be popular and having so much fun all the time, while their imperfect brains and their imperfect bodies are still developing.

Adolescents tend also to be more susceptible than most to the modern affliction of “compare and despair”, comparing their worst feeling self on their worst performing day to someone else’s carefully curated, filtered, photo-shopped, perfect self on Instagram. On Instagram, everyone looks as if they’re living their best life. They don’t share the cramming-for-exams all-nighters and their less-than-perfect grades; they share the all-night parties, where everyone looks super-glam, which you scroll through at home feeling super-glum. They don’t share the misery of dating fatigue, relationship heartbreak, friendship fallings-out or family conflict; they share picture-perfect group shots in glamorous locations—also curated, also filtered. They don’t share sh*t days at work or sh*t days at uni, or sh*t days at school; they show the fabulous fun they’re having at freshers’ week or away days—all carefully curated and filtered.

So, when I meet an adolescent perfectionist, I don’t tell them to lower their standards; I tell them it’s great that they have standards. I don’t suggest they stop comparing themselves with other people; I assure them that’s human nature. But I do wonder where the pressure to be perfect is coming from and where it’s landing: Is it internal or external? Does it apply to some areas more than others? And I explore whether that internal and external messaging aligns with their own attitudes and beliefs. I’m curious about how they measure perfectionism too—by whose standards, and whether those standards are realistic and align with their own. Ultimately, I help adolescents make sense of what perfectionism means to them, so they can be their own version of “best self” and curate their own “best life” and be content with their own version of perfect.
