Opinion: More doctors should prescribe exercise after a concussion

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

Two decades ago, a Vespa accident put me in a coma. By the time I came home from acute rehab, I had lost 25 pounds, most of it muscle, and my left side did not work as it should. After months of hospital sponge baths, soaking in a warm bath until my fingers wrinkled was blissful — and then it was time to get out.

I couldn’t. At 40, I did not have the upper body strength to lift myself from the tub; I had to shout for my wife to help. The humiliation was clarifying. I needed a way back, and what I found was exercise.

It began gently, with an Ashtanga yoga instructor who came to the house a few times a week. At first even the warrior pose was a struggle, but week after week, the two sides of my body relearned how to work together, and before long, I could get out of the bathtub on my own.

Later, I traded downhill skiing for skate skiing, the technique biathletes use. With my lingering balance problems, I fell a dozen times or more in every lesson. But once I got the hang of it, the sport was exhilarating and punishing: thighs burning, heart rate high for an hour or more on the trails. One day I told my instructor, Jack Bellorado, a rugged former wilderness guide, that I’d had a great ski and only fallen three times. He looked at me and said, “You must not have been pushing yourself hard enough.”

Nearly everyone caring for me after the accident had been trying, sensibly, to keep me safe. Jack expected something hard of me. I needed both, and medical care for the millions of Americans who suffer challenging concussion symptoms months after their injury is still much better at the first than the second.

For years, the standard advice after a concussion was to rest in a dark room until symptoms passed. Newer research is moving away from that recommendation. In a 2019 randomized trial led by John Leddy at the University at Buffalo, 103 adolescents with sport-related concussions were divided into two groups. One was prescribed aerobic exercise and recovered in a median of 13 days, compared with 17 for those in the other group, who only stretched. In a small 2024 trial, people who had lived with moderate to severe brain injuries for six months or more walked farther and faster after a block of high-intensity training than after conventional rehabilitation. This small but important study needs to be replicated in larger trials and include people with concussions.

Medicine has learned of unhealthy overprotectiveness before. In the early 1950s, heart attack patients were kept flat in bed for weeks, because exertion was thought dangerous. Cardiologists Samuel Levine and Bernard Lown questioned whether prolonged bed rest was helping patients or harming them, and, over strong opposition, got them sitting up in armchairs. Their 1952 paper in JAMA helped start a long shift. Today, supervised exercise is a standard, reimbursed part of recovery after a heart attack. And cardiac care was not alone: over the past few decades, surgeons have moved from keeping patients flat for days to walking them within hours.

Brain injury has been slower to make this turn, in part because Hippocratic caution can turn into overprotection. To many medical professionals, it may seem risky to prescribe exercise. For patients, the injured organ is the one you think with, so trying to do as little as possible can seem wise. Exertion can briefly worsen headache, dizziness, and fog, which can feel like movement is doing harm. There is a grain of truth in the caution: The brain does much of its repair during sleep, so sleeping has a central place in recovery. But adequate sleep and daytime stillness are not the same thing, and the lesson from most other fields is that movement and rest are partners, not rivals.

Exercise may help the injured brain in three ways. The first is better sleep. Sleep problems are among the most common and stubborn complaints after brain injury, and exercise is associated with improved sleep. The second is that exercise alleviates depression, another common complication of TBI. The third is more speculative. Working muscles release lactate, far more of it during hard effort, and lactate can cross into the brain, where studies, mostly in animals, suggest it may help trigger production of brain-derived neurotrophic factor, or BDNF, often called fertilizer for the brain. If sleep is when the brain does most of its repair work, a day’s exertion might help twice over: a better night’s rest, and more for the brain to work with during sleep. However likely, that remains unproven but worth testing.

Today I work with the TBI Post-Acute Care Clinic Collaborative. In conversations with clinicians, I repeatedly hear that exercise is rarely encouraged in brain-injury care and even less often formally prescribed. Patients may leave appointments with medications, referrals, and advice to stay active, but without an exercise prescription.

The evidence has limits. Results from adolescent athletes with concussion may not hold for adults with more severe injuries like mine, and such studies are scarce. Some people’s symptoms worsen with exertion. That is why care must be individualized, so patients don’t have to guess. Patients who want to use exercise to improve their recovery should have help determining what they can safely do: an assessment, a written plan with an intensity set for them, a path for progressing it, follow-up to adjust it, and supervision when they need it. Doctors can put this evidence into practice now, and insurers should cover clinically appropriate programs, as they do for cardiac or stroke rehabilitation.

In 1972, a pastor named Robert Perske wrote about tension in appropriate risk-taking. Perske, who led an association for people with intellectual disabilities in Omaha, Neb., was convinced that well-meaning overprotection was smothering the people it was meant to help. Decades before the rise of helicopter parenting, he wrote that “there can be such a thing as human dignity in risk” and “there can be a dehumanizing indignity in safety!” What people needed, he argued, was a “fair and prudent share of risk.”

Perske’s point was not that risk is good for its own sake. It was that a life arranged entirely around safety becomes a smaller life. I got out of that bathtub, and eventually onto those skinny skis, because people took care of me and pushed me. More patients recovering from brain injury should leave their appointments with an exercise prescription, with guardrails suited to their condition. The effort is theirs to make. The plan, and the support in following it, are ours to give.

Scott W. Hamilton is founder of the Brain Care Catalyst Foundation.
