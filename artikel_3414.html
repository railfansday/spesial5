5 Essential Classic Books for Beginners

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

At any given moment, Scott Simone has at least three books jockeying for his attention. He’ll never turn down a campus novel with a literary bent, nor will he hesitate to add yet another mystery to his Leaning Tower of Pisa-inspired to-be-read pile — which is built upon the bedrocks of too many short story collections, how-are-these-real true crime books, and classics waiting to be cracked open again. Topics that have him ripping through pages the fastest include closed-circle murders, unhappy professors, life-questioning philosophical deep dives, so long as they feature sharp sentences, a dark sense of humor, and at least a little malice.

He’s spent so much time in book shops over the years he is, to his wife’s chagrin, continuing to build a library that doesn’t quite respect the dimensions of the apartment meant to contain it.

If you don't want someone to read a book, tell them it’s a classic. Tell them that it changed literature. Tell them how generations of writers have tried and failed to reproduce it. Tell them no bookshelf is complete without it. Now you’ve made reading a chore. It’s homework. We seem more interested in what these books represent than what's actually between their covers. But none of that tells you whether you'll enjoy spending an afternoon with one.

And God help you if you don't enjoy one. Give up on a modern novel halfway through, and it's simply not for you. Put down a classic, and feel the judgment pour. Somebody will come along to explain what you did wrong. Perhaps you didn't understand the historical context. Maybe you missed the symbolism. Give it another go in 10 years, when you're older and a little wiser. Sometimes that's fair. But we've developed a habit of treating difficulty as a mark of greatness, as though the more you struggle through a book, the more worthwhile the experience. And that's a shame. These were novels — funny, exciting, full of people behaving appallingly — long before they became monuments to literature. And perhaps the best way to appreciate their greatness is to remember what made them worth reading in the first place.

If you think 19th-century literature is a slog, Jane Austen would like a word. And in all likelihood, it'll be a funny one. Because Austen knows how to bring the humor, especially when she's eviscerating the types of people who tend to make fools of themselves. Pride and Prejudice rotates a cast of such characters right into her firing line. There's Mrs. Bennet, who's basically willing to marry off her five daughters to whichever eligible bachelors wander into the neighborhood. There's Mr. Collins, a man so impressed with himself he has no doubt everyone else must be, too. And then there's Elizabeth Bennet, who's clever enough to recognize everyone else's shortcomings, but isn't quite so perceptive when it comes to her own. The best part: You don't need to know much about Regency England to appreciate any of this. Snobs, bores, and people who think far too highly of themselves were plentiful two centuries ago, and Austen brings them hilariously to life.

The romance is familiar enough, too. Elizabeth and the wealthy-but-insufferable Mr. Darcy spend an awful lot of time misunderstanding one another, taking offense at perfectly innocent remarks, and refusing to admit when they've been wrong. It's not so far removed from a modern romantic comedy, though there's more at stake than a few bruised egos. Money and social standing dictate much of what these people can and can't do, especially when it comes to marriage. For Elizabeth and her sisters, finding a suitable husband is as much a matter of financial security as it is romance. Yes, the language does take some getting used to, and polite society and all its rules can seem ridiculous. But Austen thought plenty of them were ridiculous, too, and she's more than happy to have a little fun at their expense.

Not every literary classic takes a fortnight to actually get going. Case in point: Around the World in Eighty Days. Jules Verne takes a simple premise, launches out of the gates with it, and never really slows his pace. Which can't exactly be said for Phileas Fogg, an English gentleman who seems a little too obsessed with punctuality. Yet he wagers £20,000 that he can travel around the globe in 80 days. This being 1872 and all, a time when travel involved nothing more than a few steamships and trains, it's an outrageous proposition. But that's all the setup Verne needs. There's no sprawling cast to keep track of and no complicated backstory to untangle. There's simply a man and a deadline — and a ridiculous number of obstacles standing between the two.

Fogg, mind you, is hardly the sort of man you'd expect to go gallivanting around the world. He's a creature of habit, so wedded to his daily routine that you'd think the slightest disruption would send him into a fit. However, Verne seems determined to test just how much the man can take. A train is missed. A connection falls through. An itinerary becomes useless. Somehow, Fogg takes it all in stride, with the composure of a man who's been told his dinner reservation has been pushed back 15 minutes. His French valet, Passepartout, isn't so accommodating, and watching him fret while his employer barely bats an eye is half the fun. And Verne makes us fret along with him. A delayed departure becomes genuinely suspenseful. Some of the novel's attitudes toward the people and places encountered along the way are decidedly of their time; but Verne's talent for turning a simple journey into one calamity after another hasn't aged much at all.

Not every difficult subject makes for a difficult book. Leo Tolstoy takes on death and regret. He delves into the despair of a life wasted in The Death of Ivan Ilyich, and he manages it in about 100 pages. The writing is surprisingly straightforward, the cast is small, and there's hardly any plot to untangle. We learn that Ivan, a Russian judge, has died before we've even had a chance to meet the man. So there's no great mystery about where this is headed. What concerns Tolstoy is how Ivan got there, and why a man who's done just about everything expected of him finds himself so unprepared for the end. It's heavy stuff, sure. But you don't need a philosophy degree or much knowledge of 19th-century Russia to understand what Tolstoy's getting at.

Tolstoy has a wicked sense of humor, which helps. Ivan's colleagues hear that he's died and immediately start calculating what it might mean for their careers. His funeral is treated as an inconvenience, something to get through before going about life. Ivan himself hasn't exactly spent his years pursuing a life of great meaning. He's been too busy climbing the social ladder and worrying about what people might think of him. Then he gets sick, and suddenly none of it seems worth much. Worse still, the people around him would sooner carry on pretending everything is fine than acknowledge what's happening to him. Tolstoy has plenty of fun exposing all this vanity and self-importance.

Toni Collette and Kit Harington are shooting a new Miss Marple and 20th Century has several more Christies in development, so the drawing room is busier than it has been in decades. Here is which of them you are, and the case for yours being the best.

Order, method, and a quiet confidence that you are the cleverest person present. Poirot is a retired Belgian police officer who solves murders by sitting still and thinking, and who is vain enough to make the reveal a performance every single time.

People tell you things because they cannot imagine you being a threat. Marple solves murders by comparing everybody to somebody she once knew in St Mary Mead, which sounds like a joke until you notice it works every time, because human beings are not as original as they think.

You have far too many ideas and roughly a fifth of them are excellent. Ariadne Oliver is a successful crime novelist who is sick of her famous foreign detective and keeps saying so, which is Christie writing about Christie and getting away with it completely.

You are steady, decent and the reason the difficult genius has anybody at all. Hastings narrates the early Poirot novels, misses most of the clues, and is the only character in the books that Poirot is unambiguously fond of, which tells you what he is really for.

You can spend eons trying to decipher Franz Kafka. Literary scholars have been at it for more than a century. But The Metamorphosis doesn't require much deciphering to enjoy. Kafka gives us the whole ridiculous premise in his opening sentence: Gregor Samsa, a traveling salesman, wakes up as an enormous insect. Gregor's reaction is almost as absurd as the transformation itself. He's worried about being late for work. He's missed his train, his boss is going to be furious, and his family needs the money. The man's no longer human, and he's fretting about how he'll explain his absence to his employer. It's horribly funny, especially because the thinking behind it isn't all that difficult to recognize.

Kafka's joke gets considerably darker as the story goes on. Gregor has spent years working to support his parents and sister, putting their needs ahead of his own. Now that he's the one who needs looking after, the family isn't exactly enthusiastic to act in kind. His sister initially takes pity on him. His father can barely stand the sight of him. Eventually, everyone gets a bit sick and tired of having an enormous insect in their house. All the while, Gregor seems more concerned about becoming a burden than he does about his own misery. It's an awful arrangement, but one that doesn't take much imagination to understand.

Nella Larsen proves you don't need a murder or mayhem for a gripping psychological thriller. She managed to do so with an old friendship and a few uncomfortable conversations. Passing is barely 150 pages, but Larsen packs enough suspicion and intrigue to fill hundreds more. It centers on two Black women, Irene Redfield and Clare Kendry, childhood friends who've taken very different paths in life. Clare passes as white, even marrying a wealthy white man who’d never guess she's Black. Odd, considering he's so viciously racist. On the other hand, Irene has built a comfortable life in Harlem, and she knows just how badly Clare's deception could end. But when the two reconnect, she can't bring herself to stay away.

There's plenty to worry about, but it's the conversations between Irene and Clare that make the novel so fraught. Larsen lets Irene do much of the interpreting for us, and we fall into the habit of taking her word for it. Clare is reckless. Clare craves attention. Clare is putting everyone at risk. And perhaps she's right about all of it. But the more we learn about Irene, the harder it becomes to separate genuine concern from jealousy and resentment. Larsen is great at making us suspicious of someone without giving us much evidence against them. So much so that an ordinary conversation can seem loaded with meaning, a perfectly innocent remark like a veiled threat. And so we find ourselves scrutinizing every little exchange, wondering who's being truthful and who's got something to hide.
