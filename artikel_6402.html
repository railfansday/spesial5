A tiny ocean animal called the leaf sheep figured out how to make its own food from sunlight

The leaf sheep is a sea slug that looks like a cartoon lamb standing in its own pasture. Oddly enough, the pasture is the strange part.

The leaf-shaped growths on its back are green because the slug keeps chloroplasts, which are the sunlight-capturing parts of the algae it eats. Those chloroplasts go on working inside the leaf sheep for days or longer.

Animals eat food for energy, and plants make their own food from sunlight. A few sea slugs do both, and the best-studied one is a bigger cousin of the leaf sheep, Elysia chlorotica, an emerald green slug from the Massachusetts coast.

In 2008, researchers found one clue to how it manages that, in its own DNA: a working copy of a gene taken from the algae it eats.

The leaf sheep’s scientific name is Costasiella kuroshimae. It is about the size of a grain of rice, according to the World Wildlife Fund, and lives in shallow tropical water around Japan, Indonesia, and the Philippines.

Two ear-shaped feelers above its eyes sense chemicals in the water, and it grazes on an alga called Avrainvillea on shallow reefs and in seagrass beds. The species was first described in 1993.

Mary Rumpho at the University of Maine led the work on the cousin with James Manhart of Texas A&M University. Their team went looking in the slug’s genes.

A chloroplast carries only a small part of its own instructions. Of the 1,000 to 5,000 proteins a chloroplast needs, its own DNA codes for fewer than 10%. The rest come from genes in the algal cell’s nucleus.

So a slug that keeps the chloroplast and loses the rest of the algal cell should end up with chloroplasts that can’t maintain themselves.

E. chlorotica keeps them anyway. A gene passing between an alga and an animal is extremely rare, or at least not well documented, and Rumpho’s team set out to find one.

The slug hatches from eggs that carry no chloroplasts, and its larvae swim free. They won’t change into juveniles without one alga, Vaucheria litorea, which grows in long threads, and the juveniles must feed on it to keep developing.

Young slugs are grayish brown. As they feed, they keep chloroplasts only in the cells lining their branched gut, and as that gut spreads, the whole animal turns green.

The chloroplasts never divide inside the slug, which lives about 10 months. In earlier work, Rumpho’s group had measured oxygen coming off slugs kept from algae for at least five months. The chloroplasts were still photosynthesizing.

The team started with the alga. They read the complete DNA of its chloroplast and found that one gene was missing. It was a gene called psbO.

That gene makes a protein that the photosynthesis process requires in order to split water and release oxygen. Sunlight wears that protein out, so a chloroplast has to keep making more of it.

The algae plant keeps this gene in its main control center, not in the chloroplast. Until now, scientists had never seen an animal naturally possess this gene.

So if the slug’s chloroplasts were still releasing oxygen five months after their last meal of algae, the protein had to be coming from somewhere. The team looked in the slug.

They found the gene there, matching the algal version exactly, and it was switched on.

A leftover bit of algae in the slug’s gut could explain that. So the team checked slug eggs, which carry no chloroplasts and no algae. The eggs had the gene too.

That was the team’s evidence that the gene belongs to the slug itself and gets passed on to its young.

Rumpho’s team also sequenced the slug’s mitochondrial DNA, the small, separate genome that animals carry in their cells. The gene wasn’t there, which left the slug’s nuclear DNA as the likely location.

How did the gene get there? The authors suggested a route. When the slug feeds, algal nuclei come into direct contact with the cells lining its gut.

If a nucleus breaks open there, pieces of algal DNA could be taken up by the slug, or carried across by a virus.

Other algal genes have turned up in the slug too. A year earlier, another group had reported three algal genes in E. chlorotica that code for proteins that help collect light, matching the alga’s almost exactly.

The authors also wrote that the slug adds weight to an idea from biologist Lynn Margulis. New kinds of life, she proposed, can arise when one organism takes in parts and genes from another. They called slugs like this “green animals.”

The team did not find where in the slug’s chromosomes the gene is, what DNA surrounds it, or what switched it on once it arrived. They also did not learn how the protein gets from the slug’s cell into the chloroplast.

The authors wrote that many more algal genes have very likely been transferred to the slug. Reading its whole genome is what it will take to settle where they are.

The study did not ask any of this about the leaf sheep. The next question is whether Costasiella carries an algal gene of its own, and its genome is the place scientists should look next.

The full study was published in the journal PNAS.
