Bogong moths have a second compass for nights when the stars disappear

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

Bogong moths use the tilt of Earth’s magnetic field to navigate at night, and their magnetic compass depends on violet-blue light.

Bogong moths use a magnetic compass to steer through the Australian night, much like migrating songbirds.

New experiments conducted by Lund University show that the moths read the tilt of Earth’s magnetic field and need violet-blue light to do so.

The research team included biologists Hui Chen, Eric Warrant, and Pauline Fleischmann.

“It is remarkable that two such different groups of animals use the same basic principle to detect Earth’s magnetic field. This suggests that very ancient biophysical mechanisms have been conserved through evolution,” said Warrant, a professor of zoology at Lund.

A Bogong moth looks unremarkable: greyish-brown wings and a brain about one-tenth the size of a grain of rice. Yet each spring, these insects set off for mountain refuges they have never visited.

Their journeys can stretch up to 1,000 kilometers. They spend the summer dormant in cool caves in the Australian Alps, then return to their breeding grounds in autumn.

The same moths make both trips, reproduce, and die. The next generation must find its own way.

Research published in 2025 showed that the moths use the stars as a compass. But they can maintain their course when clouds hide the sky, suggesting another guide is available.

To investigate, researchers caught migrating moths with light traps and brought them to a laboratory near Adaminaby, in southeastern Australia.

The building was constructed from non-magnetic materials to avoid disturbing the weak field the insects were being asked to follow.

Inside, tethered moths could fly and turn freely in an arena surrounded by magnetic coils. The apparatus recorded their headings during five-minute flights while the coils controlled the magnetic field.

Overhead, a projected sky contained randomly arranged stars. It supplied light without the familiar celestial patterns that could otherwise tell the moths which way to go.

With the normal field present, spring migrants headed roughly southwest and autumn migrants roughly north-northeast.

Rotating the field horizontally by 180 degrees sent both groups roughly the opposite way.

When the magnetic field was removed, the moths lost their shared heading.

The crucial detail was the field’s tilt relative to the ground, known as magnetic inclination.

Flipping this tilt sent the moths the other way. Reversing the field’s polarity while leaving its axis unchanged did not.

This kind of compass distinguishes poleward from equatorward travel, roughly south and north in Australia.

A conventional compass needle responds to polarity. Some insects do too, but Bogong moths followed a different rule.

“The Bogong moth’s magnetic compass works differently from, for example, the polarity-sensitive magnetic sense of desert ants,” said Fleischmann, a biologist at Carl von Ossietzky University Oldenburg.

“This suggests that there are probably several types of magnetoreception, with migratory and non-migratory animals using the magnetic field in different ways.”

The magnetic field was only part of the story. When researchers removed violet-blue wavelengths from the illumination, the moths no longer maintained their migratory heading.

Simply making the surroundings dimmer did not have the same effect. In outdoor tests, moths still oriented correctly with the full spectrum reduced to about one-eighth of its original brightness.

The team also checked whether removing those colors had prevented the insects from seeing well enough to fly.

It hadn’t. Given a realistic star pattern and no magnetic field, the moths could still navigate without the shorter wavelengths.

The problem was specific to their magnetic guidance. Their star compass remained usable.

Weak radiofrequency noise also disrupted the moths’ magnetic orientation. Together with the light experiments, that result points towards a proposed mechanism called the radical-pair mechanism.

In this explanation, light starts chemical reactions whose outcomes are influenced by the surrounding magnetic field. Those changes could give an animal information about direction.

Light-sensitive proteins called cryptochromes are possible candidates for the process. The experiments do not identify which protein provides the moth’s magnetic sense.

Pinning that down will require further work. For now, the results support a mechanism resembling the one proposed for migrating songbirds.

“What we see here is a navigational ability reminiscent of that found in migratory birds. Although insects and birds have evolved along separate paths for around 700 million years, they appear to be able to use similar biological solutions,” said Chen, a biology researcher at Lund.

Having two compasses could help the moths keep going when one source of information becomes unreliable.

Clouds can conceal the stars, while disturbances in Earth’s magnetic field could interfere with magnetic guidance.

The experiments establish how moths choose a direction. Reaching a particular cave probably also involves local sensory clues encountered along the route.

How the brain combines those clues with its two compasses remains an open question.

“The next step is to understand in greater detail how this mechanism works and how it interacts with other senses when animals navigate,” Warrant concluded.

The study is published in the journal Nature.
