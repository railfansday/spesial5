Cougars battle deep snow and freezing weather every winter, but nature is not what kills most of them

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

People, not weather, caused most deaths among tracked cougars. The hunting season, roads, and human houses each play a role.

You might expect deep snow and bitter cold to be the biggest threat to a wild animal in winter. Yet when researchers tracked cougars across five winters in the foothills of Alberta’s Rocky Mountains, they found the weather was not what killed them. People were.

Of 80 cougars fitted with tracking collars, 37 died, and people caused 29 of those deaths. Nearly all of them came between November and March, and Alberta’s cougar hunting season falls inside those months.

Where a cougar spent its time mattered too. Cats that stayed close to houses and unpaved roads died at higher rates than cats that kept away from them.

Carter Littlefair and Andrew Derocher of the University of Alberta’s Department of Biological Sciences led the study. Their co-authors, Paul Frame and Mark Edwards, work at Alberta Environment and Protected Areas, the province’s environment ministry.

Between 2016 and 2021, the team used hounds to tree cougars in the country south of Whitecourt and north of Sundre. They then darted each cat and fitted a GPS collar. The collars reported a location every seven hours.

The 80 cats were followed for a little over a year each, on average. When a collar signaled a death, crew reached the site within three days to work out what had happened.

Hunters killed 16 of the 37 that died. Another eight died in traps set for other animals, and two were hit by vehicles. Three more were killed as problem animals or by landowners with permission.

Wolves killed three adult females, two of them with kittens still depending on them. Seven deaths in all were natural, and one could not be explained. The cats share this country with wolves, black bears, and grizzlies, and their main prey is white-tailed deer.

In an average year, a collared cougar had a 60 percent chance of living through the next 12 months. That figure hides a split between the seasons.

From April through October, 96 percent of the cats survived. From November through March, only 62 percent did.

Cougar hunting in the study area opens on December 1 and closes at the end of February, or earlier once an area’s quota is filled. Hunters there use hounds to follow a track and tree the cat. Of the 37 cougars that died, 33 died in the cold months.

Since 2019, the province has set each area’s hunting quota at roughly 14 to 16 percent of the cougars it estimates live there.

In an interview with Earth.com, Littlefair said that lowering harvest limits would have the greatest direct effect on survival, since hunting caused the majority of the deaths the team documented.

Alberta’s system lets each management area’s limits be adjusted every year, separately for males and females, and the findings can help inform how they are set, Littlefair said.

The team then asked which parts of the landscape went with dying. Rugged terrain and forest edges, which the team expected to make a cat easier to spot or reach, made no difference to survival.

What mattered was access. A cougar within about 490 feet (150 meters) of a house faced a much higher risk of death than one farther away. So did a cougar within about 165 feet (50 meters) of an unpaved road.

The authors suggested a reason: a hound hunt begins with a track rather than a sighting, and hunters find those tracks along roads and cutlines before the chase starts.

“Mortality risk increased with proximity to residences and secondary roads, indicating that cougars occupying accessible areas were more vulnerable to harvest,” they wrote.

Cats that crossed cutlines, pipelines, power lines, and seismic lines more often as they traveled were also more likely to die. Logging and oil and gas work have left this part of Alberta with a dense network of unpaved roads.

Littlefair said that reducing the density of roads and other human-made disturbances across the landscape could also help lower that risk.

Familiar ground did not seem to protect them either. Of 15 cougars whose previous year’s range could be mapped, 12 died inside it.

Alberta’s rules are built to steer hunters toward males. Quotas allow more males than females, and a female with kittens is off limits. Hunters using hounds also tend to select large males.

So the team expected males to die more frequently, as they do in hunted populations elsewhere. They didn’t find that and Littlefair told Earth.com it was one of the more surprising results.

Other studies with similarly female-heavy samples have still found hunters taking more males. In this study, however, hunters killed nine females and seven males, and the team could not detect any difference in survival between the sexes.

Hunting did make up a bigger share of male deaths, with 64 percent in comparison to 35 percent for females. But with only 24 males in the study, that difference could have come up by chance.

Mothers were at no extra risk the team could detect: it found 18 litters, and females with kittens survived at the same rate as females without them.

Hunting’s 43 percent share of the deaths is smaller than the 62 to 75 percent reported in some other hunted cougar populations. Provincial records also show human-caused cougar deaths have fallen since a peak in 2015.

“One important distinction is that high mortality does not necessarily indicate an unsustainable population,” Littlefair said.

Judging that would require counts that the study did not make, of the cougars there, their kittens, and the young that reach adulthood.

The study did not measure how many hunters were out or how hard they hunted, so it can’t separate a road from the people driving on it.

The authors treat a cat’s use of roads and houses as a stand-in for its chances of meeting a hunter, not as a danger in itself.

A second gap is the number of males. With 56 females and 24 males collared, the study can say less about males.

“It would certainly be interesting to repeat the study with a more balanced sample of males and females to see whether this pattern persists,” Littlefair said.

For now, the finding covers one stretch of Alberta, where a cougar’s odds of surviving the winter depend on how close it lives to a house or an unpaved road.

Littlefair and his team believe that managers should weigh that pattern of access when they judge how much hunting a population can absorb, and where it falls.

The full study was published in the journal PLOS One.
