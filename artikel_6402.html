Intensive breathing exercises can create psychedelic ‘tripping’ effects by changing how the brain talks to itself

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

Intensive breathwork cut brain blood flow by about 45 percent in MRI scans, yet the depth of the altered state tracked brain networks, not the drop.

People who practice intensive breathwork (fast, nonstop breathing) for an hour describe something close to a psychedelic trip. They recount vivid mental imagery, a sense of unity with the universe, and no drug involved.

Inside an MRI scanner, the brain lost nearly half its usual blood supply during that experience. Yet how far the blood flow fell had nothing to do with how deep the experience went.

Christian Schmitz led the work at the Central Institute of Mental Health in Mannheim, Germany. His colleagues came from the Medical Faculty Mannheim of Heidelberg University and the MIND Foundation in Berlin.

His team scanned the brains of 30 experienced breathwork practitioners during a single session, with no comparison group, so the findings are preliminary.

“Many people who practice intensive breathwork report profound changes in perception and self-experience, yet the underlying brain mechanisms remain poorly understood,” Schmitz said.

“Our study suggests that these experiences are linked not only to marked changes in cerebral blood flow, but also to alterations in how key brain networks communicate.”

Breathing hard and fast for a long time removes carbon dioxide from the blood faster than the body makes it.

Carbon dioxide is one of the main signals that keeps the brain’s small blood vessels open. When it drops, those vessels tighten and less blood gets through.

The team measured blood flow through the brain’s grey matter at the moment each person rated the experience strongest.

At this point, blood flow had fallen from about 30 to about 16 milliliters per 100 grams of tissue per minute. That is a drop of about 45 percent, which is a large change for something a person does on purpose.

Earlier studies have reported signs of the brain not getting enough oxygen when flow falls by 50 to 60 percent. The average here came close to that limit and stayed below it.

A 2025 study of the same kind of intensive breathwork measured a drop of 41.6 percent, and Schmitz’s team wrote that the two results agree.

The 30 volunteers were between 26 and 64 years old, and half were women. Most had practiced intensive breathwork for one to three years, typically less than once a month.

Anyone with a history of a psychiatric or neurological disorder was left out, and so was anyone who had taken a psychedelic in the previous three months.

Each person lay in the scanner and breathed in a continuous circle, with no pause between breaths, for as long as they chose. That ran from about 36 to 96 minutes. Music played the whole time, either their own or the study’s.

Every 12 minutes, the room light came on and the person pressed one of four buttons to rate the experience against the strongest breathwork session of their life.

Medical staff supervised every session, and monitoring continued for at least an hour afterward.

The experiences were strong: on the four-point scale, the average rating at the peak was 3.5, and the most common answer was the top mark.

Within an hour of the session’s end, each person filled in a standard questionnaire on altered states. The profile that came back was dominated by a sense of boundlessness and a drop in alertness, with milder visual changes.

Psychedelics produce a closely similar profile, the authors wrote. The scores were lower, though, than people typically report after a moderate to high dose of LSD or psilocybin.

A person whose blood flow fell the most was no more likely to report an intense experience than a person whose flow fell the least. The size of the drop predicted neither the button ratings during the session nor the questionnaire scores afterward.

One reading, the authors wrote, is that the drop works like a threshold. Once blood flow falls far enough, altered states become possible, and falling further doesn’t deepen them.

The team also looked at the default mode network, a set of brain regions that are active together when a person is resting and thinking about themselves.

Psilocybin and LSD reliably weaken the coordination inside that network, and the team expected intensive breathwork to do the same.

Resting scans before and after the session showed no overall change in how tightly those regions worked together. But the people whose network connections weakened the most reported the most intense altered states.

The team found that link under one way of cleaning the scan data and not under another, so they called it tentative. The drop in blood flow didn’t predict the network change either.

So the authors described two partly separate things: a brain getting far less blood and, in some people, a reorganizing network that goes with the experience itself.

The team presented the work at the ECNP Congress in Munich in October.

Everyone in the study was an experienced practitioner, so how a first-timer’s brain responds is an open question.

The network scans were taken after the breathing stopped, not during it, because the chest and head movement of circular breathing ruins that kind of scan. Any change that faded quickly was missed.

The team didn’t measure carbon dioxide in the breath, and there was no comparison session of plain rest. Music played throughout, which the authors wrote can change the same network on its own.

Hana Adolphi at the Central Institute of Mental Health co-coordinated the study and is a co-author on the publication. “Breathwork is a legal, inexpensive and non-pharmacological intervention that may provide a valuable tool for studying altered states of consciousness,” she said.

“Nevertheless, this was a relatively small study, and the findings should be considered preliminary. Larger studies will be needed to determine whether these brain changes have clinical relevance,” she added.

Whether the network change happens during the breathing itself, rather than after it, is the question this design couldn’t answer.

The authors wrote that future studies need scanning methods that can cope with the motion of hard breathing, and a plain resting session to compare against.

The full study was published in the journal Progress in Neuro-Psychopharmacology and Biological Psychiatry.
