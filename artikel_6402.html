Wildlife experts rescued over 90 venomous primates that were supposed to be extinct for nearly a century

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

The critically endangered Bangka slow loris has been rediscovered in Indonesia after decades without scientific records, but its habitat remains at risk.

The Bangka slow loris, a small venomous primate that had not appeared in the scientific literature since the 1930s, still lives on Bangka Island in Indonesia. The species is listed as Critically Endangered, and possibly extinct, on the IUCN Red List.

Between 2015 and 2025, the Alobi Wildlife Rescue Center on Bangka rescued more than 90 of the animals. It released 86 of them back into forests on the island.

Staff at the center published those records in October 2026, in a study with partners from zoos in Austria and Indonesia. The conservation group Re:wild announced the findings.

The Bangka slow loris, Nycticebus bancanus, was described in 1906 from Kelabat Bay on Bangka. For decades it was treated as a subspecies of other slow lorises, until a 2013 study raised it to a species of its own.

The IUCN Red List gives 1937 as the year the loris was last recorded, a date the authors trace to a 1937 publication describing three adult males. Museum material suggests those specimens may date from 1930 to 1932, so the last record could be older still.

Re:wild lists the Bangka slow loris among the most wanted primates in its search for lost species.

Benji Barca, the exploration program manager at Re:wild, credited the rescue center’s work.

“The team at Alobi has spent more than a decade quietly rescuing, healing and releasing these animals, and their careful records are what have finally now brought this primate back into scientific view,” Barca said.

“A species can disappear from the scientific record without disappearing from the lives of the people who share its home,” he noted.

The center kept detailed records for 43 lorises rescued from 2021 to August 2025. Sixteen were found in houses or gardens and six on roads, and at least three of the 43 died.

Seven had injuries suspected to come from electric shocks, and five came from captivity or the wildlife trade. Three turned up on plantations, one was caught in a fence and one was attacked by a dog.

Alobi rescued Bangka slow lorises in all four regencies of Bangka Island and in the city of Pangkal Pinang. That spread suggests the primate may still live across much of Bangka.

Timon Glaw of the Haus des Meeres Aqua Terra Zoo in Vienna, Austria, is the study’s corresponding author.

“We now know the species has survived in several parts of Bangka, and we urgently need a long-term conservation strategy built on population surveys, genetic studies and, above all, protection of its remaining habitat,” Glaw said.

“The next challenge is to make sure it does not actually become extinct,” he added.

The 86 released animals went to seven sites. Bukit Mangkol forest park received 37, Permisan nature park received 22 and the Mount Menumbing area received 15.

The four other sites took six, three, two and one animal. They include Gunung Maras National Park and the BIO protected forest.

How many of the released lorises survived over the long term is not yet known, because no follow-up data exist.

The authors call for future releases of rescued slow lorises to be monitored, as part of an in situ conservation program that protects suitable habitat.

Slow lorises are the only primates known to be venomous. They lick a gland on the inside of the upper arm and mix its secretion with saliva, producing a bite that can be painful and cause swelling.

The venom’s main role in nature seems to be defense against parasites and against other lorises, a 2013 review of slow loris venom concluded.

In extreme cases, a bite can send a person into anaphylactic shock, which has sometimes proved fatal.

In a preliminary test, leeches placed in contact with a mix of the gland oil and saliva all died.

Langka Sani, who leads the Alobi Wildlife Rescue Center and co-authored the study, points to habitat loss as the main danger.

“We are glad to contribute to the scientific record to show that the Bangka slow loris is not lost, but the species is still very close to extinction,” Sani explained.

“Its natural habitat on Bangka Island has shrunk significantly because of illegal tin mining and massive land clearing for oil palm plantations.”

Forest habitat on Bangka fell by 62 percent between 2000 and 2012, according to a 2017 analysis by Supriatna and colleagues cited in the paper.

Settlements, roads, power lines and the pet trade also threaten the loris, Re:wild noted. Five of the rescued animals had been kept in captivity or caught up in the wildlife trade, even though slow lorises are protected by law.

The authors also recommend a conservation breeding program, molecular studies to confirm its identity and targeted surveys on Bangka, Belitung and Borneo. They ask the IUCN to reassess the loris, especially its “Possibly Extinct” tag.

The study was published in the journal Spixiana.
