There Are No Small Roles for Penélope Cruz

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

The veteran star commands our attention in every film she appears in.

Penélope Cruz is lying down on an airport lounge sofa. She’s on her side, cheek against her hand on a striped pillow. Her hair swoops under her chin. She looks so cozy that, from this angle, her gray blazer could be a blanket. “Sorry that I’m like this…” she says softly. Her eyeglass lenses are flecked with the reflection of her screen. “I’m very jet-lagged because I was here for only a day and a half, and yesterday we shot for 16 hours.” By “here,” she means Los Angeles, and by “we,” she means the cast and crew of Nancy Meyers’s hotly awaited first feature-length film in over a decade, out in late 2027. The next day, Cruz will be on a red carpet in London celebrating La Bola Negra and Bunker, two of her four movies out this year. You can’t blame her for getting some rest while she can.

In 2026 alone, Cruz has appeared onscreen as a noir detective in Maggie Gyllenhaal’s Frankenstein reimagining, The Bride!; a sex therapist who hosts orgies and forces her neighbors to have hard conversations about marriage during an awkward dinner party in Olivia Wilde’s The Invite; a book editor in Bunker, whose husband takes on a questionable job for a billionaire (opposite her real-life spouse, Javier Bardem); and an entertainer for Spanish Civil War troops in La Bola Negra.

When the Spanish directing duo Los Javis (Javier Ambrossi and Javier Calvo) approached Cruz for the role of Nené in La Bola Negra, they worried she would turn it down because it wasn’t big enough for an actor of her caliber. The character only appears in a couple of scenes, but Cruz connected with the script and its bold portrayal of queer love across generations. “It’s incredible we still have to fight for rights that should be as normal as breathing,” she tells ELLE. She spent months preparing: She studied cuplé, a cabaret style of late 19th-century Spanish theater; practiced dance choreography; and saw a voice teacher to overcome her fear of singing onscreen. (She hasn’t done it since her role in Nine 17 years ago.) “I’m always scared of singing,” she tells me. “I think it’s the most vulnerable thing.” Her resulting performance as Nené—with 1930s skinny brows, a platinum wig, and flashy musical numbers—leaves a lasting impression. No role is too small for Cruz.

This is the woman who won an Academy Award for playing a fierce, unpredictable artist in Vicky Cristina Barcelona (making her the first and only Spanish actress to win an Oscar), but who also appears in Sex and the City 2 for literally one minute. She’s been a furiously grieving mother in Ferrari and the girl of Tom Cruise’s dreams in Vanilla Sky; she’s played many mothers (even a pregnant nun) in Pedro Almodóvar’s oeuvre, and Jack Sparrow’s ex in the Pirates of the Caribbean franchise. She’s in a unique position as one of the most revered actresses in Hollywood—with three additional Academy Award nominations under her belt, plus an Emmy nod for playing Donatella Versace—but she can just as easily slip into a jumpsuit to play a secret agent in Zoolander 2. “I’ve always felt invited to be a part of the industry, and I feel very grateful for that,” Cruz says. Some of her best-known roles might be the firecrackers and bombshells, but her sensitive, maternal, even silly characters are just as memorable. She can’t be put in a box. She proved that this year.

Cruz is still discerning with her roles, especially the ones opposite Bardem, whom she married in 2010. They have two teenagers, a son and a daughter. “We don’t want to do it too much, but once in a while it’s great,” she says of working together. “It’s just not something we want to do all the time. It would be too risky.” Bunker is the latest of their collaborations, which also include 1992’s Jamón Jamón, her first film and where they first met, and Vicky Cristina Barcelona. “We have 33 years of friendship and then a lot of years of relationship as a couple. Of course, a lot of things are the same, but you both have changed and grown together,” she says. “And that’s going to be present in the work…you can see and understand each other with one look.” Bunker’s director Florian Zeller charmed them, she says, and it helped that the shoot was in their home city, Madrid. After showing off her range this year, Cruz is already gearing up for a new kind of role: director. For the past four years, she’s been working on her first film, a documentary with a subject matter she isn’t yet ready to share. By helming the doc, which she expects to finish next year, she joins a small but growing number of female filmmakers, and she looks forward to a time when it’s no longer newsworthy. “It’s sad that it still has to be a conversation,” she says. But at the same time, “we cannot ignore it and look the other way and pretend like there is any kind of equality in those terms.”

As Cruz heads into the new year, there’s the Nancy Meyers film and other new characters on the horizon. But for now, in an airport lounge—now sitting upright, munching on snacks—Cruz is looking forward to being home with her family. And then getting back to work.

I feel it was through a lot of characters that I discovered in all the films that I watched, but especially all the films from [Pedro] Almodóvar. I would watch his films and feel like, See, I’m not strange. I’m not strange because I say I want to act. I’m not strange because in class I’m thinking about a million things…daydreaming and fantasizing. He made me feel normal. He made me feel that it was okay to be like that, but my parents did as well. They never judged me in those terms, because I think they’re artists in their hearts.

I think more and more younger people are going to the cinema, and I cannot lose hope. And with Netflix giving La Bola Negra a record amount of time in a theatrical release [47 days to play in theaters before streaming, the company’s longest window ever], I mean, everything helps. If one does it, the other ones will do it, and then more people will do it. And then [viewers] will have more options to go and see a film you want to see in a theater. And we won’t have to close them all, because that would be such a heartbreaking thing.

[It’s good] for young generations to have the opportunity to experience that, to experience what it is to go and see a film in a dark room full of strangers where you’re not looking at your phone, you don’t have interruptions, you are just there.

Salma [Hayek Pinault], because she’s one of my best friends. And she basically took me to her house when I arrived in L.A. for my first movie. I was going to a hotel alone. And she said, “I’m going to pick you up.” She didn’t let me go to the hotel. She took me to her house. It’s like family: Even if we don’t see each other all the time, we’re really, really close.

I had this vision: I just saw the scene in my head of me singing Sade to Seth. I arrived to the set and said, “Olivia, I think at that moment before anything happens, the way that we get to connect with each other is with me singing Sade to him, and she would know the lyrics and everything.” And she said, “Oh, let’s listen to it together.” And then I played her the song, which she already knew, and she completely understood what I was talking about, and we did it. And we just felt that it was working because it was really funny and absurd. We all brought things like that.

I don’t like giving advice, but if they press me, I always say the same thing. “You are a young person beginning in your career and you really want my honest advice? Stay away from drugs.”

The fact that when you have a writer-director who is a woman, it is commented on in the interview or is talked about as something extraordinary, something that is not just normal. You don’t say that about women being in other departments in film, but you get asked about it when it’s about your director or writer-director. So that is unfortunate, that it’s evolving at such a slow pace.

I love her as a director and as a woman. She’s the coolest and she’s brilliant. She has an amazing eye, [a] great brain. She has humor. Just look at what she did, the way she shot this movie all in one apartment, the way she edited, the way she had space for all of us. What she did in the editing room was protect every single one of us. With her being the lead in the movie. She protected us and also her character, but always [based on] what is the best for the film. She always moved from that place, honestly, of what is best for the film in each one of her decisions, and I really respect her for that. Right now she’s one of my favorite directors.

I’m sure there are benefits, for example, for medicine, for finding ways and tools to protect our planet [amid] climate change. For things like that, it can be incredible. But the problem is that at the speed it’s going, there’s not enough time to regulate things so it doesn’t become something that can become a real threat.

That’s where it gets so tricky: Who’s creating that balance? Who’s creating a boundary and the limits of what we all can handle? They try to sell the benefits of it, saying, “People will have all this free time on their hands.” That always freaks me out, because I’ve been happy in my life because I’ve had purpose. Imagine if young people a few years from now won’t have to get prepared for something, or dream about something and work hard to achieve their dreams, because they would be treated like robots in a cycle of: You wake up, you eat. But what do you do with your time? What is your purpose? What do you have to invest your time and your passion into?

I worked a lot since I was a teenager, especially in my twenties and my thirties. And then I knew if I had children, that was going to be my total priority, and I always wanted to raise them myself. I always felt that was the biggest reward from having worked so hard all those years: to have the opportunity to work less after I had them, make one movie per year, or two if they’re not too long, and do it in a way where I haven’t had to be away from them.

That has been the biggest blessing, because I’ve worked mainly in the summer, where we have traveled together. Or if it’s something short, like The Invite, it was during Easter and adding one week in the beginning, one week at the end. I’m very lucky that I’ve been able to work, but also that I put all those seeds there for that moment when I became a mother, not to have to separate [from my kids] more than very short trips.
