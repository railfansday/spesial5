Rob Riggle Saw ‘Nickels’ As a Way to Branch Out from Comedy

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Riggle and director Spencer King discuss the drama, about a man who comes to believe a neighbor may be the reincarnated spirit of his late brother.

For Rob Riggle, Nickels was a chance to show off another color in his performing palette. The actor is known for his comedy chops, with scene stealing work in the Jump Street movies and Step Brothers, but in the new film, he plays a man who has long grieved the death of his brother in a motorcycle crash decades earlier. After a college-aged neighbor Mitch (Aaron Holliday) has his own motorcycle accident, Riggle’s character, Tom Monroe, starts to wonder if this young man has become a reincarnation of his brother. There are similiarities too eery to miss, though the audience could interpret it a number of ways, including perhaps the neighbor is misleaidng Tom. The film took shape thanks to another Tom — Briarcliff boss Tom Ortenberg, who got ahold of the script from Tim Tuchrello and approached director Spencer King, who’d grown up with Ordenberg’s son. From there, Ortenberg suggested Riggle for the lead, and the actor signed on after a Zoom with King, who beamed in from a truck stop in Utah, where he was working on his previous movie, The Wilderness.

Over a quick 19 days, King and Riggle filmed the movie around Los Angeles, with Odessa A’zion playing Riggle’s onscreen daughter and Elizabeth Tulloch also starring as his wife. After a theatrical release last month, the film is available on PVOD via Briarcliff.

Rob, you’ve been on a lot of sets. Was there anything unusual about this experience?

ROB RIGGLE When you shoot a film that’s on a tight on schedule, there’s just no time to screw around. You’ve got to focus. And that’s why having a director that has a vision and knows what he or she wants to grab is a must-have before you can move to the next setup. Spencer brings that level of confidence to the set.

SPENCER KING It’s also why you need a lead actor who is an ex-Marine. Keep everyone in line. (Laughs.)

As a guy with a beard, I appreciated yours here, Rob. Whose choice was that?

RIGGLE I think it was my girlfriend’s idea. And then I got it approved by Spencer. That also just cuts down on makeup time a lot.

There’s a potential supernatural element here. Spencer, did you have a dial in your head of how far you wanted to take it?

KING For me, it’s a film where you can kind of bring your own faith into it. You can bring your own perspectives on the world and your own spirituality. The film isn’t trying to convince you of thinking any differently. For me, the interesting tidbit was not, is he the brother or is he not? It’s, does he believe he’s the brother? Or does Mitch believe he’s Tom’s brother? Or is he messing him?

Rob, you got to shoot this at home in L.A. How often have you shot in L.A. over the last decade?

RIGGLE It probably has been over a decade. I’ve shot some television here, but as far as a feature film, it might have been the Catalina wine mixer [from 2008’s Step Brothers]. It’d been awhile. You can’t put a price tag on being able to go sleep in your own bed every night. It’s a really special deal. So I consider myself incredibly lucky that we got to do that here.

Anytime you make a movie on this scale, you’ve got to call in favors. What were some of those you called in to make this owrk?

KING Equipment-wise, we called in every favor. Luke Dyra, our DP, called in all the favors and we pulled a lot of post favors.

RIGGLE Producers were also doing a lot of outreach. They were working the phones all the time. They were like, “All right, we got this location. All right, we got this motorcycle. Oh, we got this spot.” They were cooking with gas the whole time.

KING For me, this was, this is one of my favorite types of movies. It’s something Rob and I talked about in the production of it. A lot of my favorite movies are when more comedic actors go serious. Eternal Sunshine is one of my favorite movies. Adam Sandler’s done it a handful of times, Will Ferrell and Stranger Than Fiction, and I really thought this was one of those. This has a chance to be that for Rob in a lot of ways. Not that Rob hasn’t done it, but it’s such a different look. I think that sometimes comedians are building up to a punchline. So there’s a natural sequence for working up to a crescendo of sorts. What I felt on set and in the edit was Rob naturally was able to pinpoint the inflection point of the scene and in some sort of subtle way be able to deliver that scene’s “punchline.”

RIGGLE Well, you’re very kind to say that. I think comedic actors, myself included, the reason we get to keep doing comedies is because hopefully we do them well. But it’s like a palette of colors. I’ve got a lot of colors on the palette. Everybody’s seen red. I can do that, but I got other colors. I can do green, I can do yellow, I can do blue, whatever you want. I think a lot of actors, especially comedy actors, don’t get the opportunities. So that’s another reason I was so grateful to be part of this.
