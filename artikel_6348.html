AI training helped some women more job interviews, but not as many as their rivals got

Tanggal: 2026-11-11

In a hiring test, AI training lifted interview calls for underrepresented women from 11 to 14 percent, while others got 25 percent without it.

Learning a popular skill like AI is supposed to help you get a job. But a new study found that for women who already get fewer interviews, having an AI certificate only helped a little bit.

In a London hiring experiment, it lifted their rate of interview invitations from about 11 to 14 percent.

Prospective employers invited comparable women outside those groups, with no certificate at all, to interview 25 percent of the time. The certificate narrowed the gap, but most of the gap was still there.

Nick Drydakis, an economist at Anglia Ruskin University (ARU) in Cambridge, England, ran the experiment in 2025. He directs the university’s Centre for Inclusive Societies and Economies.

Governments, employers and training providers have been promoting AI training as a route into work. Most research on AI and hiring, though, has looked at bias inside screening software.

Drydakis asked what employers do when the applicant is the one with AI skills.

Between January and June, his team answered 1,016 real job ads in London, sending three matched applications to each one. That came to more than 3,000 applications in all.

Every applicant was a fictional woman applying for a full-time private-sector job that didn’t require a degree. Each held a Level 3 vocational qualification, a practical course usually started between ages 16 and 18, plus a one-year apprenticeship.

The study ran as four separate experiments. In each, one application came from a woman in the majority group, and two came from a woman in an underrepresented group.

The underrepresented groups were Black British women, women aged 50 instead of 28, lesbian women, and women who disclosed being on the autism spectrum.

Sexual orientation showed up as a partner’s name, Helen rather than George. Autism showed up as a short line such as, “I am on the autism spectrum and known for my strong focus and attention to detail.”

One of the two applications from the underrepresented group also listed a six-month AI training certificate.

Its description covered using AI tools to automate office paperwork and handle data, aimed at the routine digital work these jobs involve.

Before any applications went out, 71 people, including HR staff, employers, students and hiring researchers, checked that each signal read clearly and that the matched applications looked equally strong otherwise.

Every application went out within a day of the ad appearing, and the team saw no sign that any employer noticed the matched sets.

Across all four experiments, prospective employers invited women from the majority groups to interview 25 percent of the time. Women from the underrepresented groups, with matching qualifications and no certificate, were invited 11 percent of the time.

The gap appeared in every experiment. Because the applications were matched, Drydakis concluded that employers were responding differently at the screening stage.

With the AI certificate, the underrepresented women’s rate rose to 14 percent. That’s about 28 percent more invitations than the same women got without it, yet still far below the majority group’s 25 percent.

“AI training can help open doors, but it should not be viewed as a substitute for fair recruitment practices,” Drydakis said.

The boost wasn’t the same everywhere. Women who disclosed autism were invited about 7 percent of the time without the certificate and 11 percent with it, against 24 percent for women who didn’t disclose.

Lesbian women’s invitation rate rose from about 13 to 18 percent, against 26 percent for heterosexual women. Both of those gains held up in the statistical tests.

For women aged 50, the rise from about 10 to 12 percent was smaller, and its statistical support depended on the test used.

The invitation rate for Black British women showed a small change, from 15 to 17 percent. Because of this small change, the experiment cannot determine whether the certificate provided any benefit at all.

The interview invitations also differed in what the jobs paid. Underrepresented women without the certificate were more often invited by employers advertising lower wages, about 7 percent lower per hour on average.

With the certificate, that pay gap shrank to about 4 percent. It didn’t disappear.

So the certificate helped in two ways, more calls and better-paid ones. However, neither brought the women level with the majority group.

Drydakis has argued in earlier work that AI skills act as a form of AI capital, something workers can build that pays off in the job market. This experiment fits that idea, up to a point.

“If we want technological change to create opportunities for everyone, investment in digital skills must be matched by strong efforts to tackle discrimination and ensure qualifications are assessed consistently across all applicant groups,” he said.

No majority-group applicant ever carried the AI certificate. That means the study can’t say whether the training would help every applicant just as much. If it did, the gap between groups wouldn’t shrink at all.

The study also stopped at the interview invitation. It didn’t follow job offers, actual pay, or how anyone was treated at work. Nor can it tell whether employers acted on prejudice or on guesses about who would do the job well.

All the applicants were women seeking jobs without a degree requirement in one city, so the results may not hold for graduate jobs, men, or other places.

A certificate that stood out in 2025 may also become routine as AI training spreads, and repeating the experiment in a few years would show whether its value holds up.

The full study was published in the journal Industrial Relations: A Journal of Economy and Society.
