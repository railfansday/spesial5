Crews tearing up a 1950s parking lot at Dinosaur National Monument hit fossils, but the real find was a dinosaur tail with nine vertebrae

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Under a Dinosaur National Monument parking lot, a paleontologist found a likely Diplodocus tail with nine vertebrae in the first dig there since 1924.

In September 2025, road crews at Dinosaur National Monument in Utah peeled back the asphalt outside the Quarry Exhibit Hall, the most popular stop in the park. They were there to rebuild a parking lot. The first scoop of the backhoe brought up pieces of fossil bone.

Over the next five weeks, the job turned into a dinosaur dig.

Park paleontologist ReBecca Hunt-Foster and her assistant followed a trail of bones through the rock until they had uncovered nine tail vertebrae and the leg and arm bones of a long-necked dinosaur, most likely Diplodocus.

Its relatives still turn up in surprising places, including the first Diplodocus ever found outside North America.

It was the first fossil excavation in that part of the historic Carnegie Quarry since 1924, the National Park Service said. In an account published this fall, Hunt-Foster described how the last bones of the tail were lying right against the old asphalt.

The spot has a long history. On August 17, 1909, Earl Douglass, a paleontologist from the Carnegie Museum in Pittsburgh, spotted eight dinosaur tailbones sticking out of a sandstone ledge in the Utah desert. “It was a beautiful sight,” he wrote in his diary.

His crews spent the next 13 years digging there and collected more than 317 metric tons of fossils and rock between 1909 and 1922, according to Hunt-Foster, much of it shipped back to Pittsburgh.

The Smithsonian dug on the east side of the quarry in 1923, and the University of Utah in 1924. In 1915, President Woodrow Wilson set aside the quarry and 80 acres around it as Dinosaur National Monument.

All that digging produced a lot of waste rock. It was dumped off the eastern side of the quarry, and in the 1930s, New Deal work crews cleared more debris while improving the road.

Those tailing piles later became the parking lot for the Quarry Exhibit Hall, which opened in 1958 around a wall of rock where visitors can see about 1,500 dinosaur fossils still in place.

In May 2025, the park was due to begin an $11.2 million project to upgrade roads, parking areas and overlooks along the route to the Quarry Exhibit Hall.

Because the work crossed fossil-bearing rock, the park brought on a seasonal paleontological assistant, Rudolph Hummel, to watch the construction.

The biggest worry was the parking lot. Fossils overlooked by the early excavators, or judged not worth keeping, could be sitting in the old tailings. The bone-bearing sandstone itself might also turn up.

The road and the exhibit hall closed from September 8 to late October 2025. Monitoring in the parking lot began on September 10, after the asphalt cap came off.

On September 11, the team found the first identifiable bone, a vertebra from a meat-eating dinosaur, likely Allosaurus. Smaller finds followed every day as the crew dug down five feet. Construction sites have a way of turning up fossils, as a Los Angeles project showed.

The construction workers got caught up in it too. Many of them jumped in to search for fossils by hand rather than by backhoe alone, Hunt-Foster wrote, and one worker spent two days power-washing a section of the sandstone so it could be studied.

On September 16, after that washing was done, Hunt-Foster was documenting the exposed sandstone when she spotted a fossil bone in its cross section.

It turned out to be a large limb bone plunging down into the rock. Construction in that corner of the lot was paused.

After a week of digging, the pair found a tail vertebra lying over the long bone, which they identified as a sauropod tibia. Then a second vertebra appeared, and a third, all still connected. Hummel followed them east while Hunt-Foster worked west.

Six more tail vertebrae turned up, along with the bones of the lower arm. By then, the team had concluded that the tail likely belonged to Diplodocus, the most abundant dinosaur in the Carnegie Quarry.

They also found a partial turtle, plenty of freshwater clams and carbonized wood lying directly under the skeleton.

By September 26, the team had reached the end of the tail. The vertebrae grew smaller toward the east, and the last one was missing its back half, cleanly sheared off and tilted up toward the old surface.

Hunt-Foster and Hummel suspect that the tip of the tail, if it was preserved at all, was plowed off when the road was first built in the late 1950s. Several of the last vertebrae were in direct contact with the asphalt from the lot’s first paving.

Photos from the visitor center’s opening on June 1, 1958, show the lot was still gravel, and it does not appear to have been paved until around 1959, she wrote.

All of this happened while the new parking lot was being built around the dig. Dump trucks full of fill rolled past and rollers packed down the ground around them. “In a short amount of time, we were sitting in a deep trench surrounded by a fresh parking lot,” Hunt-Foster wrote.

Volunteers, a Utah Conservation Corps crew, park coworkers and construction staff helped wrap the bones in protective field jackets. The first small jacket came out on October 13. On October 17, the construction crew’s backhoe operator lifted out the rest.

In all, about 3,000 pounds of fossils and rock came out of the 2025 dig. The bones are now being cleaned and studied at the Utah Field House of Natural History State Park Museum in Vernal, Utah.

Visitors can watch the work in the museum’s fossil preparation lab. Some fossils from the excavation are also on display at the Quarry Exhibit Hall.

The parking lot is finished, and the Quarry Exhibit Hall is open daily except on Thanksgiving, Christmas and New Year’s Day.

The story is not over. Hunt-Foster says a second part of her account will cover continued excavation work in 2026 and additional discoveries.

The information was obtained from the National Park Service’s January 2026 announcement of the excavation, ReBecca Hunt-Foster’s article The Parking Lot Sauropod of Dinosaur National Monument: Part 1 in Park Paleontology News, and the park’s pages on Earl Douglass and the historic Carnegie Quarry.
