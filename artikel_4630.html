How Music Can Help Us Move Through Climate Anxiety

Updated November 18, 2026 | Reviewed by Lybi Ma

In November, at the WHO Health Pavilion at COP31 in Antalya, Türkiye, a packed room of climate scientists and policymakers got to their feet and danced. On stage, a collective of women musicians played songs built from the sounds of water: melting glaciers, swollen rivers, rising seas. After two weeks of talk about emissions, clean energy, and finance, the climate crisis stopped being something we discussed and became something we felt, together, in our bodies.

Many of us know the opposite feeling: another headline about record heat or flooding, a jolt of dread, and we scroll on, more alone with it than before. Climate anxiety is a reasonable response to a real threat, but carried alone, it paralyzes us. In that room, something moved.

Most people do not experience climate change as a graph. It is intense heat, difficult breathing, and worries about water: too little, too much, or whether it’s safe to drink. Women often carry the heaviest load, as do indigenous peoples, long keepers of these waters.

This COP also put health on the agenda by opening with a health day. The numbers explain why: The Lancet Countdown links fossil fuels and climate change to an estimated 3.3 million deaths a year from air pollution and extreme heat alone. The toll on our minds is harder to count, but it is just as real. Heat disrupts sleep and attention, and the slow dread of what is coming shows up as anxiety and paralysis. A WHO Europe report released during the summit, Strengthening Mental Health in a Changing Climate, links floods and wildfires to anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress, and calls for mental health to be part of climate policy.

What helps? The WHO report looked at 20 interventions in 15 countries. Most aimed to build resilience and agency close to where people live. Agency is exactly what the arts can help people find. Art speaks human.

As a drama therapist and scientist, I am interested in how art can help us—projects using music, storytelling, and film to amplify the climate emergency. This isn’t therapy per se, but it draws on the same processes that creative arts therapists use with clients: giving form to hard-to-name feelings, and sharing them so they become bearable. It does two things that research and practice keep pointing to.

It invites people in as authors. Each artist found her own way into water. People engage differently when they are recognized as experts in their own lives.

It turns feeling into connection. In a survey of 10,000 young people in 10 countries, 59 percent were very or extremely worried about climate change. In my clinical work and in the classroom, I see people shut down when fear and grief have nowhere to go. Music gives those feelings a shared rhythm.

There is good reason to think this helps. Eco-anxiety is closely tied to difficulty regulating emotions, and a meta-analysis of controlled studies found that listening to music reduces anxiety. In a study that followed adults over three years, those who increasingly turned to music to manage their emotions also became more likely to tackle problems head-on. Music doesn’t only soothe. It can help us act.

"When we are emotionally and empathetically tied to the natural world, we are reminded at a more profound, instinctive level of our interdependence with it."—Yo-Yo Ma, in Music and Mind, edited by Renée Fleming

This echoes what my colleagues and I found in developing the WHO Europe-Jameel Arts & Health Lab policy brief on the role of the arts and culture in addressing the health impacts of climate change, and it reinforces findings from the first Culture Global Stocktake for Climate Action, also launched at COP31. Culture and cultural practices reflect and shape what we value, and it can build the trust and belonging that help people act on what they know.

Listen to these sounds of nature. Then ask yourself: what is your relationship to water where you live? What would it take to protect it, together? Then let the listening lead somewhere. Review these six acts for water that help us move from feeling to understanding to action:

● Learn how much water your daily life uses.

● Go deeper with films and talks from communities living with water crises.

● Join a group protecting rivers and oceans.

● Find out how you can help restore local wetlands, streams, and soils.

● Share your story. Make something with what comes up: a sketch, a few lines, a song. It doesn't have to be good.

If you work with young people or adults, try that last act as a group. Try a simple structure: open in a circle where people name what they feel; write a short song together from a local challenge, a mood, and a beat; perform it; then reflect on creating together rather than alone.

What I saw in Antalya gives me hope: a room full of committed people discovered they could still be moved, and could still move together. In my experience, hope is less a feeling we wait for than something we practice with others. The futures we can feel together are the ones we begin to build.
