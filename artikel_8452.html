Drinking Camino Wine Hiker Trash Style

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

After the meal the lovely old lady cooked last night, we had to partake of her breakfast too. She cooked us a wonderful meal of super fresh bacon (I think they killed the pig across the street last night) and eggs with deep orange yolks.

It was such a warm and welcoming stay. We gave her a hug, said goodbye, and got back on the trail at 8:00 AM.

It was a beautiful cool morning, under a bluebird clear sky. The dirt path initially paralleled a paved road before cutting off through the countryside. As we descended, we could see hundreds–if not a thousand–people on the path 1,000 feet below us. We would soon be exiting the Galician Mountains.

The views were amazing. Off in the distance, we could see that we were leaving the taller mountains behind.

The trail descended sharply into Fillobal, a small town with just a few buildings. Immediately after, we started down an old road again where erosion over time had eaten away at the path, leaving it worn 20 feet deep into the hillside.

Moving along at a pace of over 3 miles per hour, the path entered a dark forested area that looked like the green tunnel of the Appalachian Trail:

At the next town of Pasantes, we were disappointed in our hunt for caffeine–there were no cafes in this working farm village.

The less traveled, working villages are much more accommodating with better food and friendlier service than the touristy towns like Molinaseca. The best way to spot an authentic town? Look for cow poop on the streets.

Exiting Pasantes, the rock-paved street turned into dirt again as we once again re-entered another green tunnel. This sunken road was a magical, almost fairytale-like experience with even larger trees darkening the deep forest areas around us.

We continued descending to Triacastela, where we joined with our “tramily”, Jill and Robert. We stopped at a cafe to talk about the nomadic life over coffees and baked goods.

Triacastela is the considered the end of the Galician Mountains. Here, the Camino splits:

We walked through a narrow valley with the river below us. The path paralleled a paved road, featuring several boardwalk bridges where the river flowed directly underneath.

The mist from up to 50-foot waterfalls rose from the steep rock canyon walls, providing natural air conditioning against the warming day.

Eventually, the path exited into San Cristo do Real—an almost abandoned time capsule:

Leaving the village, we followed an old road cut into the hillside, lined with large chestnut trees and high, moss-covered rock walls. Birds sang in the canopy overhead while the bubbling creek played music below.

We climbed up to a village of white washed buildings, passed a closed 1-room church where we peered inside for a cool breeze, and tackled one last hill.

At the top, we caught our first view of the massive Samos Monastery below. A 12-foot-tall stone wall lined both sides of the path as we descended into town.

After dumping or packs on the top floor of our apartment building, we went out for food. While sitting at an outdoor table, we watched an older man leave ¾ of a bottle of wine behind. Like good hiker trash we made sure it didn’t go to waste.

Later, we joined a tour of the monastery, which was recently restored following past abandonment and a fire.

We intended to return for 7:30 PM mass, but as we got back to our room, exhaustion set in and we fell asleep almost immediately.
