Why One Good Thing Leads to Another

Posted November 13, 2026 | Reviewed by Tyler Woods

After reading the responses to my last post about the art of asking questions, in which I described the importance of becoming better listeners to ensure more meaningful connection with others, I’ve been thinking about something else: how one small action can set other good things in motion.

Here’s what got me thinking. A woman from a media company that uses puppets to teach elementary-school children important skills reached out. She was so excited about the idea of teaching genuine curiosity to young people that she is talking with her staff about creating episodes on asking interesting questions.

She wrote: I'm part of the team behind Sunnyside Roadshow, a daily, five-minute social-emotional learning program for elementary school children that uses puppetry, storytelling, and humor to help develop important life skills.

"As I read your article, I found myself wondering: Can we begin teaching children, at an early age, the art of being genuinely curious about other people? Kids are naturally eager to share their own experiences, and understandably so! But could we help them learn to pause, listen, ask another question, and show interest in what someone else is saying?

…Rather than simply teaching children not to interrupt and act on impulse, what if we could help them discover the joy of being genuinely interested in another person's experiences? Could we intentionally model curiosity through our characters' conversations, showing children how asking one thoughtful follow-up question can make someone feel heard and valued? If they worked on these skills together in the classroom, wouldn't we help raise more thoughtful humans?"

A post about ways to deepen conversation through becoming better listeners might lead to children learning how to show interest in others. Those children could bring that curiosity to their friendships, their families, and eventually their adult relationships. We have no way of knowing how far those ripples might travel.

Other people wrote that the post prompted them to reflect on their own conversational habits. They recognized themselves in the tendency to tell stories and talk about themselves rather than ask questions, and vowed to be more inquisitive.

That’s how it starts. Someone does something. Someone else responds. And on and on.

And it doesn’t require writing a post or creating a children’s program.

Think about the times you checked out at Trader Joe’s and were asked, “What are your plans for today?” or “Got anything fun planned this weekend?” For some customers, that might be the only conversation they have all day. What seems like a casual question can be a moment of genuine connection. And think about the difference that brief connection might make—not only for the person leaving the store, but also for the people they encounter throughout the day.

A few months ago, as I was checking out of Trader Joe’s, a clerk asked me about my plans, and I mentioned that I was having a party.

He ran off and returned with a gorgeous bouquet of flowers—all my favorite colors. He had no idea how much that unexpected kindness meant to me. And now, months later, I’m telling you about it. His gesture is still traveling.

My mother understood something about this, too. A New Yorker, she would often pay the toll for a complete stranger in the car behind her when crossing the George Washington Bridge.

Imagine pulling up to the tollbooth, reaching for your money, and being told that someone had already paid. Someone you didn’t know and probably would never meet. I loved that about her.

Did those drivers go on to do something kind for someone else? I don’t know. But my mother’s gestures stayed with me. That, too, is a ripple.

We often think that making a difference requires a grand plan, lots of time, or something extraordinary to offer. Meanwhile, we overlook the opportunities right in front of us: asking a question and truly listening to the answer, offering a sincere compliment, including someone who seems left out, or letting someone know that something they did mattered.

So here’s my challenge: Do one small thing today that might set other good things in motion.

Treat it as an experiment. Notice what happens—to the other person and to you. You may see an immediate response, or you may never know where your gesture leads.

Either way, tip over that first domino. And watch the fun begin.

And if the spirit moves you, email me and tell me what happened. I’d love to hear.
