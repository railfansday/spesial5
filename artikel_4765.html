Can Finding Perfection Backfire?

Things were starting to feel rather boring at home—the same old places, the same old food, the same old sunsets. I’ve learned to be very wary of boredom, because it’s a close cousin to one of my least favorite symptoms of depression: anhedonia. Anhedonia is the inability to experience pleasure; things that used to give you pleasure (that amazing Malibu sunset, for example) no longer move you the same way.

It was clearly time to get away from my usual haunts. I needed a startle, an awakening, something that would reset my pleasure circuits so life didn’t feel so humdrum. Simply put, I needed a good healthy dose of awe.

Travel is one of the best ways I know to stoke the wonder inside me. Fortunately, my partner, Colin, was up for it. We thought and thought about where to go, and then, one day, I just knew. I wanted rejuvenation? I wanted rebirth? Then why not go to the very heart of it?

That meant, of course, heading to Florence. Historians credit Florence as the birthplace of Europe’s revivification after the Middle Ages, and it sounded simply ideal: museums overflowing with masterpieces, architecture so beautiful it could take one’s breath away. Literally. The so-called “Stendhal syndrome” originated there, when in 1817 French author Stendhal took one look at the Basilica of Santa Croce and fainted, overwhelmed by its magnificence.

I certainly wasn’t hoping to faint dead away, but to be so transported by beauty sounded heavenly. It sounded exactly like the cure I was seeking.

My anticipation grew with each passing day, until the trip was practically all I could think about. Colin and I were especially looking forward to seeing Michelangelo’s famous statue of David. At very long last, we found ourselves standing outside the Galleria dell’Accademia. There was a long line to get in, but what did it matter? David awaited.

When the guards started admitting people, there was some jostling, but eventually the rooms grew wider and the crowd, although still very much a crowd, grew thinner. I let go of Colin’s hand to search through my purse for my camera. When I found it and looked around for him, I was so astonished I nearly dropped my phone. There, illuminating Colin’s face, was the very sensation I had traveled so far for and hoped so much to find: awe.

I followed his gaze, and then I saw it, too: Michelangelo’s David. He dominated the room and dwarfed the tourists scrambling to capture his image. They encircled him, but he remained untouched, his smooth white marble immune to their adoration. I marveled at his potent form, every sinew of his body taut and ready for battle, his gaze resolute and unwavering. He stood forever on the edge of glory.

No, dear reader, I didn’t faint—although a pregnant woman next to me grew dizzy and had to be helped to a seat by a guard. The crowd, which had been so lively outside, was strangely quiet, as if in church. And it did feel like a sacred space. I knew, without being able to articulate it at the time, that I was in the presence of something holy, a talent so astounding that only a higher power could bestow it.

And contrary to everything I had expected, it didn’t instantly cure my discontent. I felt very strange—disconnected from my surroundings, and deeply disturbed. What was going on? I didn’t know how to explain my feelings to Colin, because I didn’t really understand them myself. All I knew was that compared to David, everything else seemed plebeian.

We went back to our hotel. There were frescoes on the walls and ceiling of our room that I’d delighted in just a few hours before. But now, I was only aware of their flaws. And worse yet, I was aware of mine. For all my artistic ambitions, I’d never create anything even remotely approaching a David.

I couldn’t help but wonder what other artists had felt in the face of incomparable beauty—and I wasn’t surprised by what I found out. Camus summed it up: “Beauty is unbearable, drives us to despair, offering us for a minute the glimpse of an eternity that we should like to stretch out over the whole of time.”

Exactly. So now what was I going to do, knowing that the rest of life would never reach the level of perfection I’d just witnessed?

“Let’s go to lunch,” Colin said, always the pragmatist. “You’ll feel better after you eat.”

A half-hour later, I sat in front of a large, steaming bowl of fusilli al pesto and toyed with my fork. I was sick of being so damned sensitive. Why couldn’t I just be moved by perfection, and let it go?

Then I took a bite of my fusilli. Oh my God, it was the most delicious thing I’d ever tasted. The pasta was just firm enough, the pesto fragrant and slightly sweet, with a teasing, subtle undertone of garlic. I looked up at Colin and smiled. He smiled back—relieved, no doubt, that I wasn’t moping anymore.

So maybe I couldn’t be perfect. Maybe I was meant to be like Salieri in Amadeus, fated to simply appreciate perfection. That didn’t mean I had to be miserable. That afternoon in Florence, sitting across from the love of my life, a perfect moment came and went, and I was there to know it.
