The Mental Edge: 6 Tools to Use to Perform Better at Anything

Mental skills, also called psychological skills, are those mental abilities that allow us to do everything from getting up off the couch, getting through a rough day, managing a pressurized situation, and realizing our potential. These can be learned and honed to meet our individualized needs, and with practice, facilitate both small and large changes in our everyday and game time performance.

Want to be more motivated? Want to learn to handle stress better? Want to improve your run times? Want to start exercising? Want to do better on tests? Want more confidence? You get the idea. These skills can help.

Some foundational mental skills for performance optimization are deep breathing, relaxation skills, self-talk, a reset routine, cognitive reframing, and visualization/imagery.

Deep breathing is a foundational mental skill to incorporate into your mental skills playbook. Deep breathing is key to physical health, mental health, pain management, stress management, emotional control, and cognitive focus in high-stakes situations. Regularly practicing deep breathing is key to being able to use it during challenging moments to refocus and gain control of your physiological response.

There is a wide array of relaxation skills, but three effective ones are deep breathing, specifically diaphragmatic breathing, progressive muscle relaxation, and meditation. Relaxation techniques decrease stress and improve stress tolerance and improve health and cognitive performance.

For getting motivated, staying focused, and accomplishing goals, there is nothing quite like self-talk. That running dialogue in our heads is extremely powerful and feeds our self-confidence, attentional focus, problem solving abilities, and ability to stick with things even when they are hard. Instructional self-talk gives us the edge we need to remember how to execute complex problems or learn a new skill. If you have ever learned the breaststroke for example, you repeated the instructional mantra “pull, breathe, kick, glide” to yourself many times. Motivational self-talk, on the other, fuels our confidence, focus, and drive. “You got this!”

When we make a mistake or get thrown off our game by an external variable, sometimes we allow ourselves to be derailed. Performance suffers; we lose focus and become less effective. With a conscious reset routine at the ready to intervene, it is possible to rebound quickly and effectively. A good reset routine is short and incorporates an acknowledgment of the mistake or issue without judgment, a slow, deep breath, and a quick instructional self-talk cue to get back on track. For example, a flight student who has made a mistake during a training flight might 1) acknowledge the mistake without judgment, 2) take a deep 5-7 breath (inhale slowly for 5 counts, exhale slowly for 7 counts), and 3) refocus with the instructional self-talk cue, “Run the next checklist.” After a mistake on the field, an athlete might 1) acknowledge the mistake without judgement, 2) take a slow, deep breath, and 3) refocus with “Next play.”

Cognitive reframing involves recognizing maladaptive/self-defeating thoughts, examining them objectively, and reframing/replacing them with a realistic and practical thought. The goal here is not unrealistic positivity, but useful and task-focused language. For example, after a mistake during flight training, a student might think “I’m terrible, I’m going to fail out.” This thought is defeatist, absolute, and catastrophic. After examining the thoughts objectively, replacements might be: “I made a common mistake and need to refocus my study time on this area. I’ve worked hard to get here and otherwise I am doing well.” Cognitive reframing or restructuring has been shown to reduce self-critical rumination, increase positive affect, and decrease negative affect in athletes. Cognitive reframing can be used in the moment and is a valuable strategy to move forward after a setback.

Visualization and imagery are techniques in which we mentally simulate a task (e.g., emergency procedures or a golf swing) in order to better execute that task when needed. Mental rehearsal stimulates neurocognitive processes in much the same way as actually engaging in the activity. Visualization has been shown to enhance mental control, improve motor skills/technical skills and increase self-confidence, and can be used alone or in conjunction with other training strategies. Visualization, imagery, and mental rehearsal are key components of elite performers, including the Navy’s Blue Angels, who routinely engage in chair flying, in which maneuvers are mentally practiced in conjunction with motor movements (see The Blue Angels documentary, 2024, produced by Amazon MGM Studios).

Benefits of mental skills are that they are free, accessible to everyone, and have high levels of effectiveness, which can be applied to virtually any aspect of life. Whether your goal is to manage everyday stress, build confidence, bounce back from setbacks, earn your wings, win a championship, or perform at your best when it matters most, these skills can make a meaningful difference. Like physical skills, mental skills require practice to become effective and readily available when you need them. Start small, choose a skill or two that fits your needs, and practice consistently. Over time, you’ll build a mental skills playbook that you can make work for you.
