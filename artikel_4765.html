What Is the Strongest Predictor of Divorce?

How do you avoid getting divorced? By definition, all married couples seek to avoid their marriage falling apart. In wedding ceremonial vows, couples make commitments to each other, which typically include standing by one another’s side in sickness and health, and in times of prosperity and poverty. Marriage is meant to last forever, but it doesn’t always work out as intended. In the United States, 43-46 percent of marriages end in divorce, and that number rises for second or third marriages.1

Divorce is painful and costly. Getting divorced increases one’s risk of experiencing financial strain, drug and alcohol abuse, suicide, poorer health, and interrupted parenting if the couple has children. Despite the high prevalence of divorce and the substantial consequences associated with it, research on why marriages end has largely developed in separate camps: one focused on how couples communicate, another on the personal vulnerabilities each spouse brings into the marriage, and a third on couples’ financial and socioeconomic circumstances. These perspectives had never been directly compared to see which best predicts divorce. Recently, a team of social scientists published an article digging deep into this question.2

The researchers began by recruiting 368 heterosexual newlywed couples from the Los Angeles Project on Newlywed Development who had gotten married within the past 6 months. The study began in 2009-10 and concluded in 2021-22. Over these 12 years, the researchers tracked a variety of metrics from the couples and, of primary interest, which couples got divorced (or were separated). Six key predictor variables were collected, which include:

The vulnerability variables included a score composed of adverse childhood experiences, history of physical/sexual abuse, problematic substance use (past 9 months), negative affect (past 30 days), and parents’ marital status. Communication style was assessed by video recording couples’ discussion of a topic or issue in their relationship, which was carefully coded by trained research assistants. Socioeconomic status included typical metrics of education, income, work stability, financial strain, and neighborhood median income. Worth noting is that the sample itself was not necessarily representative of the general U.S. population (lower income than average, largely Hispanic).

By the end of the study, 39 percent of couples were divorced. When all six of the key predictor variables listed above were used together to estimate who got divorced, the evidence pointed to the wife’s vulnerabilities as the only statistically significant predictor of divorce. The wife’s vulnerabilities also predicted a secondary variable measuring how quickly couples ended their marriage. In other words, the more of these vulnerabilities a wife reported (adverse childhood experiences, physical/sexual abuse, problematic substance use, frequent negative emotions in the top 25 percent of the sample, or not being raised by married parents), the more likely the couple was to divorce, even after accounting for the other five predictors.

Although speculative, the authors propose a few reasons why this finding occurred. Women tend to handle the lion’s share of domestic and emotional labor in marriages. Women also tend to be more responsive to partners’ needs, have better memory and awareness of relationship events, and notice problems more often. In sum, women with more vulnerabilities may be more likely to reach the decision to exit more often and more quickly.

But are vulnerabilities deterministic of divorce? Not quite. The same study found that communication mattered in an important way. Among couples who communicated especially well, including more warmth, humor, and effective problem solving, and less hostility, the added divorce risk tied to wives’ vulnerabilities largely disappeared. The baggage women carry into a marriage appears real, but strong interpersonal communication seems to buffer against it. The findings point to two places worth investing in: reducing childhood adversity in the first place and offering couples support that works with those personal histories. Knowing where the risk lies doesn’t seal a couple’s fate. It tells them where to put in the work.
