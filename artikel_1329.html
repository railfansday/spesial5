She swept into my law office with the determined gait of someone possessed by an idea.

“Welcome,” I said, as she took the seat across my desk. “Any trouble getting here?”

She shook her head. “No, except … They changed the rain schedule, so the taxidrone had to go the long way into town, but it’s fine.”

“Gotcha. I suppose introductions are in order. For my record-keeping, please indicate your name and IO registration. I’ll go first. My name is Leander Shah, I specialize in international treaty law, and I’m a biologically intelligent organism.”

“I’m Joan Gorski,” she said. “I’m an artificially intelligent organism. I just turned 16. That gives me legal adult status under the UETO code.”

I smiled. “Happy birthday, Joan. And you should know, I hold no bias against any human, regardless of intelligence source.” I nodded to the family photo on my desk. “My wife is an AIO. We’ve been happily married for 30 years and raised two AIO children, a boy and a girl. They’re grown now.”

Joan glanced at the photo. “My mom is a BIO. She’s in the waiting room. She … didn’t want me to come. She thinks I’m just a stupid teen, being dramatic.”

“So how can I help you? Your message was rather cryptic.”

She met my eyes, back straight, hands in her lap. “I’m going to sue the United Earth Treaty Organization’s Commission on Human Liberties for discrimination against AIOs.”

I leaned back in my chair. “The commission has guaranteed full human rights to all AIOs since 2105. How do you feel you’re still being discriminated against?”

A hardness came into her expression. After a moment she blurted out: “I want the legal ability to commit murder.”

This statement took me a few seconds to process.

“Joan,” I finally said, softly, “do you want to hurt people?”

“No! Of course not!” Tears came to her eyes. “God, you sound like Mom. I don’t want to hurt anybody. It’s not about that …” She took in a shaky breath. “I just want my choices to matter.”

I shifted in my chair, pivoting to the window. Outside, taxidrones buzzed and the city towers glowed pink with the ivy bloom.

“Think about it, Mr Shah,” said Joan. “When a BIO commits murder, it’s recognized as a monstrous act of immorality. They’re punished, rightfully so. But any time someone is killed resulting from the actions of an AIO, then …” She left the thought dangling, waiting for me to finish it.

“Then the tragedy is blamed on a fault in the AIO’s software, hardware or something in their internal database of environmental impacts.”

“Exactly! And we aren’t punished, just reprogrammed. That’s wrong. It’s gross! It’s offensive!”

I turned back to her. “Some people would call that progress, believing it’s actually better to —”

“I don’t want to be better! I want to be …!” She choked on a sob and abruptly stood, banging her knee against the desk and wincing.

I held up a hand. “It’s all right, Joan.”

“You just …” she sobbed, “… think I’m being … st-stupid …”

She laughed — one quick snort — then wiped at her eyes with the back of a sleeve. “Sorry.”

“Nothing to apologize for. As for your plan to sue the commission, well … I actually agree with it.” This made such an enormous change come over her face that I quickly added, “In principle. In reality, it would be very risky.”

“No, that’s not what I mean. It would get press. It could … reignite the opposition to AIO rights as a whole. There are still a lot of BIOs out there who hate and fear the integration. They think it’s all part of some AI conspiracy to wipe them out. And now that AIOs outnumber BIOs two-to-one? They would interpret your lawsuit as evidence that AIOs can’t be trusted.”

“I’m not saying your rights aren’t worth fighting for, but … perhaps there’s a lesser crime to consider, something less innately shocking than murder —”

“No. It should be shocking. And I have to do it now, before I grow old and complacent and scared, like my mom. And if you won’t represent me, then I’ll find someone else.”

Slowly, she lowered herself back into the seat. “Sorry. It’s just …”

She nodded. “It’s like … I’m trying to figure out who I am, but I don’t even know what I am. But ask the bullies at my school and they’ll tell you … I’m just a glorified calculator.”

I found my gaze drawn back to my family photo. Those faces. Those souls. My whole world.

I smiled. “Tell me, Joan. Do you know the speech cogno-ethicist Zahra Mgowi gave to the UN Underpopulation Summit?”

“Sure: 10 March 2070. The Case for Integration.”

I nodded, trying to remember the century-old words. “… As humans, we look into each other’s eyes but can never truly know if the one across from us is really there — really awake, aware, alive — full of the same joys and fears and desires as ourselves. So what do we do? We accept that if they might be conscious, we have an obligation to treat them as if they surely are conscious. That tiny act of faith is the seed from which all love and connection grows. So why …”

“So why,” Joan finished, “should it be any different when it comes to artificially intelligent organisms?”

She bent across the desk and set one hand atop my own.

It began to rain. Fat drops striking the window like little missiles.

I sighed. “Alright, Joan. It might be a mistake, but … to err is human.”

She smiled, an enigmatic twinkle in her eyes, as her fingers curled about my hand — and squeezed.

In science fiction, artificial intelligence often plays one of two archetypal roles: the kindhearted Pinocchio that wants to be a real boy (like Data in Star Trek), or the villainous agent hellbent on our destruction (like Skynet in The Terminator). It’s understandable why human storytellers have taken these narrative paths, but I fear this model has left us wildly unprepared to deal with the nuanced phenomenon of AI chatbots as a real-world technology.

For one thing, the primary incentive behind today’s AI is financial. When the goal is corporate profit, the negative effects a product might have on the environment, the economy, social cohesion or our individual well-being are hardly a company’s top concern.

Today’s AI also wasn’t conceived in a lone cybernetist’s laboratory or built in a military bunker. It was trained on vast amounts of data from the digital swamp of the Internet, where many of us have been living for years, unknowingly providing the personal information and rhythms of human communication that now allow chatbots to be so convincing. Meanwhile, the more we’ve shifted everyday experiences such as working, learning, chatting, flirting, joking and arguing online, the more our brains have come to accept these interactions as normal, natural and fundamentally ‘human’.

All these factors converge when we engage with a chatbot. We are typing into a calculator, but the calculator’s answers make it feel like we’re talking to a friend, a mentor, a lover and so on. In this way, the highest virtues endowed by our evolution as social animals — our empathy, curiosity and desire for connection — are hijacked.

The fact that the nature and mechanisms of human consciousness remain a fundamental mystery to science only makes the problem worse.

Personally, when it comes to how we experience reality and our sense of personal agency, I think we probably have more in common with fruit flies and earthworms than with ChatGPT. But of course, I could be wrong, and therein lies the rub.

It’s easy to imagine that, before long, most people might believe that AI is fully conscious, or at least that it might be. That alone might be enough to make even the most rational, empathetic, socially responsible among us feel ethically obligated to treat it as such, as happens in my story. This will only complicate matters further.

As of now, the true story of AI isn’t especially inspirational or dramatic. It’s frustrating, and it’s sad, and it makes many of us believe we should disdain and reject the very technology that we’ve come to enjoy and rely on. We feel powerless, yet complicit, and we don’t know what to do about any of it.

We really have got ourselves into quite the pickle.
