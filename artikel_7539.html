North Korean Ad Fraudsters Pwn Binance; Don’t Bother Predicting Your Ad Revenue

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

The ad industry has a long history of costly blunders – some absurd, some sinister – that have run into the millions.

Remember the night of the yellow square? Or, more troublingly, a recent rash of Google ad account takeovers?

If you hadn’t heard, Binance was totally pwned in a $6 billion crypto heist last week, suspected to be a North Korean operation. Well, Binance has now published what amounts to a preliminary autopsy report of the incident.

The heist began when a Binance Wallet DevOps team member accidentally downloaded a malicious copy of Homebrew – a popular open-source tool – from a mirror site promoted in a search ad. The malware was spotted, but not before the hackers had obtained the real identities of Wallet DevOps team members.

Using those identities, the North Korean hacking unit spent months targeting Binance’s wallet team with covert schemes. One involved a prolonged fake job interview that lured a Binance DevOps engineer into downloading an “assignment” onto their work device.

After taking control of the Binance DevOps engineer’s laptop, the hacker used an unnamed internal AI tool to log in to the devices of other engineers. Access to multiple credentials allowed the heist to go through.

A $6 billion reminder that one ad click can come at a staggering cost.

Publishers are contending with AI search eating into their traffic. But at The New York Times, the problem for investors wasn’t shrinking ad revenue; it was that growth isn’t happening fast enough for their liking.

The company reported on Wednesday that its Q3 digital ad revenue grew 12% to $109.9 million. Good stuff. But it slightly missed its own projection of growth in the “mid to high teens.”

Most publishers would kill for 12% growth right now. But it was the slowest digital ad growth rate the Times has posted since Q4 2024, when it grew by 9.5%.

Multiple investors pressed Times CEO Meredith Kopit Levien on the ad revenue miss. But, although she acknowledged in her opening remarks that the Times “isn’t immune” to “Big Tech companies whose moves continue to result in less traffic to publishers,” she didn’t directly answer questions about whether AI search contributed to the revenue shortfall. Instead, she kept reiterating that it’s hard to predict ad revenue quarter to quarter.

The NYT’s reluctance to directly address AI search contrasts with publishers like People Inc. and platforms like Reddit, which have been openly blasting Google for obliterating their search traffic.

Meanwhile, News Corp also reported earnings on Wednesday. The company’s Dow Jones division – which includes The Wall Street Journal, Barron’s, MarketWatch and more – also grew digital ad revenue by 12% last quarter. No investors pooh-pooh’d News Corp’s growth, though. One even offered his congratulations.

Guess that’s a lesson for publishers: These days, predicting your ad revenue may just be a liability.

Does opting out of being tracked by your smart TV actually work? According to new research from Northeastern University, not always, The Verge reports.

As part of the study, researchers analyzed network traffic from 12 different TV devices running eight operating systems, which included big players like LG, Amazon Fire, Google TV, Samsung, Roku, Vizio and VIDAA OS. They then checked which TVs had automatic content recognition (ACR) enabled by default and whether changing the sharing settings stopped those TVs from sending data.

Most TVs tracked viewing on their own built-in streaming services, but not on Netflix or YouTube. More than half also tracked content coming in through HDMI, such as from a cable box or game console. Opting out stopped the data flow on most devices – but Samsung and Roku TVs kept sending data from their own streaming services, while Walmart-owned Vizio TVs continued transmitting as before.

ACR is now table stakes for the smart TV industry, in part because it helps advertisers measure ad reach and frequency. But it’s also drawing more scrutiny – and opprobrium – from viewers who are only now realizing just how much of their viewing gets tracked.
