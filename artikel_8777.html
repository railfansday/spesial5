Canyon’s PACE Cockpit Has a Clever Answer to One of Integrated Bars’ Biggest Problems

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Five drop shapes and three width settings add flexibility to a proprietary cockpit.

Gear-obsessed editors choose every product we review. We may earn commission if you buy from a link. How we test gear.

Integrated cockpits can be light, aerodynamic, and good-looking. They can also make simple jobs harder than they need to be. Adjusting your position can mean cutting a steerer tube. Replacing a broken handlebar can mean buying a whole new cockpit. And routing brake hoses through everything can turn a small change into a long afternoon.

I’ve given Canyon’s PACE system plenty of criticism in my reviews. It’s proprietary, and integrated systems come with real compromises. But after swapping the drops on the Endurace CF SLX 9 I have in for review, I want to give Canyon credit for a thoughtful design choice.

The main section of the PACE cockpit is called the T-Bar. Canyon’s system lets you adjust cockpit height by up to 20 mm in 5mm increments, without cutting the steerer. The cockpit is also made up of three pieces, with the drop sections bolted to the T-Bar. That offers a couple of advantages.

First, it makes the bar width adjustable. The T-Bar has three width positions, 25 mm apart, for a total range of 50 mm. The actual widths depend on the drop shape. See the table below.

The bolt-on drops also let Canyon offer five shapes with different dimensions. If the drops that come on your new Canyon don’t suit you, you can swap them for another option.

My Endurace review bike came with the Compact drops. They have a 108mm drop, width settings from 390 to 440 mm, and 8 degrees of flare. I prefer a narrower bar with a deeper drop and more flare, so I switched to Canyon’s Control drops. They have a 120mm drop, adjust from 370 to 420 mm, and feature 17 degrees of flare.

*U.S. prices at the time of writing. Bar tape and fixing screws are included.

Here’s what I needed to do to make the swap: unwrap the bar, loosen and slide off the controls, remove the old drops, install the new ones, then tighten everything and rewrap the bar. Canyon includes fresh bar tape with the replacement drops.

Canyon also includes a clever cardboard tool that helps you evenly align your controls. I loved it!

With most one-piece integrated cockpits, switching to a different bar shape or replacing a broken drop means replacing the whole assembly. Depending on the bike’s routing, that may also mean disconnecting or replacing brake hoses and bleeding the brakes.

On the PACE, the brake hoses run behind the drop sections rather than through them, so I didn’t have to disconnect or cut them. That meant no brake bleed, no risk of ending up with a hose that’s too short, and no replacing the entire cockpit.

That also matters if you crash and break a drop. With a one-piece handlebar and stem, a broken bar can mean replacing the whole assembly. With PACE, you can replace the damaged section.*

*If you break a drop, check the PACE T-Bar, or stem, for damage, too. Replace it if necessary.

The system can also make bike travel less nerve-racking. If your bar tape is set up so the controls can slide on and off without rewrapping, you can unbolt the drops and let them hang out of the way. The bike packs a little narrower, and the handlebar ends are less exposed to side impacts. I’ve had bars damaged in transit more times than I can count, so I appreciate this feature.

PACE is still proprietary, and those concerns don’t disappear because one part of the system is clever. But integrated cockpits often force riders to accept limited adjustability, few shape options, and costly replacements. Canyon has found a useful way to make its handlebar easier to fit, repair, and travel with. For that, it deserves some credit.
