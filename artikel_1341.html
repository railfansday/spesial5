Mating decisions are often attributed to the sensory signalling between prospective sexual partners. Yet these interactions are also shaped by the broader environmental context in which they unfold, to appropriately align sexual arousal with reproductive opportunities1,2,3,4,5,6. Here we show that in Drosophila erecta mating is strictly contingent on the ecological and social environment generated as flies densely aggregate in groups on a food patch. We find that food volatiles directly promote male sexual arousal, triggering individuals to sample and pursue potential mates, giving rise to dynamic interactions across the group. The ensuing visual motion transforms each male’s visual field, which in turn further amplifies his arousal, generating a multisensory feedback loop that coordinately promotes courtship across individuals. D. erecta’s strict dependence on environmental cues appears latent in related species, such as Drosophila melanogaster, where food odour can promote arousal7 but is dispensable for vigorous courtship. Comparative circuit analyses show that species-specific thresholds for sexual arousal reflect variation in how olfactory input modulates conserved nodes controlling courtship drive, rendering food volatiles a strict sensory gate only in D. erecta. Together, our findings highlight how environmental cues not directly tied to sexual signalling can profoundly influence reproductive behaviour and reorganize the social landscape to ensure mating occurs in contexts where reproductive opportunities are abundant.

Social cues are essential for coordinating interactions between individuals, facilitating the emergence of group dynamics including mating, aggression and collective foraging8,9,10. Yet social interactions do not occur in isolation. Rather, they unfold in a broader environmental context that structures when, where and how social behaviours are expressed. Courtship and mating, for example, often take place at ecologically privileged locations—food sources1,2, prominent visual landmarks3,4 or suitable nesting sites5,6—where many individuals aggregate, either to increase their encounters with prospective sexual partners or to access key resources that promote reproductive success. In these settings, a central component of the environment is the social collective itself: the multisensory backdrop that emerges from the movement, sensory signals and interactions across all individuals in a group11,12,13,14. Social behaviours are therefore likely shaped by both the static properties of the physical environment and the dynamic features of the social landscape. Yet the neural mechanisms that integrate these distinct sensory streams to regulate social arousal states and structure interindividual interactions remain largely unknown.

The mating behaviours of Drosophila offer a powerful inroad to explore how animals adapt to complex ecological and social landscapes. Species in the Drosophila genus vary widely in their ecology and geographic distribution15,16,17,18,19,20, raising the possibility that mating strategies have evolved in concert with the diverse sensory environments in which reproduction occurs. Many species, including members of the Drosophila melanogaster subgroup, mate on their host plants, typically decaying ephemeral substrates where many individuals congregate17, creating a complex social backdrop for reproduction. Other species reproduce in more isolated social settings. Drosophila elegans, for example, establish individual territories and mate on their host flowers19, whereas species of the Hawaiian subgroup perform competitive lekking in the vegetation surrounding their hosts20. The evolution of Drosophila courtship behaviours may therefore have been shaped by the distinct sensory ecology of a species’ mating site and the structure and dynamics of the social groups that form there.

Reproduction and population density in Drosophila erecta, a close relative of D. melanogaster, are tightly linked to the seasonal availability of its host fruit—the screwpine Pandanus21—creating a narrow window of opportunity for individuals to meet and mate22. Such restricted access raises the possibility that D. erecta has evolved behavioural adaptations that couple reproduction to sensory cues emanating from its host, allowing environmental signals not associated with sexual communication to shape mating behaviour. Here we show that the olfactory volatiles emanating from a food patch exert both direct and indirect control over courtship in D. erecta. We find that food volatiles serve as a potent environmental trigger in this species, driving aggregation and serving as a stringent sensory gate to control a male’s sexual arousal. In parallel, environmental food odours shape the visual landscape in which courtship unfolds by encouraging males to approach and sample prospective mates, amplifying visual motion to transform the sensory backdrop of the social scene. D. erecta males are highly sensitive to this increase in visual motion, in contrast to D. melanogaster, pointing to the evolution of a multisensory feedback loop that coordinates male sexual arousal across members of the group, linking courtship to the broader social context. We show that species-specific differences in the sensitivity to environmental cues seem to arise from variation in how olfactory information impinges on conserved components of the courtship circuitry, indicating a potential neural substrate for the rapid tuning of sensory thresholds that govern sexual arousal. Together, these findings show how both ecological and social cues shape species-specific reproductive strategies. Moreover, our observations underscore that the social landscape forms an integral part of the environment—both reflecting the collective dynamics of the group and actively shaping the sensory experience of each individual in it.

To investigate how environmental factors shape the reproductive decisions of species with different ecological preferences, we compared the dynamics of groups of D. erecta and D. melanogaster (five males and five females) provided with a small patch of agar perfumed with food volatiles (diameter 21.5 mm) at the centre of a large arena (diameter 190 mm), a configuration intended to mimic the presence of a localized feeding site (Fig. 1a). Although D. erecta and D. melanogaster have distinct hosts and divergent olfactory preferences16,23, both feed on the yeast microbiota that metabolize decaying fruits and thus display attraction to volatiles arising from fermentation15,24. We therefore used apple cider vinegar (ACV), a broadly appetitive fermentation product, as a generic ‘host’ odour to compare how the chemical environment influences mating behaviours. Flies were fed and maintained as virgins in these assays to minimize their motivation to track towards the odour source for purely metabolic needs or in search of an oviposition site.

We found that in the context of the localized ACV patch, these two ecologically disparate species displayed distinct patterns of behaviour. D. melanogaster broadly explored the arena, giving rise to a dispersed spatial distribution where individuals interacted mostly in pairs and only intermittently in larger groups (Fig. 1c, Extended Data Fig. 1a–e and Supplementary Video 1). By contrast, D. erecta individuals rapidly approached the central ACV patch and remained there for extended bouts (Fig. 1b,e and Extended Data Fig. 1a,b), forming dynamic clusters of three or more interacting flies (Extended Data Fig. 1c–e). D. erecta did not congregate on a patch composed of agar alone (Fig. 1b,e, Extended Data Fig. 1b,f,g and Supplementary Video 1), indicating that food volatiles act as a potent aggregation cue in this species. Consistent with the notion that aggregation serves a social function in D. erecta, more than 90% of their copulations occurred on the ACV patch, in comparison to the spatially dispersed mating observed in D. melanogaster (Fig. 1d,f and Extended Data Fig. 1h). Comparable differences in aggregation and mating behaviour were also apparent when D. erecta and D. melanogaster were presented with a food patch made from the ripe pulp of D. erecta’s native host Pandanus candelabrum (Extended Data Fig. 1i–l), aligned with evidence that ACV shares several key volatiles with the bouquet of fermenting Pandanus fruit25,26,27. Notably, D. erecta refrained from mating entirely in the absence of ACV or Pandanus (Fig. 1f), highlighting the importance of the food patch as a site of reproductive opportunities in this species.

The ACV patch could facilitate mating in D. erecta by simply aggregating individuals, creating a dense social landscape where interactions between males and females are enhanced by proximity. Alternatively, ACV may directly elevate the sexual arousal of males or females on their arrival to the patch. To differentiate between these possibilities, we assessed the courtship dynamics of groups of five males and five females confined to a smaller chamber (diameter 38 mm, 4% area of the large arena; Fig. 1g), allowing us to decouple the effect of spatial proximity from the influence of the chemosensory environment. D. melanogaster males courted vigorously in this arena, independent of the presence of ACV (Fig. 1h and Supplementary Video 2). By contrast, D. erecta males courted only when ACV was present but rarely in its absence, despite the high density of flies (Fig. 1h,i and Supplementary Video 2). Indeed, D. erecta remained dispersed on an agar-only patch (Fig. 1k and Extended Data Fig. 2a), with males rarely approaching or pursuing individuals of either sex (Fig. 1m and Extended Data Fig. 2b,c). In the presence of ACV, however, the spatial distribution of D. erecta individuals was transformed, displaying a pronounced peak at 3 mm (Fig. 1k)—the interfly distance characteristic of courtship pursuit—along with a broad tail, reflecting the dynamic interactions of males as they indiscriminately advanced towards any individual who came into their field of view (FOV) (Fig. 1m and Extended Data Fig. 2d). The rare instances of D. erecta courtship on agar were transient, in contrast to the persistent pursuit observed in the presence of ACV (Fig. 1l,m). ACV thus seems to influence mating behaviours in D. erecta not simply by aggregating males and females but by restructuring their social dynamics.

Different strains of D. melanogaster vary markedly in their sensitivity to courtship cues28. Nevertheless, several strains, including a Cameroonian isolate collected within the overlapping geographic range of D. erecta, courted vigorously even in the absence of ACV (Extended Data Fig. 2e), indicating that the lack of reliance on this environmental signal is a broadly shared trait in this species. Although the limited availability of D. erecta strains precluded a comparable analysis of intraspecific variation, these results indicate that the food volatiles of ACV in D. melanogaster and D. erecta cannot be readily explained by variation within species and instead point to species-level differences in the sensory control of courtship. We therefore extended our analyses to related Drosophila species with distinct host ecologies. Unlike D. erecta, its sister species Drosophila orena, which specializes on Syzygium waterberries18, the closely related generalist Drosophila yakuba16 and the more distantly related specialist Drosophila sechellia, which breeds on Morinda citrifolia17, all vigorously courted conspecifics in both the presence and absence of ACV (Fig. 1j). By contrast, ACV suppressed courtship in D. elegans, a specialist that reproduces on Ipomoea blossoms19, potentially reflecting the limited chemical overlap between ACV and the volatile bouquet of its floral host29. Thus, although the influence of the chemosensory environment on reproductive behaviours varies across species, D. erecta males appear to have evolved a unique dependence on food volatiles to promote their courtship.

How does the chemosensory environment transform the structure of the D. erecta social landscape? Because interactions between individuals were more frequent on ACV, we reasoned that food odours may heighten the salience of visual objects. To explore this possibility, we presented individual males with a stationary fly-sized target—a high-contrast hemispherical bead, 3 mm in diameter (Fig. 2a)—and assessed whether ACV altered their interactions with it. D. melanogaster males were largely indifferent to this visual target, independent of the presence of ACV, spending most of their time circling the perimeter of the arena. D. erecta males, by contrast, remained in close proximity to the bead, an effect that was enhanced by ACV (Fig. 2b,c, Extended Data Fig. 3a–d and Supplementary Video 3). Optogenetic activation of most olfactory sensory neurons using the Orco promoter (Fig. 2d), which replicates the attractive valence of ACV in D. melanogaster30, was sufficient to promote attraction to the bead in D. erecta males on agar, confirming that olfactory input underlies this shift in visual salience (Fig. 2e,f, Extended Data Fig. 3e,f and Supplementary Video 4). Visual approach was not enhanced by the same optogenetic manipulation in D. melanogaster males, underscoring that in D. erecta, olfactory input exerts a species-specific influence on how males interact with their visual surroundings.

Despite the strong attraction of D. erecta males to the bead on ACV, they frequently walked away before returning to it, generating a distinctive radial pattern as males alternated between approach and retreat (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Video 3), suggesting that the olfactory environment may also promote visual exploration. To examine this, we placed five D. erecta males in a large arena (diameter 190 mm) decorated with a lattice of 13 beads arranged in a regular square array (Fig. 2g; distance along edge 30 mm). In the presence of ACV, males spent most of their time near one of the targets but also frequently traversed between them (Extended Data Fig. 3h,i), often taking the shortest route (Fig. 2h–l and Supplementary Video 5). On agar, males restricted their exploration to a small fraction of the arena, remaining at their starting location or near the bead closest to it and rarely visiting any other visual targets (Fig. 2h–l, Extended Data Fig. 3h,i and Supplementary Video 5). Comparing the trajectories of solitary males showed that they display a similar shift in their behaviour in the presence of ACV (Extended Data Fig. 3j), indicating that visual sampling is driven by the chemical, not social, context. Without the bead lattice, males on ACV showed no spatial bias and remained broadly distributed across the arena (Extended Data Fig. 3h). By contrast, the visual exploration of D. melanogaster males was unaffected by the chemosensory environment (Extended Data Fig. 3k–o). Olfactory cues thus selectively promote exploration in D. erecta males.

Beads, although attractive visual targets to males, rarely evoked courtship displays such as the wing extensions associated with song production, indicating they are not perceived as potential mates (Extended Data Fig. 3p). Indeed, replacing a single bead in the array with a decapitated female shifted the search dynamics of males: on encountering the female, males often terminated their exploration and maintained her at close range (Fig. 2m,n and Extended Data Fig. 3q,r). Males occasionally resumed exploration of nearby beads after encountering the female but now often sang as they approached (Fig. 2o), indicating that the distinct cuticular pheromones carried by D. erecta females altered the males’ interpretation of visual targets, triggering a shift from exploration to courtship. Males on agar failed to locate the female in the bead lattice (Fig. 2p), suggesting that their limited exploration in the absence of food volatiles hinders their ability to detect or pursue prospective female mates. Thus, ACV promotes locomotion in D. erecta males to drive more frequent sampling of visual objects in their environment, supporting a form of ‘social foraging’ in which they search for reproductive opportunities rather than food resources.

In D. melanogaster, courtship is triggered by recognition of an appropriate conspecific female, a behavioural switch mediated by a network of male-specific P1 neurons that integrate multisensory signals—including direct excitatory input from pheromone pathways—to gate entry into a persistent arousal state31,32,33,34. D. erecta females, like their D. melanogaster counterparts, produce sex- and species-specific cuticular hydrocarbons35 that robustly excite the P1 neurons of conspecific males36, indicating that they similarly serve as chemical determinants of mate recognition. Food volatiles could therefore facilitate D. erecta courtship simply by encouraging males to approach females and sample their pheromonal cues. Yet even in the rare instances when D. erecta males initiated courtship on agar, their pursuit was fleeting (Fig. 1l,m), pointing to the possibility that food volatiles are required not only to promote encounters with females but also to sustain male sexual arousal in this species.

Consistent with this, although optogenetic activation of P1 neurons in D. melanogaster is sufficient to evoke persistent courtship37,38, in D. erecta it triggered only transient courtship bouts towards an immobilized female in the absence of ACV, with males rapidly resuming indifference after the optogenetic stimulus (Fig. 3a,b). By contrast, in the presence of ACV, P1 neuron activation drove sustained courtship in D. erecta males, suggesting that although P1 neurons promote entry into a state of sexual arousal, olfactory cues are required for its perpetuation. This olfactory dependence extended to pursuit of a moving visual target. In the absence of ACV, P1 neuron activation evoked only brief bouts of courtship towards a fly-sized 3-mm projected dot rotating on the floor of the assay chamber, whereas in the presence of ACV, males pursued and sang to the target spontaneously, even without P1 activation (Fig. 3c–f and Supplementary Videos 6 and 7).

To determine whether continuous olfactory input is required to sustain a male’s arousal, we leveraged the fact that tethered males will spontaneously court a fly-sized visual target projected onto a panorama, faithfully tracking its motion while singing to it38 (Fig. 4a). D. erecta males were largely indifferent to this visual target alone but initiated robust pursuit upon presentation of ACV (Fig. 4b,c,e,f, Extended Data Fig. 4 and Supplementary Video 8). Notably, courtship tracking was transient, with males rapidly terminating their pursuit at the offset of the odour stimulus, reinforcing that ACV is required for the perpetuation of arousal (Fig. 4e,f and Extended Data Fig. 4b–d). D. melanogaster males, by contrast, commenced courtship pursuit at the onset of the visual stimulus and persistently tracked it for the duration of the assay (Fig. 4c,e,f), consistent with evidence that visual cues are sufficient to arouse males of this species, even in the absence of further sensory feedback33,38 (Extended Data Fig. 5).

In D. melanogaster, P1 neurons dynamically modulate the gain of LC10a visual projection neurons via a multisynaptic pathway38,39 (Fig. 4d), forming a concise visuomotor circuit that mediates a male’s faithful courtship pursuit when he is sexually aroused. To assess whether this gain control mechanism is conserved, we generated selective genetic drivers that allowed us to record activity from P1 or LC10a neurons in D. erecta males during courtship pursuit. Despite the divergent sensory dependencies of courtship across species, the activity of P1 neurons was correlated with bouts of pursuit in both D. erecta and D. melanogaster (Fig. 4e and Extended Data Fig. 6). Likewise, LC10a visual responses were amplified during courtship in both species, with robust activation each time the target swept in the progressive direction across the male’s ipsilateral visual field during epochs of active pursuit (Fig. 4f and Extended Data Fig. 7a–c). These observations point to a conserved circuit organization in which the gain of LC10a visual responses is dynamically modulated by a male’s sexual arousal to orchestrate mate pursuit in both species. However, whereas visual cues alone are sufficient to vigorously arouse D. melanogaster males, D. erecta males additionally require continuous olfactory input, which appears to act as a stringent species-specific gate to sustain courtship in this species.

Notably, neither LC10a neurons nor the subset of P1 neurons labelled by our genetic driver were activated by ACV (Fig. 4g), indicating that food volatiles do not sustain arousal through direct olfactory input to these conserved circuit components. Moreover, ACV did not alter P1 responses to female pheromones in either D. erecta or D. melanogaster males (Fig. 4h), reinforcing that food odours do not simply tune the excitability of this integrative node to control a male’s arousal. Rather, we found that ACV activates an array of distributed neurons labelled by the sexually dimorphic Fruitless (Fru) and Doublesex (Dsx) transcription factors (Extended Data Figs. 7d–j and 8). These include subsets of neurons innervating the anterior optic tubercle (AOTu), to which LC10a neurons send their axonal projections, and the lateral protocerebral complex (LPC), which is richly innervated by P1 neurons, consistent with olfactory pathways responsive to environmental food odours converging with sexually dimorphic courtship circuits.

Within the LPC, conspecific female pheromones and ACV activated spatially segregated populations of dsx-expressing neurons, suggesting that sexual and environmental chemosensory signals impinge on these central courtship circuits through parallel pathways (Extended Data Fig. 8). This functional organization aligns with connectomic analyses in D. melanogaster, which show that distinct sensory pathways controlling male courtship act through different subsets of P1 neurons and their downstream targets34. Similar spatially segregated responses to female pheromones and ACV were also apparent in the LPC of D. melanogaster and D. yakuba males (Extended Data Fig. 8), species that court conspecifics regardless of the presence of food volatiles (Fig. 1h,j). The conservation of ACV-responsive populations across species may therefore provide a neural substrate for the latent potential of food odours to promote courtship when other sensory cues are experimentally impoverished, such as in the presence of inanimate visual targets (Extended Data Fig. 5). Despite this conservation, among the species we examined, D. erecta males uniquely depend on ACV to sustain courtship of conspecific females, even when P1 neurons are activated to trigger a state of sexual arousal (Fig. 3b,d). Thus, environmental olfactory signals appear to be differentially weighted across species, serving as a stringent gate for persistent sexual arousal in D. erecta, potentially through sexually dimorphic regulatory nodes that interact with, but remain distinct from, the circuitry mediating mate recognition. In support of this possibility, D. erecta males still discriminate between potential mates even when highly aroused on a food patch, avoiding courtship of unsuitable heterospecific females on the basis of the detection of their inhibitory pheromones (Extended Data Fig. 9a).

How do environmental food volatiles and visual cues coordinately shape male behaviour in naturalistic contexts, where many flies aggregate on a food patch? By driving D. erecta males to sample and pursue visual targets (Fig. 2), ACV results in an increase in fly locomotion that scales with the size of the group (Extended Data Fig. 9b–d). In the context of larger aggregates of flies, this odour-dependent shift in locomotion dramatically altered each male’s visual experience (Fig. 5a–e), expanding the fraction of his visual field occupied by other flies (Fig. 5d) and increasing the visual motion sweeping across his retina (Fig. 5a–c,e). Notably, males and females continued to generate motion cues even when copulating, as D. erecta females reliably began to spin while mounted (Supplementary Video 2). The motion generated by interacting individuals on a food patch thus transforms the visual backdrop, suggesting that it may convey broader information about social context to further control male arousal. Indeed, even on agar, males displayed correlated bursts of locomotor activity and courtship (Fig. 5f,g, Extended Data Fig. 9e,f and Supplementary Video 9), consistent with the motion cues emanating from nearby flies transiently elevating male arousal. These observations point to a feedback loop in which ACV both promotes courtship and reshapes the sensory landscape in which courtship unfolds.

Aligned with the synergistic effect of food odours and visual motion on arousal, we found that individual D. erecta males on ACV displayed more vigorous courtship when part of a larger group (five pairs of conspecifics) than when presented with just a single female in isolation—an effect not observed in D. melanogaster (Fig. 5h). Courtship towards a conspecific female was similarly enhanced by the presence of a same-sized group of Drosophila simulans, underscoring that these courtship-promoting cues are not species-specific (Fig. 5i). Moreover, male courtship in D. erecta was elevated by simply projecting two continuously rotating dots onto the assay chamber floor, highlighting that visual motion is sufficient to promote arousal (Fig. 5j and Supplementary Video 10).

Male courtship in D. erecta thus appears to be influenced by both the chemosensory environment of a food patch and the dynamic motion of the social group on it. To explore the composite effects of food odour and visual motion, we recorded the group dynamics of five pairs of D. erecta males and females on agar or ACV, approximated each fly’s centroid position as a high-contrast ellipse and then played back these visual motion cues to a ‘test’ D. erecta male and female pair (Fig. 5k and Supplementary Video 11). We observed that courtship in D. erecta males was enhanced by playback of the visual scene recorded from flies interacting on ACV (Fig. 5l). Courtship of the test pair on ACV was comparable when presented with either the visual scene recorded on agar or the scene recorded on ACV but slowed to match the velocity of flies on agar. D. erecta males therefore appear to be sensitive to the speed of visual targets, rather than their distinct dynamics. Notably, males on ACV, presented with the visual motion cues recorded on ACV, displayed the highest level of courtship, supporting the synergistic effects of the chemosensory and visual environment. Neither the presence of ACV nor playback of the social scenes recorded on ACV further increased the vigorous courtship displayed by D. melanogaster (Fig. 5m). Together, our data indicate that the chemosensory environment of a food patch promotes and perpetuates a male’s sexual arousal in D. erecta by driving males to sample and pursue visual targets, which in turn reshapes the visual social scene to further reinforce mating behaviours (Fig. 5n).

In Drosophila, courtship unfolds as a tightly coupled feedback loop between prospective mates, continuously shaped by the reciprocal exchange of sensory signals between male and female. Here we examined how this feedback loop is influenced by the ecological and social context of mating sites, showing that in D. erecta, both the olfactory volatiles emanating from a food patch and the collective dynamics of flies congregating there coordinately control courtship. We find that food odour regulates male courtship in D. erecta through two distinct mechanisms: first, by gating a male’s arousal to promote pursuit of potential mates; and second, by further enhancing his arousal through the increased visual motion of other individuals on the food patch, creating a feedback loop that elevates courtship in environments where reproductive opportunities are likely to be most abundant. D. erecta has thus evolved a striking dependence on environmental cues as an integral part of its reproductive strategy.

D. erecta’s distinct sensitivity to its environment may represent an adaptation well aligned with its specialization on Pandanus fruit, a seasonally restricted host that is only transiently available for reproduction. Breeding populations of D. erecta surge during the two-to-four-month period when Pandanus fruit is present and then sharply decline as the availability of fruit wanes21,22. During seasons when Pandanus is scarce, D. erecta can opportunistically subsist on other fruits, but in greatly diminished numbers, pointing to the inherent cost of specialization on ephemeral resources21,22. Although it remains unclear whether D. erecta’s reliance on food odours for mating is a direct adaptation to the seasonality of its host, such strict dependence underscores how Pandanus serves as a key ecological hub supporting the reproductive cycle of this species.

Variable access to mating sites, however, is not limited to D. erecta, as many drosophilids use fleeting host substrates to reproduce15,17,24. The chemosensory environment of mating sites may therefore modulate sexual arousal more generally. Consistent with this, the ability of ACV to promote courtship appears to be maintained in a latent form across Drosophila species, emerging only when males are presented with a relatively impoverished two-dimensional visual target (Extended Data Fig. 5) or in darkness7. Likewise, ACV has been shown to alter the salience of visual objects during flight in D. melanogaster40, indicating that the arousing effect of food volatiles is broadly conserved, with its behavioural expression quantitatively tuned by species-specific sensory thresholds. The modulation of reproductive behaviours by chemosensory cues not directly related to sexual signalling thus seems an evolutionarily malleable trait, shaped by the ecology and social landscape in which species evolve. Indeed, although ACV, a chemical mimic of fermenting substrates, enhances the sexual arousal of species that mate on decaying fruit, it inhibits courtship in the flower breeder D. elegans. Sexual arousal can therefore be enhanced or suppressed by environmental contexts, underscoring the bidirectional influence of the chemical environment on reproductive behaviour.

In D. erecta, the sensory threshold for arousal appears to be particularly high, rendering sustained courtship strictly dependent on continuous olfactory input. Indeed, transient activation of P1 neurons is insufficient to drive the persistent pursuit that other Drosophila species display37,38, indicating that olfactory input acts downstream of P1 neurons or on other circuit elements to perpetuate a male’s arousal. Consistent with this idea, ACV not only sustains P1-mediated arousal but can independently promote courtship pursuit. Although the precise neural locus of ACV’s action remains to be elucidated, the comparable activation of Dsx+ neurons in D. erecta, D. melanogaster and D. yakuba suggests that species-specific gating of courtship reflects quantitative reweighting of conserved olfactory inputs rather than recruitment of novel pathways. For example, D. melanogaster can become highly aroused through visual input alone, consistent with evidence that subsets of P1 neurons receive direct LC10a input34. P1 neuron activity in turn modulates the gain of LC10a responses38, forming a recurrent circuit poised to perpetuate visually guided courtship pursuit. One intriguing possibility is that ACV-responsive olfactory pathways gate this feedback loop in D. erecta, such that both vision and food volatiles are required for males to sustain their sexual arousal.

Such subtle variation in conserved elements of courtship circuits underscores how these pathways can diversify while maintaining their core function, enabling different species to rapidly adapt to their distinct ecological needs41. This flexibility may be facilitated by their modular architecture. Pheromone responses in P1 neurons, for example, are unaffected by ACV (Fig. 4h), and responses of Dsx+ neurons in the LPC to pheromones and ACV are spatially distinct (Extended Data Fig. 8), indicating that olfactory and pheromonal pathways remain at least partly segregated. Such an organization may preserve the ability of males to discriminate between conspecific and heterospecific females even when highly aroused on a food patch (Extended Data Fig. 9a). The modularity of courtship circuits36,42,43 thus favours the independent diversification of distinct circuit elements, allowing D. erecta to evolve behavioural sensitivity to environmental cues and exploit fleeting reproductive opportunities such as those provided by its host fruit.

Beyond the direct modulation of circuits perpetuating sexual arousal, ACV also serves to structure the social landscape at multiple levels. First, ACV triggers D. erecta flies to rapidly track to the odour source. Flies in our experiments were fed and sated, suggesting their aggregation on a food patch is driven by reproductive rather than metabolic needs. Second, once on the food patch, ACV promotes males to sample visual targets, presumably in search of sexually receptive females, transforming the visual scene through their collective motion during this form of ‘social foraging’. Finally, because D. erecta males are highly sensitive to visual motion, their courtship is further enhanced by the presence of moving visual targets. This in turn orchestrates the behaviour of nearby individuals such that their bouts of pursuit become synchronized, an effect most apparent on agar, where periods of immobility are punctuated by intermittent bursts of activity across the group.

Aggregating in larger groups increases a male’s access to potential mating partners but at the same time promotes competition, which is thought to be fierce on a crowded food patch44,45. Reproductive success in these competitive environments requires that males fend off their rivals and isolate a female for long enough to initiate copulation46. As a consequence, a male’s sensitivity to visual motion may confer an advantage in gaining access to potential mating opportunities by triggering him to chase after any visual object that may be a receptive female or courting pair. Indeed, on a food patch, males become attracted to all visual objects and indiscriminately approach a male, a female or a bead (Extended Data Fig. 2d). The drive to compete therefore likely structures the collective activity observed in groups of D. erecta.

Although Drosophila is not typically thought to engage in collective social behaviours, the dynamics of D. erecta on a food patch may follow the same principles that structure schooling fish13, swarming locusts12 or murmurations of starlings14,47. In these systems, simple local interaction rules—such as attraction and repulsion among neighbours—align the behaviour of many individuals into concerted actions12,48,49. Likewise, in D. erecta aggregates, the behaviour of neighbouring flies serves as an environmental reference that modulates courtship behaviour: not through direct signalling between prospective sexual partners, but through generic visual motion cues that indicate the broader environmental context. However, in contrast to canonical examples of collective behaviours—where information is thought to be cooperatively shared to optimize the fitness of all individuals in the group—D. erecta’s sensitivity to group dynamics probably evolved to enhance individual reproductive fitness to succeed in a competitive environment. Our results thus indicate that collective behaviours can emerge from diverse selective pressures, giving rise to coordinated actions even in species not generally considered ‘social’.

Population behaviour was summarized by the fraction of males courting across assays over time.

Males used for optogenetics assays were tethered as described above in ‘Tethered virtual reality assays’. Assays lasted 210 s beginning with a 30-s baseline followed by visual stimulus delivery for the remaining 180 s. One 1.5-mm optic fibre (Edmund Optics) was coupled to a high-power red LED (650 nm, LED Engin) mounted on a heat sink (Ohmite) and placed directly above the head of the fly. Orco+ olfactory sensory neurons were activated by pulsing light at 5 Hz with a pulse length of 0.1 s for 60 s after 90 s. Tracking index was calculated as described above.

Males were tethered as described above. After recovery, the plate was filled with saline (108 mM NaCl, 5 mM KCl, 2 mM CaCl2, 8.2 mM MgCl2, 4 mM NaHCO3, 1 mM NaH2PO4, 5 mM trehalose, 10 mM sucrose, 5 mM HEPES, pH 7.5 with osmolarity adjusted to 265 mOsm) covering the fly’s head. The cuticula above the antenna and between the eyes was cut with a 30-gauge needle and removed with forceps without anaesthesia to provide optical access to the central brain. The tracheae covering the top of the brain were removed using forceps, after which flies were transferred to the ball to recover in darkness for 45 min.

Experiments were performed with an Ultima two-photon laser scanning microscope (Bruker Nanosystems) with a Chameleon Ultra II Ti:Sapphire laser. Samples were excited at a wavelength of 920 nm, and emitted fluorescence was detected with a GaAsP photomultiplier tube (Hamamatsu). Images were acquired in Prairie View (v.5.6; Bruker) with a ×40 Olympus water-immersion objective with 0.8 NA. To reduce noise caused by light emitted by the projector during imaging, the red and green LED channels were switched off. In addition, a piece of blue-light filter paper (Rosco) was placed in front of the projector lens. After the male’s resting period on the ball, the objective was lowered over the brain to identify the brain region of interest (ROI). We centred a small ROI yielding an imaging rate of 4–8 Hz. Laser power was kept low to reduce stress on the fly. ROIs were drawn in a single hemisphere, depending on the neural population imaged. For P1 and LC10a imaging, an ROI was drawn in the LPC and AOTu neuropil, respectively, where axonal projections were densest. For fru and dsx imaging, a larger ROI was drawn, encompassing neurites in both the LPC and adjacent AOTu to capture neural activity simultaneously.

For experiments with a moving visual stimulus, experimental parameters and calculation of the tracking index were as described in ‘Tethered virtual reality assays’. For pheromone tapping experiments, a virgin conspecific female (4–8 days old) was mounted on a micromanipulator serving as pheromone stimulus and presented in sets of six touch bouts 30 s apart to allow neural activity to return to baseline between taps. Pheromone and ACV responses in dsx+ neurons in the LPC were assessed in the same ROI for each animal, where the order of presentation of odour and pheromone stimuli was randomized.

Imaging data were postprocessed and analysed using Fiji (v.2.14.0, NIH). For each experimental recording, frames were motion corrected and aligned along the x and y axes by the Template Matching plugin. Residual background noise from the projector was reduced from raw time series using the Despeckle function. Finally, an ROI was drawn across the entire population of interest containing neuropil to extract the average fluorescence. Fluorescence was normalized in MATLAB for each recording using the average fluorescence over the first 30 s of an experiment as baseline (F0). ∆F/F was defined as ∆Fi/F0 = (Fi − F0)/F0, where i denotes the current frame. Because imaging data were collected at a lower frame rate than behavioural data, behavioural data were downsampled using linear interpolation at the imaging time points. Data from pheromone and ACV imaging of Dsx+ neurons were normalized by matching ∆F/F of pheromone responses across species and adjusting odour responses accordingly.

MATLAB was used for statistical analyses and plotting. The Shapiro–Wilk method was used to test normality, and the appropriate parametric/non-parametric statistical test was used. In cases for which several comparisons were made, appropriate post hoc tests were conducted as indicated in Supplementary Table 1. All statistical tests used were two-tailed.
