None of this week’s threats has proved real, but each one still cost a diversion, a search and a rebuilt schedule. Whoever sent them, the hoaxes have added to a list of disruptions, from conflict-driven fuel costs to closed airspace, that is making routine flying in Europe ever more challenging.

Bomb threats disrupted at least 27 flights across Europe on Thursday, the second wave this week. No explosives have been found.

French fighter jets intercepted Aer Lingus flight EI402 from Dublin to Rome, carrying more than 150 passengers, and escorted it to the Évreux air base in Normandy. The French prime minister’s office said the pilot had not cooperated as expected, without giving more detail. Bulgaria’s air force escorted three airliners to the ground. In Madrid, Air Europa and Iberia aircraft were halted, emptied and searched after Spanish authorities received three emailed threats in less than 20 minutes, the Associated Press reported. At least 27 flights across Europe were disrupted Thursday by bomb threats, the second wave in a week, and no explosives were found in any case. French fighter jets intercepted an Aer Lingus Dublin–Rome flight carrying more than 150 passengers and escorted it to an air base in Normandy, Bulgaria's air force escorted three airliners to the ground, and Madrid authorities searched Air Europa and Iberia aircraft after receiving three emailed threats in under 20 minutes. Earlier threats on Monday and Tuesday hit flights operated by Wizz Air, Ryanair, LOT, and Scandinavian Airlines, plus a cruise ship in Greece. No government has said who is responsible, and Bloomberg reported no pattern in the carriers or routes targeted. Matthew Borie of Osprey Flight Solutions told the AP the threats could be copycats, a single person or group, or "hybrid warfare" by state actors, and pointed to a possible Russian link; Moscow has denied European accusations of sabotage this year. The operational math is the problem: each threat an airline acts on means a diversion or stopped departure, a full search, often a replacement aircraft, crews timing out, and passengers rebooked — and Skift has reported that insurance typically doesn't cover those commercial losses. The hoaxes arrive while European carriers are already absorbing jet fuel prices that have more than doubled year over year, Middle East airspace closures that lengthen Asia routes, and drone incursions at airports.
