Elizabeth Debicki Was Tailor-Made for This Moment

Tanggal: 2026-11-11

The star found two projects worth waiting for: Wicker and The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth.

Every item on this page was chosen by an ELLE editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

You don’t turn down David Fincher. Elizabeth Debicki was shocked and thrilled when an email from the acclaimed director landed in her inbox. He had signed on to direct The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth, a continuation of the story of the stuntman played by Brad Pitt in the 2019 Quentin Tarantino-directed film Once Upon a Time in...Hollywood, and he wanted her to audition. She thought it was a hoax. It wasn’t. “There are no-brainer directors to say yes to. David would be one,” Debicki explains. She has some experience with no-brainer directors—after all, she worked with Christopher Nolan, Baz Luhrmann, Steve McQueen, James Gunn, and Guy Ritchie, all before turning 30.

It’s clear why she’s so in demand. Her talent and striking looks, of course—for the curious, she’s just over 6’2”—but she also has a rare balance of emotional availability and strength. After making her feature film debut as cynical flapper Jordan Baker in Luhrmann’s The Great Gatsby in 2013, she’s been a woman struggling to survive after the death of her abusive husband in Widows, and the steely and mysterious partner to an arms dealer in the BBC series The Night Manager. In Cliff Booth, Debicki plays a successful agent in a male-dominated Hollywood, who finds herself drawn to Pitt’s character in the film, a role that won him an Oscar for Once Upon a Time in...Hollywood. Taking part in a continuation of a beloved film presents a lot of pressure, but Debicki entered the story seamlessly, with the chops to go toe-to-toe with one of the most famous actors in the world.

Her most famous role, a transcendent performance as Princess Diana in seasons 5 and 6 of The Crown, won her a Golden Globe and tremendous praise. But when the show ended in 2023, it wasn’t clear what would come next. “I was really looking for a while, after I did The Crown, for something I wanted to do,” Debicki says. “It took a little while for me to find those jobs. When you play a real-life person, you can come out really synonymous with a piece of work, and people think of you solely in relation to that piece of work, which is totally unsustainable. You do a bit of reeling after you do a job.”

She was waiting, a bit anxiously, for the right part when she read the script for Wicker, the story of a fisherwoman (Olivia Colman) who marries a dream husband that her town’s basket weaver made for her. “I was really grateful when it landed in my inbox, because I had done that thing you do when you give up and say, ‘Good luck with that,’” she says. “But usually when you do that, that’s when you get jobs.” The wry fairy tale features Debicki as the Tailor’s Wife, a powerful figure in a small fishing village. Debicki’s haughty and socially controlling character can’t handle the changes in status that come to her town after the wicker husband appears. “It was very funny, so I could stretch in it,” she says. “It had this light through it.”

Debicki, in a subtle but elegant jeans-and-jacket pairing, is warm, affable, and talkative in person when we meet for tea in a warm and cozy café at the Warren Street Hotel on a very rainy day. Her poise comes naturally: She grew up in a household of performers in Australia. Her parents were ballet dancers, and her mother ran a dance studio after she retired from performing. “My dad used to smuggle us into the back. So I saw a lot more ballet and abstract dance than most 6- to 12-year-olds,” she says of her cultural education. She also danced herself.

Shortly after graduating from theater school, Debicki got the chance to audition for Gatsby with Luhrmann. It was a true long shot for an actress who only had one credit to her name, but Debicki got the part, playing an iconic character alongside Carey Mulligan and Leonardo DiCaprio. Getting the role so early in her career was disorienting for Debicki. “I just remember thinking, That was it. I guess I’ll never work again,” she says. The film took over a year to be released, and Debicki returned to the Australian home she shared with roommates, where her mattress sat on milk crates. “It was whiplash,” she recalls. The experience showed her that a career in acting wouldn’t always move forward in a steady line. “I don’t think I have a trajectory, certainly not one that ascends upward. You learn how to deal with the flow of a job. You’re not in control of how the job is overseen, and you just keep going onward and looking for work.”

What Debicki currently has her eye on? Season 2 of Widow’s Bay. “Call my agent!” she says. “I’ll do anything!”

[When I auditioned for The Great Gatsby] I was 20 and I’d never been to America. I flew in; I went to The Standard Hotel on Sunset Boulevard. I had the audition the next day. Then there was a knock on the door, and I looked through the peephole. There was Baz Luhrmann’s magnified fish-eyed eyeball. I was standing in my bare feet in The Standard Hotel thinking, That’s Baz Luhrmann.”

Then I went over to the Chateau Marmont, and I auditioned with Tobey Maguire. It was so Hollywood. I was 20 and I was like, “This is ridiculous. I’ll probably never come back here again.” So I went down to the restaurant and ordered a glass of champagne and a grilled cheese sandwich. Then I looked over my shoulder, and up on the balcony was Leo [DiCaprio]. Leo was peeping over and Baz was saying to him, “That’s her.” I remember thinking, “Fifty-fifty—I’ve either got the job or I don’t.”

Steve McQueen created an environment on the set of Widows that made us feel like we were making something really important. It was this beautiful moment of life where I was in Chicago in summer with these amazing women on the set. We just had so much fun. It was a great feeling in the city, so much music and food. It was just a dream.

I got my hair done at a hair salon by a friend of my sister’s. She curled my hair into some pin curls, and it was a really windy, rainy day in Melbourne. So I put a grandma plastic cap on and I walked around the city like that for a couple of hours because I valued my pin curls over my own dignity in order to get that audition looked at.

The only times I’ve really learned something is when I’ve failed miserably, usually in front of a lot of people. There are times when you learn something because you actually allow the thing to take you in another direction than you planned. There’s never not a degree of humiliation in that, and that’s really important. There were times when I would hear the grips in the background: “Oh fuck, that was bad.” And inevitably something would come out of that somehow.

The thing that’s taken me the longest to learn is what to do with my real life. When acting is the thing that you obsess over, that interests you most, that you’re the most driven to continue to do, it’s a bit of a compulsion, and your real life can get waylaid. It took me a while to learn, in my late 20s and into my 30s, that it helps you with your work to have a life that you like and that you want to live.

Comedy. I never really do it. In the industry, you get pigeonholed in a way that is both helpful and unhelpful. I’m really like a clown, but I don’t play that lots.

I find it really difficult to manage my own reception. When I go on Instagram, I feel bad about myself pretty quickly. After 20 minutes, your brain will go, “Something’s wrong. Something’s wrong. What is it?” Then I sit there and think, What’s going on? The mechanics just make people think comparatively. Personally, for whatever my own sensitivities [are], I can’t really tolerate it. I don’t have it because it makes me feel panicked and bad about myself.
