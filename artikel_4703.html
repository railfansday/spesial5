Should Borderline Become Relational Sensitivity Disorder?

Updated November 8, 2026 | Reviewed by Jessica Schrader

For more than half a century, borderline personality disorder (BPD) has survived an unusual amount of conceptual scrutiny. Initially regarded as a variant of schizophrenia, it has subsequently been reconceptualized by some as a mood disorder, a post-traumatic disorder, or an impulse-control disorder. Others have called for the diagnosis to be abandoned altogether. Each time, the syndrome has proved more durable than its critics expected (Gunderson, 2009). Today, however, the most serious threat to the diagnosis comes from a different direction: the movement toward dimensional models of personality disorder.

ICD-11, the World Health Organization's diagnostic system, has already replaced the traditional personality disorder categories with a single diagnosis rated by severity and described by maladaptive trait domains. Borderline survived only as an optional "borderline pattern" specifier, a concession many regarded as a political compromise. The DSM-5 Alternative Model for Personality Disorders moves in a similar direction. If this trend continues, borderline psychopathology risks being reduced to a configuration of trait scores, despite decades of evidence supporting its validity as a distinct psychiatric syndrome.

Against this backdrop, a group of clinicians, researchers, and people with lived experience in the United Kingdom has launched the Relational Sensitivity Project. Founded by Orestis Zavlis, with Peter Fonagy, Peter Tyrer, Michael Moutoussis, and Lorraine Liu among its founding members, the initiative asks the World Health Organization to remove the borderline pattern specifier from the personality disorders section of ICD-11 and to recognize relational sensitivity disorder as a separate psychiatric diagnosis. It would not be a subtype or modifier of personality disorder.

The proposed criteria will look familiar to anyone who has worked with borderline patients: fear of abandonment, unstable relationships, an unstable sense of self, intense and reactive affect, impulsive efforts to soothe distress, and self-harm. The argument is that these features are neither broad enough nor stable enough to count as personality in the usual sense. They cluster in emotional and relational life, and they frequently remit over time (Zanarini et al., 2012).

It is particularly striking that Tyrer and Fonagy are both involved. Tyrer chaired the working group that developed the dimensional ICD-11 model, while Fonagy, with Anthony Bateman, developed mentalization-based treatment specifically for borderline patients. Their collaboration suggests that the case for preserving borderline psychopathology, even under a different name and classification, is not confined to any one theoretical camp.

Interestingly, the proposal aligns closely with the work of the Harvard psychiatrist John Gunderson, who did more than anyone to establish BPD as a valid and reliable psychiatric diagnosis. Gunderson (1984) believed that the core psychopathology of BPD involved a profound disturbance in interpersonal functioning. Borderline patients are exquisitely sensitive to rejection and abandonment, and their symptoms rise and fall with their perception of the availability and responsiveness of the important people in their lives. He called this interpersonal hypersensitivity (Gunderson & Lyons-Ruth, 2008). "Relational sensitivity" is, in many respects, a new name for Gunderson's central insight.

Late in his career, Gunderson (2013) also argued that BPD should be moved to Axis I of DSM, separating it from the other personality disorders, which he viewed as more amenable to dimensional classification. He proposed the same reclassification for antisocial personality disorder. The Relational Sensitivity Project arrives at much the same conclusion by a different route.

In my forthcoming book, The Borderline Dilemma (Karnac Books), I make a related argument. Psychiatric classification should be guided primarily by the validity of our diagnostic constructs rather than by a priori commitments to either categorical or dimensional models. Some personality disorders are clearly dimensional, but borderline psychopathology has substantially greater empirical support as a distinct psychiatric syndrome than most of the other personality disorders. Although BPD involves underlying personality traits, the disorder cannot be adequately reduced to their combination (Paris, 2007). I am therefore not convinced that BPD belongs among the personality disorders as traditionally conceived.

Where I part company with the proposal is the name. I favor retaining the term borderline, in part because it preserves recognition of the frequent psychotic symptoms that helped define the syndrome. Under interpersonal stress, borderline patients may experience paranoid ideation, dissociation, and hallucinations; one study found a point prevalence of hallucinations of 43% (Niemantsverdriet et al., 2017). Historically, the word borderline reminded clinicians that these patients may temporarily regress to psychotic modes of experience while retaining the capacity to return to intact reality testing.

I am also skeptical that a new name will, by itself, defeat stigma. Stigma attaches less to words than to the conditions they denote, and it tends to migrate to new terminology. What ultimately reduces stigma is better understanding and more effective treatment. If a change is needed, the term originally used by Grinker and colleagues, the borderline syndrome (Grinker et al., 1968), would mark the disorder's distinct status while preserving continuity with more than half a century of research.

Nevertheless, I am sympathetic to the broader nosological argument advanced by Zavlis and colleagues. As dimensional models increasingly reshape personality disorder diagnosis, there is a strong case for preserving borderline psychopathology as a distinct category rather than reducing it to a configuration of maladaptive traits. The specialized treatments that have transformed the outlook for borderline patients, including dialectical behavior therapy, mentalization-based treatment, transference-focused psychotherapy, and good psychiatric management, were developed for a specific syndrome, not for isolated trait dimensions.

Whether it is called borderline personality disorder or relational sensitivity disorder, the more important question is whether the syndrome itself remains recognizable as a coherent and clinically meaningful form of psychopathology. On that question, the Relational Sensitivity Project and I are on the same side.
