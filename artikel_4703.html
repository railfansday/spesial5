The Boon of Being Highly Sensitive

Are you more easily overstimulated than others by what’s going on around you? Maybe you’re easily distracted or stressed by loud noises (it’s the kitchen exhaust fan for me), bright artificial lighting, uncomfortable clothing, or other people’s moods. If so, you may be among the 30% of people who are highly sensitive to sensory input from the environment.

We all navigate the world using our senses. It’s just that some of us seem to take in more and be more impacted by what we take in. Scientists are still making discoveries about sensory processing in different situations and for different people.

Here’s a fascinating example — did you know that human hands can sense the presence of something without touching it? Last year, a group of researchers published the first study demonstrating remote sensing in humans (Chen, Crucianelli, Versace, & Jamone, 2025). Participants in their study were able to detect that an object was buried in sand when their fingers were still 6.9 cm away from the object. How? Humans can perceive subtle changes in the sand caused by the object. What’s even more incredible is that humans were 30% more accurate than robotic sensors. Specifically, the robotic sensors had more false positives (“sensing” an object when there was not one). The researchers suspect humans are better able to interpret touch sensations because our nervous systems are designed to filter noise, weigh uncertainty, and make decisions about ambiguous situations.

Clearly, there's still a lot to learn about sensory processing in humans. These days, it’s widely accepted that some of us are more sensitive to the environment than others. As we understand more about sensory sensitivity, more research is focusing on an idea intuitive to many of us: sensory processing sensitivity comes with great benefits.

Dr. Elaine Aron began researching sensory processing sensitivity (SPS; also called high sensitivity) in 1991. SPS is well summarized as “lower sensory thresholds, susceptibility to overstimulation, deeper processing of environmental information, heightened emotional and physiological reactivity, and increased awareness of subtle stimuli” (Weyn, Greven, Schmidt, Gillebert, 2025).

SPS isn't a diagnosis. It's a normally distributed personality trait or aspect of temperament. This means we all fall somewhere on the sensitivity continuum. While 20-30% of us are highly sensitive, 40-50% of us are moderately sensitive, and 20-30% of us have low sensitivity (Lionetti et al. 2018). Evolutionary theories suggest there is an advantage for humans if some of us (not too many) are highly sensitive (Greven et al. 2019).

Sensory sensitivity is heritable. Studies have found that 47% of the variability in sensory sensitivity is explained by genetics (Assary, 2020). It’s also biologically based. Neuroimaging studies have found that folks with SPS have greater activation in brain areas involved in sensory processing (e.g., awareness, integration, empathy, preparation for action, higher-order visual processing).

Sensory processing sensitivity is typically measured using the Highly Sensitive Person Scale, which was revised earlier this year (Pluess et al. 2026). It's an 18-item questionnaire that captures six domains of sensitivity: 1. sensitivity to details 2. depth of processing 3. social sensitivity 4. sensitivity to positive experiences 5. emotional reactivity and 6. overstimulation.

If you (or someone you’re close to) has high sensory sensitivity, you don’t need me to tell you about its challenges, which can include overstimulation, exhaustion, and stress. What might not be top of mind are the benefits that can come along with high SPS. You may be surprised to learn that folks who have high SPS may actually be better positioned to thrive in supportive, positive environments (Damatac et al. 2025).

If you’re someone who is more easily overstimulated by unpleasant sensory input like the kitchen fan, you’re likely also more easily positively influenced by positive input such as a view of the gorgeous fall leaves. And the positive benefit is likely to be longer-lasting. This makes logical sense. It’s also been supported by the research. In one example, Lionette et al. (2018) found that folks who had high SPS had a more positive response to happy video clips as compared with people who had lower sensory sensitivity. Research has also found that folks with high SPS can actually experience more benefit from psychological interventions and social support as compared with people with lower SPS (Damatac, et al. 2025; Nocentini, Menesini, & Pluess 2018).

This sensitivity to positive experiences overlaps with another dimension of SPS — aesthetic sensitivity. Aesthetic sensitivity describes the deep emotional and physiological response to beauty, art, and nature often experienced by folks who are highly sensitive.

Chacón et al. (2024) found aesthetic sensitivity among folks with high SPS strongly correlated with the personality traits openness and agreeableness (a replication of previous findings), as well as adaptive coping strategies including emotional expression and regulation. They found that folks with high SPS and high aesthetic sensitivity more effectively navigated emotionally dysregulating challenges in daily living, such as problematic situations at work.

Another recent study found that folks with sensory sensitivity demonstrated higher cognitive flexibility, which is associated with adaptive coping and problem-solving (Shippers, Hoogman & Greven, 2025). The study also found that folks with high SPS who were more sensitive to aesthetic beauty scored higher on measures of curiosity and humor.

If you’re highly sensitive, you're likely more inclined toward creativity and empathy. Laros-van Gorkom et al. (2025) found that folks with high sensory sensitivity are more likely to be more empathic and more likely to have creative ideas. In their study, folks with high aesthetic sensitivity were more likely to engage in creative activities.

Those of us who have high sensory sensitivity experience awe more frequently. For example, Dunne et al (2024) found that folks with high SPS were more likely to experience a profound emotional and physiological response to the beauty of nature. This experience of awe directs attention away from ourselves and toward our natural surroundings, which deepens our connection to the natural world. The experience of awe is widely known to enhance well-being.

This rally around the advantages of SPS is extremely encouraging. It confirms that sensory sensitivity can be a strength promoting well-being. In positive environments, folks with SPS are actually uniquely positioned to flourish.
