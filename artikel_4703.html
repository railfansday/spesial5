When Stress Drives Us to the Bathroom

Stress can drive many people to the bathroom. That stress can come in the form of an overbearing boss, an abusive partner, or just bad news. These people are wired to redirect stress to their gut, and more specifically, defecation. Welcome to anxiety poop.

Just seeing something stressful can make many of us nauseated. You don’t need to actually ride a rickety roller coaster; just looking at it can provide a frisson of anxiety.

You may think you don’t have this issue, but everyone has a strong gut-brain connection. Before a presentation, we get butterflies in our stomach. When we have to make quick decisions under stress, we defer to our gut feelings. When we have to do something difficult, it can be gut-wrenching.

But how is the brain connected to the gut?

Anxiety poop is a great example of the gut-brain axis at work. Stress, whether caused by the outside environment or internal microbes, causes serotonin to be released in the gut. That induces peristalsis and an urge to find a bathroom STAT. Not fun, but also not uncommon.

Dialed up high enough, anxiety puts out an all-points bulletin to the body. It affects the fight-or-flight response (sympathetic), as well as the rest-and-digest response (parasympathetic).

The sympathetic fight-or-flight response is wired to the brain via spinal nerves. When triggered, we produce adrenalin and cortisol — the stress drugs — which push us to hulk out, either to fight or run.

Meanwhile, the parasympathetic rest-and-digest system is wired to the brain by the vagus nerve. When this response is triggered, special cells lining the gut secrete serotonin.

We typically think of serotonin as a chemical messenger in the brain. That’s what SSRIs target. But 90% of your serotonin is in your gut, and it doesn’t act like an antidepressant. Instead, it’s in charge of peristalsis, the rhythmic constrictions that pulse your food through the gut. Under stress, a lot of serotonin is released, pulsing your food with a vengeance.

Why has evolution fashioned such behavior?

There are two good reasons: One is the need for speed. If a tiger starts to chase you, you drop what you’re holding and run. For some extra weight loss, your body uses the serotonin technique to evacuate your bowels, making you lighter on your feet. Embarrassment can come later, if you survive the tiger. As a bonus, the energy you would have spent on digestion is now available for sprinting.

This is great for dealing with tigers, but less-than-suitable for menacing bosses or spouses. Constantly being in this state of hypervigilance can damage your gut lining, especially since the fight-or-flight response has usurped all the blood meant for the gut.

The second evolutionary reason is because your anxiety circuits have been repurposed for food poisoning. You have cells in your gut that can detect an invasion by pathogens. They communicate this info to the brain which decides, based on the strength of the signal, whether to purge or not. It tends to err on the dramatic side, and a severe bout of food poisoning will cause speedy evacuation from both ends of the digestive tract.

These two signals of stress—one from the outside and one from the inside—are handled by the same brain circuits. Nature is frugal, and she loves to repurpose anatomy. One way or another, anxiety is going to disrupt your gut.

That makes this circuitry cyclic: A disturbed gut can create anxiety, and anxiety can disturb the gut. This can lead to chronic conditions like IBS, Crohn’s, IBD, and gastric reflux. Over time, it can snowball into systemic inflammation, threatening the health of all your other organs.

We have little control over our external stress, although some people find meditating can help them deal with a crazy world. But we have plenty of control over our diet and exercise.

It can be hard to break habits—who wants to voluntarily quit candy or cocktails?—but the payoff is surprisingly large. Once your microbiome is happy, it becomes harder to perturb. A healthy gut can fend off pathogens better than your immune system can. That builds resilience and lowers your natural anxiety.

And all of that can keep you from pooping your pants under stress. So give it a try.
