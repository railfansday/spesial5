These 12 Follow-Ups to Grammy Album of the Year Winners Didn’t Receive a Single Nomination: Full List

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Harry Styles is in good company. Such music legends as Carole King, George Harrison and Lionel Richie experienced the same fate.

Harry Styles was doubtless disappointed on Monday (Nov. 16), when Kiss All the Time. Disco, Occasionally., his follow-up to the Grammy album of the year winner Harry’s House, didn’t receive a single Grammy nomination. Styles may take some small comfort in knowing that he’s in good company. Such music legends as Carole King, George Harrison and Lionel Richie experienced the same fate with their follow-ups to Grammy album of the year winners.

Since 1970, 12 follow-ups to Grammy-winning albums have failed to receive a single Grammy nomination. (Before 1970, album release patterns were much different, with artists frequently releasing multiple albums in a year.)

Many of these follow-ups to Grammy-winning albums did well on the Billboard charts. Seven of the 12 (including Styles’ album) topped the Billboard 200. Four of them (again including Styles’ album) both topped the Billboard 200 and spawned at least one single that topped the Billboard Hot 100. One of them – Bee Gees’ Spirits Having Flown topped the Billboard 200 for six weeks and spawned three No. 1 hits.

By definition, Grammy album of the year winners are hard acts to follow. King’s Tapestry, Fleetwood Mac’s Rumours and the Bee Gees-led Saturday Night Fever soundtrack are three of the most iconic and era-defining albums of the 1970s. Any follow-up was probably going to suffer by comparison.

Some of these overlooked follow-ups, notably ’s Fleetwood Mac’sTusk, have risen in esteem over the years.

Here are follow-ups to Grammy-winning albums since 1970 that failed to receive a single Grammy nomination – not for the album or any of its tracks.

Follow-up to: Blood, Sweat & Tears (1970 winner)

Notes: The biggest hit from the album was Carole King and Gerry Goffin’s “Hi-De-Ho,” one of King’s last hits-to-order before she emerged as a superstar in her own right the following year.

Notes: King may have erred in releasing Music so soon after Tapestry – just 10 months later, before Tapestry had even swept the Grammys in March 1972. (Albums were released at a much quicker clip back then.) “Sweet Seasons,” the only single released from Music, cracked the top 10 on the Hot 100. Another song on the album, “It’s Going to Take Some Time,” was a big hit for the Carpenters.

Follow-up to: The Harrison-led, all-star live album The Concert for Bangla Desh (1973 winner)

Notes: This was Harrison’s first studio album since the triple-disk opus All Things Must Pass in 1970. Living in the Material World spawned a No. 1 hit on the Hot 100, “Give Me Love (Give Me Peace on Earth).”

Notes: This double-disk album spawned a pair of top 10 hits on the Hot 100, “Tusk” and “Sara.” That’s just half of the number of top 10 hits that Rumours had produced, but a return to earth was inevitable after an album as colossal as Rumours.

Follow-up to: Bee Gees-led soundtrack Saturday Night Fever (1979 winner)

Notes: This was the brothers’ first studio album since 1976’s Children of the World. “Too Much Heaven,” “Tragedy” and “Love You Inside Out” all reached No. 1 on the Hot 100. If they weren’t quite as epic a three-play as the trio’s Fever singles (“How Deep Is Your Love,” “Stayin’ Alive” and “Night Fever”) had been, they were very good (especially the first two). This album deserved more love than it got at the Grammys.

Follow-up to: Christopher Cross (1981 winner)

Notes: In 1981, Cross became the first artist in Grammy history to sweep each of the “Big Four” Grammy categories – album, record and song of the year plus best new artist. That over-the-top success created a backlash that Cross might have been able to avoid had he just won best new artist. In any event, this album failed to match its predecessor, despite yielding two big hits, the peppy “All Right” and the treacly ballad “Think of Laura.”

Notes: Toto IV included such smashes as “Rosanna” (five weeks at No. 2 on the Hot 100) and “Africa” (No. 1). “Stranger in Town,” the biggest hit from this follow-up, topped out at No. 30.

Follow-up to: Can’t Slow Down (1985 winner)

Notes: This was Richie’s third solo album. It included “Say You, Say Me,” which had been released nine months earlier and had topped the Hot 100 and won an Oscar for best original song. The album also spawned two more top 10 hits, “Dancing on the Ceiling” and “Love Will Conquer All.” After Dancing on the Ceiling, Richie didn’t release another studio album for nearly 10 years.

Follow-up to: Two Against Nature (2001 winner)

Notes: Everything Must Go was the duo’s ninth and final studio album.

Follow-up to: Taking the Long Way (2007 winner)

Notes: Grammy voters were making a statement in voting for Taking the Long Way – actually, several statements. They were standing up for freedom of speech and venting their anger at President George W. Bush and the Iraq war. That angst had died down by the time this long-awaited album was released than 13 years later.

Notes: “Believe” was the only Hot 100 hit from the album, reaching No. 31.

Follow-up to: Harry’s House (2023 winner)

Notes: “Aperture” reached No. 1 on the Hot 100. “American Girls” came close, peaking at No. 4.
