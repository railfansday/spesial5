From minimalist to maximalist: Ars staffers show off their home offices

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Our home offices range from tasteful to “please explain yourself.”

Ars Technica is a place that has always leaned into remote working. Since its beginnings in 1998, Ars has never had a single, central office—a setup that makes for a great commute to work, with little risk of getting stuck in traffic.

As Ars staffers step away from their desks for a few days to spend some time with family and friends, we wanted to give you a look inside our home offices. We’ve got all sorts of setups, tucked into basements, dens, spare bedrooms, apartment corners, and lofts. They run the gamut from minimalist “here’s my laptop and monitor” setups to “I really like collecting things.” Without any further ado, let’s dig in:

As an apartment dweller in New York City, I’ve usually set up my home work station in a small corner of my residence along with sometimes decamping for local cafes or coworking spaces. My current hodgepodge of equipment is probably on the minimal side compared to many members of the Ars team.

I’m usually multitasking on my basic monitor display and work laptop configuration, with the monitor sitting on a stack of several books (apologies in particular to Mary Beard’s excellent history of the Roman Republic, titled SPQR).

Accessories plugged into the work laptop include a simple Lekvey Ergonomic Mouse and a Logitech K380 keyboard that I’ve often brought along with me to public workspaces. Sometimes I’ll use the built-in Macbook Air keyboard, but I like having the laptop display elevated, whether I’m working at home or elsewhere.

My personal Apple M4 Macbook Air usually sits on the side, where I primarily use my old, battered pair of Sennheiser HD 518 headphones to listen to music during the work day.

I have a simple standing desk, but it’s not the easiest to change positions, so I wouldn’t recommend it for anyone who wants to adjust frequently. Whenever I’m sitting, I sometimes switch between a simple wooden chair from Ikea and a more ergonomic chair.

Aside from an assortment of coasters and a tea container turned pen holder, my desk also holds a painted dinosaur sculpture (courtesy of my young son) and two versions of the Spot It! Matching game (also for my son’s entertainment).

Last but not least, I have a pair of Powerblock dumbbells tucked under my work desk. I wouldn’t say they feature heavily in my work day, but they’re easily accessible if I have 15-20 minutes to spare.

Greetings from my basement hobbit hole! My desk sits across from a workbench area that was already here when we got the house. I have labeled drawers full of PC parts, tools, and cables accumulated over decades. The drawer organization was quite the upgrade over my previous solution of “chuck everything in unlabeled storage tubs.”

My current PC setup is an AceMagic W1 mini PC running Fedora, and a Blackview Intel N150 I keep on Windows for audio recording (PipeWire is fussy). The two are connected to my inputs and monitors via a KVM so I can switch back and forth at the press of a button.

The monitors are mismatched thrift finds. One is an HP, the other Sansui. The TV is a similar story—an old Samsung LCD that set me back $10 at the thrift store. I use it as a third monitor for watching PC streams. An Onn Android box is also connected, though it rarely earns its keep.

Below the TV sits a Lenovo ThinkSmart View, a repurposed B2B video-conference gadget modded to run LineageOS. It handles smart-home controls and tracks my solar production during the day. There’s also a Flashforge 5M printer in the corner, and the pegboard wall holds tools, Mega Man figures, and about 10 years’ worth of convention badges.

The back closet is the scary part: old consoles, spare drives, boards, and parts for every project I’ll get to “eventually.” The chair is a Dowinx gaming chair I grabbed on a Slickdeals whim—it’s fine, but I wouldn’t recommend it to anyone.

The rug-patterned Dude mousepad really ties the desk together, man.

I’m something of an outlier at Ars, in that I use Windows most days. I’m no great lover of Windows 11, but I still use it thanks to years of computing inertia. My machine is custom, built in early 2024 with a Raptor Lake Core i7 (that thankfully did not fall victim to the overvolting bug) and an RTX 4070 Ti. It has long been my preference to build my own PC for gaming and work to ensure easier repairs and upgrades. Although with the prices of components being what they are, I hope all these parts last a very long time.

The primary monitor on the bottom is an Alienware ultrawide OLED. I got it for gaming, but it’s also a nice productivity display that gives me lots of room to keep windows visible. Above that, I have an Asus ProArt LCD calibrated in sRGB for photography work. I’ll get to the keyboard, but I do want to point out my preferred mouse, a Logitech MX Vertical. I have carpal tunnel issues, and this thing has been a lifesaver.

I believe organizing my desk before taking the photo would be a form of lying, so rest assured this is what it looks like on an average day. I have a few organizers to keep phones and various accessories from getting scattered around my Uplift sit-stand desk (seen here in sitting mode), which I quite like. The chair is a Herman Miller Aeron, which I don’t like as much. It’s fine and has plenty of adjustments, but the mesh is very hard. You should only buy one on sale, and if you do, get a cushion.

Above the desk are some Star Trek cross-stitches, courtesy of my talented wife. The dangling ’90s-era computer components are a mobile I put together many years ago, featuring a neat Intel i486 DEC socket board and some SDRAM.

I’m not a particularly neat person. My natural state is to have two or three projects half-exploded all over every surface: piles of 3D-printed prototypes, spools of wire, a soldering iron, laser-cut acrylic for a pinball project, the packaging from something I recently opened and need to sort through. Those aren’t random examples; that’s the contents of my workbench behind me on the opposite wall of my office as I type this. It’s a bit of a mess.

To combat that tendency, I make a concerted effort to keep the desk where I spend so many hours of the day calm and a place where I can focus. It doesn’t always last! But I’m always at least striving for it, and what I find helps is having a very intentional design where everything has a place.

My desk surface is made from rubberwood butcher block, on Flexispot motorized legs. I recently picked up another rubberwood slab to build the cantilevered shelf. The supports neatly tuck away my M1 Max Mac Studio, which is still a great little workhorse with 64 gigs of RAM, and my Arturia audio interface, which I use to record line-in sources like synthesizers.

On the left is a task lamp and a pen holder with six discrete holes. This fits in with the intentional design part: I have to pick the six things I want handy, and I can’t just pack it with more and more stuff. Currently that’s my Apple Pencil, a Pentel mechanical pencil, an Olfa mini snap-off blade knife, and a selection of three Pigma Micron pens in different line widths.

The right side holds an Aiyima 10th anniversary hi-fi stack: A20 amp, D20 DAC, and T20 pre-amplifier. The speakers are SVS Ultra Evolution Nanos. They’re sitting on the latest version of some 3D-printed stands I’ve been designing. I print them with hollow legs and then fill them with concrete for mass and stability.

Since I know Ryan is going to flex on everyone with his keyboard collection, I have to try my best to keep up with my daily driver. It’s a Percent Canoe v2 case, running Holy Panda switches modified with 63 gram SPRiT springs for a slightly lighter touch, and GMK Bento keycaps. This was a pandemic build project, and it has served me well since.

I used to run multiple monitors, but I’ve found that I don’t focus as well with them and prefer a singular spot to pay attention to. I’m using an Apple Studio Display because I’m very picky about pixel density. I opted to order it without the stand so I can run it on an ergo arm.

When I built the shelf for my desk, I fabricated a mounting bracket for the arm out of schedule 40 steel pipe and a 3D-printed sleeve that sits in a pocket CNC’d out. Since it sits flush on the underside, I don’t have to use a clamp that would extend down into the space where I keep my Mac and audio interface.

When I need a secondary display, or I want to use my Apple Pencil in Photoshop or Illustrator, I use an iPad Pro. I used to have a dedicated Wacom monitor, but I find this much more convenient both in terms of space efficiency and ergonomics. If I’m not really leaning into hand drawing or lettering, I do most of my graphic work with a mouse, which is currently a Logitech MX Master 3S.

To wrap up the final items, the chair is an Aeron I bought new in 2003. I’ve had to repair it a few times over the years, but it’s either been things I consider wear items (wheels, armrests, gas cylinder) or Herman Miller has replaced under warranty. It was a big purchase for me at the time, but it’s ended up being a great investment. That’s Sophie draped over it, one of our four cats.

Tucked next to my desk, behind the subwoofer under the little side table, is my home server and networking gear. The server runs Unraid and is primarily used for my Jellyfin media collection and Home Assistant. Networking is a combination of Unifi and Eero, but at some point I’d like to ditch the Amazon gear and go fully native Unifi.

I spend a lot of time in my office. It’s not just a place to edit, write, and do other Ars-y things. It’s also my rehearsal space and a place to relax and unwind when I need some me-time. Long-time readers won’t be surprised to see Apple hardware front and center. I’ve been using Macs since college, and they’ve done alright by me ever since. My daily driver is a first-gen Mac Studio (M1 Max) with 64GB of RAM, which drives two Apple Studio Displays. As a long-time mouse hater, I use a Magic Trackpad along with my extended Apple keyboard. Underneath the desk is a UPS and a Thunderbolt disk enclosure that is one of my two backups.

The bookshelves are where my home networking gear lives. That includes a Synology DS920+ that backs up all of the computers in the house, serves up media, and runs my instance of Mealie. The Eero 6+ handles home networking, with everything in my office except my MacBook Pro connected via Ethernet. As much as I’d like to live a paperless existence, the HP printer-scanner-copier combo still gets some use. They’re difficult to see, but to the right of the G4 Cube (sadly inoperable) on top of the bookshelf sit an Apple TV and M2 Mac mini, which hook up to the 43-inch TV. My graphite iBook and eMate are now resigned to propping up bibs from races and other cycling events.

This is my office, which is the place where I am much of the time. The chair is a Herman Miller Embody and is one of the best things I’ve ever spent money on. The desk is a standy desk that I don’t stand at nearly often enough; the work PC is a Mac Studio; and the gaming PC is a frankenbox with a cockpit running Windows 10 LTSC (from my cold dead hands, etc., etc.). The Linux boxen are headless and are mostly banished to the closet.

The paintings (including the awesome Apollo 12 SCE-to-aux one) are all originals by the inimitable Stephen Quick, who does a hell of a job with commissions. What started as a funny idea about a Warhol-esque take on the many faces of Kurt Russel kinda turned over a couple of years into somewhat of a “these are some actors whose work I like” series. My buddies and I went to a lot of movies when we were teenagers, so the imagery sparks good memories for me.

The rest of the office has space stuff in it, especially the part behind where I was standing to do the pano sot. I’ve heard it said that when a man hits his 40s, he must choose between becoming obsessed with World War 2 history or smoking meats; I did kind of a variation on “World War 2” and fell deeply into historic human spaceflight. Apollo is kind of my jam, and some weekends you can even catch me docenting at Space Center Houston’s Saturn V exhibit! (Yeah, it’s maybe not as pretty as the exhibits at Marshall or KSC, but the JSC Saturn V is the only one made of flight stages end to end!)

Welcome to the mess of wires, monitors, and handheld fidgets that is my loft desk. Let’s take a quick tour along with the rest of my gaming cave/mid-life crisis Mario collection that lives right by it.

My office setup is probably a lot simpler—basic?—than most of my Ars colleagues. I use an adjustable wood-top desk that allows me to stand or sit while I work. I do almost all of my writing and editing on a MacBook Air, and I have a spare available in case I need it. Sometimes I travel with both MacBooks so I can use one for writing and the other for monitoring a rocket launch, press conference, or other event. I also work a lot from my porch, where I can enjoy the fresh air and quiet mornings on our ranch in rural Texas.

An iMac and a second monitor stand behind the laptops. I generally use them to watch conferences, rocket launch livestreams, weather radar, or live news. It just depends on what’s happening any given day. One of the things I love about the space beat is there’s something happening nearly every day, and it’s possible to cover much of it from home. There are often multiple things happening the same day, so the dual-screen setup becomes especially handy then. For example, I can use one monitor to watch what SpaceX is doing at Starbase, Texas, and another to watch a rocket launch in Florida. I use a ring light behind my desk for TV interviews or podcast appearances.

Absent from my desk is my large collection of space swag stowed away in storage boxes. Perhaps I will bring it out to add to my workspace for a future Ars Staff Source. For now, I use a mouse pad from the European Space Agency that I’ve had for at least 15 years. The mouse pad features ESA’s astronomy missions that were in development or operations at the time. All of the missions have now launched, and some have long been decommissioned. It’s a good reminder of how long it takes to design, develop, build, and launch science missions. Anyone who says this stuff is easy is being dishonest.

This image is a bit misleading, in that it implies I work on the pictured PC (RTX 4070, Ryzen 5 3600X, 32GB RAM) and sit at my desk while doing so. That’s occasionally true, but more often, I can be found wandering around my apartment with my M4 MacBook Air during work hours. I don’t even sit at my desk to game much anymore—on the off chance that I’m playing a game the Steam Deck can’t handle natively, I’ll stream it from my PC to my Steam Deck using Moonlight and Apollo (previously Moonlight and Sunshine).

You may also notice that my desk, from a company called Uplift, is adjustable. As it turns out, I don’t enjoy standing while working, so the ability to loft my tabletop skyward has been chiefly deployed in the service of making it easier to retrieve something I’ve dropped down there.

At night, my desk often moonlights as a craft area; I swap out my keyboard (a Wobkey Rainy75) for a cutting mat, and I’m ready to build some Gundam, assemble a book nook, or tackle a crochet pattern (a few amigurumi are scattered around my desk to keep me company). You can see some of my supplies on the left, but I have other stuff stashed in drawers and various containers that I can bring to my desk as needed.

My wife Anna and I rent an apartment in Chicago, and we’ve chosen the city life over, well, space. As such, my 10-by-10-foot office space serves multiple functions. I use it primarily for work, but since Anna and I are both big gamers and she is often camped out with the PlayStation 5 in the living room, I have my own gaming setup in the office. It’s cramped, but it works!

I do editing, news hits, and most other work during the day, but I’m a night owl at heart and find that I can only do heavy writing—like on longer feature articles—at night, so I’m picturing both the daytime and nighttime view here. I have a collection of Govee lights (Govee Hexa panels, several RGB Edison bulbs, and a Star Light Aurora galaxy projector) to max out the vibes for those nighttime focus (and gaming) sessions.

We moved into this place in the dead of the pandemic, and I bought most of the stuff in this office during that year or shortly afterward, so it’s a very 2020–2021-coded array of hardware.

The desk is a Secretlab Magnus Pro, and the chair is a Steelcase Gesture. I love the desk, but I have mixed feelings about the chair. I don’t like the material of the Herman Miller Aeron, but people kept telling me to buy an ergonomic chair for long-term back health, so I bought the other one everyone recommends. To be frank, I’m never fully comfortable in it, though, and wish I had just said “life is short” and bought something comfy and bad for me!

The main machine is a 14-inch MacBook Pro with an M1 Max, 64GB of RAM, and a 2TB hard drive—still holding strong after five years. I also have an Alienware Aurora R10—hear me out—prebuilt gaming desktop with a Ryzen 9 5900X and an RTX 3080. When I bought it, it was during the COVID-times GPU shortage, and there was absolutely no other way to get the GPU I wanted. The system is loud, hot, and hard to work with; I want very much to replace it with something custom, but the need has not yet arisen. I tend to hold on to stuff until it literally stops working.

As for monitors, the center monitor is a 27-inch, 1440p, 240 Hz OLED Asus XG27AQDMGZ. The left and right monitors are both 27-inch 4K, 60 Hz IPS Dell U2720Qs. I’ve got a pair of Audioengine A2+ speakers, plus a Blue Yeti X, a Logitech Lighta Glow, and the good ol’ Logitech C920 for video and voice calls.

Then there’s input: a Keychron K1 Pro keyboard, a Razer Orochi V2 mouse, and a Sony DualSense controller. I’ve got a Belkin charging station for my iPhone 17 Pro, AirPods Pro, and Apple Watch Series 7, plus AirPods Max on a headphone hanger on the front of the desk. The CalDigit E5 Thunderbolt hub is doing a lot of work here!

I feel fortunate to have a job that allows me to occasionally apply my lifelong love affair with video games. My favorite game of all time is The Elder Scrolls V: Skyrim (Morrowind is a close second), and I like Bethesda games in general. I’ve got a working (but mostly useless) Fallout Pip-Boy, which ought to count for something! I also have a few other Skyrim knickknacks hanging about. And if you’re a Star Trek: The Next Generation viewer, you might have already noticed the desk mat.

I do enjoy playing games at my desk sometimes, but after I sit at the computer all day, I often want to be somewhere else when winding down. So pictured here is the other half of the room. The leather armchair I use for console gaming is paired with a leather ottoman (the colors don’t match because, again, COVID-era supply limitations).

I have an LG C1 OLED TV with a PlayStation 5 Pro, a PlayStation 3 that is backward-compatible with PlayStation 1 and 2 games, a Nintendo Switch I barely touch, and three Analogue FPGA retro consoles: the Super Nt, Mega Sg, and 3D.

It’s a tiny room to spend so much time in, but after almost seven years in this apartment, it certainly feels like home!
