It’s almost Halloween, and much like everyone else, I have been sucked into the tradition of watching horror movies with my wife before we take our daughter trick-or-treating. One of the movies I’m still thinking about from last week is Backrooms. It is the highest-grossing film A24 has ever released, and most of it takes place in rooms that look like a hospital at 3 a.m.

Yellowed walls. Fluorescent tubes that buzz loud enough to notice. Carpet that has absorbed something you would rather not identify. Corridors that turn left, then left again, until you’re somehow back where you started. If you trained at a big academic center, you have walked these halls, and probably still dream about them.

The film grew out of Kane Parsons’s YouTube series about a dimension of endless, empty rooms. Instead of the creature feature I expected, I got a picture of the doctor who is close to financial independence and can’t bring themselves to leave.

Full disclaimer: only read ahead if you’ve watched the film because there will be spoilers!

Clark is a failed architect running a struggling furniture store in San Jose. He drinks too much, his marriage is over, and he has started sleeping at the store. One night he goes down to check the breaker box, notices a glowing slit in the wall, and falls through it.

On the other side is a copy of the world that is slightly wrong. Rooms that almost look like rooms. Furniture that seems to have melted at the edges. Hallways longer than the building could possibly hold. Later in the film it gets described as a faulty, misremembered version of reality.

That description fits medical training better than anything I’ve heard in a wellness lecture. Residency is a copy of adult life with the proportions off. You have a title and real responsibility for people’s lives, but you’re 31 with a negative net worth, and you haven’t made it to a friend’s wedding without checking your phone in years.

Real life is always one room ahead: after boards, after fellowship, after the loans, after partnership. So you keep walking.

After a few trips in and out, Clark leaves his therapist a message. He’s staying in the Backrooms, and he won’t be coming back. He has found a way out of the failing store, the drinking, the empty house. The place he picked is worse than the one he’s leaving, but it’s familiar, and it doesn’t ask him to deal with anything.

The FIRE crowd has a name for this: one more year. You hit your number, or get close enough to see it, and you sign another contract anyway. The reasons all sound sensible. The market is choppy. The kids start college in four years. The group is short two partners.

Besides, what else would you do on a Wednesday morning?

Plenty of physicians keep working after financial independence because they love the work, and that’s a good outcome. The question worth asking is whether you’re staying because you chose to, or because the hallway is the only place you know how to stand.

Early in the film, Clark shoots a cheap TV commercial for the store dressed as its pirate mascot. It’s a little embarrassing and a little sweet. It is the version of himself he has to perform to keep the business alive.

By the end of the film, the costume becomes a towering, distorted Clark in the pirate outfit, peg leg and all, is waiting for him in the Backrooms. Clark tries to calm it down the way you’d talk to a dog you raised from a puppy. It kills him.

The Backrooms in this film make monsters from what people carry in, their memories and their feelings, and build with it.

Doctors have a costume too. The white coat, the title, the steady voice you use to tell a family the scan came back bad. You need it, and your patients need you to have it. The trouble starts when it stops coming off after the shift. You introduce yourself as Dr. at your kid’s soccer game. The hobbies lapse one at a time. Every close friend works in your department. Your income, your marriage, and your sense of being a decent person all run through one job.

Then the job goes sideways. A lawsuit, a patient death you can’t stop replaying, an RVU target that climbs every year, a private equity buyout that changes everything except your title.

What turns on you in those moments is the professional self you spent fifteen years building, now bigger than the person wearing it.

The second half belongs to Clark’s therapist, Dr. Mary Kline (Renate Reinsve). Worried about her patient, she goes to the store and walks into the Backrooms after him.

She doesn’t walk in clean. Mary is still working through the demolition of her childhood home and her mother’s institutionalization. A professional helper carrying a stack of her own unprocessed losses into someone else’s crisis is a fair description of a lot of people in medicine.

Inside, Clark ties her to a chair in a fake dining room. Three human-shaped figures sit around the table with them. They don’t move or react to anything. Clark shows her they’re made of an edible white substance, and it becomes clear the Backrooms feed on them.

It might sound odd, but I couldn’t watch that scene without thinking about the Maslach Burnout Inventory. It measures three things: emotional exhaustion, a fading sense of accomplishment, and depersonalization.

Doctors are familiar with depersonalization. Patients start to feel like tasks, and after a while you feel like one too. You’re at the table every day, and you look fine from across the room, while the place slowly takes what it needs from you.

The pirate gets rid of Clark comes for Mary next. Her survival is noteworthy.

Cornered in a lopsided replica of the furniture store, she snaps the monster’s peg leg with a hat rack, then hits it in the face with a chunk of concrete from her childhood home. The thing she has been grieving the whole movie turns out to be the thing she fights with.

For a physician, that chunk of concrete is whoever you were before medicine. The kid who played bass in a garage band. The person who built bookshelves, or ran trails, or cooked for twelve friends on a Saturday. Those parts didn’t vanish when you matched. They got buried under a decade of training.

The doctors I’ve seen leave clinical work well, or drop to part-time without falling apart, usually started digging some of that back up long before they needed it. They had a corner of life the job wasn’t allowed into, and they’d been living in it for years by the time they gave notice.

This is where the analogy gets uncomfortable.

Mary’s fight with the pirate sets off a gas trap. Researchers in hazmat suits haul her out to a facility run by Async, a company that used to make MRI machines before it found the Backrooms and made them its main business.

Whether or not the filmmakers meant anything by it, a medical imaging company ending up in charge of the maze is a hard detail to ignore if you work in a hospital.

In the last scene, Mary sits in an interrogation room across from Phil, one of Async’s scientists. She asks whether they’re going to let her go. He tells her the decision isn’t his.

None of these are sinister on their own. Each one just moves the decision a little further from you and a little closer to a contract, a lender, or an employer.

For a physician, that’s what financial independence is really for. It’s less about retiring at 45 and more about who gets to answer the question Mary asks.

The film’s final shot is a copy of Mary, deformed and silent, sitting in a rough replica of that interrogation room somewhere deep in the Backrooms. The place has learned her. Whatever happens to the real Mary, a version of her stays behind.

Medicine leaves a copy of you behind too, and that isn’t all bad. The judgment, the pattern recognition, the calm you have in a code are yours for life. The goal isn’t to get out with nothing of the doctor left. It’s to make sure the doctor isn’t the only thing left.

If you bought into a multifamily syndication around 2021, you remember the deck. Glossy photos, a tidy IRR, a confident exit year. Then rates climbed, valuations slid, and plenty of investors watched distributions pause while their capital sat locked inside a deal that no longer penciled out.

Private real estate lending puts you in a different seat. The lender gets paid ahead of equity, holds a lien on the property, and collects interest from monthly loan payments rather than waiting on a sale that may never hit its number. You give up the home-run upside. In exchange, nobody calls you about a broken water heater at 2 a.m., a perk I’ve come to value after enough overnight shifts.

In a recent webinar, I joined Dr. Christy Burakovsky and Chris Lopez of Property Llama to discuss how private real estate loans are underwritten, where the actual risks lie, and what investors should ask any fund before wiring a dollar.

We also covered the importance of looking beyond projected returns and understanding the fundamentals behind a lending strategy.

The takeaway? Before investing, know how the loan is structured, what protects your capital, and what happens when a deal doesn’t go according to plan.
