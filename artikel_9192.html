So You Want to Run an Artist’s Estate?

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

A how-to guide on all things inventory, planning, appraisals, conservation, and more.

Much has been written about the “Great Wealth Transfer,” a predicted $80+ trillion phenomenon that will enrich younger generations as their parents pass away in the next two decades. For those who have inherited — or expect to inherit — the estates of visual artists, the challenges of this “transfer” can be especially complex. Works of art, which can carry both emotional and sentimental value alongside asset value, require special care and consideration. The resulting pressures and responsibilities can divide families and create legal challenges.

For example, when the artist Sam Francis died in 1994, he left over 1,000 artworks, several businesses, extensive real estate holdings, ten cars, and a herd of cattle. His will provided money for a foundation called “The Sam Francis Museum,” but his vague instructions did not specify its activities. Disputes among his heirs — Francis had four children and had been married five times — and the need to extensively document his artwork further complicated matters. Settling all parties' claims and establishing the Sam Francis Foundation took nearly five years.

The following text, which touches on topics facing estates, was developed in consultation with experts in fields that serve estates and trustees. There are also many organizations doing important work to advance this field, including the Center for the Preservation of Artists' Legacies, the Artists' Legacy Foundation, and Legacy Lab New Mexico.

When it comes to choosing trustees, Joe Oliveira, the sole trustee of the Nathan Oliveira Estate, said, “Choosing trustees can be difficult, and they may not necessarily be family members. In my own case, I happen to be the most logical candidate. A single trustee like myself can make decisions efficiently and consistently, while still listening to and considering the ideas of other family members.”

One of the most crucial challenges estate managers face is understanding and cataloging an estate’s full contents. This includes not only works of art but also related ephemera, such as correspondence and personal effects. Unique ephemera, like Joan Brown’s fingerprint-covered telephone on display at her SFMOMA retrospective, can strengthen future exhibition narratives.

Stuart A. Chase, a former museum director who now advises estates, suggests starting with an inventory. “In my experience, most heirs have no idea what they have. You can’t do anything without a catalog. You can’t approach anyone. It can be very simple: I use CatalogIt, which is straightforward to use and charges a nominal fee.”

When I took Chase’s advice and used CatalogIt to document my art collection, I looked under the bed and found a folder of unframed drawings a friend gave me years ago that I'd completely forgotten about. The app allowed me to print a copy of the completed inventory, which I added to my will.

An appraisal, an independent assessment of a property's value or condition, is essential to managing an artist’s estate because it involves the IRS. A Fair Market Value appraisal is typically required to file estate taxes and establish new cost bases for each work in the event of future sales by an heir. It will contain detailed reports for each object, including artist, title, date, dimensions, medium, provenance, condition, and the specific valuation method used. The IRS requires what it calls “qualified appraisals” for this, as well as for non-cash charitable donations to qualified institutions and other gifts.

Choose appraisers carefully, and select those who are highly recommended. Deborah Gerstler Spanierman, a longtime art advisor and certified appraiser, recommends engaging “qualified and trustworthy allied professionals: appraisers, archivists, estate and foundation attorneys to handle the estate tax filing. Empower them to do their jobs and to work with the artist’s gallery, if they have one, which will all eventually support the artist’s legacy.”

Artist-Endowed Foundations differ from other philanthropic measures because they create programs that feature the artist’s work. These may include organizing and lending to exhibitions, developing and publishing catalogue raisonnés, or operating education centers.

Christine Vincent, the managing director of the Aspen Institute, an artist-endowed foundation initiative, explains: “Artists' estates and Artist-Endowed Foundations (AEFs) are entirely different things. Artists' estates are typically operated by private individuals and, as such, are not tax-exempt charitable entities. AEFs are tax-exempt charitable organizations with a mission to benefit the general public. An artist's estate may evolve into an AEF, for example under the estate plan of an artist's surviving spouse, but an AEF can never devolve into an artist's estate.”

Foundations also typically own the artist's intellectual property, including copyrights, trademarks, and in some cases, rights of personality. These are also used for public benefit, both to educate the public about the artist's oeuvre and to generate funds to support foundation programs. A governing board, often made up of art professionals and, in some cases, family members, typically guides the foundation’s activities.

Checking an artist’s archive for condition issues is essential. Identify which works need immediate care, which will need future restoration and/or repair, and act as promptly as possible. You might have to make difficult decisions about works that should be discarded or destroyed with the utmost consideration. Seek expert help — from conservators and other art professionals — when works require special treatment or present unique problems.

Where you store work also affects its condition. “Know how to properly store works on canvas and paper in temperature-controlled spaces, and insure them to avoid any future or potential loss,” says Jamieson Grillo, the sole trustee of the Estate of John Grillo. “If the estate can afford it, make sure you get your artwork conserved by a professional conservator with past experience and a proven track record in conserving similar art. Also, share any conservation reports with galleries you are working with.”

Galleries with long-term relationships with artists will likely want to continue that partnership after an artist passes away. But estates seeking new representation should start by identifying suitable galleries, looking at those that handle works similar to the estate’s.

Prioritize and prepare before contacting any galleries. Gallery owner Hollis Taggart swears by making it as easy as possible for the gallery by providing as much detail as possible. “The more organized an estate is, the more likely a gallery will be to take on the heavy lifting of representing that estate.” Taggart also advises a clear vision: “Be as supportive as possible by organizing an inventory list of the works so the gallery knows right up front what the estate holdings are, and how big a project is involved.”

Placing works in museum collections helps enhance and maintain an artist’s reputation and legacy. But museums often hesitate to accept donations because of limited exhibition and storage space. Patti Smith, an art collections manager at the Stephenson Ceramics Studio, suggests approaching them thoughtfully and presenting only high-quality works, or collaborating with a gallery to develop a “gifting exhibition.” She says, “I helped develop one for the estate of artist Richard Wilt (1915–1981) that documented a body of his work and displayed it in the gallery. We approached new museums as well as museums that already had his work. Since there was demand for purchasing the work, once the museums made their selections, the work was offered up for sale, and 100% of the proceeds went to the Richard Wilt Foundation at the University of Michigan Stamps School of Art and Design.”

Estates should have websites that include an artist biography, a timeline, a gallery of works, and contact information. This can help anyone doing research — including appraisers and dealers — and may also help locate lost works.

A social media presence, especially regular Instagram posts, keeps an artist’s legacy in the public consciousness. Mary Clare McKinley of the Richard Diebenkorn Foundation says, “When estates and foundations harness the power of social media, it can be a brilliant tool for education, legacy and community building. In my opinion, investing in research is always a good decision. Then, consider the platform a way to share information less formally than publishing a book, and creatively explore ways to use it: Enjoy the flexibility, interactivity, and immediate access to a wider public.” She shares that the Diebenkorn Foundation’s Instagram posts “highlight scholars making exciting discoveries, support museum exhibitions, feature [their] own intriguing archival finds, and suggest new visual connections to Diebenkorn’s work.”

According to the Center for Art Law, when an artist passes, their estate has two seemingly conflicting tasks: "to protect an artist’s work by exercising exclusive rights over them, and to disseminate their artwork to the public to establish a lasting legacy and placement in the art world.” But this dilemma runs parallel to another issue: Should estates prioritize revenue, or improve public access to art?

One recurring issue involves image use and rights, and estates typically turn to licensing agencies for support. For example, Artist’s Rights Society guides clients through obtaining copyright and intellectual property clearance, legal support, and advocacy. Recently, as estates have tried to balance revenue and reputation, and deal with the easy proliferation of web-based images, some have become less concerned with enforcing image rights.

Auction results, now widely available through online databases, are key public indicators of an artist’s salability. Selling an especially strong work at auction is a gamble some estates take to establish a benchmark for an artist whose work lacks a previous auction record. Estates of recognized artists may want to partner with an auction house to mount a significant sale.

To do so, an estate needs to meet certain standards. Wendy Battleson, senior vice president at Bonhams, says, “Auction houses, in general, are looking for an estate with an established record of sales at relatively high values (five to six figures minimum), ideally sold publicly. We also look at the amount of inventory available in order to continue to tell the artist’s story over a period of time. The beauty of working with an auction house is that pricing is public, and there is rarely a concern that the art has been sold for less than market rate. With a dealer, the sales are typically private, so you don’t know if you could have achieved a higher price.”
