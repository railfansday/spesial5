What's in a Label With Neurodivergence, and Why You Should Care

Posted November 17, 2026 | Reviewed by Michelle Quirk

Recently, I was having coffee with a few colleagues when the topic of labels came up. One of our clients said they self-identified as an “autistic person" versus a "neurodivergent person." This is a distinction worth noting since neurodivergence can be viewed as a broad term. Everyone's eyes turned to me in anticipation of my response because, of course, I'm known around the office for having what my children call "hot takes." Without hesitation, I shared mine: "I support specific labels across the neurodivergent community, as long as they don't define an individual's entire identity." My team was divided, but let me explain my stance.

As someone who's part of Generation X, when I was a child, there were far fewer labels. No one was diagnosed with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) or learning disabilities. If there was an academic challenge, the kids figured it out or didn’t. As for me, I was a fun, fearless, and resilient child. No one told me who I was, and I embraced that way of thinking, often finding solutions along the way. Fast forward to today, this isn't the case at all in any aspect of our society. Labels are a way people identify themselves, especially those within the neurodivergent community. And within these labels, there is identity-first language and person-centered language, which we've touched on in the past. See here for more background if you’re too busy to scan through my previous blog posts.

These terms aside, if you're not working in the field like me, and labeling your child makes you uncomfortable, that's a good thing. It means you're thoughtful and sensitive. But I'll be clear: A label stating an actual diagnosis is imperative. In my line of work, I create programs for people with disabilities, and many clients have ADHD and/or autism. I often find myself using a label to support client advocacy and secure supports. In cases like this one, a label is vital. If a client doesn't have a formal diagnosis of autism, they might not receive services from a school system, disability development services, or health insurance. For them, the lack of a label means no consideration for applied behavior analysis (ABA) therapy; the answer is simply "no."

Over the years, I've met many clients who proudly share their diagnosis: "This is who I am, and accept me for me." They identify with a label because it helps them explain how they differ from the general population, and, in some cases, that label becomes a central part of their identity. This is where it can get tricky.

One strong example is Asperger's syndrome, once a widely used diagnosis, which has largely faded from everyday use and has been absorbed into the diagnosis of autism. Many people with Asperger's still use the term and feel that the shift erases part of themselves and others' history. Without a distinct category, their needs may even be overlooked. Some believe they’ve lost a sense of self, and I really feel for them. I can't imagine someone telling me something I've connected to for years essentially no longer exists.

A great way to find a healthy balance when approaching labels within neurodivergence is to find a community of people who share your child's background. When integrating, your child will likely see that, yes, they share a label and some similarities, but they have their own unique personality and interests. This realization can also help a child who might feel isolated by fostering connections.

At the end of the day, every person is an individual, and yes, labels are a necessary part of life and play a huge role in our healthcare system. However, again, I'm not the one labeling my clients; I ask them to tell me, and if that's challenging, I work with their parent or caregiver for insight. Ask, don't assume.

After working for so many years with families with children with disabilities, I can assure you that as a parent, you can deeply impact the way your child sees themselves and trust and believe they're so much more than the label they might currently identify with. Encourage them to accept who they are, know that they bring something special to the world, and continue to find the wonderful qualities and characteristics that go far beyond a label because that's what makes them special and unique. The diagnosis is just one part of who they are. I know my younger self would be shocked by my new acceptance of labels, but she was never defined by one thing, and neither is your child.
