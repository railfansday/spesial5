The December Check-Writing Ritual Now Costs You Money

Every December I do two things badly. I shop for my wife and daughter on the last Saturday before Christmas, elbowing through a mall packed with everyone else who planned poorly, and I write my charity checks in that same panicked week.

The shopping habit costs me dignity. Starting this year, the check habit costs money.

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act rewrote the charitable deduction for 2026. Medscape’s Physicians and Taxes Report 2025 put the median marginal tax rate for doctors at 35%.

Pile student loans, a late start on retirement savings, and a short earning window on top of that rate, and a wasted deduction stings us harder than most people.

Below are the rules that changed, the strategies that still work, and seven moves to make before the year runs out.

Charitable gifts now count on Schedule A only above 0.5% of adjusted gross income. Take $100,000 of AGI: the floor is $500, and a $10,000 gift shrinks to a $9,500 deduction.

Physician incomes make the haircut bigger: a couple with $600,000 of AGI forfeits the first $3,000 they give (my arithmetic, not a sourced figure). That slice is usually gone for good.

Thomson Reuters’ Practitioner’s Tax Action Bulletin says the floor creates no carryover on its own, and the disallowed amount survives only when you already have a carryforward from blowing past the AGI percentage limits.

Then there’s the cap. Filers in the 37% bracket (taxable income above $768,700 on a joint return and $640,600 for singles in 2026) get 35 cents of tax benefit per deducted dollar. Schwab’s illustration: a $10,000 gift that once saved $3,700 now saves $3,500.

The mechanism is a “2/37” reduction applied to the lesser of your total itemized deductions or your income above the 37% threshold, so it trims mortgage interest and state taxes too, and it lands after the floor has taken its bite.

A couple with $600,000 of taxable income never touches this cap, a detail that goes missing when the “35 cents” headline travels alone.

Congress handed something back as well. The standard deduction rose to $32,200 for joint filers ($16,100 for singles), and non-itemizers can deduct up to $1,000 of cash gifts, or $2,000 on a joint return.

The fine print is strict: cash only, public charities only, nothing to a donor-advised fund or a private foundation. Residents, fellows, and attendings without a mortgage should grab it, since it asks for nothing beyond a receipt.

Each strategy below hinges on whether you itemize, and that answer changed for plenty of physicians this year. The cap on state and local tax deductions, stuck at $10,000 since 2018, sits at $40,400 for 2026.

High earners get less of it. Above $505,000 of modified AGI, the cap shrinks by 30 cents per extra dollar until it lands back at $10,000 once income reaches $606,333.

A couple at $560,000 keeps $23,900 of room, and at $600,000 they keep $11,900. Inside that band every additional dollar of income also erases 30 cents of deduction, which pushes the real marginal rate on those dollars well above the bracket printed in the tables.

The bigger cap expires, reverting to $10,000 in 2030. And physicians who own a practice taxed as an S corporation or partnership can ask whether their state offers a pass-through entity tax election, which lets the business pay the state income tax and deduct it outside the cap.

Deadlines vary by state, and some states penalize you for skipping estimated payments on the entity tax, so a January 1 start for 2027 can beat a rushed December election. (I practice in Florida, which has no personal income tax to route through an entity, so the election does nothing for me.)

You can run the test in ten minutes. Add your capped state and local taxes, your mortgage interest, and your planned gifts above the floor. If the total clears $32,200, you itemize.

If you hold appreciated stock in a taxable account, this is the easiest win on the list.

Say you own $10,000 of Apple bought for $4,000. Sell it and you owe 20% on the $6,000 gain, or $1,200, leaving $8,800 for the charity. Hand over the shares and the charity receives the full $10,000, you deduct $10,000, and nobody pays the tax. Most physicians in that bracket also owe the 3.8% net investment income tax, which lifts the real bill on the sale to $1,428.

Small charities often can’t accept a stock transfer. A donor-advised fund solves that by taking the shares, selling them tax-free, and sending cash grants to your church or your kid’s school.

Flip the logic for losers. Sell them, bank the loss against other gains, and give the cash, because donating a stock that’s underwater throws the loss away.

A donor-advised fund works like a charitable holding account. You contribute cash or stock, take the deduction that year, let the balance sit invested, and recommend grants to charities on your own schedule.

The large brokerage-affiliated sponsors make setup easy, and you name a successor to direct whatever remains after your death.

Before 2026 the appeal was bunching: stack two years of gifts into one, itemize that year, and take the standard deduction in the off year.

Picture a couple who gives $52,000 annually, which produces $312,000 of deductions over six years. Giving $104,000 every other year and claiming the $32,200 standard deduction in between produces $408,600. That’s $96,600 more deducted for identical dollars to identical charities.

(The example is simplified: no other itemized deductions, with the floor and cap ignored.)

The floor adds a second job. Give every year, and you pay the 0.5% toll every year, but bunch and you pay it half as often. For a couple with $600,000 of AGI giving $30,000 a year, that’s $18,000 of deductions lost over six years versus $9,000 with every-other-year funding (again, my illustration).

Bunching has a hole in it. If state taxes and mortgage interest already carry you past the standard deduction every year, the off-year standard deduction gains you nothing, and the floor savings are all that’s left.

Qualified charitable distributions send money straight from a traditional IRA to a charity. The 2026 limit is $111,000 per person, or $222,000 for a couple, and the gift counts toward the required minimum distributions that begin at 73.

Take a $300,000 RMD: a $111,000 QCD leaves tax owed on the remaining $189,000.

The biggest advantage gets too little airtime. A QCD stays out of your income entirely, so the 0.5% floor and the 35-cent cap never touch it, and it works whether you itemize or take the standard deduction. Because it lowers AGI, it can also help keep you under the income tiers that raise Medicare Part B and Part D premiums.

Meet two of our regulars (their numbers are illustrative).

Dr. Addams is a W-2 hospitalist in Texas. She and her husband report $420,000 of AGI, pay $14,000 in property tax and $12,000 in mortgage interest, and give $8,000 a year in cash. Her floor is $2,100, so itemizing would total $31,900, a hair under the standard deduction.

She takes the $32,200 plus the new $2,000, or $34,200 a year. Suppose she puts $14,000 into a donor-advised fund this December and gives $2,000 in cash directly next year. Her two-year deductions rise from $68,400 to $72,100.

That extra $3,700 is worth about $900 at her 24% rate, a pleasant bonus and nothing more. Her bigger win is funding the account with appreciated index fund shares.

Dr. Smart is a 1099 anesthesiologist in California. He and his wife report $700,000 of AGI, pay $30,000 of mortgage interest, and give $40,000 a year. Their income sits above $606,333, so the SALT deduction is back to $10,000, yet they itemize every year anyway.

Bunching saves them one $3,500 floor every two years and little else, and their taxable income stays under the 37% line, so the cap never bites.

The real money sits in two other places: donating stock to dodge a 23.8% federal tax on the gains, and a pass-through entity election that deducts California tax at his S corporation.

Late in the year partners start whispering about buyouts.

A physician selling in 2027 should plan to fund a donor-advised fund in the year the income spikes and start harvesting losses now to offset the coming gain. In a Roth conversion year, size stock gifts near 20% of projected AGI, which leaves room under the 30% limit because December income estimates are never exact.

Donating part of the practice itself before closing can work, and the Tax Court record shows how it fails. In Estate of Hoensheid v. Commissioner (2023), an owner gave shares to a donor-advised fund shortly before a sale that had become a practical certainty.

The court taxed him on the gain under the anticipatory assignment of income doctrine, then denied his deduction because the appraisal fell short of the rules. A gift like that has to happen when the deal can still fall apart, with a qualified appraisal in hand. State rules on who may own a medical practice add another barrier, so hire the attorney before you sign anything with a buyer.

Starting January 1, 2027, a federal credit of up to $1,700 applies to cash given to a scholarship granting organization that funds K-12 scholarships. A credit cuts your tax bill dollar for dollar, which beats a deduction worth 35 cents.

The catches matter. Your state has to opt in, the federal credit shrinks by any state credit you claim on that same gift, and you can’t deduct what you credit. If you already support a school scholarship fund, ask whether it qualifies before you write the 2027 check.

A word on what “by December 31” means. A check counts for 2026 if you mail it by December 31, and a credit card gift counts on the day of the charge, even when you pay the bill in January. Stock counts when the transfer completes, which is why brokers want instructions early.

December 31 is the legal deadline. The practical one arrives while you’re still eating leftover turkey, so call your CPA this week and save that last Saturday before Christmas for the mall.
