A24’s TV Division Is Quietly Having as Strong a Year as Its Film Team

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Maybe it’s just because we can’t get enough headlines and discussion about “Backrooms” and “You Can See Everything,” but despite the banner year A24 has had at the box office, its TV division has quietly had nearly as strong of a year.

The indie label has produced more shows in 2026 than in any year in its history, including sleeper commercial and critical hits across the board, and even managed to land shows on virtually every streamer in town.

Led by Sam French, the U.S. head of TV at A24, the studio this year has cranked out the third season of “Euphoria” on HBO Max, the second season of “Beef” and the third season of “Survival of the Thickest” both on Netflix, “Margo’s Got Money Troubles” on Apple TV, the “Friday the 13th” prequel series “Crystal Lake” on Peacock, and most recently the British sitcom “It Gets Worse” on Paramount+ led by the UK Heads Piers Wenger and Rose Garnett. Its unscripted division, led by Johnny Hausfater, has also ramped up aggressively, with shows like “Neighbors,” “Born to Bowl,” and “Oobah: Scams for a Better Tomorrow” all on HBO Max. Not unlike its film slate, its series have been a blend of auteur-driven prestige and genre shows with a twist. The David E. Kelley series “Margo’s Got Money Troubles” is a tongue-in-cheek, real-world satire on motherhood and the modern economy about a young single mom played by Elle Fanning who needs to turn to OnlyFans for extra cash. On the other side of the coin, “Crystal Lake” follows masked killer Jason Voorhees’ mom and stands out from other modern horror TV shows by, as our critic wrote, not trying to secretly be a family drama or a slapstick comedy or some other genre or metaphor, but it just amplifies the grisly kills in the best way it can.

The recurring theme between these shows isn’t genre but the stamp of quality that A24 lends.

While something like “Beef” saw a steep drop in viewership of a reported 60 percent between its first and second seasons, “Euphoria” in its return was up 17 percent in its finale ratings compared to Season 2. “Crystal Lake” was also a big hit on Peacock, its top show on the streamer last month, logging 1.3 billion minutes watched over its first 11 days, making it the fourth-highest scripted original series ever on Peacock. A24 now has roughly 30 to 40 shows in various stages of development, pre-production, or active production, with its executive team expanding from about 15 from just 3 people in its early days.

It’s been able to ramp up firstly because of its partnership with numerous TV buyers across the major streamers, but also because of working with largely first-time creators and showrunners. While “Crystal Lake” is a notable exception, A24 generally is operating on its TV side the same way it does with its film, leading with the artist and the project they want to pursue rather than the IP.

That has produced some very personal and idiosyncratic projects over the years like “Ramy” and “The Curse,” the latter of which was the kernel of getting Nathan Fielder to working with A24 again on “You Can See Everything.”

The breadth of content A24 has amassed on its TV side and continually making shows that have the same barrier-pushing flavor and stamp of quality that has become known with A24’s brand is what will help make it as essential a part of the company’s overall business and valuation as its film and distribution business. But we’re seeing even its TV division branching out and embracing IP and tentpoles in the same way its film side has.

Coming up for A24 are a Claire Danes and Ewan McGregor drama “The Spot” on Hulu and “The Apology” with Gwyneth Paltrow on Netflix, which already got a two-season order and was an example of a show that grew out of Paltrow being convinced to try TV after already working with A24 on the film side for “Marty Supreme.” The studio is also is working with Ben Stiller on a New York Knicks documentary series for HBO and a competition cooking show based on the video game “Overcooked” for Netflix. A24 could’ve made a Knicks series with anyone, but teaming with a superfan like Stiller was another example of putting the creator above the concept.

Yet, both a strength and weakness of its TV model is that it does not have a distribution mechanism the way it does on the film side. It’s not bogged down with an unprofitable streamer, a dwindling linear cable channel, or a broadcast network, but that makes it reliant on the many partners it has built up across the industry.

Warner Bros. merging with Paramount arguably reduces the buyers in the market, even if there are still two streamers at Skydance. Netflix just had a massive shakeup of its leadership team that could easily have a different attitude toward A24’s series. And the overall industry trends around the end of Peak TV mean it’s always a volatile business for independents in the TV space.

But no one saw “Backrooms” or “You Can See Everything” coming either, and A24 has as good a chance as any to have lightning strike twice with the next “Euphoria” or “Beef.”
