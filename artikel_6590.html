Is It Rude to Bring Your Own Food to Thanksgiving?

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Before you show up with a casserole (or a bucket of KFC), here's what you need to know about Thanksgiving guest etiquette.

Thanksgiving is a holiday meant to bring people together, yet it has somehow become one of the more divisive days of the year. Do you serve canned cranberry sauce or get fresh cranberries to make one from scratch? Should sweet potatoes be combined with marshmallows? And what time are we eating dinner?

For the record, I say serve both kinds of cranberry sauce, get marshmallows off my sweet potatoes and we eat dinner whenever the host says dinner is ready. I can already feel the consternation boiling up if you disagree. But that is all part of the magic of the holiday!

I think it’s beautiful that no two families celebrate Thanksgiving the same way. Although, with all that preparation and planning, emotions can run high. It is very difficult to cook for large groups of people, and even more so when people have strong opinions about the meal. So if a guest brings their own food, is that considerate or rude? Should they bring enough to share or just what they want to eat?

As a food writer and frequent host, I am going to tell you how to navigate this etiquette dilemma.

Once upon a time, a member of my extended family brought KFC to my house because he did not like what my mom was cooking for dinner. This is undoubtedly rude and totally not OK. But it’s also a more extreme example of what people think of when they imagine “bringing your own food” somewhere.

The short answer here is: no, it’s not OK to bring your own food to Thanksgiving dinner unless it has specifically been identified as a potluck-style meal. You can—and should!—ask the host if you can bring something, but if they say no, you need to respect that.

A more realistic answer is probably it depends. I know, that’s kind of annoying. But I think this is a really nuanced question, and I want to do it justice. There really isn’t a one-size-fits-all answer. Here are some scenarios:

I’m sorry to break it to you, but this isn’t a real reason. It’s definitely a bummer, but sometimes that’s just how things go. Make some light-hearted jokes about it in the group chat with your cousins, and suck it up. This is one meal on one day, and it is not a good enough reason to bring your own food. Have a big breakfast before heading over, and take very small portions. If anyone notices, a simple “I’m saving room for dessert” is a perfect get-out-of-jail-free card.

I suggest bringing appetizers or snacks instead of something for the main meal. Snacks are often overlooked when there is already a turkey in the oven and a pot on every burner. Tell the host you’re bringing a veggie tray and some chips and dips to snack on before the meal. Plus, it’s a separate thing and doesn’t really step on the host’s toes the way bringing an entire lasagna would.

First, know that I support you. But second, this is a you problem, and I think you know that. Load up on dinner rolls and volunteer to make a pot of coffee and do the dishes after the meal. It doesn’t have to be a big deal, and you can get back to regular food on Friday.

This is the biggest exception and one that merits its own section. If you or members of your immediate family have food allergies, you can absolutely bring your own food—but it’s really important to coordinate with the host. It shouldn’t feel like an ambush. Communicating to the host that you will be bringing gluten-free rolls or a dairy-free macaroni and cheese to ensure that you have something safe to eat is totally fine.

And I would argue that this is more relevant with kids than adults. It’s really important for kids to feel welcome in your home and to enjoy this holiday safely with all their family members. Anything you can do to alleviate stress or anxiety is not only good manners but also totally in the spirit of Thanksgiving.

It’s good guest etiquette to communicate with the host in advance. Once you receive the invite, volunteer to bring something. If you’re a champion baker, offer to make the pies or a nice, crusty sourdough loaf. You can even say you have a great Brussels sprouts recipe that’s been a hit with the family lately and you’d love to share it with more people. Or, simply ask if they would prefer a bottle of red or white wine.

Thanksgiving might be the most food-centric day of the year. But in my experience, the food becomes secondary as I sit around the table talking and laughing with my loved ones. There is always a moment where the stress melts away and I’m filled with an overwhelming sense of joy and gratitude. And that, my friends, is what the holiday is all about.
