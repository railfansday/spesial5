Embracing My Age, One Gray Hair and Aching Knee at a Time

I smiled to myself as I thought about the doctor’s diagnosis of “moderate osteoarthritis” in my left knee.

Why did I smile? Because after 21 years of managing HIV, along with high blood pressure and cholesterol, exercising regularly, and eating a healthy diet, I still occasionally need reminding of a simple fact: I am 68 years old.

Taking care of myself doesn’t exempt me from getting older.

A friend who lived next door to me in our college dorm put it well during a conversation about our aging bodies: “Accept. Accept. Accept,” he said.

Or, to borrow the spirit of Sophia Petrillo, the sharp-tongued Sicilian mother in The Golden Girls: You get old. You sag. You get over it.

Mind you, accepting the changes that come with aging doesn’t mean giving up on your health or “letting yourself go.” We can keep moving, eat well, take our medications, and get the care we need. We can also recognize that even conscientious self-care doesn’t give us complete control over our health.

For me, acceptance means doing what I can while making peace with what I can’t change.

One of my first steps toward embracing my eventual status as an “elder”—while thoroughly rejecting the label “elderly”—had to do with my hair. A second step, years later, did too.

Many men know the familiar retreat of a hairline toward the classic U-shape of male pattern baldness. For some, a full head of hair is so bound up with their sense of masculinity that they will spend thousands of dollars trying to restore it.

In my 20s, as my hairline climbed higher, I suspected where things were heading. I vowed that if I ever found myself arranging extra-long strands over the bald spots, I would shave it all off.

Michael Jordan had already made a persuasive case that bald could look terrific. So off went what remained of my hair.

Looking in the mirror, I didn’t shriek in horror. Instead, I thought, “Well, it doesn’t get balder than this. And, you know, I’m okay.”

It helped that I found myself strongly attracted to a man with a bald head. If I could find it appealing in another man, perhaps I could appreciate it in myself.

My second liberation involving hair came four years ago, when I decided to grow a beard. Around the same time, I let the hair on my head grow for a few days, mainly to take stock of what remained.

I generally shaved my head and face every day because I disliked the stubble. I hadn’t seen my natural hair color in years. Imagine my surprise when gray and white appeared among the remnants of my once-dark hair.

For a couple of days, I tried a beard-coloring product, hoping to recapture my old dark brown. Instead, I got a shade of blue-gray steel I had never seen growing naturally from a human head.

Having already embraced my bald head, I decided to embrace my gray and white beard, too. No more trying to reclaim something that used to be. The beard changed my appearance. I looked like a man who could, quite reasonably, pass for his actual age.

I certainly didn’t enjoy having what looked like a very large needle stuck into it for the corticosteroid injection the orthopedist offered. I don’t like needles. You might think I would be accustomed to them after years of blood draws to check that my unwanted viral houseguest remains under control.

But I accepted the injection because I want to keep doing the things I enjoy.

Osteoarthritis is more complicated than the familiar shorthand of “wear and tear,” and it isn’t an inevitable consequence of aging. Still, my diagnosis reminded me that caring for my body also means adapting to its changing needs.

Accepting my bald head and gray beard has helped me practice that adaptability. Embracing the identity of “elder” asks something similar of me: to appreciate the man I am now and take care of him. I want to be a 68-year-old man who looks and feels as terrific as he can. I want to hike, swim, and simply walk without undue pain or limitation. I want to enjoy these years and do what I can to make that possible.

And I regard having these years at all as a privilege.

In my 20s and 30s, so many friends my age died from AIDS. The same virus that medication now keeps under control in my body took their lives before those treatments existed.

They never had the chance to discover what they would look like at 68.

I live my life to honor their memories. A bald head? A gray beard? Other people’s negative opinions weigh not one whit in how I regard them. To me, they are emblems of having been given the honor of living 68 years—and counting.

I still smile at the reminders that, however carefully I mind my health, time is doing its work. These days, getting up from a crouch sometimes requires karate-type noises to accompany the effort.

I’ve always preferred the idea of wearing out from living fully to rusting away from disuse. These days, I’m also learning to give the equipment a little more maintenance.
