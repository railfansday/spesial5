A Condo Costs Less Than a House – Unless the HOA’s Reserves Run Short

A condo or other attached home usually costs less to buy and takes less work to keep up than a detached house, but a board of owners controls its fees, rules, and repair savings. Before you choose one, compare the monthly fee with a house’s upkeep, and check that those savings cover the big repairs ahead.

In October 2026, the median existing condo or co-op sold for $365,100, about $57,000 less than the $421,900 median for a single-family home, the National Association of Realtors reports. Condos also cluster in pricier markets, the group notes, so a condo can let you live where a house is out of reach.

The monthly fee replaces part of what owning a house costs. It covers bills a house owner pays alone, such as exterior repairs, landscaping, and usually the building’s insurance, plus shared amenities like a pool. In exchange, the homeowners association’s elected board sets the fee, decides how much to save in a reserve fund for big repairs, and writes the rules on renting, pets, and renovations.

That fund decides whether the condo stays cheaper than a house. When it can’t cover a new roof or elevator, every owner gets a special assessment, a one-time bill for their share. Fannie Mae won’t buy mortgages on units in condo or co-op buildings that need critical repairs, which leaves fewer mortgages for you and your eventual buyer.

Say a $365,000 condo charges $400 a month, or $4,800 a year. Fannie Mae’s maintenance rule of thumb, 1% to 4% of a home’s value a year, puts upkeep on a house of the same value at $3,650 to $14,600, so the $4,800 fee sits near the low end. The house’s figure leaves out insurance, and the condo owner still repairs and insures the unit’s interior, so the fee mostly replaces bills you’d pay on a house anyway. The price gap is the condo’s real saving.

Any attached home means sharing walls with neighbors. A townhome often includes the land under it, so its fee is usually smaller than a condo’s, and more repairs fall to you. A co-op sells you shares in the corporation that owns the building, and its board approves each buyer.

Before you make an offer, ask the seller for the association’s reserve study, which lists the big repairs coming and how much is saved for them.
