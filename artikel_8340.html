The Top 30 Ski Resorts in the West (2027)

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

Choosing a ski resort for your next trip is about more than snow totals and vertical drop. Sure, you want great snow, lifts that keep you moving, and terrain that makes you want to take one more lap. But last winter reminded us just how much conditions can shape a ski experience—and how difficult it can be to judge a resort when much of its terrain isn’t open or skiing the way it should. A lean snow year across much of the West made for a challenging season, and those conditions appear to have left their mark on this year’s rankings, even at some of North America’s most celebrated destinations.

Every spring, we ask readers to weigh in on the resorts they’ve visited, rating their experiences across 18 categories that span the full spectrum of a ski vacation. From snow, grooming, lifts, and terrain variety to dining, lodging, nightlife, and overall character, these rankings reflect the collective wisdom of skiers who’ve experienced these places firsthand. But this year’s results come with an added wrinkle: When terrain is limited and conditions are far from ideal, it’s not always easy to separate a resort’s inherent strengths from the circumstances of the season. The latest survey went out last April, and the results are in.

The 2027 West rankings, below, reveal which resorts are getting the most right, where they truly shine, and where there’s room to improve. Some familiar names took significant tumbles. Others weathered the challenges and climbed higher. These shifts suggest that even resorts with exceptional terrain, amenities, and reputations aren’t immune to a difficult winter. At the same time, these are overall vacation-experience rankings, not a referendum on snow alone. Our reviews break down each resort’s biggest strengths and weaknesses—on and off the slopes—to help you figure out which destination best fits what you’re looking for in a ski trip.

Think of this as advice from your fellow skiers, informed by a winter that didn’t always make for easy comparisons. These rankings aren’t a one-size-fits-all verdict on where you should spend your winter vacation, nor do they tell the whole story of what a resort can deliver in a better snow year. Explore the rankings, see how your favorites stack up, and discover a few places that might deserve a spot on your list. And if you disagree with the results, even better. Big-mountain skiing meets a bona fide ski town, with enough terrain, dining, nightlife, and family-friendly diversions to keep everyone happy—if you can navigate the crowds.

Don’t let Park City’s No. 30 ranking fool you: This resort has a lot going for it. Readers praise its sheer size, varied terrain, and off-mountain amenities, making it one of the West’s most well-rounded destinations for groups with different abilities and interests. With more than 7,300 acres of lift-served terrain, there’s room for just about everyone, from families cruising groomers and exploring beginner-friendly zones to experts hunting steep, gladed pitches like Condor Woods and the high-alpine bowls of McConkey’s.

Then there’s the town itself. Historic Main Street is the real deal, packed with restaurants, bars, shops, and places to stay, while the broader resort scene offers plenty to do long after the lifts close. Park City scores among the best in nightlife, après, dining, and lodging, and readers appreciate that non-skiers can have a vacation here, too. Families also give the destination high marks, thanks to its mix of terrain and off-slope activities. “I love how close the resort is to town and all the restaurants and other amenities,” commented one survey respondent.

Access is another major advantage. Salt Lake City International Airport is close enough that visitors can often land and be skiing within hours, making Park City an easy choice for a long weekend or a family trip. “It’s the easiest large resort to get to in the U.S. with an incredible amount of terrain,” said one SKI reader. But that convenience is a double-edged sword: Crowds can clog base areas and lift lines, while parking headaches and high prices leave readers wishing the overall experience were a better value. Lift bottlenecks remain a particular sore spot, but lift network upgrades coming for the 2027-’28 season hope to move the needle.

This season brings significant upgrades at Canyons Village, including a new enclosed gondola to replace the aging Cabriolet and a five-story parking garage. Those changes should improve the arrival experience, though new parking fees will add another cost for some visitors. Through it all, Park City’s appeal is undeniable: enormous terrain, a lively town, and easy access from a major airport. “It’s a big mountain with lots of options,” said one Park City skier. “Fun and varied terrain and (usually) great snow.”

“Good for both hard-chargers and solid intermediates.  Lots of varied terrain.  Easy to navigate without taking a lot of lifts.”

At Canyons Village, a new enclosed 10-passenger Skyway Gondola replaces the open-air Cabriolet, with a mid-station serving nearby developments and a five-story garage is expected to open in early December with $29 per day (free after noon and for carpools of four-plus).

On the events front, Shaun White’s The Snow League comes to the mountain Jan. 21–23, 2027, bringing elite halfpipe snowboarding and freeskiing competition, live music, and fan events. Park City has rebuilt its 22-foot Eagle Superpipe for the competition, and the pipe will open to the public after the event.

Jaw-dropping Lake Tahoe views, sprawling terrain, and a lively après scene make Heavenly as much about the good times off the slopes as the skiing itself.

At Heavenly, the scenery is almost as memorable as the skiing. Perched above Lake Tahoe, with terrain straddling the California-Nevada border, the resort delivers one spectacular lake view after another across 5,000 acres of skiable terrain. There’s plenty for a range of abilities, from mellow groomers for families and newer skiers to wide-open blues and approachable black diamonds. Even longtime locals can’t resist pausing to take in the view. “I’ve lived in the area for almost 20 years and those lake views still almost knock me off my skis,” confessed one Heavenly fan.

Heavenly earns high marks for nightlife, dining, and lodging, with stellar après spots, craft breweries, and live music around South Lake Tahoe, plus the casinos and entertainment of nearby Stateline, Nevada. Whether you’re looking to keep the party going or settle in for a long dinner after a day on the mountain, there’s plenty to do beyond skiing.

The biggest frustration? Getting around. Readers dinged Heavenly for crowded lifts, including the gondola, and guest service remains an area for improvement. Some base-area facilities could also use a refresh. Still, those complaints have a way of fading when you crest a ridge, turn a corner, and find Lake Tahoe stretched out below you in all its glory. “There’s no shortage of beautiful runs—especially when the sun is shining and the lake is visible from anywhere on top of the mountain.”

“Heavenly has great grooming systems, so no matter how bad the snow gets, the groomers are fun to ride.”

A family-friendly favorite with forgiving groomers, surprisingly adventurous corners, and one of the easiest getaways from Denver.

As the closest major Epic Pass resort to Denver, Keystone draws plenty of Front Range locals alongside visitors looking for a full mountain vacation. The good news is that both are on tap at this family favorite. The frontside can get busy, particularly on weekends, but there’s more to Keystone than its popular cruisers. Head farther out to explore The Outback or venture into Bergman Bowl, where high-alpine terrain offers adventurous intermediates and families a chance to explore beyond the usual groomer circuit. “Love Keystone and its low-key nature,” shared one fan. “Fun terrain, especially Bergman Bowl and the glades on The Outback.”

For families, Keystone has a lot to offer. Free parking, efficient lifts, a strong ski school, and a location just 90 minutes from Denver make the logistics easier, while base-area wagons help parents haul gear and tired kids. The village isn’t the most authentic, but it has what skiers need, plus fun-for-families extras such as character parades and seasonal events. Don’t miss the annual snow fort atop Dercum Mountain—it’s always a hit with younger skiers. “Keystone is the unquestionable gem of the I-70 corridor,” exclaimed one excited reader.

The resort also scores well for grooming, which is good news for skiers who like confidence-inspiring runs. But don’t assume the mountain is all mellow blues and beginner-friendly terrain. Stronger skiers can find challenging lines on North Peak plus glades in The Windows. Keystone may not rank among Colorado’s most demanding mountains, but those willing to venture beyond the frontside can find plenty to keep them interested. “There’s legit steep terrain here if you know where to look,” said a survey respondent.

Where Keystone falls short is local flavor. Its purpose-built village and convenient setup make for an easy vacation, but the atmosphere can feel more functional than distinctive compared with Colorado’s historic ski towns. Still, for families and Denver-area skiers who prize convenience, well-groomed runs, and straightforward logistics, Keystone remains a dependable choice.

“In a bad snow year (which this one was), Keystone’s snowmaking and grooming made it possible for them to outdistance everyone around them. This was the place to go all season. More than half of my total ski days were here this year.”

Keystone officially debuts Kindred Resort, its first premier luxury ski-in/ski-out hotel, at the base of the River Run Gondola. The new property brings upscale lodging and easy slope access to River Run Village.

Western spirit, family-friendly skiing, and a lively town make Steamboat a welcoming all-around getaway, even when the snow doesn’t cooperate.

Readers appreciate a resort that feels welcoming rather than intimidating, and Steamboat checks that box once again despite a small slip in the rankings. On the mountain, Steamboat offers a satisfying mix of groomers, tree skiing, and more adventurous terrain. Mahogany Ridge’s advanced and expert glades and bowls expand the options for stronger skiers, while the double-black glades Christmas Tree Bowl rewards those willing to venture beyond the cruisers. Still, challenge remains a weaker category in the survey, so experts seeking relentless steeps may want to temper their expectations.

For families and mixed-ability groups, though, the range of terrain—and the resort’s strong service culture—makes it easy to build a ski day that works for everyone. Steamboat scores high once again for its welcoming family vibe. The resort drives that point home with an enticing family-friendly perk offering free skiing for kids 12 and under from Nov. 21 through Dec. 15—no adult purchase required. “The resort really caters to families with young kids by offering some interesting areas for those kids to ski,” said one reader. “The ski school for young kids really leverages these areas, too.”

Steamboat’s other great asset is the town itself, but the much improved base village is beginning to give it a run for its money. The base has matured into a lively place to spend time after skiing, with upscale restaurants, an ice rink, casual slopeside bars, and a bit of nightlife that gives the resort a genuine après scene. But, downtown Steamboat Springs is still a huge draw. “Love the cool cowboy vibe downtown!” exclaimed one fan. It’s a place that retains its own identity, from Western art galleries to legendary F.M. Light & Sons, where you can shop for cowboy hats, boots, knickknacks and more. It’s this blend of mountain convenience and authentic character that keeps Steamboat feeling distinct from the more generic resort villages.

Parking remains a frustration, particularly during busy periods, though new policies aim to encourage carpools and make demand easier to manage. The resort’s popularity can also mean crowds, but recent lift and base-area improvements have helped skiers get moving more efficiently. It’s these types of skier-first initiatives that help Steamboat remain a compelling choice for visitors looking for a welcoming Colorado vacation with plenty of Western flavor.

“It’s not a ‘see and be seen’ kind of ski resort. You go here because you love to ski or you want your family to love to ski. You love the idea of the Wild, Wild West and you’d love to be riding a horse across a high mountain plain. You appreciate the simple things in life, and while this place is getting more popular, you’re hoping it always keeps its mountain-town feel.”

Steamboat is adding Barrow’s Bend, a new beginner-friendly green trail connecting Rendezvous Lodge to Sunshine Peak Lift, and honoring late local Olympian Moose Barrows. They’re raising the Maverick’s Halfpipe base, reducing the snow needed to build it by an estimated 40 to 60 percent and helping it open earlier. Advanced skiers and riders can also sign up for a new full-day Heli-Ready guided experience designed to build skills and confidence for heli-ski terrain.

Getting here is getting easier, too, thanks to air service expanding to 18 major U.S. airports, including new flights from Portland, Ore., and additional options from Austin and Houston.

Six peaks of varied skiing meet a lively historic town packed with great restaurants, après, nightlife, and classic Colorado character.

Breckenridge dropped 10 spots in this year’s rankings, a slide likely influenced by last winter’s disappointing snow across much of the West. But one lean season can’t erase what makes Breck such a reliable all-around ski vacation. Across six peaks, the resort serves up everything from cruisey groomers to steep, high-alpine terrain, with enough variety to keep a mixed-ability group happy for days. “Breckenridge has something for every type of skier,” said one reader. “Mellow greens for beginners and kids, plenty of intermediate terrain, and very challenging hike-to terrain.”

From a vacation perspective, one of Breck’s main draws is all it has going on off the slopes. The historic town has the kind of energy that makes a trip feel like a proper getaway, with a walkable Main Street lined with independent shops, excellent restaurants, lively bars, and live music venues. Readers gave the resort high marks for lodging, dining, nightlife, and après, reflecting a destination where there’s plenty to do after the last chair. “I love the apres-ski at the base of Peak 8 and Peak 7,” shared one survey respondent. “And there are so many restaurants on Main Street depending on your taste and budget.”

The drawbacks are familiar to anyone who has visited during peak periods. Weekend crowds can snarl the base areas, parking is expensive and sometimes inconvenient, and the overall cost of a Breck vacation can add up quickly. On-mountain dining also tends to feel pricey for what you get. Savvy skiers know to start early, move away from the busiest base areas, and explore beyond the most obvious runs. The resort’s size helps, but timing still matters.

For skiers who want a mountain with plenty of terrain and a town that stays lively long after the lifts close, Breckenridge remains a solid choice. Its ranking may have taken a hit after a challenging snow year, but the combination of varied skiing, comfortable lodging, standout dining, and a genuine ski-town atmosphere keeps it firmly in the conversation for a full-fledged Colorado getaway. “You really can’t beat the variety of terrain and the town of Breckenridge is so quaint!”

“While every ski area I’ve been to has its pros and cons, something about Breck always keeps me personally wanting to go back. I’ve been a handful of times now and still feel like there is still more terrain that is safely approachable even if it is slightly outside of my current skill level. And the town is the perfect place to spend a day off the slopes.”

Breckenridge is bringing back its historic “Kingdom of Breckenridge” identity for 2026–’27, reviving a name rooted in the town’s independent spirit. Look for enhanced customer-first initiatives such as new rental delivery options and updated on-mountain dining choices.

A welcoming, family-friendly mountain with well-groomed runs, genuinely helpful staff, and a refreshing value proposition.

Making its debut in SKI’s Resort Guide rankings, Tamarack Resort offers a different kind of destination experience: approachable, relaxed, and refreshingly down-to-earth. Set in Idaho’s West Central Mountains above Lake Cascade, Tamarack wins points for the things that can make or break a family ski trip, from friendly staff who go out of their way to help to well-groomed runs that make cruising the mountain a pleasure. It’s not trying to be the flashiest resort in the West, and that’s part of its charm. “Skiing the way it should be,” said one survey respondent. “No crowds, stress-free powder days, amazing food, the best top-to-bottom groomers, and incredible views.”

Tamarack’s strengths are especially apparent for families and recreational skiers. Grooming helps keep the skiing approachable, while the welcoming atmosphere makes it easy and less intimidating for everyone to find their footing. The resort also scored well for value, making it an ideal target destination for budget-minded skiers. Here, the experience feels less about luxury trappings and more about making skiing fun and accessible. “Tamarack is like having your own private mountain,” shared a reader. “ For a small resort, it gives you a lot of bang for your buck!”

The trade-off is that Tamarack’s terrain may not satisfy skiers looking for endless variety or expert-level challenges. Strong intermediates and families can find plenty to enjoy, but advanced skiers seeking steep, technical terrain may want a bigger mountain with more options. And while Tamarack has the essentials for a mountain getaway, its amenities are more simple and down-home compared to some of the pricier destination resorts on this list.

But for skiers who value a friendly welcome, dependable grooming, and a more affordable family ski vacation over luxury and high-adrenaline terrain, Tamarack makes a compelling case. Its first appearance in the rankings reflects a resort that knows what it does well: an approachable mountain experience where the people can be just as memorable as the skiing. “It’s a hidden gem. Don’t tell anyone!”

“Tamarack has been my secret go-to resort for over 20 years. The mountain is great and it’s never crowded. I am often the only person I can see on a run. And now that the amenities are coming online it’s even better.”

Tamarack is joining Ikon Pass as a bonus mountain this winter. Ikon Pass holders get two free days at the resort. On the slopes, skiers can take advantage of free guided tours with mountain hosts and a refreshed rental fleet with new standard and demo skis. Off the slopes, the resort is adding seasonal experiences and events, including full-moon yoga, snowshoe and wine walks, and a keep an eye out for the Miracle Bar pop-up this winter.

5000-plus acres of bowls, glades, and cruisers meet a lively European-style village with world-class dining and après—if you can stomach the crowds and prices.

Vail has been a fixture in our Top 30 for good reason: Few resorts deliver such an expansive menu of terrain alongside a full-fledged destination experience. The mountain slipped several spots this year, with last winter’s disappointing snow conditions likely playing a role, but its core appeal remains intact. Across 5,280 skiable acres, there are the legendary Back Bowls, technical glades, wide-open cruisers, and enough variety to keep different ability levels happy for days. You can spend a morning chasing powder stashes and an afternoon cruising long blues without ever feeling like you’ve skied the same run twice. “Nothing compares to the Back Bowls, Shangri-La Glade on a powder day, or ending the day on Riva Ridge,” said one longtime Vail skier.

Another strength is the resort’s pedestrian village, with its Bavarian-inspired architecture. It’s one of the liveliest in Colorado, packed with lodging options, restaurants, bars, and places to keep the day going long after last chair. Readers continue to reward Vail for its dining, lodging, and après scene, which help make it a true vacation destination. Recent lift upgrades have also helped move skiers around the sprawling resort more efficiently.

Of course, Vail’s popularity comes at a price—literally. Lift tickets, lodging, meals, and drinks can add up quickly, and the crowds remain a sore point, especially on weekends and holidays. Parking is another familiar headache. Weekday visits can offer a more relaxed experience, but anyone planning a peak-period trip should expect to share the mountain with plenty of other skiers. The snow also needs to cooperate. In a lean season like last winter, even Vail’s massive terrain can’t always deliver the experience its reputation promises, but consensus was that mountain ops did a commendable job with what they got: “Groomers on the frontside were very well maintained and skiable despite the conditions,” said one skier.

Still, Vail remains one of Colorado’s most complete ski vacations. The combination of big-mountain variety, comfortable places to stay, excellent dining, and a village with real energy is hard to beat. It may not be the best choice for budget-conscious skiers or those who prize solitude, but for visitors looking to pair serious skiing with a memorable resort experience, Vail continues to deliver.

“Vail is the pinnacle of Colorado skiing. The most and best variety of terrain. High-speed lifts exactly where they should be. Powder that sticks around for days. And some of the best bars, shops, and restaurants of any ski town.”

Vail is doubling down on off-slope entertainment this winter. Dobson Arena debuts as a reimagined 56,000-square-foot music venue in the heart of the village, and pop-up mountaintop performances at Eagle’s Nest and other locations are in the plans. The resort’s Après All Day series is doubling in size, with more than 50 artists scheduled to perform on weekends throughout the winter.

On the mountain, Epic Ascent adds a concierge and priority lift-line access to private ski lessons, and dining updates range from a rebuilt signature burger to the return of the Ice Bar at the entrance to the Back Bowls.

A high-alpine playground with 360-degree summit skiing, deep snow, and a delightfully adventurous vibe—without the destination-resort price tag.

After a long hiatus, Mt. Bachelor is back in our rankings, and it brings a distinctly Pacific Northwest flavor to the list. This 9,065-foot volcano in Oregon’s Central Cascades is a fickle beast: One minute the skies are clear, the next wind and fog turn the summit into a Middle Earth landscape. But that wild, weather-dependent personality is part of the appeal. When conditions cooperate, skiers can explore 360 degrees of terrain off the summit, with wide-open alpine runs, natural wind lips, and playful features that make every lap feel a little different. “You can always find less-crowded terrain,” said one reader. “The layout is easy to navigate.”

Bachelor’s 4,300-plus acres offer fast groomers, side-piste stashes,  hemlock forests, and adventurous above-treeline terrain. “On a good snow year you can ski anywhere off the top of the mountain,” said a survey respondent. “There’s really no place quite like it!” The mountain earns high marks for its lifts, too, helping skiers make the most of the sprawling layout. Snow is another major draw: With annual snowfall topping 450 inches, Bachelor can stack up deep midwinter conditions, making it a favorite for powder seekers and tree-skiing devotees alike.

The resort also scores well for value and parking, a welcome combination in an era when a ski day can feel like a logistical and financial undertaking. Bachelor’s laid-back atmosphere puts the emphasis on skiing rather than resort trappings, and the mountain’s scale means there’s plenty to explore without the crowds and complications of some better-known destinations. Still, the experience has its trade-offs. There’s no lodging at the base, and on-mountain amenities are limited, so visitors looking for a full-service village best look elsewhere.

Fortunately, nearby Bend offers a lively home base, with breweries, restaurants, and places to stay. For skiers who value big terrain, deep snow, efficient lifts, and a little adventure over luxury, Bachelor is a worthy addition to the rankings—and a reminder that sometimes the best days come with a bit of wildness.

“Big mountain with excellent lift system, good snow, and grooming. With the base at 6,000 feet it has a long season and rarely seems to lack snow. I have skied here many times and it rarely seems to be crowded during the week.”

Mt. Bachelor is upgrading its Northwest Express chairlift with rebuilt top and bottom terminals, a new electric motor and diesel backup, and buried power and fiber lines designed to improve reliability during storms.

A laid-back local favorite with deep snow, playful terrain, and wallet-friendly prices, Brighton keeps things refreshingly unpretentious.

Tucked into Big Cottonwood Canyon alongside Solitude, this community-minded mountain wins skiers over with its welcoming, no-frills vibe, reliable terrain, and prices that feel refreshingly reasonable by Wasatch standards. It may not have the name recognition or polish of some of its neighbors, but Brighton has a loyal following and a character all its own. “I never really went to this resort but headed there with the lack of snow elsewhere and was amazed by the quality of skiing,” said one reader. “Skied here for a week and will be back next year!”

The snow is a major draw. Brighton’s position in the Wasatch makes it a compelling option for powder seekers, with terrain that rewards everyone from casual cruisers to skiers looking to duck into the trees. “Brighton had some of the best snow in the Wasatch last year,” shared one survey respondent. And with value among its top-scoring categories, the resort offers a relatively affordable way to tap into Utah’s famously deep snow. For visitors who care more about fresh turns than fancy amenities, Brighton can feel like a well-kept secret, even if it’s hardly undiscovered.

Of course, easy access has its downsides. Brighton’s proximity to Salt Lake City makes it convenient for locals and visitors alike, but canyon traffic, parking headaches, and crowds can complicate a powder day. The base area is modest, with limited dining and après options and little in the way of nightlife. Readers also gave the resort lower marks for dining and guest service, underscoring that Brighton’s appeal lies more in the skiing than the extras.

Still, Brighton’s climb to No. 22 reflects the appeal of simplicity: good snow, fun terrain, a friendly atmosphere, and an appealing price point. It may not be for skiers who want a bustling village or a full slate of amenities, but for those who measure a great day by the turns they make, Brighton delivers.

“Brighton is a great local choice that has really evolved over the last few years thanks to increased investment and attention from Boyne. Expanded night riding, the Crest 6, and a new mid-mountain lodge make for a great experience.”

Brighton celebrates its 90th anniversary this winter and is expanding night skiing to six nights a week. There are also new ticket options including an Off-Peak Pass and a Flex Pass for three, four, or five days of skiing. On the mountain, the Milly Chalet has been revamped as a quick-service Mexican restaurant serving tacos and margaritas.

A fiercely independent spirit, Southwestern soul, and some of the continent’s most challenging in-bounds terrain make Taos a mountain skiers fall hard for.

Taos climbed two spots this year despite the fact that last winter’s lean snow season hit the Southwest particularly hard—and that’s saying something. The Reader Resort Survey still shows what makes Taos special: a true sense of character, terrain that demands respect, and skiers who are eager to return year after year. Taos has an old-school spirit and a spicy Southwestern personality that’s unique to ski country.

Taos scored among its best in the challenge category, thanks to marquee steeps on Kachina Peak and hike-to terrain in West Basin. “I have skied here for over 50 years and am still discovering new lines and runs,” admitted one longtime skier. Yet Taos isn’t exclusively for experts. Its 1,294 acres are manageable enough for families to explore together, while traditional European-style Ski Weeks and the slopeside Blake Hotel lend the resort a distinctly alpine feel—with a New Mexico twist.

Taos also scored well for parking, an unsexy but practical perk that can make a ski day feel easier. The trade-off is a relatively small base village, with limited lodging and restaurant options compared with larger destinations. Après and grooming aren’t its strong suits, either. For a fuller taste of the region, make time for the town of Taos, where galleries, chile-forward cuisine, and the historic Taos Pueblo—a UNESCO World Heritage Site—offer plenty of reasons to stay off the mountain for a while. “Lots to do once you’re off the slopes and the New Mexico food is to die for!” said one survey respondent.

For skiers who value personality over polish, Taos has a way of getting under your skin. Its combination of serious terrain, independent character, and skier loyalty suggests that Taos’ devotees are in it for the long haul. When the snow cooperates, this mountain’s mix of steep lines, high-desert scenery, and Southwestern flavor is tough to replicate.

“Taos Ski Valley has kept its original charm. Definitely no corporate vibe here. When the snow is good, it’s a wonderful place.”

Taos has invested heavily in mountain improvements for 2026–’27, including additional glading, early-season snowmaking capabilities, and a satellite-based snow-depth mapping system to help crews groom more efficiently.

Big-mountain terrain, steep lines, and a spectacular Lake Tahoe backdrop make this a must-ski for experts willing to brave the crowds.

Palisades Tahoe is back in the rankings, and it’s easy to see why. Year after year, the resort earns high marks for challenge and terrain variety, two categories that matter most to skiers who like their mountains steep, technical, and a little intimidating. From legendary lines off KT-22 to big-mountain terrain that can keep even the strongest skiers busy, Palisades delivers the kind of skiing that reminds you why you fell in love with the sport in the first place. “More lift-served extreme terrain than most other places,” said one Palisades fan. “It’s incredible!”

One thing that sets Palisades apart from the rest is its proximity to Lake Tahoe, one of the most memorable backdrops in North American skiing. While the views aren’t quite as in-your-face as they are at Heavenly, they’re nothing to scoff at. And when the lifts stop spinning, Palisades has an après and nightlife scene with plenty of character, including iconic bars where swapping stories about the day’s lines is part of the ritual.

The biggest challenge is getting to the skiing itself. Parking reservations, crowded lots, and high parking fees remain sore points, especially on powder days when everyone seems to have the same idea. Reader also said that on-mountain dining can also use a boost, both in food quality and variety. And while Palisades can deliver unforgettable conditions, snow quality and grooming didn’t score as consistently well as its more celebrated terrain.

Still, for skiers who prize steep pitches and skiing in the tracks of the greats, Palisades Tahoe is worth the effort. Catch it on a storm day, and the combination of serious skiing and that Tahoe scenery can make for a bucket-list day on snow. “Great place to push your abilities if you want to, or just cruise away in the sunshine!”

“Great variety between the two mountains—legendary steeps at Palisades, excellent bowls and advanced terrain at Alpine. On the Alpine side you can find fresh tracks all day on a powder day.”

The resort is expanding snowmaking along Mountain Run and adding three diesel-electric snowcats equipped with technology that measures snow depth in an effort to provide a better early-season experience.

For learners, a new Progression Pass bundles three group lessons with lift tickets and rentals; skiers who complete all three lessons earn a season pass for the rest of the winter. Families can also take advantage of Kids Ski Free Week, Jan. 25–31, 2027, with free lift tickets for children and 20 percent off rentals, demos, and group lessons.

Impeccable grooming, attentive service, and family-friendly luxuries define this polished Rocky Mountain resort, where the skiing is excellent and the price of admission is steep.

The mountain offers plenty to keep skiers busy, even if its reputation for polished corduroy sometimes overshadows its more demanding terrain. Intermediates can cruise wide, beautifully maintained groomers, while families can explore the welcoming learning terrain at one of several beginner’s areas. On the other end of the spectrum, skiers can test themselves on sections of the Birds of Prey World Cup course when it’s not in use, or seek out the rocky, technical lines of the Stone Creek Chutes. The lifts keep things moving, and the resort’s attention to snow preparation makes even a routine groomer lap feel like a treat. “Surprisingly good options for advanced to expert skiers,” shared one Beaver Creek fan. “And since no one is seeking out that terrain, it’s never crowded.”

Off the slopes, Beaver Creek embraces its upscale identity. The pedestrian village, with its ice-skating rink, firepits, and Alps-inspired architecture, is designed for lingering, whether that means a leisurely dinner, a drink after skiing, or an afternoon with the kids. Dining and lodging are major strengths, with options that make it easy to turn a few days on the mountain into a full-fledged getaway. Families, in particular, benefit from the resort’s thoughtful touches, from its ski school to its signature Candy Cabin.

From the moment you pull up to the storied Sun Valley Lodge to your first carve down a freshly groomed run on Bald Mountain, there’s a sense that someone here has thought of everything. Consider it old-school hospitality with very good skiing attached.

Baldy, as the locals call it, is the main event. Its sustained fall-line pitches are tailor-made for skiers who like to lay over big, satisfying turns on perfectly manicured corduroy. The grooming is among the best around, and modern lifts make it easy to rack up laps without wasting half the morning in line. Families and newer skiers can head to Dollar Mountain, where approachable terrain offers room to build confidence. The trade-off? Sun Valley doesn’t have the sprawling mix of terrain found at some larger Western resorts, and natural snowfall can be less reliable than at its snowier neighbors. But the mountain operations team has earned its reputation for making the most of what falls from the sky. “Excellent snowmaking and grooming,” opined one Sun Valley skier. “The resort maintained terrific conditions—far better than any other U.S. resort—despite the lack of snow this season.”

Just down the road, Ketchum delivers the sort of genuine mountain-town character that can be hard to find amid the condos and carefully curated villages of newer resorts. Locals and visitors mingle over après drinks at the mix of trendier bars and well-established watering holes. The airport in nearby Hailey makes getting here easier than you might expect, with service from four major airlines connecting the area to eight U.S. cities. It’s still a trip that takes some planning, but the payoff is a ski vacation with a distinctive sense of place. “Sun Valley is magical. The town of Ketchum is a perfect ski town,” said one reader.

“Sun Valley always provides an exceptional experience. Their snowmaking and grooming is impeccable, always. While there may not have been much snow out West this season, I consistently skied over 50k vertical days! The village, restaurants, and Ketchum as a whole is a skiers paradise!”

Sun Valley is replacing two lifts this winter: Lookout Express will be upgraded to a detachable six-person lift and the Christmas fixed-grip quad will become a high-speed quad. They also added 50 acres of glades on Bald Mountain.

Sun Valley has also earned a first-in-the-nation DarkSky certification, recognizing its commitment to protecting the naturally dark night skies that make this corner of Idaho special.

Finally, for ski-racing fans, the Audi FIS Ski World Cup Finals return to Bald Mountain on March 19–25, bringing the world’s best racers to one of the sport’s most storied American venues.

A mountain of epic proportions, a lift network built for maximum laps, and enough Lone Peak bragging rights to last all winter.

Big Sky may have relinquished its No. 1 crown, but dropping to No. 5 is hardly a fall from grace. This is still one of North America’s most impressive ski experiences, with a seemingly endless supply of terrain, wide-open spaces, and a lift network that makes getting around a whole lot easier than it used to be. In southwestern Montana, Big Sky does everything on a big scale—and largely delivers. “When it comes to terrain, infrastructure, snow quality and natural splendeur, Big Sky is the real deal!”

The headliner is Lone Peak, whose 11,166-foot summit serves up steep chutes, exposed lines, and big-mountain terrain. But you don’t have to be a send-it-all-the-time expert to appreciate the place. Intermediates can rack up miles on rolling groomers and wide-open trails, while beginners have room to find their feet without feeling swallowed by the mountain. With so much terrain spread across the resort, there’s always another zone to explore and another reason to come back.

Boyne Resorts has spent years making that sprawling playground easier to ski, and the results are hard to miss. Heated high-speed lifts, including the eight-person Madison 8, keep the laps coming, while the Lone Peak Tram delivers skiers to the summit in less than four minutes. The new-last-season Explorer Gondola is the latest upgrade, connecting Mountain Village and the upper mountain, making it easier to spend more time skiing. It hasn’t gone unnoticed: “Big Sky has spent an extensive amount of money on new lifts,” commented one SKI reader. “Trams, gondolas, six- and eight-person bubble heated seat chairs. By far the closest to the European experience.”

Of course, skiing at this scale comes with a price tag to match. Readers dinged Big Sky for expensive lodging, on-mountain dining, and add-on costs, including parking and the tram ride to Lone Peak. The village could also use a little more après-ski energy once the lifts stop spinning. Town Center, a couple of miles down the road, offers more options for dinner and drinks, but Big Sky isn’t exactly the place to book if your ideal ski vacation involves dancing until last call. Then again, when the mountain is this good, an early bedtime and first chair might be just what the body needs.

“I skied there for four days and can ski another 40 and never get bored. Endless double and triple black lines off Lone Peak.  More and better expert terrain than anywhere I have seen in the U.S., but also fabulous terrain variety.”

It’s the first full winter for the 10-person Explorer Gondola and Kircliff Observatory. The gondola connects the base village with the Lone Peak Tram, and Kercliff, located at 11,166 feet, delivers sweeping 360-views across three states and two national parks.

Nearly 550 inches of snow a year, terrain worthy of its cult following, and a refreshingly old-school approach to skiing.

Some ski resorts sell a lifestyle. Alta sells skiing—and lots of it. Heading into its 89th season, this Little Cottonwood Canyon classic has climbed from No. 6 to No. 4 in our rankings, a reminder that you don’t need a flashy village or a thrilling après lineup to win over skiers. Alta is unapologetically skier-centric, resolutely low-key, and gloriously uninterested in keeping up with the Joneses. “A true skiers’ paradise.”

The snow helps. A lot. Alta earned the top spot in our snow category, thanks to nearly 550 inches of famously light Utah powder in an average season. When a storm rolls through, the mountain’s bowls, chutes, and steep pitches deliver the kind of deep, floaty turns that can make even a seasoned skier feel like a convert. “It’s powder heaven,” says one cult of Alta member. “All the best skiing days of my life have been at Alta.” But unlike some hardcore-favorite mountains, Alta isn’t exclusively for experts. There’s plenty of intermediate terrain, too, even if the steep-and-deep stuff tends to hog the spotlight.

The experience feels refreshingly old-school, from the modest base village to the emphasis on skiing over spectacle. Lodging and dining are available right at the mountain, and the overall cost can compare favorably with flashier destination resorts, particularly if you value time on snow over a resort-town nightlife scene. Families who appreciate a simpler ski vacation may find plenty to love here, though the lack of non-skiing diversions and the mountain’s skiers-only policy mean Alta isn’t everyone’s cup of cocoa.

Getting there can be the rub. Little Cottonwood Canyon traffic is notoriously challenging on powder days, weekends, and holidays, and a storm can turn the trip uphill into an exercise in patience. Staying in one of Alta’s village lodges can spare you some of the daily commute, but for everyone else, an early start and a flexible attitude are wise. Of course, once you’re floating through fresh snow, the traffic-induced grumbling tends to fade. That’s the Alta effect: You swear you’ll never sit in that canyon traffic again, then wake up the next morning and do it all over again. “When I die, you can bury me at Alta.”

“The terrain is phenomenal, but it’s not for everybody. It’s definitely a vibe.”

The multiyear renovation and expansion of Albion Lodge is complete, including the new Albion Grill with expanded patio seating. There’s also more opportunity for women to build skills and confidence with an expanded lineup of women’s camps, clinics, and lessons, led by experienced female instructors.

World-class lifts, seriously good food, and big-mountain skiing just 45 minutes from Salt Lake City. No wonder skiers keep coming back.

Snowbasin has been a reader favorite for years, and they’re not letting up: The Wasatch resort holds steady at No. 3 for 2027. Just 45 minutes from Salt Lake City, Snowbasin delivers big-mountain skiing with an unusually smooth experience from the parking lot to the last chair. That combination of easy access and painless parking is no small feat in Utah.

What really sets Snowbasin apart, though, is how well it does just about everything. The lifts are fast, the grooming is dialed, the guest service is excellent, and the on-mountain dining is a cut above the rest. The grand lodges feel more like mountain retreats than places to grab a quick lunch, and the terrain offers something for just about every kind of skier. Cruise the wide-open groomers off the DeMoisy Express, or head for the bowls, steeps, and cornices around Needles Cirque when you’re ready to get your heart rate up. It’s a mountain that can keep a mixed-ability group happy without anyone feeling like they’re compromising.

That polished experience extends to the people behind it. Snowbasin consistently earns high marks for guest service, and its dining options deliver both variety and quality. In other words, the mountain knows that a great ski day involves more than vertical feet. You’ll find plenty of places to refuel in style, whether you’re settling in for a leisurely lunch or just warming up between laps. “Everything is sublime!” said one happy Snowbasin skier. “The bathrooms are top-tier and the food is scrumptious, great gluten-free options compared to other resorts.”

There is one notable trade-off: No lodging at the base means Snowbasin isn’t a true destination resort in the traditional sense. And if you’re looking for a lively après scene right outside the lifts, you’ll need to look elsewhere. Luckily, nearby Ogden offers a range of places to stay, eat, and unwind, while Salt Lake City puts even more options within reach.

The bigger challenge these days is that the secret is decidedly out. Snowbasin has become a popular alternative when Little Cottonwood Canyon closes. With the resort slated to host alpine racing at the 2034 Winter Olympics, that spotlight will only grow brighter. The upside for skiers? Continued investment in lifts, terrain, and infrastructure that should make an already excellent mountain even better. “They’ve invested heavily in the lift infrastructure—the new Becker lift this season was a tremendous improvement and makes getting over to Strawberry so easy,” shared one survey respondent.

“Even in a low-snow winter, Snowbasin shined with a great experience. Their staff is the best in the industry, and the amenities are unbeatable. If you’re stuck on groomed trails, there really isn’t a better place. But on a powder day, this place really performs.”

Big-mountain terrain, jaw-dropping scenery, and a ski trip that won’t break the bank. What’s not to love?

A jump from No. 15 to No. 2 is no small feat, but Lake Louise has plenty going for it. Start with the value: Thanks to the favorable Canadian dollar, your ski-trip budget goes further here. Then there’s the skiing. Set within the spectacular wilderness of Banff National Park, Lake Louise serves up 4,200 acres of terrain with a distinctly Canadian Rockies flavor. Fast, confidence-building groomers? Check. Steep, rugged lines on the backside? Absolutely. Wide-open bowls, tree skiing, and enough variety to keep even seasoned skiers exploring? You bet. The scenery is almost unfairly good, too. As one reader put it, “Fantastic steep and deep chute skiing. No lines and amazing snow. Can’t be beat.”

The resort’s latest terrain additions make an already sprawling mountain even easier to explore. New connections link key areas of the mountain, while expanded glades give intermediate and advanced skiers more options in the trees. Snowmaking improvements on Richardson’s Ridge should also help deliver more consistent conditions when Mother Nature needs a little backup.

All of that said, you won’t find slopeside lodging or much in the way of après at the ski area itself. For that, head to Banff, a lively mountain town about 35 miles away with plenty of restaurants, bars, shops, and places to stay. Free, regular shuttles connect the town to the resort, so a rental car isn’t a must. And while Lake Louise may not have the buzzy village scene of some destination resorts, that’s part of its appeal: You come here for the mountain, the wilderness, and the kind of big-skiing experience that still feels refreshingly unpretentious. As one aficionado summed it up: “Lake Louise is simply the greatest. Love the mountain. Love Banff.”

“Lots of snow this year. I had some amazing powder days at Louise. The back bowls are some of my favorite areas to ski as well as the West Bowl. Even on non powder days, I enjoyed cruising in the alpine on the frontside.”

The resort adds Grove Glade, a groomed green run linking Merlin’s directly to the new Richardson’s Ridge chairlift, while Pikaboo and Prunepicker improve connections between key zones. Expanded intermediate and advanced glades offer even more tree-skiing options.

The resort is also upgrading the Top of the World chairlift to improve loading capacity, speed, and reliability, and expanding snowmaking coverage on Richardson’s Ridge for more consistent conditions.

A high-alpine playground with naturally reliable snow, fast lifts, and jaw-dropping Canadian Rockies views. Now, for the first time, it’s North America’s favorite ski resort.

There’s a reason skiers keep coming back to Banff Sunshine. Actually, there are several: big-mountain scenery, reliably good snow, a long season, and terrain that ranges from mellow groomers to legitimately intimidating steeps. “High elevation means great quality and plentiful snow throughout the very long season,” wrote one Sunshine fan. “Always finding new fun spots even though I’ve been skiing here for years.”

And then there’s the setting. Just 15 minutes from the lively town of Banff, Sunshine feels like a world apart once you’re riding the gondola up through the trees toward its sprawling, above-treeline playground. It’s the kind of place that reminds you why you ski in the first place.

This year, our readers put Banff Sunshine at the top of North America’s 50 best resorts for the first time ever, bumping it up from last year’s No. 2 ranking. It makes sense. Sunshine has invested in its lift system in recent years, making it easier to explore the mountain and a whole lot more comfortable to ride on blustery days. Bubble enclosures on select lifts are a particularly welcome touch at a resort where exposed alpine terrain is part of the appeal—and where the weather can be as big as the views.

The skiing itself offers plenty of ways to spend a day. Cruise rolling groomers, roam across wide-open bowls, or work your way toward the steeper, more technical terrain. For experts, Delirium Dive is talked up a lot, but don’t overlook some other sleepers, including Stampede and Saddledome on Goat’s Eye Mountain. Sunshine may not have the sheer variety of some larger North American resorts, but its mix of approachable laps and high-alpine challenges makes it an easy place for a group with different abilities to enjoy skiing together.

Then there’s the snow. With a summit just shy of 9,000 feet and no snowmaking, Sunshine depends entirely on Mother Nature—and has the elevation and climate to make the most of it. Its season regularly stretches into late May, giving skiers a shot at spring turns long after many other resorts have called it a year. That extended season, combined with the big-mountain setting, is a pretty compelling argument for making the trip. “Banff is my favorite place to ski,” opined one survey respondent. “The mountain is beautiful, the terrain is varied, and it’s not overcrowded. The town of Banff is perfect. Like a postcard. Most beautiful place in North America.”

The one catch? You can’t exactly roll out of bed and onto the lifts from a sprawling slopeside resort village. Sunshine Mountain Lodge is the ski area’s only overnight accommodation, and scoring a room means waking up to a rare kind of quiet, with first tracks practically on your doorstep. Everyone else can stay in Banff, where free shuttles make getting to the mountain refreshingly simple. The town has everything from affordable hotels to more upscale digs, plus enough restaurants, bars, bowling alleys, and other diversions to fill an evening—or several. The combination of wild alpine skiing by day and a proper mountain town by night is hard to beat.

What’s New The ski area is amping up its après offerings this season. The GOAT Club returns to Goat’s Eye Lodge for a second season of live music, DJs, and après parties, and the new Coco Lounge, on the deck of the Centennial Lodge, will serve cocktails and food with fireside seating and slope views. If you need a warm-up between laps,  Coco Lab Coffee & Hot Chocolate Bar is now at Goat’s Eye Lodge serving hot drinks. There’s also a new cocktail bar if you want something a little stronger.
