20 Bartering & Swapping Sites – Pick One by What You’re Trading

Books, games, household goods, services, and places to stay each have swap sites built for them, so start with the one made for what you’re trading. Before you list, check who pays shipping, what membership or fees cost, and how the site rates traders, since in most swaps you’re counting on a stranger to send their half.

A swap counts as income to the IRS even when no cash changes hands. Its Tax Topic 420 says what you receive from bartering goes in your gross income, at its fair market value, for the year you receive it.

That makes a trade worth the same care as a purchase, starting with where you make it. Each kind of trade has sites built for it, and they differ most on three things: who pays to ship, what membership or fees cost, and what protects you when the other side doesn’t deliver.

Mailed trades are safest on sites that rate their members, where a new trader can start small with people who have a trading history. Trades of bulky goods and local services happen in person, so meet in a public place.

Craigslist’s barter section lets you post what you have and what you’d take for it. Posting there is free, since barter isn’t one of the categories Craigslist charges for. Trades are local and in person, so follow its own advice and meet in a public place such as a bank or police department, never at your home.

List an item on Facebook Marketplace or in a buy-and-sell group on Facebook, and say what you’d take in trade. Listing is free. Marketplace has ratings for buyers and sellers, so check a trader’s ratings first, then meet in a public place.

Listia runs auctions in points instead of dollars. You earn points when other members buy your items and spend them on things you want, and signing up gets you your first 50 points free. Past those, your points come from listing your own things, so you’ll need to give some away before you can get much.

Everything posted on Freecycle must be free, so it’s a place to give things away and pick up what neighbors give, not to trade. Membership is free, and volunteers moderate each local group.

The Buy Nothing Project runs local gift groups in its app and on Facebook, where neighbors give and ask for things at no cost. The basic app is free, and a paid Pro tier adds extras. Before you buy something you’ll use only briefly, ask your Buy Nothing group for it.

Rehash swaps clothes and accessories by mail. A member requests a trade, and once you accept, each of you mails your side. There are no listing or trading fees, just your own postage. If a trade goes wrong, Rehash’s remedy is to give the other member negative karma, so check a trader’s karma first.

BookMooch runs on points. You earn 1 point for each book you mail to a member who asks for it, or 3 if it goes abroad, and a book you receive costs the same. Joining is free, and your one cost is postage on what you send. For every two books you receive, you must send at least one.

PaperBackSwap trades paperbacks, hardcovers, audiobooks, and textbooks by mail. Mail a book another member requests, and you can pick one from the club in return. You pay postage on what you send, and the books you receive arrive postage-paid. Each request costs a book credit plus a 99-cent swap fee. The $25-a-year Standard plan waives the fee on every request and the $15-a-year Limited plan on 30 requests a year, and each mailing you send with the site’s printable postage earns one fee-free request.

SwapaCD and SwapaDVD trade original, playable CDs and DVDs by credit. Each disc you mail earns a credit, and each disc you request costs at least one credit plus a 49-cent fee, and the sender pays the postage.

GameTZ lists the games, music, movies, and books its members have and want, flags the matches, and charges no fees to trade. Each side mails its half, then rates the other on speed, honesty, and courtesy. As a new member, you’re expected to mail your half first, so start with small trades with members who have good-trader stars.

BoardGameGeek’s math trades swap board games among many members at once. You list the games you’ll give up and the ones you’d take for each. An algorithm then decides who mails what to whom, so you end up with a game you prefer to your own or keep yours. Your main cost is shipping, which the site’s guide warns can easily top $10 for a heavy game. Each trade’s moderator sets its rules and deadlines, so read them before you enter.

In a time bank, an hour you spend helping one member earns an hour of help from any other member, whatever the work. TimeBanks.org, a charity, keeps a directory of local time banks. Because hours are the only currency, a time bank is only as useful as the services its local members offer, so look at its listings before you join.

Shared Earth pairs gardeners and farmers with landowners who have space to spare. You search listings near you and work out a plan with the landowner, such as sharing what you grow. It’s a project of the nonprofit Sustainable America and asks users for donations. The terms are yours and the landowner’s to set, so put them in writing.

HomeExchange members swap homes for vacations, either directly or with GuestPoints earned by hosting, which let you stay somewhere without matching anyone’s dates. Membership costs $235 a year for unlimited exchanges, and the site verifies members and their homes. You’ll host members in your home to earn stays, so it suits a traveler with a place others want to visit.

On Intervac, you and a family somewhere else stay in each other’s homes over the same vacation dates, so you both need plans that line up. A year’s membership costs $130, or $230 for two years, and new members get a free 21-day trial.

Couchsurfing hosts let travelers stay for free, and you can host in return. Hosting or asking for a stay takes a paid membership, and ID verification, which hosts favor, is a separate yearly add-on. Read a host’s references from past guests before you send a stay request.

Workaway hosts give volunteers food and a place to stay in exchange for about 4 to 5 hours of help a day, 5 days a week. An individual account costs $69 a year. Workaway checks every profile before it goes live but doesn’t visit every host, so vet a host yourself before you go.

GoSwap is for permanent trades of big assets, such as homes, other property, businesses, cars, and boats. A listing costs 4 credits, at 50 cents a credit. Once both sides accept a swap, each pays 500 credits, worth $250, per listing for most assets, a fee refunded only if the swap ends before its documents are unlocked. Treat a deal there like any property or vehicle sale, with inspections, a title check, and a lawyer.

On BizX, a barter exchange, businesses sell goods or services to other members for BizX dollars, each worth $1, and spend them in the network. There’s no membership fee, but BizX charges a cash fee of 6% to 7.5% on each buy and sell. Trade dollars are only good inside the network, so join only if its members sell what you’d buy.

ITEX runs a similar marketplace in ITEX dollars, open to individuals as well as businesses. It charges a 6% cash fee when you buy or sell, plus $25 in cash and 10 ITEX dollars every four weeks. As with BizX, the dollars you earn spend only with other members.

A barter exchange such as BizX or ITEX files Form 1099-B with the IRS for its members’ trades, and you should get a copy. Trades you arrange directly don’t come with a 1099-B, though people who trade services may have to file Form 1099-MISC. Either way, the IRS says barter income from your business generally goes on Schedule C, and other barter income on Schedule 1.

Write down what each trade was worth on the day you make it, since that fair market value is what you’d report as income.
