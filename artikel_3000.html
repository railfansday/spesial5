Your AI Agents Need Job Descriptions

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Sunil Padiyar is the Chief Technology Officer (CTO) of Trintech, a global leader in AI Financial Close solutions.

​When an AI agent enters an enterprise workflow, technology leaders need to ask more than, “What can it do?” They also need to ask, “Under whose identity will it act, and what authority will that identity carry?”

An agent may be capable of reading records, changing them and approving transactions, even if its assigned role requires only one of those capabilities. Connecting it through a broad service account can grant more access than the work requires. That is an architecture decision disguised as a configuration detail.

We would never hire an employee, give that person unrestricted access to financial systems and simply say, “Use your judgment.” We define a role, grant appropriate access, establish approval authority and create accountability. As AI agents begin doing real work, they need the digital equivalent. The more consequential the work, the more precisely that job description needs to be defined.

A useful way to define an agent’s role is to treat it like a job description. Spell out the work it owns, the information and systems it can reach, the actions it may take, the limits it must respect and the situations that call for a handoff.

Consider an agent assigned to prepare journal entries. Its role could include retrieving supporting data, drafting an entry and attaching evidence. It could submit the draft for review, while approval and posting remain outside its authority. A conflicting record or an entry above a defined threshold could send the work to a person with the supporting evidence attached.

I would define an agent’s role across six areas:

• Role: Give the agent a bounded responsibility, such as preparing journal entries for review, rather than a broad mandate to “manage the close.”

• Access: Grant only the data and system functions needed for that responsibility.

• Authority: Specify the business actions the agent may take, such as drafting or submitting an entry, and which require separate approval.

• Boundaries: Set prohibited actions and thresholds that restrict what the agent can do.

• Escalation: Define when the agent must stop, who receives the case and what context they need to assess it.

• Accountability: Assign an owner to review permissions, monitor activity and update the role as the process changes.

These areas connect, but they answer different questions. Access determines what information or system functions an agent can reach. Authority determines what business actions it may take. An agent may need to read a ledger to prepare an entry; that access should not automatically let it approve the entry or change the ledger.

The order matters. Teams often begin with a task and a connection to the tools needed to complete it. They should define the agent’s authority before making those connections. Starting with an existing service account and relying on prompts or process guidance to limit the agent can leave important restrictions outside the system’s access controls.

Each agent should also have a distinct identity, so its actions can be traced to that agent instead of disappearing into a shared account. At the point of action, the system should verify that the agent’s role authorizes the requested operation. The resulting record should show what the agent did, under which role and with what authorization. System owners can then review activity and change or revoke access as responsibilities evolve.

The controls should reflect the consequences of the action. An agent that organizes documents may have more latitude than one that changes financial records, alters supplier details or releases funds. Actions with a material effect may require human approval, a second authorized identity or both.

Agents do not share human judgment or responsibility. The job description analogy is useful because it makes a design requirement visible: Before an agent acts independently, its identity, permissions and authorization checks should match the work it has been assigned. Autonomy should never mean unlimited authority. The safest path to more autonomous AI is to become more precise about the authority we give it.
