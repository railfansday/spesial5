What Does Silence Mean in Your Relationships?

I've worked with several couples where the same pattern occurs again and again. They get into a disagreement, and then things escalate until one person goes silent. Silence can have many meanings, but it's not always clear what it means.

Some partners, faced with silence, panic. They don't know what the silence means. They feel shut out and rejected; they escalate and engage more, or they, too, withdraw. But silence isn't universally negative. Silence can be positive, but for many couples, navigating it feels like a minefield that can blow up at any moment.

Researchers have identified a few different patterns in relationships that lead to poor outcomes, like the demand-withdraw pattern and the pursue-withdraw pattern (Christensen & Heavey, 1990; Schrodt et al., 2014). These patterns are ultimately unproductive because the issue never really gets resolved, and they generate a lot of conflict. Conflict, by itself, isn't negative per se (Gottman, 1994). Gottman's research shows that many couples fight, and it isn't destructive if they fight smart and repair any damage done (Gottman & Silver, 1999).

So, let's look at the really harmful form of silence, which comes from the temporal need-threat model (Williams, 2009). He found that the silent treatment threatens four basic needs: belonging, self-esteem, control, and a sense of meaningful existence. Murphy and Hoover (1999) argue that “hostile withdrawal” is a form of emotional abuse. Schrodt et al. (2014) conducted an analysis that included 74 studies of the demand-withdraw pattern, and they found that this pattern predicts lower relationship satisfaction.

Silence can also come from a person feeling overwhelmed. This is what psychologist Gottman (1994) identified as “flooding,” leading to "stonewalling.” It occurs when a person becomes so physiologically aroused that it feels like there are too many inputs at once. The person can't process what's being said or all the emotions it triggers, and so the system overloads and shuts down. One person’s overwhelm can transfer to their partner. Butler et al. (2003) observed that suppressing emotion in conversation increased their partner's blood pressure, so even overwhelmed silence can negatively impact their partner. People who are more avoidant may use "deactivating strategies" (Mikulincer & Shaver, 2016), such as disconnecting from their partner during conflict. In my experience, this can also escalate and intensify conflict because it becomes not just a disagreement, but a threat to the connection.

But as I said before, silence isn't always negative; it may occur because one partner wants to process and reflect before continuing the conversation. Kross and Ayduk (2017) found evidence that supports the effectiveness of self-distancing strategies. This involves a person stepping away from a conversation to reduce emotional reactivity and improve reasoning. This is a healthy use of silence. My caveat is that partners should express their need for silence and time to process and reflect, which will help them engage in the conversation more productively. Some people are think-to-talk, and others are talk-to-think. You need to know what kind of person you are and what your needs are, and then be able to explain that to your partner.
