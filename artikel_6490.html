Stubborn Asteroids Around White Dwarfs Resist Becoming Dust

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

White dwarfs are the remnants of a stars like our Sun. For their sizeâthey're roughly the size of Earthâthey're extremely dense, and have about as much mass as the Sun. That means they have enormous gravitational power, enough power to tear apart objects that get too close.

For a long time, white dwarfs appeared to be alone in space, not including any stellar companions. Then observations showed that some of their surfaces were polluted with metals. Astronomers surmised that these metals were raining down onto their surfaces from rocky debris. Then came observations that some white dwarfs were surrounded by debris disks. After that, even more piercing observations found evidence of rocky bodies in the disks: small planets, moons, or asteroids.

An example of these is WD 1145+017, which seems to be orbited by several small rocky bodies, being torn apart and raining debris down onto the white dwarf's surface.

Scientists are curious about this process and how effectively white dwarfs can shred smaller bodies and form debris disks. As they find white dwarfs with disks, asteroids, and even planetary systems, explanations need to keep pace.

In new research in The Astrophysical Journal Letters, researchers examined how efficiently white dwarf tidal forces can break asteroids apart. The research is titled "Size Limits on Tidal Debris around White Dwarfs: The Kilometer-size Barrier." The lead author is Jordan K. Steckloff, senior scientist at the Planetary Science Institute.

"Compact disks of planetary debris orbiting white dwarfs provide a crucial window into our understanding of evolved planetary systems," the authors write. "The formation of these disks has been widely modeled with tidal fragmentation of minor planets that are rubble piles with no internal strength."

That's a problem because real rubble piles have some cohesive strength. Also, all three of the asteroids we've sampledâItokawa, Ryugu, and Bennuâare rubble piles. The force that holds these types of asteroids together is called the Van der Waals force. It's a weak, short-range force that attracts and repulses atoms and molecules. But as gecko feet show, it can be very effective.

In their work, Steckloff and his co-authors show what happens when this strength is included in modelling: "breakup of these rubble piles sets a maximum fragment size, and we calculate this size for water ice, iron, and material densities corresponding to the lunar highlands, Vesta, and the Earth," they write.

The authors say that as a consquence, models of disk formation around white dwarfs need to include another dust-generating factor in order to explain the dusty disks in observations. That likely means collisions rather than tidal forces.

The force that shapes the end-state of the disk is called PoyntingâRobertson drag. It explains how solar radiation affects dust grains around stars, including white dwarfs, making the dust spiral inward. This only works if the dust grains are small enough to be subjected to the drag but not so large that radiation pressure drives them away. This work shows that fragmentation itself isn't enough to create dust this small.

Observations in our Solar System agree with these results. When the comet Shoemaker-Levy 9 approached Jupiter in 1992, the gas giant's powerful tidal force pulled the comet apart. But not into dust; instead, the comet was torn into fragments 100m to 1,000m in diameter, which then plunged into the planet.

This Hubble Space Telescope image shows comet Shoemaker-Levy 9 broken into fragments after it approached Jupiter in 1992. Two years later the fragments approached Jupiter again and plunged into its atmosphere. Image Credit: NASA.

The research extends beyond asteroid fragment sizes in disks. It also shows that fragments maintain their orbits. They experience only tiny, fractional changes in their velocities "on the order of one part per million to one part per 100 million," the authors explain. "Such tiny changes in speed result in negligible changes to the orbits of the fragments, which remain in practically the same orbit as prior to disruption."

There are a lot of factors involved in the creation of a white dwarf's dust disk, and this work places an important constraint on one of them. "Understanding the progression from an intact minor planet to a sea of dust that is accreted onto a white dwarf is an outstanding challenge," the authors write. "In this Letter, we demonstrate a sharp constraint on the first step of this process." The work shows that some type of grinding process like collisions is required to generate white dwarf dust disks.
