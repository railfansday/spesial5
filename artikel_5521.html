As diabetes association’s turmoil continues, what does it mean for patients?

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

Five months after the American Diabetes Association fractured over a political protest and its extended fallout, one question remains unanswered.

What’s the bottom line for people living with diabetes?

Although any impact on patients may be hard to trace now, experts told STAT, they expressed concern that the impasse could stymie advancement in diabetes care. At the heart of the matter is the role the ADA and other professional organizations play in setting guidelines and sharing research, whether in their journals or at wide-ranging meetings that attract thousands to present and to learn.

The ADA dispute began after five members were ousted from its 2026 scientific meeting in New Orleans. The sparring continues, fueled by high-profile resignations and dismissals, a 1,221-member boycott, and the appointment of unidentified editors to manage journals. In October, an internal ADA review acknowledged some missteps but doubled down on its rebukes of members who objected to Trump administration policies decimating scientific research and proposing political reviews of individual projects.

A scheduled appearance by Jay Bhattacharya, director of the National Institutes of Health, inspired the five members to pass out copies of an editorial published in an ADA journal critical of diminished NIH funding. He did not appear, but armed security officers evicted those who shared the printouts outside the hall where he was expected to speak.

Bhattacharya was not the speaker originally suggested for the meeting, Mark Atkinson, who resigned as ADA’s 2026 scientific sessions planning committee chair, told STAT. His choice was to assemble a panel of academic experts to discuss artificial intelligence and its influence on diabetes care, but ADA leadership and its advocacy committee selected Bhattacharya instead.

These and other details regarding the dispute have accumulated, along with high-level resignations. But when asked if the continuing controversy matters to patients, the answer from four prominent voices is yes, but maybe not yet.

To illustrate his concern for the ADA’s future, Atkinson cited examples of how the ADA helped patients in the past.

“The ADA is one of many agencies that have advocated for lower insulin costs,” he told STAT. “They’re one of many agencies that have argued for increased federal funding for research. They’re advocates for standards of care.”

David Nathan, a noted researcher and founder of the Diabetes Center at Massachusetts General Hospital, is far more concerned about the broader impact of federal funding cuts on the scientific enterprise than he is about ADA tumult. Still, he’s also hearing from people who link that ongoing national crisis to what has happened at ADA.

They’re afraid of what’s going to happen to diabetes research, he said, starting with the NIH withdrawing support and then aggravated by the ADA discord.

“It’s really one blow on top of another,” Nathan told STAT. “All of this has coalesced to make everyone anxious, uncertain about the future of diabetes research. And the ADA should at this point be standing up and being something that we can all rally behind. Instead it is being fractured by its own actions.”

In a written response to STAT’s request for comment, the ADA press team said Tuesday, “The ADA has outlined the next steps that we plan to take to learn and improve based on the facts that came to bear from the review. This includes a review of event protocols, procedures, and code of conduct.”

In the near term, the 2027 ADA guidelines to be issued in December were already close to completion, physicians familiar with their production told STAT. The ADA confirmed their publication online in Diabetes Care next month. The agenda for the next annual scientific sessions, slated for June in Washington, D.C., is not yet set.

Meanwhile, the journals have been in limbo.

Since June, the ADA member boycott has erased the editorial masthead for Diabetes Care, its flagship journal. Editor-in-chief Steven Kahn, one of the five escorted out this past June, is part of the boycott, as are deputy editors. Chris Kohler, vice president of publications at ADA, resigned in protest when an interim editorial board was appointed without making their names public.

Kohler’s resignation letter said the appointment of unidentified interim editors violated editorial independence, scientific integrity, transparency, and accountability.

“I cannot, in good conscience, endorse or assume responsibility for an approach that I believe will compound the very problems it is intended to solve,” his Nov. 6 letter said.

Nathan, too, was severe about the unidentified editorial board. “To put it in strike terminology, these are scabs,” Nathan said. “I hate to tarnish them in that way, but they are working against this boycott. And the ADA has not announced the name of a single one of them. That’s weird.”

ADA told STAT the names would be made public.

“While the boycott continues, the ADA will remain relentless in our work to provide the resources that healthcare professionals are counting on. That is why we have established an interim editorial board for Diabetes Care that consists of highly experienced and respected healthcare professionals who have dedicated their careers to caring for people living with diabetes, including endocrinologists, primary care physicians focused on diabetes care, diabetes educators, and more,” the ADA press statement said. “The names of the interim editorial board will be placed on the Diabetes Care website soon.”

Elizabeth Selvin, a deputy editor at Diabetes Care who is also boycotting ADA, expressed concern that a weakened journals might dilute future guidelines.

“I worry that the CEO is treating the journal as a marketing tool for the organization rather than a scientific asset that should be protected and governed by standard publishing ethics,” she wrote to STAT about chief executive Charles Henderson. “A concern is that because typical governance structures to ensure editorial independence are not in place, we need to simply trust that ADA is not making decisions that will benefit industry more than patients.”

In its message to STAT, the ADA said the current editorial team’s term was set to end in December 2027. ADA has opened applications for a new editor-in-chief and said it will work with the selected candidate to confirm a transition plan and the appointment of deputy editors and associate editors.

In addition to the journals, large meetings are another forum for scientific exchange.

John Buse, an endocrinologist at the University of North Carolina and a former president of the ADA, is looking elsewhere.

“I am currently not submitting papers to any ADA journals, but there are many other outstanding journals to pick from,” he wrote to STAT. “It pains me, as of my 600 or so publications, no journal has published more of them than Diabetes Care historically.”

Journals are the way to communicate, Atkinson said.

“Without having medical journals, you don’t have a real way to impact how patients are treated in their care,” he said about the impasse. “This has just got to end soon because the losers in all of this are people with diabetes.”

The same holds true for scientific meetings. Buse plans to attend the Endocrine Society meeting in Toronto, which he has previously gone to only infrequently. He does regularly go to the European Association for the Study of Diabetes, which will meet this year in Budapest, calling it a strong meeting with much to offer. Another option is the American Association of Clinical Endocrinology meeting in San Diego.

“I do hope the issues are resolved soon and that I will attend the ADA, but the plan today is not to attend the scientific sessions in D.C. in June,” he wrote. “If the negotiations with ADA go nowhere and the boycott persists, there is great risk that the ADA loses its standing as the authoritative voice in diabetes. I am sure that the Endocrine Society, AACE, EASD, and others could carry the mantle in the future if the diaspora of thought leaders persists.”

Nathan said he and his colleagues are also thinking about where to present the results of their research, predicting scientists will find other venues. And so will participants and sponsors.

“Industry is interested in having a large audience at these annual meetings. If they start leaving, or if their support starts weakening, the ADA will suffer from it,” Nathan said. “As will people with diabetes. As will the scientific enterprise. I mean, this is just a colossal blunder on the part of the ADA.”

The ADA said there are plans to meet with some of the people leading the boycott to work towards a resolution.”

“They seem to be procrastinating, frankly, with us,” he said. “We have tried to get a meeting with them for easily more than four months. I think they’re hoping that this will just go away, and it’s not going away.”
