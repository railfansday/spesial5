Christie’s Notches a $145.9 M. Sale of 21st-Century Art, Led by a $32.9 M. Basquiat, amid Flagging Energy

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

By Brian Boucher, Harrison Jacobs November 19, 2026 10:49pm

Christie’s finished out New York’s fall auction week on Thursday evening at its Rockefeller Center salesroom, bringing in $145.9 million in a 56-lot sale of 21st-century art (after works by Jeff Koons and Ed Ruscha, estimated to sell for as much as $11 million between them, were withdrawn). Leading the sale was a $32.9 million Jean-Michel Basquiat painting that fell just shy of its low estimate.

The sale included 20 works from the collection of Antonio Homem, longtime director of the storied Sonnabend Gallery, which was founded by his adoptive mother, Ileana Sonnabend. Sold to benefit their foundation, the works totaled $41.9 million. Also on offer were 10 works from the estate of beloved New York philanthropist Agnes Gund, who died last year, totaling $25.7 million. After the withdrawals, the sale was estimated to bring between $126 million and $192 million; the hammer total on the night fell considerably shy at $117.7 million. Over the two-hour sale, the house found buyers for 51 lots, for a sell-through rate of 91 percent. Works by John Currin, William Eggleston, Wade Guyton, Gerhard Richter, and Yayoi Kusama failed to sell.

Many prominent advisors were in the room, including Erica Barrish, Amy Cappellazzo, Ralph DeLuca, and Gabriela Palmieri, as well as well-known dealers like Marianne Boesky, Jeanne Greenberg Rohatyn, and Christophe Van de Weghe. But there were a notable number of empty seats, and the atmosphere was unmistakably enervated after a long week of sales.

The sale started inauspiciously, with the first 19 lots, all from the Sonnabend collection, failing to hammer above their estimates. Bidding picked up only on the final lot of that trove, Jean Prouvé’s Table “Centrale” (“Trapèze”), petit modèle. The piece sold after five minutes of bidding between several specialists and a bidder in the room, before Sara Friedlander, Christie’s chairman of postwar and contemporary art in the Americas, nabbed it with a winning bid of $1.3 million. With fees, the total came to $1.7 million on a $700,000–$1 million estimate. But, all told, 10 of the 20 Sonnabend lots hammered below estimate. The collection hammered for $33.4 million, or $41.9 million with fees, against a pre-sale estimate of $35.25 million to $53.15 million (minus the withdrawn Koons, Wild Boy and Puppy). Not great any way you slice it.

The most notable of the Sonnabend works was Andy Warhol’s 1962 painting Campbell’s Soup Can (Turkey Noodle), tagged at $6–$8 million. The soup cans show one of Warhol’s most iconic images and are practically synonymous with Pop art. Adrien Meyer, global head of private sales and the auctioneer at Tuesday evening’s 20th-century sale, traded bids with a bidder in the room, who nailed it down with a $7.3 million bid, translating to $9 million all in.

Also from the trove was Roy Lichtenstein’s Large Spool (1963). Standing nearly six feet high, the work had been off the market since the Sonnabends bought it from Leo Castelli Gallery four years after it was painted. It has hung in museums all over the world, and its sale comes amid a major Lichtenstein retrospective at New York’s Whitney Museum of American Art. Auctioneer Yu-Ge Wang tossed out a handful of absentee bids from the rostrum before Friedlander got it for her client with a $7 million bid, for a total of $8.6 million with fees. The work’s pre-sale estimate was $10 million to $15 million.

Jeff Koons’s six-foot-high sculpture Hulk (Friends), 2004–12, also from the Sonnabend collection, shows the comic-book hero with small creatures perched on his shoulders and was estimated at $3.5–$5.5 million. It hammered after only a few bids at $3.3 million, or $4.15 million with fees, to the house’s Michael Baptist. (Koons was also a seller this week, bringing seven artworks to auction at Christie’s on Tuesday night, totaling $104 million.)

Bidding improved marginally from there, but for the most part, this was a sleepy sale. Even the biggest attraction of the night, Basquiat’s much-touted painting Untitled (Leonardo and His Five Grotesque Heads), which dates to 1983 and measures seven feet square, failed to generate much excitement. Wang opened the bidding at $23 million, and for a few long moments it seemed there would be no bids. Then Ana Maria Celis, head of postwar and contemporary art, rescued the painting, beating out Alex Rotter, Christie’s global president and global chairman of 20th- and 21st-century art. It hammered at $28 million and sold for $32.9 million with the house’s fees.

The hammer price was just shy of the $30–$50 million estimate. But the work, inspired by a sketch by Leonardo da Vinci, had been off the market for two decades, so the seller no doubt made a tidy profit. The painter’s auction market has famously exploded in the intervening years: every one of his paintings to sell for more than $10 million, all 83 of them, has done so since then.

The large canvas, featuring the artist’s trademark scratchy messages and the titular sketchy heads against an orange background, had appreciated enormously since its previous trips to the block. It appeared at Sotheby’s London in 2000, when it fetched just above $400,000, within estimate, and again at the same house five years later, when it tripled in value to bring an above-estimate $1.3 million.

Vija Celmins’s 1990 painting Untitled (Ocean) was one of the few lots of the evening to generate a genuine bidding war. The painting came from the Gund estate; Gund bought it the year after it was painted. It drew bids from Joanna Szymkowiak, head of private sales; Arco Yu, a Christie’s Hong Kong specialist; and a woman bidding in the room. For four minutes, the three traded incremental bids until the room bidder won out. Still, that brought the hammer to only $5.75 million, just under the $6 million low estimate. With fees, the total came to $7.1 million.

The 10 Gund works fared better than those from the Sonnabend collection, hammering for $20.4 million on a $16.95 million to $24.65 million pre-sale estimate. With fees, the total squeaked past the high estimate to reach $25.7 million.

There wasn’t much rhythm to the proceedings. A 1990 Gerhard Richter Abstraktes Bild painting from the estate of legendary art dealer Marian Goodman, who long represented the artist and bought the work from him that year, carried a $6 million to $8 million estimate. It failed to sell after garnering no bids. Three lots later, another Abstraktes Bild, from 1991, measuring three feet square and dominated by red, blue, and green, sparked a short contest between Friedlander and Rotter. Rotter eventually won it with a $7.6 million bid. With fees, the total came to $9.4 million. The painting’s pre-sale estimate was $7 million to $10 million.

“The estimates were too high for this market,” Greenberg Rohatyn told ARTnews on her way out of the salesroom partway through the night. “We need to readjust, and the houses need to be at the forefront of managing expectations.”

Boesky agreed that the estimates were too high. “And the energy was low,” she said on her way out. “The lots that were estimated too high were punished, and those estimated low were rewarded.”

On a brighter note, a few records were notched: Jim Nutt ($1.1 million), Antonio Santín ($486,400), and Julia Jo ($409,600).

The Jo, a swirling, gestural abstraction of fiery oranges and yellows breaking against cool blues and teals, was the night’s final lot, estimated at $30,000–$50,000. Sold to benefit the Coalition for the Homeless, the abstraction generated arguably the most spirited bidding of the evening. Wang opened bidding at $20,000, and it quickly climbed into the hundreds of thousands as a cacophony of specialists and a bidder in the room called out bids. It eventually went to the room bidder at a hammer price of $320,000, or $403,200 with fees. That set off a hearty round of applause, at least for one lot.

The sale finished out an auction week that started Monday with a two-part $378.7 million Sotheby’s auction including works from cable television magnate Ted Baum and interior designer Ruth Baum as well as a various-owner The Now and Contemporary sale. Christie’s staged a nearly three-hour 20th-century sale on Tuesday that totaled $626.6 million, led by a record-breaking $85.8 million abstraction by Wassily Kandinsky, closely followed by an $82.3 million landscape by Gustav Klimt. The same night, Phillips sold $104.4 million in modern and contemporary art on Tuesday, led by a $44.6 million Amedeo Modigliani sculpture. On Wednesday, Sotheby’s staged a $777.8 million Impressionist and modern art sale, led by a record-breaking $190.4 million Vincent van Gogh.
