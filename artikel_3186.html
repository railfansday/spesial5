Advisor Insights: Election Weather, a Fee War, and Inflation’s Return

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

Midterms, a Nasdaq correction, and a December rate decision: talking points for this week’s client calls.

Welcome back to Advisor Insights, brought to you by The Motley Fool! This week the market handed advisors a trifecta of client-call material: Election Day, an AI-driven selloff that pushed the Nasdaq into correction territory, and a Fed that held in October but left the door to a December hike wide open.Stocks rallied Wednesday after the midterms, then gave it all back and more on Thursday as chip stocks plunged on fears of tougher AI regulation and a slower data-center buildout. The S&P 500 fell nearly 4.4%, its worst day since April 2025 and about 6% below its October record close, while the Dow lost 3.4%. The VIX jumped to 26.7, and the 10-year Treasury slipped to about 4.84%.Inside: how to talk about midterms without letting the ballot box steer a portfolio and what a record-setting ETF launch year means for your due diligence. Also, two planning conversations worth having now: spotting early financial warning signs of cognitive decline, and rescuing small 401(k) balances left behind at old employers.

Voters went to the polls Tuesday, and Democrats are projected to take control of both the House and the Senate, according to AP projections. Regardless of political affiliation, expect the same client question: “Should we be doing something?” History’s answer is mostly “no,” and it comes with receipts.

The honest caveat: This isn’t a textbook setup. The 10-year Treasury is near 4.8%, September CPI ran 3.7%, the Fed could raise rates again on Dec. 9, and the Nasdaq Composite has fallen into correction territory on AI-spending worries. History describes; it doesn’t promise.

Client-ready line: “An election changes the weather. Your plan was built for the climate. Nothing about a vote count changes your goals, your time horizon, or your need for income, so nothing about the plan needs to change either.”

The annualized return of the stock market (as measured by the performance of U.S. large-cap stocks) from 1928 to 2025 was 10.02%, according to the data of NYU professor Aswath Damodaran. But the number of individual calendar years that the market returned between 9.0% and 11.0% is just three (1968, 1993, and 2004). In other words, don’t expect anything close to that fabled 10% historical average return in any given year.

Meta Platforms is eyeing at least $20 billion from a dollar-and-euro bond sale, its first-ever euro debt. That comes just a week after it unveiled roughly $25 billion in stock offerings. On the same Monday, Alphabet launched a seven-part, €6 billion ($6.7 billion) euro bond deal that drew more than €17 billion in orders. The catch is that lenders are demanding more. Alphabet paid richer yields than on an earlier 2026 deal, and Oracle is sweetening terms on a $10 billion sale. AI enthusiasm hasn’t vanished, but the price of funding it is climbing.

According to a study published in the Journal of the American Medical Association, Medicare beneficiaries living in single-person households who are diagnosed with Alzheimer disease and related dementias began missing bill payments up to six years before the diagnosis. They also had an increased risk of subprime credit scores 2.5 years prior to diagnosis. That time frame is confirmed by a study published by the National Bureau of Economic Research, which found that the financial fallout from dementia began six years before diagnosis.

What it means for your practice: The canary in the coal mine may be sitting in a client’s bill-pay history, years before anyone says the word “diagnosis.”

New research from Vanguard and PensionBee suggests that workers who leave small 401(k) balances behind when they change jobs have few good options (InvestmentNews, Nov. 2). Auto portability through the Portability Services Network, which moves a small balance into the new employer’s plan, cut cash-outs for balances under $1,000 from about 50% to about 9%, but it did much less for balances of $1,000 to $7,000 (39% to 31%). The network’s reach is also thin: It covers only about 3% of U.S. 401(k) plans, and just 6% of the roughly 220,000 participants rolled through it have been matched to a new plan, leaving the rest in interim safe harbor IRAs. Those accounts can be costly, as PensionBee found that 68% of automatic rollover IRAs were worth less after one year, and most of their assets sit in cash-like investments earning roughly 0.5% to 2%. Ultimately, this means workers may be cashing out and losing retirement savings, or leaving them parked in low-yielding accounts where fees can outpace earnings.

The 2027 Social Security cost-of-living adjustment

Announced Oct. 14 and the largest since 2023.

Why It Matters to Advisors: A COLA headline isn’t a net-income number. Part B premiums are deducted from most retirees’ checks, and over this decade they have averaged 5.3% annual increases against 3.1% for COLAs. With September CPI at 3.7%, retirees will feel prices faster than a COLA arrives, so it’s a good week to stress-test withdrawal rates and healthcare-cost assumptions in client plans.
