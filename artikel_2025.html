John Clark's granddaughter opens up on bid to immortalise Lisbon Lion with statue

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

"People knew the Lisbon Lion, but to me he was just grandad"’ says Clare Clark, articulating how it felt to be the apple of the eye of a Celtic legend.

“I still tear up every time I think or talk about him. It has been really hard since he left us last year. He was just such a big part of my life.”

John Clark passed away at the age of 84 in June last year. A surgeon in urology, his granddaughter still finds it hard to process the loss.

As a medical student at Aberdeen University, she would maintain the long-established practice of calling the old Bhoy every night at 10pm. Just to check in and get a rundown on his day.

“I actually called him Gan Gan. He was always just Gan Gan to me. So much so that when my mum or my gran talk about him even now they still refer to him as GG. It stuck.

“He was one of the driest and funniest men you could meet. He would always have a sly wee one liner that he delivered so softly it was easy to miss it altogether.”

The best story told about Clark – Luggy to friends and team-mates – dates back to the summer of 1981. Celtic were in New York to compete in the Trans-Atlantic Challenge Cup, a four team pre-season tournament also featuring the New York Cosmos, Seattle Sounders and Southampton.

In a hotel lift, winger Davie Provan was standing with the club’s assistant manager of the time when the doors opened and Pele, arguably the greatest footballer of all time, walked in.

Performing promotion duties for Escape To Victory, the Brazilian icon recognised a former opponent from a friendly international between Scotland and Brazil at Hampden prior to the 1966 World Cup.

“Scotland, Hampden, you number six – a very good player,” said Pele in warm tones. The unassuming Clark shook the great man’s hand in acknowledgement and smiled politely.

When the lift reached his floor, Pele walked away with a final wave. The doors closed and Clark turned to Provan – the winger’s jaw still pinned to the floor – and asked straight-faced, “Who the hell was that?”

A football anorak, he had an encyclopedic memory for dates, opponents and games. Wagging a finger in the air, he would recall the name of a guy he marked in 1964 as if it was yesterday. If he failed to recognise Pele, it was a first.

Confirming the veracity of the story, his granddaughter tells The Fixture: “My mum always told me that lift story was true. He used to laugh about it.” Most people did, to be fair.

For the best part of six decades, Clark served Celtic as a player, coach, assistant manager, long-standing kit man and club ambassador. Willie Maley might be the only man in Celtic’s history to give more years of his life to the club.

A central defender who formed a formidable partnership with Billy McNeill, he was just five foot eight and referred to almost everyone he met as “big man”. Yet, when he passed away, it felt like the Scottish game had lost a gentle giant.

A winner of four league titles, four League Cups and three Scottish Cups, his crowning glory was Lisbon 1967 when Celtic were crowned champions of Europe.

The 60th anniversary of that Inter Milan victory falls next May, with Jim Craig, Bobby Lennox and Willie Wallace the only Lions still alive to mark the occasion. Craig spoke in these pages of his own battle with Alzheimer’s this year and all three

Lions face health challenges likely to limit their involvement in the celebrations.

For the Clark family, last year’s loss will only add to the poignancy and, in a quest to preserve his memory, the John Clark Legacy Trust has been established to raise funds for a statue in his honour.

A special charity walk is scheduled for three stages on Saturday, November 21. The first stage will cover Chapelhall to Billy McNeill’s statue in Bellshill. The second will move to Jimmy Johnstone’s plinth in Viewpark. The final leg will see those taking part walk to Celtic Park.

“Grandad would have said, ‘I don’t want people to put themselves out’.

“He would say, ‘You don’t need to do that’.

“He would have thought there were more remarkable people who deserved it more than him…”

Celtic supporters would disagree with that sentiment. Signing for the club at the age of 17, Clark’s first appearance came at Arbroath’s Gayfield in 1959 and his first goal in a Scottish Cup replay at Hibernian two years later. Between April of 1965 and September ’67 he turned out in 140 consecutive matches with no injuries, no squad rotation. If he was fit, he played.

Where other members of Jock Stein’s greatest team were natural extroverts who revelled in wine, women and song, Clark was more considered; an unassuming man with a dry sense of humour who was uncomfortable in the spotlight.

“He was always a very hands-on grandad,” adds Clare. “He went under the radar. He wouldn’t do anything to be big or flashy.

“I remember the day he unfurled the league flag at Parkhead [in 2015] and he was nervous because he didn’t know how people would react to him doing it. He was humble like that.”

A Parkhead regular from the age of three, a matchday ticket would usually come with a £10 or £20 note attached without fuss or ceremony.

“It was always just the small gestures he preferred. We high fived on Christmas Day because we were never a touchy-feely family. But that was how he was, undemonstrative.

“He was quietly there in the background if you needed anything and he was one of the kindest and most patient people. So much so that I probably had him wrapped around my finger at times.”

Clark’s own father, John senior, was a railway engineer who died in an accident in Watford when his son was just 10.

Thrust into the role of bread-winner for his mother and siblings, a local farmer allowed him to sell eggs and potatoes to make ends meet.

“During the 50th anniversary celebrations for the Lisbon Lions, the BBC did a documentary and that was the first time we as a family had really heard my grandad talk about the loss of his father,” said Clare.

“It had probably affected him more than he wanted to say. But I don’t know if he would have been the man he was without going through those hardships.

“His siblings Michael, Maureen and Margaret are still here and they idolised him because he had to stand up and do things he didn’t want to do.

“Aunt Margaret has no memory of her dad. And granddad kind of filled that role for her. He always wanted to do the best for his family.”

Clark actually had two families in life. The first was his wife Eileen, children Martin and Marie and their three grandchildren. The other were the band of brothers famously assembled from within a 30-mile radius of Glasgow to become the first British winners of the European Cup.

There are times, now, when the current team’s performances and results in UEFA club competitions feel like an affront to the memory of the halcyon days. While Thursday’s win over Besiktas avoided a record run of five straight defeats in Europe, the 60th anniversary will serve as a stark reminder of how far standards have fallen.

“It will be hard next year for a number of reasons,” says Clare. “It probably will be the last big anniversary with any of the Lions there.

“They are getting older and there is a personal side to that. We are really close with the family of Bobby Lennox, one of the last living lions.

“When grandad was alive, he would speak to Bobby every day. During Covid they would be on the phone a couple of times a day, it was the thing that kept them going.

“You would hear grandad telling Bobby to make sure he got out and got his steps in round the garden or discussing a game on the TV.

“They were like brothers actually. And we know the McNeill family well, because we were all in Aberdeen together. And it’s sad there are not many Lions left now.

“This 60th anniversary will be very different from the 50th, that’s for sure.”

Approached by North Lanarkshire Council last year, the John Clark Legacy Trust is not the first established to raise money for a Lisbon Lions statue. It is the most recent.

“We want to get the statue in Chapelhall, the same way as Billy [McNeill] has one in Bellshill, Bobby [Lennox] has one in Saltcoats and Tommy [Gemmell] has one in Craigneuk,” said Clare.

The estimated cost of the memorial will be £120,000, with council grants and a series of fundraising events assisting the cause. Roughly 21 kilometres, the charity walk is expected to take five hours and any additional money raised will be put to use in the Chapelhall community.

“We want to carry on pursuing the issues that grandad valued and he really did value the people of Chapelhall,” added Clare.

“Everyone knew him even though he hadn’t lived there for 40 years.

“In this day and age, with the cost of living and putting children through football training every week, kids who are not going to be the next Celtic or Rangers stars still deserve the opportunity to play the game.

“Our plan is to work with local schools and teams to help fund their programmes to get as many young players as possible involved in the game. I think that’s what granddad would have wanted.”
