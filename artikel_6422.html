China restored its mangrove forests, and something remarkable happened

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

A 22-year study reveals how China's mangrove restoration efforts transformed coastal habitats and coincided with a dramatic rise in recorded bird diversity.

For decades, China’s coastal mangrove forests were cleared to make room for shrimp farms, ports, and development. By the 1990s, roughly two-thirds of these once-extensive forests had disappeared.

Then China launched an ambitious restoration effort, transforming large stretches of its coastline and creating new opportunities for wildlife to recover.

A study led by Ningbo University tracked what happened next. The researchers used 22 years of satellite imagery across five coastal provinces plus Hong Kong and Macao.

The team analyzed 13,308 Landsat images through Google Earth Engine, and bird survey records were pulled from the Global Biodiversity Information Facility.

The images revealed that the mangrove area grew 166 percent between 1998 and 2020, from around 10,600 hectares to more than 28,000.

Recorded bird species nearly tripled, from 148 to 397. The number of IUCN-threatened species found in these habitats almost doubled.

The sharpest growth came between 2003 and 2008 – 4,558 hectares in five years, the fastest expansion in the study period.

That expansion followed China’s 2000 Wetland Protection Action Plan, which helped direct attention and resources toward coastal habitat recovery. Later environmental protections reinforced those efforts.

Guangdong, Hong Kong, Macao, and Guangxi accounted for more than 88 percent of the national increase. Hainan added 1,678 hectares, while Zhejiang and Fujian – too far north to sustain them reliably – saw modest gains.

The latter occasionally even lost what had been planted, because cold snaps kill seedlings and typhoons don’t negotiate.

In 2008, Typhoon Nuri wiped out 80 percent of newly planted mangrove seedlings in Guangdong in a single pass. This is what restoration in dynamic coastal environments actually looks like up close.

The logic seems simple: plant trees and birds arrive. But the actual mechanism – which the Ningbo team reconstructed using partial least squares path modeling – is much weirder.

Mangrove area had the strongest direct effect on species richness. But the strongest direct driver of overall bird diversity wasn’t the forest itself – it was phytoplankton.

Expanding mangrove forests can influence water chemistry and nutrient flows in adjacent coastal waters.

The researchers found that mangrove expansion was associated with changes in coastal conditions that supported phytoplankton, an important foundation of marine food webs.

These changes may have benefited fish, shrimp, and the birds that feed on them.

Climate, meanwhile, had only indirect effects. It made conditions more suitable for mangrove growth as temperatures rose and rainfall increased.

Average vegetation cover across mangrove stands rose from 31 to 38 percent over the study period.

This suggests the forests were not only expanding but also becoming denser, providing more of the sheltered habitat that birds need for breeding and roosting.

But the researchers also identified an important uncertainty: how much of the apparent increase in bird diversity reflects ecological recovery, and how much comes from improved monitoring?

China’s birdwatching community has exploded over the past two decades, riding rising incomes and platforms like eBird.

Species that were always present but never recorded are now being logged. The jump from 148 to 397 species almost certainly mixes true ecological recovery with improved detection, and there’s no clean statistical way to separate them.

While this acknowledgment doesn’t invalidate the findings – the forest recovery is unambiguous in the satellite data – it should temper any instinct to take the bird numbers at face value.

There’s a second caveat worth noting: mangrove expansion sometimes converts intertidal mudflats, and mudflats are irreplaceable foraging habitat for migratory shorebirds.

Net gain in trees can mean net loss for specific species. Conservation math is rarely as clean as headline percentages suggest.

China’s experience demonstrates that sustained restoration efforts can help reverse decades of mangrove loss.

But it also shows that ecological recovery is complicated, shaped by storms, changing habitats, and the needs of different species.

Restoring coastal ecosystems requires more than planting trees. It requires understanding the entire web of life they support.

The study is published in the journal The Innovation.
