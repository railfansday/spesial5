The winning offer on a house isn’t always the highest, because sellers weigh how likely each one is to close, and how soon. Price yours from recent sales of similar homes, then compete with a fully underwritten preapproval, strong earnest money, and the seller’s preferred closing date. Keep the inspection and financing contingencies that protect your earnest money.

In a bidding war, the tempting move is to drop a contingency, since to a seller each one is a way for the buyer to walk away. But drop the inspection or financing contingency, and backing out because the home inspection found problems or your loan fell through can cost you your earnest money, the good-faith deposit that comes with your offer.

Keep both, and make yours the offer most likely to close instead. Set the price from recent comparable sales, what similar homes nearby sold for, then strengthen the rest of the offer:

Say a seller gets three offers. A buyer $5,000 under the top bid can still win by adding a 10-day inspection window, a 30-day close, and up to $10,000 of appraisal-gap coverage. She never gives up her inspection contingency.

Counteroffers commonly ask for a higher price or a different closing date, so decide before you offer how far you’ll move on each. On price, your limit is the value recent sales support plus the most cash you could put toward an appraisal gap, and that total is also your escalation cap. If winning would take more than that, or waiving a contingency, let the house go.
