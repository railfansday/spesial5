CBD Didn't Top Placebo for Behavioral Problems in Kids With Intellectual Disability

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

— Randomized controlled trial adds to evidence of lack of benefit

Cannabidiol (CBD) was no better than placebo for treating severe behavioral problems in kids with intellectual disabilities, a randomized clinical trial in Australia showed.

In an intention-to-treat analysis, patients randomized to CBD had similar behavioral improvement to those who received placebo as measured on the Aberrant Behavior Checklist, Irritability (ABC-I), which captures irritability, aggression, and self-injurious behavior as measured by parent reporting, reported Daryl Efron, MD, of The Royal Children's Hospital in Victoria, Australia, and colleagues.

Over 8 weeks of treatment, ABC-I scores fell from 32.2 to 21.7 in the CBD group and from 30.0 to 19.2 in the placebo group, resulting in a mean difference of 2.4 (95% CI −1.5 to 6.3), they reported in JAMA Pediatrics.

Moreover, rates of adverse events were similar between groups, with 63.1% of those in the CBD group and 60.6% of those in the placebo group experiencing at least one adverse event, the researchers reported.

And there was no advantage for CBD over placebo in any subgroup analysis, including among patients with a diagnosis of autism spectrum disorder (ASD), who accounted for 84% of trial participants.

"The results suggest that CBD should not be used to treat severe behavioral problems in this population," Efron and team wrote.

In an accompanying editorial, Adi Aran, MD, and Dalit Cayam-Rand, MD, both of Shaare Zedek Medical Center in Jerusalem, Israel, noted that the findings were similar to those of a North American multicenter trial conducted among youths ages 6 to 17 years with ASD.

"Together, these findings suggest that the lack of benefit of purified CBD is not confined to youths with intellectual disability but extends to youths with ASD across a broad range of cognitive abilities," they wrote, pointing to an additional two trials of youths with ASD that showed no behavioral benefit for CBD.

Overall, the trials demonstrate "the magnitude of the placebo response even among youths with intellectual disability," which "underscores the need for considerable caution when interpreting apparent benefits of novel treatments based on uncontrolled studies or clinical experience," they wrote.

Finally, Aran and Cayam-Rand noted that "this may not be the end of the story for medical cannabis for youths with neurodevelopmental disorders," as ongoing research is evaluating CBD combined with other parts of the cannabis plant such as terpenes and small amounts of tetrahydrocannabinol.

The parallel design, double-blind, placebo-controlled randomized trial by Efron and colleagues took place from May 11, 2021 to May 8, 2025 across three children's hospitals in Australia. Youths between the ages of 6 and 18 with an intellectual disability and severe behavioral problems were eligible. Intellectual disability was defined as an IQ of less than 70 within 2 years of enrollment and a deficit in adaptive functioning.

In all, 132 patients were randomized 1:1 to either placebo or to 10 mg/kg/d CBD isolate in oil, both taken orally for 8 weeks, and then a 4-week follow-up. Eight patients were either lost to follow up, randomized in error, or had incomplete answers, and 17 discontinued treatment, resulting in 61 patients in the CBD group and 63 in the placebo group.

Three-quarters of patients were male and the mean age was 11.2 years. Most patients were taking a concomitant psychotropic medication at baseline (87%).

Parent-reported scores on the ABC-I subscale assessed at 8 weeks was the primary outcome. Exploratory outcomes included the four remaining subscales of the ABC (social withdrawal, stereotypic behavior, hyperactivity or noncompliance, and inappropriate speech), as well as anxiety, participation in activities, sleep, parenting stress, and parent mental health.

Efron and colleagues noted the study was limited by its heterogenous study sample. Plus, the fixed-dose of 10 mg/kg/d CBD was based on a pediatric epilepsy study, but other research has used smaller doses. While authors believe this dose was adequate to identify a treatment effect, it's also possible that behavioral effects could occur at lower doses, they noted.
