YouTube says it won’t terminate Russell Brand’s channel after rape conviction

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

YouTube has come under fire in the past for how it handles content posted by people suspected of heinous crimes, but its policy has remained the same: It will only terminate a channel if someone is convicted in a court of law, and if their YouTube content is directly related to their crime.

Now, the platform has refused a request from the Prison Service of England and Wales to remove content from Russell Brand‘s channel.

The comedian and actor will be in prison for over a decade after being convicted of three counts of sexual assault and one count of rape, stemming attacks on four different women. Presiding British High Court judge Mr Justice Bennathan described Brand as a “sexual predator” whose behavior demonstrated a “series of lies” and “staggering arrogance.”

In the upload, Brand asserts innocence, telling viewers, “As you know, I am not guilty for the offences I have been jailed for, so something strange is happening.”

Authorities contacted YouTube via “an established mechanism the prison service has with tech companies to request deletion of material,” per the BBC, to ask that Brand’s video be removed.

But YouTube isn’t willing to scrub it–at least not yet.

“We are aware of the new content and our teams are investigating,” a spokesperson from YouTube parent Google told the BBC. “We suspended Russell Brand’s channel from the YouTube Partner Program in September 2023. The channel has not been able to monetize on YouTube since then.”

That’s a pretty typical order of operations for YouTube. In past cases with creators like Adnan Ahmed, Austin Jones, and Ian Rylett, YouTube suspended monetization on their channels when allegations surfaced and the legal process began, and then terminated their channels once convictions were filed.

The assaults Brand was convicted for all happened before 2009; he made his YouTube channel in 2007, but didn’t start uploading videos until 2012. YouTube could potentially claim they’re not related based on timeline alone, since Brand–who was already a celebrity outside of YouTube–didn’t use his channel or digital clout to connect with his victims.

So the question here is: Does YouTube amend its policies to ban convicted criminals–or specifically convicted sex offenders–from posting on its platform? What if their new content is directly related to their crimes, as Brand’s is?

United Kingdom Prime Minister Andy Burnham got involved after YouTube’s initial denial, telling the platform (and other socmed platforms) to “show some decency and take these videos down.”

“[It’s] time that we focus on the victims and think about them,” he added, instead of “hearing from a convicted criminal.”

“I would call on those social media companies to take these interviews down,” Burnham added. “Why are we having to hear from him? We shouldn’t be, and certainly [victims] shouldn’t be hearing from him.”

The attention on YouTube’s refusal has sparked a bit of a Streisand effect. Commenters are flocking to Brand’s video, sneering at Burnham, the U.K. government, and activists for their attempts to remove it.

“Had no intention to watch until the government told me not to,” one top-liked comment says.

“If the government tells you not to watch a video, you really need to watch that video to find out why,” another says.

Other socmed platforms have cut Brand off. Instagram, Facebook, and WhatsApp–all owned by Meta–have culled his accounts, thanks to policies that prohobit convicted sex offenders from owning and/or operating accounts.
