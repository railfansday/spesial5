3 Things to Do When Your Adult Child Pulls Away

Just last week, my parent coaching client looked at me quizzically while describing a recent text from her son. The text was just a photo of her son's dog wearing (or at least trying to wear) a cap of a major league baseball team. She said, "Dr. Jeff, it's been two months since I last heard from him, and of course, I immediately smiled when I saw the pic. But I had no idea what to do next." This client elaborated that her fingers led her to compose and immediately delete five responses prior to sending them. She added, "No matter what I do, he will pull away again, anyway."

The client above was struggling with how to respond after a long lag in communication with her adult child. She didn't know whether to joke about the cute picture, or tell her son she missed him, or ask further about Thanksgiving. For any parents in similar situations, it can be maddening to feel unsure about how to respond. And, if you have not spoken to your adult child for over six months or even years, the absence of that relationship has a huge, seemingly constant presence. By this I mean that often in these partial or full-on estrangement situations, the reality is that minimal or no contact does not take away from the internal emotional upheaval that comes from the desire to reconnect.

It is easy to feel helpless in the face of not seeing your adult child or not having the level of connection that you value. In my parent coaching work, I encourage parents to focus on three things they can do no matter how long it's been since they last heard their grown child's voice.

Finish this sentence: "What I miss most about my grown child today is__________." Some parents might reply that they wish they knew how their son was doing. Maybe, as in the early example of the dog photo, a parent misses lighter moments of laughter. Maybe a song reminds you of your adult child.

Identifying what you miss is tangible and healthy because it helps you process difficult feelings like sadness, disappointment, nostalgia, or loneliness. For example, that treasured memory of you and your daughter going for a ride and belting out a shared favorite song may weigh heavily on your mind. Consider sharing these thoughts with trusted relatives, friends, a therapist, or a parent coach. Sometimes it helps to have someone just say, "Tell me more."

Consider your adult daughter saying to you, "When I tell you what bothered me that you did when I was growing up, you kept twisting it to make it my fault and giving me advice." You may remember trying to help. But hold on, because, at the same time, maybe you can recall trying to give her advice when she said, "Dad, can I finish my point?"

Looking at your own behaviors in this way gives you an honest point of reflection to start. You could then say, "I've been reflecting on times I rushed in to give you advice, and it got in the way of better understanding what you were going through, and I'm sorry." This type of dialogue not only shows that you are taking responsibility, but you are also modeling self-awareness to your adult child.

In my book, You Ruined My Life—Now You Owe Me!, I explore how easily a parent's regret can turn into a feeling of indebtedness. In the parent coaching trenches, I have seen how tempting it is for parents to promise their adult children something to bring them closer. Please keep in mind that you can offer a sincere apology and still take your time to decide what you can reasonably do or give. For parents who are totally out of contact, I suggest you write down what you regret and keep it private.

This third point is really hard for parents missing their adult children. If several messages go unanswered, give your adult child room to reach out. If they asked for no contact, honor that request. Believe it or not, adult children in these situations often share with me that they still love and even miss their parents, even when they outwardly state they want no contact. Just remember what I wrote above: The absence of contact still has a great presence in both your mind and that of your adult child.
