Maintain your car battery—or bring a dead one back to life.

The 12-volt battery is one of the least complex components in modern vehicles but one of the most crucial. If it’s not in good condition, the engine won’t start, and none of the vehicle’s fancy systems will work. Car batteries have a limited lifecycle and deteriorate with time. This can happen for many reasons, including extended inactivity, extreme temperatures, or even something as simple as accidentally leaving the lights on after you turn off the engine.

That’s why it’s handy to have an external car battery charger so your engine is ready to go when you need it. A charger can also help extend your battery's life, for instance, in a vehicle you don't drive often—a weekend getaway camper or an old classic cruiser. Some chargers also have a jumpstart feature, eliminating the need for jumper cables (and the inconvenience of a required second vehicle).

You might be surprised to hear that electric vehicles use the same 12-volt car batteries as their internal combustion counterparts. Regardless, modern vehicles rely on this ancillary battery to power lights and onboard electronics when the ignition is off. If you store a vehicle for long periods, you’ll want a charger to keep the battery from fully dying and turning into a paperweight. (It’s also nice to know it will start without jumper cables.) But a battery charger can make a difference in plenty of other situations.

If you regularly experience below-freezing temperatures at night, a charger with a maintenance function can significantly extend your battery's life. If you rarely drive more than a few miles at a time, a charger can prevent your battery from discharging too far—leaving you with a no-start condition. Last but not least, older vehicles often struggle to recharge slightly depleted batteries on the move, so starting the day with a fully charged battery can also extend the life of your alternator and charging system.

Charging power is traditionally measured in amps. Think of amps as the amount of juice that can be put back into the battery at any given time; for example, a 10-amp charger puts twice as much charge into the battery as a 5-amp one. A quick stroll through a listing of battery chargers can be confounding, though. Some have 40 amps, and some have 0.75 amps, and yet they do the same thing. In reality, the difference is easy to understand. The more power a charger has, the more quickly it can bring a dead battery back to life. The tradeoff is cost, size, and weight.

If you’re charging a small motorcycle battery or maintaining a good-condition battery in a cherished collector car, then a 0.75-amp charger will do just fine. If you’re worried that your pickup won’t start in the winter, 2 to 10 amps is a better bet. Any capability above that is mostly for mechanics who need to quickly charge or recondition batteries in the shop.

Every charger here can “maintain” a charged battery, keeping it from slowly discharging between uses. Most chargers can also bring a depleted battery back up to full power. A few chargers offer specialized modes like “desulfation,” which helps a battery regain its chemical balance through carefully timed charge-and-discharge cycles. A “quick charge” can very rapidly (but safely) prepare a battery for use. A few battery chargers also offer a “jump start mode” that can start a car or motorcycle with a dead battery, but this is rare, and most of the time you’ll need a separate jump starter for that task.

Most chargers in this roundup are designed for conventional lead-acid (flooded cell) or Absorbent Glass Mat (AGM) batteries, which you’ll find in newer vehicles. If you have a vehicle with a lithium-ion battery, like some new BMWs, or a customized car with ultra-compact batteries from manufacturers like Braille, you’ll need a charger with that capability—different battery chemistries require specific charging protocols.

If you are dealing with a cramped garage, or travel between storage spaces throughout the seasons, consider a “wall wart” or direct-mount charger.
