From here, I borrow the language of my faith. I hope readers who do not share that faith will read it as an old thought experiment.

I have wondered since childhood: if Eden was perfect, why was the forbidden fruit there? Why plant a tree in the middle of the garden only to forbid eating from it? Without the tree, there would have been no failure.

Thinking about the super second brain led me to read this question differently.

Imagine Eden without the tree. Adam cannot leave God there, because there is no way to leave. We do not call it love when a being who cannot leave stays beside someone. I read that tree as a door left open so that staying can be a choice.

English key to the original diagram. A tree stands in the center; a door has been left open. The open way out makes remaining in the garden a choice.

Figure 16. Because the tree is there, staying becomes a choice.

In the language of Chapter 12, the forbidden fruit is friction. Only where there is friction does love become a choice.

The ‘complete observation’ of Chapter 8 looks different here, too. Adam in Eden lived before a God who knew all of him. From the beginning, there was no switch for turning off being known. What remained open to him was one door through which he could leave. Here I receive the switch I set aside in Chapter 8 in another form. Even if I cannot choose whether to be known, I can choose where to stay while fully known.

I am not using this table to make a theological claim. I want to show how I organize my thoughts by moving between the languages of these two worlds.

I would like you to notice the place of atonement. In Chapter 6, I said that self-esteem is needed to withstand accurate criticism from a second brain. The firmest ground for self-esteem I know comes from outside achievement. In a world where AI remembers more and judges better than I do, self-esteem built on achievement cannot hold.

In the prologue, I pushed fidelity to 100 percent. Now I need to revise that number.

100 percent is not somewhere I can arrive and stay, because I keep changing. The birth of a child brings fears that did not exist yesterday. Greater authority brings unfamiliar temptations. Loving someone new opens rooms inside me I did not know were there. Great pain rearranges the order of my values.

New relationships, new desires, new power, new pain. As long as I live in the world with a body, these keep arriving, and each arrival makes me a slightly different person. The self-model that was at 100 percent yesterday is at 98 percent this morning.

So the SSOT is never finished. And the material with which to rewrite it—the new me, not yet recorded anywhere—arises only at the standpoint of lived presence. Only when I, with a body, undergo something is there something new to write.

Eden is not a world where choice has disappeared. It is a world where, despite being able to choose otherwise, one chooses love.
