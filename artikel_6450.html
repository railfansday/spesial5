Where is freshwater wildlife under the most pressure? A new map offers surprises

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

A global study maps 45 threats to 21,490 freshwater species and reveals where conservation plans based on land wildlife can miss areas of high risk.

Rivers, lakes and wetlands are home to nearly a third of all known vertebrate species.

A new global study maps where 45 human pressures, from crop farming to dam construction, are most likely to affect freshwater wildlife.

A team led from McGill University analyzed 21,490 species and found that freshwater threat hotspots often differ from those on land.

For the threats compared, freshwater mammals generally faced greater pressure than land mammals, with a smaller gap for reptiles.

Christophe Brabant, a doctoral student in biology at McGill University, led the work with biology professor Lars Lønsmann Iversen, a member of McGill’s Bieler School of Environment.

The researchers drew on expert assessments to identify the threats facing each species.

They combined those assessments with species’ known ranges to map where the threats are most likely to affect them.

The researchers identified three threats that were especially widespread.

Across river sub-basins worldwide, they estimated that each affected roughly one in four freshwater species present, on average. Farmland growing crops came first.

Dams and other water management came second, and runoff from farms and forestry third.

Europe and northwestern Africa’s Maghreb region carry the heaviest combined load of all 45 threats. In Europe, the dominant problem is pollution from cities, farms and industry.

In the Maghreb it is pollution plus dams and heavy water use. Other hot spots include the Nile, the Niger Delta and the Rift Valley lakes of East Africa.

The lightest loads are where few people live, in Siberia and northern Canada, and in the Sahara, Arabian and Australian deserts.

Global averages hide big differences between animals. Fish are mainly hit by fishing across 37% of their range and by dams across another 30.5%.

Farming was the leading threat across 45.8% of amphibians’ combined range and 31.45% of the combined range of dragonflies and damselflies.

The authors linked this pattern to the animals’ reliance on both land and water at different stages of life.

Freshwater reptiles and mammals face a different enemy. Hunting and other direct use are the main threat across 37.8% of reptile ranges and 43.4% of mammal ranges.

That covers the wildlife trade, traditional medicine, animals caught in fishing gear and animals killed on purpose.

In an interview with Earth.com, Brabant noted that dams, farmland, and farm pollution are widespread, but their weight changes from region to region and group to group.

“This means there is no one-size-fits-all approach to freshwater conservation,” he said.

The raw material was the Red List kept by the International Union for Conservation of Nature, which records for each assessed species the threats experts have seen affecting it.

The team took five groups that have been assessed in full: amphibians, fish, mammals, reptiles, and the dragonflies and damselflies.

For each species, they combined its range map with its threat list.

“By combining this data, we could calculate, for any sub-watershed, the probability that a species occurring there is affected by a particular threat,” Brabant said.

One map per threat per group made 225 maps, which the team stacked into a single index of combined pressure.

Species with small ranges were given more weight.

A threat recorded for a species found in one lake can be placed on the map with confidence, while one recorded for a species spread across a continent can’t.

To test whether the maps mean anything for survival, the team compared them with two separate records.

In the 5% of river basins where extinction risk is modeled to be highest, 71% of the 45 threats were more likely to affect a species than their global average.

The second record was the Living Planet Index, which tracks counts of individual animal populations over time.

Across 2,568 freshwater vertebrate populations from 600 species, populations were shrinking faster in basins with a heavier combined threat load.

Neither record was used to build the maps, so the match is not circular.

The authors wrote that it suggests the maps capture where threats are already pushing freshwater species toward decline.

The study also lined up the freshwater maps against earlier ones made the same way for land vertebrates.

For all seven threats that had been mapped on land, freshwater mammals were more likely to be affected than land mammals.

Freshwater reptiles showed the same pattern with a smaller gap, which the authors put down to the time reptiles still spend on land.

The hot spots were in different places, too. Where freshwater fish and frogs are under the most pressure is not where land vertebrates are.

“What surprised me most was how different the geography was,” Brabant told Earth.com. Conservation planning has often relied on information about land species, he said.

That is why freshwater species and their threats need to be written into plans explicitly if the plans are going to reach them.

The Red List treats each threat as if it acted alone, and the authors wrote that many of them combine in ways that could make the damage worse.

The maps estimate how likely species are to be affected in each area. They don’t measure how much harm each threat causes.

Range maps describe where species live today, so places a threat has already emptied may not show up.

Asked which places he would look at first, Brabant said threat alone isn’t enough. Europe and northwestern Africa lead on accumulated threats, while many tropical regions stand out for the number of species affected.

“Areas of high richness and high threat suggest that threat mitigation is most urgent, whereas areas of high richness and low threats would be most suitable for proactive protection,” he said.

What would help most right now, he said, is reducing the pressures: cleaner water, protected habitat and rivers reconnected. But the most useful action depends on the place and the species.

The next step is matching each basin’s threats to the conservation actions that address them, which the maps alone can’t do yet.

The full study was published in the journal Science.
