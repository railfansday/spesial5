Why Can’t I Stop Over-Drinking on My Own?

“Why can’t I get my drinking under control? I know I’m drinking too much. I’ve promised myself countless times that I’ll cut back or stop altogether, but sooner or later I end up doing the same thing again.”

I’ve heard some version of this question thousands of times during my 50-plus years of clinical practice as an addiction psychologist. Many are high-functioning individuals capable of exercising considerable discipline and self-control in other areas of their lives. Yet when it comes to drinking, they repeatedly fail to follow through on their intentions.

Why does this happen? And why do some people find it so difficult to change their drinking habits without professional help and/or medication?

Consider someone who wakes up after a night of heavy drinking feeling miserable, embarrassed, and absolutely determined never to repeat the experience. Several days later, however, after a stressful workday or during a social gathering, having a few drinks once again seems innocuous. Yesterday’s determination is displaced by other motivations, some operating outside conscious awareness.

Consider the case of a successful businessman who sought my help several months ago after a series of alcohol-related incidents. Two years earlier, he had lost a highly coveted executive position after behaving inappropriately while intoxicated at a company function. Devastated by the experience, he stopped drinking and vowed never again to jeopardize his career. Regrettably, several months later, having secured another desirable position, he attended a business dinner followed by several hours of heavy drinking with co-workers at an after-hours club. Despite his earlier resolve and the painful consequences of his previous drinking, he again became severely intoxicated and behaved in ways that once more put his professional standing at risk.

How could someone who had already paid such a steep price repeat essentially the same behavior? This is precisely why I titled my first book on addiction treatment, published in 1989, Willpower’s Not Enough. Overcoming a deeply ingrained addictive behavior usually requires more than determination, good intentions, and repeated promises to oneself.

Repeated exposure to alcohol and other psychoactive drugs can gradually change the way the brain functions. These changes involve neural mechanisms responsible for reward, motivation, stress regulation, learning, and self-control. With repeated heavy drinking, the brain can develop a neurochemical relationship with alcohol that makes continued drinking increasingly compelling, even when the person desperately wants to stop.

Drinking can begin to operate on something resembling “autopilot,” no longer responding reliably to conscious intentions or rational consideration of its consequences. What initially served a pleasurable or useful purpose becomes a self-perpetuating pattern that continues despite destructive consequences. The behavior has become maladaptive, even self-sabotaging.

Over time, drinking becomes associated with particular situations, emotions, and expectations. Arriving home after work, walking into a favorite restaurant, or feeling anxious or upset may automatically trigger the thought that drinking would make everything feel better. These associations become stronger with repetition. Eventually, the urge to drink may arise before the person has consciously considered whether drinking is a good idea and what the potential consequences could be.

Another complication arises from the fact that alcohol itself impairs judgment and volitional control. Someone who fully intends to have only two drinks may find that, after consuming them, a third or fourth seems perfectly reasonable. The very substance they are trying to control interferes with their ability to control it. A person’s intention and determination not to overdrink can quickly dissolve in alcohol.

For many people, drinking serves an important “self-medication” function. It provides temporary relief from anxiety, depression, loneliness, anger, and other uncomfortable emotional states. It may also attenuate intrusive thoughts, persistent worry, or relentless self-criticism.

Alcohol’s ability to relieve or anesthetize psychological discomfort can make it especially difficult to give up. Drinking is no longer simply about enjoying a pleasant experience. It becomes a way of escaping an unpleasant one. This can multiply alcohol’s reinforcing value.

For someone who depends on alcohol to manage emotional discomfort, giving it up may feel like losing an important coping mechanism, even when drinking has become a serious problem. The irony is that excessive drinking often worsens the very symptoms it temporarily relieves. The result can be a vicious cycle in which drinking provides short-term relief while contributing to the conditions that make further drinking more likely.

Repeated unsuccessful attempts to control drinking can gradually erode a person’s confidence and motivation. Each episode of excessive drinking re-activates feelings of regret, self-criticism, and promises to do better next time. Eventually, some people begin to believe they are incapable of changing. I’ve seen this happen many times, particularly among high-functioning executives and professionals accustomed to solving problems through hard work, discipline, and persistence. They find it difficult to understand why those same qualities haven’t enabled them to overcome their drinking problem.

Treatment offers more than encouragement to exercise greater self-control. It helps identify the psychological, behavioral, and biological factors that keep excessive drinking going. This may involve recognizing situations that trigger drinking, interrupting automatic responses, developing alternative ways of managing emotional distress, and acquiring practical strategies for dealing with urges and setbacks.

For some, medication can make a substantial difference. FDA-approved medications such as naltrexone and disulfiram can help reduce drinking or support abstinence, depending on the medication and treatment goals. GLP-1 medications, such as semaglutide (Ozempic or Wegovy) and tirzepetide (Mounjaro or Zepbound), appear to be especially promising based on emerging research and clinical experience, although these drugs are not currently FDA-approved for treating alcohol use disorder.

Not everyone who drinks excessively needs professional treatment or medication. Some people successfully change on their own. Others benefit from brief guidance, while some require more sustained treatment. Nor does seeking treatment necessarily mean committing to lifelong abstinence. For some people, abstinence is clearly the safest and most realistic goal. For others, a harm reduction approach that supports learning to consistently moderate drinking may be possible.

When someone asks me, “Why can’t I overcome this problem on my own?” I often suggest a different question: What is it about my drinking that I haven’t yet understood or learned how to change? The issue isn’t necessarily a lack of motivation, intelligence, or strength of character. Years of repeated drinking may have established psychological habits and neurobiological changes that are difficult to reverse through determination alone.

Willpower certainly matters. But when addictive behavior has acquired a momentum of its own, willpower alone may not be enough to change its course.
