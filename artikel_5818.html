Elizabeth Holmes Documentary: Doctors Weigh In

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

— MedPage Today reporters offer further insights into recently covered topics

The following is a transcript of the podcast episode:

Rachael Robertson: Hey everybody! Welcome to MedPod Today, the podcast series where MedPage Today reporters share deeper insight into the week's biggest healthcare stories. I'm your host, Rachael Robertson.

To kick things off, our perspectives editor Jenny Friedman will be talking about some recent op-eds about Nathan Fielder's documentary "You Can See Everything," which is about the disgraced Theranos CEO Elizabeth Holmes. Some medical organizations have really similar names but very different politics. Cheryl Clark will walk us through some of these groups that would be easy to confuse for each other. To close out, Kristina Fiore will share her reporting on a fluke that occurred during an internal medicine exam that has many test-takers frustrated.

Audiences have been flocking to theaters to see "You Can See Everything," which is Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim's new documentary about Elizabeth Holmes.

To refresh your memory, Holmes is the disgraced former CEO of blood testing company Theranos. She was convicted of defrauding investors and sentenced to 11 years in prison. The documentary places Fielder in the home of Holmes and her partner Billy Evans just 34 days before she is set to start her prison sentence. The film explores the question: Who is Elizabeth Holmes really?

Let me just share a few seconds of the trailer to set the scene.

Nathan Fielder: You really feel you did nothing wrong.

Fielder: So where did things get lost? Like how are you where you are right now?

Robertson: Perspectives editor Jenny Friedman has published a couple op-eds on the documentary, and she's here to discuss them.

So, Jenny, I understand we published two op-eds from doctors sharing their takeaways from the documentary. Are they taking a renewed look at the consequences of the Theranos scandal and Holmes' conviction?

Jenny Friedman: Not exactly. The intent of the documentary isn't to renegotiate Holmes's guilt; a jury and judge have already done that. The film also isn't trying to re-examine the consequences for investors or patients. Instead, the purpose is to paint a portrait of Holmes's psychology and let viewers decide for themselves what's real and what's fake. That fits really well into filmmaker Nathan Fielder's standard M.O. of blurring the lines between reality and fiction.

So, in one of our op-eds, a forensic psychiatrist, Susan Hatters Friedman, MD, draws from her clinical experience of working with women convicted of fraud in order to analyze the version of Holmes who we see on screen, and she offers insight into certain traits and behaviors that Holmes portrays. But she reminds readers and viewers, to keep in mind the collateral evidence of what we already know about Theranos and the conviction of Holmes. And if you recall, the evidence is overwhelming.

Robertson: Right. I mean, that's fascinating and really impressive that she was able to do that while also abiding by the Goldwater Rule. What's the second op-ed about?

Friedman: The other op-ed, written by psychiatry resident Chloe Lee, MD, MPH, looks at the relationship dynamics portrayed on screen between Holmes and her partner Billy Evans. And some viewers might leave the movie thinking of Holmes as the victim because on screen she plays a doting mother, while Evans exhibits controlling behaviors. But is that really her, or is she capable of manipulating a partner's abusive tendencies to her advantage? Those are the primary questions that psychiatry resident Chloe Lee is trying to address in her op-ed. And while Dr. Lee, like the rest of us moviegoers, can only see what's displayed on screen, she highlights that no version of Holmes's reality looks like a happy ending.

Robertson: Well, I know you and I had a lot of thoughts about this movie that we've already discussed, and I'm sure a lot of our viewers would also be interested to see what everybody's talking about. Thanks, Jenny.

Robertson: Over the last few years, several medical organizations have appeared with names that sound a whole lot like other more established physician and research groups. A MedPage Today analysis found that while two groups may have similar names, their views on science can be really different. Cheryl Clark is here to tell us more about this.

Cheryl, can you give us some examples of these newer associations with similar-sounding names to older ones.

Cheryl Clark: Yeah, right, Rachael. You know, we listed seven instances where the names of these new groups sound an awful lot like well-established ones. This is really important because for many of these new entities, when you scroll down and look at their links, you find that they have causes or goals that are diametrically opposed to or fighting against causes that organizations whose names they resemble support.

The biggest, or perhaps most recognizable soundalike example, is the nonprofit Children's Health Defense. That's the one that, last I checked, carries Robert F. Kennedy Jr.'s picture at the top of his website, because under his chairmanship in 2018, the name was changed from World Mercury Project to Children's Health Defense. Now, this group has worked to restrict access to childhood vaccines, and it has suggested that vaccines and some environmental contaminants are the underlying cause for autism, despite evidence to the contrary.

But Children's Health Defense sounds a lot like Children's Defense Fund, another nonprofit. Children's Defense Fund is a 53-year-old, well-established organization focused on reducing child hunger and gun violence, and improving health and nutrition, education, and school safety.

Now, both of these organizations want philanthropic donations, but it is interesting that on its website, the Children's Health Defense, that's Kennedy's group, is aware of the potential for confusion. That they want to make sure financial gifts are not inadvertently rerouted. The website says: "Please note, we are often confused with Children's Health Fund or Children's Defense Fund, so be careful not to inadvertently select the wrong recipient."

And by the way, Children's Defense Fund, the long-established group, did tell me that some have confused the two organizations, but they are quick to set the record straight.

Robertson: What are some of the other sound alike groups that have different goals and purposes?

Clark: Right. Well, another interesting one is the American College of Family Medicine (ACFM). Now, it sprouted up in 2023. Sounds official enough, but the ACFM opposes mandatory abortion training and maintains that "abortion is never medically necessary and should not be offered or performed by any health professional." The ACFM sounds an awful lot like the 79-year-old, well-established American Academy of Family Physicians (AAFP), whose membership rolls include 124,000 family doctors. The AAFP has fought back against many Trump administration health policies and activities.

And then there is AAPLOG, or the American Association of Pro-Life Obstetricians and Gynecologists, which in 2013 broke away from the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, which has advocated for better maternal health since 1951. AAPLOG has suggested that abortion is linked to depression and substance abuse, and supports the "physician's right of conscience in medical decision making" regarding abortion.

Robertson: There's an obvious question about whether these new organizations purposefully tried to use names that mimic older ones to attract donations. What do you think about that?

Clark: Well, Rachael, I just don't want to touch that one. Although it is an obvious question, groups want to sound official, of course, but whether they were hoping to garner credibility or financial support that way, I have no idea.

Robertson: Well, thank you so much, Cheryl.

Robertson: Some physicians who took the internal medicine board exam on August 13 said that they experienced technical difficulties, and now hundreds of people have been issued indeterminate results on those tests. Kristina Fiore is here to tell us more about this situation.

Kristina, what kind of technical difficulties did they run into?

Kristina Fiore: Yeah, so last month I had a handful of test-takers reach out to me after they received their board exam scores. They all took the test on August 13, and they all described issues when test center staff tried to log them back in after their breaks.

Basically, there are four sections to the test, and you get a break after each one. But sources said that it took an additional 20 to 30 minutes after returning from the breaks to get back into their tests. They all said that they received an email from the American Board of Internal Medicine (ABIM) on the evening of August 13th after taking the test, acknowledging the problem. Now, ABIM said that the program that its testing partner Pearson uses to check people back into the test center after a break malfunctioned, but that email was all that they heard for about two months until the results came back in October.

Robertson: What happened with those results?

Fiore: ABIM told me that 371 test takers received an "indeterminate" result. These are people who did not receive a passing score. An ABIM senior vice president explained that they did this because they're concerned about the... not that they're concerned about the accuracy of their scoring, but because they "cannot know how experiencing a delay logging in to the exam might have affected individual candidates and their performance."

But test-takers I spoke with take issue with that. One of them said they scored unusually low across all subject areas. They questioned whether there could be a frame shift error. This is someone who showed me that they were performing well above average on all of their practice tests. Now, another test-taker said that during her tests, she couldn't select some of the answers and had to click a few times for it to take. So overall, they're concerned that something else went wrong.

Robertson: What has ABIM said to these test takers?

Fiore: When it issued the indeterminate results, it refunded them their $250 exam fee and said that they have the opportunity to retest in the next testing cycle. The problem is that the cycle doesn't start until next August, which means these busy doctors will have to prepare for this test all over again next year, and a lot of them say that that's time they don't really have. ABIM told me that the pass rates on all of the testing days this year were similar and on par with other years, and that they're confident that the scores are accurate. So they are not budging on offering a retake before the next testing cycle.

Robertson: I mean that's a tough situation. Thanks for your reporting here, Kristina.

Robertson: There were more than 500 cases of measles in the past 2 weeks. That's more cases than the U.S. used to see in any given year, excluding this year and last. Pennsylvania is still struggling with 166 cases in the last 2 weeks. The Midwest is also seeing cases rise in states like Wisconsin, Minnesota, Indiana, and Ohio.
