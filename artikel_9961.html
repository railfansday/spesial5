The license requires: > The above copyright notice and this permission notice shall be included in all copies or substantial portions of the Software. The GPLv2 is quite a bit wordier, but it's basically the same idea: > [...] provided that you conspicuously and appropriately publish on each copy an appropriate copyright notice and disclaimer of warranty; keep intact all the notices that refer to this License and to the absence of any warranty [...] Neither license prevents rebranding (in my opinion it's a real weakness of free software licenses). Both require minimal attribution in the form of a copyright notice, but not more. Neither prevents aggressive monetization. I guess the GPL makes it easier to prove that some downstream is in fact based on the project, if they comply with the license and publish source code. But what are you going to do once you prove that? Issue a takedown? For what license violation? When it comes to LLM rewrites, perhaps the GPL is a bit clearer about applying to "a work based on the Program" even if it does not contain (as MIT puts it) "substantial portions of the Software". But I'm skeptical that there's a real legal difference. The main difference the GPL makes, of course, is that the Asahi project could theoretically incorporate changes from downstream back into the project. That is the GPL's reason for existing. But in this case, the Asahi project describes the downstream code as "lazily rewritten", as using "LLM assistance" (which the Asahi project bans), and as being prematurely-released copies of their "in-development" code. It really doesn't sound like they have any interest in using it. So I'm somewhat mystified what the GPL is supposed to accomplish. That said, I have no firsthand knowledge of the downstreams they're talking about.
