One day of fatty food was enough to trigger a surprising response in the mouse brain

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

A high-fat diet changed brain cells involved in eating and weight control within a single day in male mice, revealing an unexpected early response.

Weeks of fatty food are known to blunt a group of brain cells in mice that hold back eating. That reduced activity may contribute to further overeating and weight gain.

Earlier studies measured the cells after at least two weeks on a high-fat diet, leaving their initial response unclear.

Yi Lu and Mark Rossi at Rutgers University examined that initial response and found a different pattern.

After a single day of high-fat food, the cells in male mice fired more easily than before, which the authors read as the brain trying to limit weight gain.

In female mice, the same cells already fired easily on a normal diet, and the fatty food didn’t change that.

The cells are in the lateral hypothalamus, a region near the base of the brain that helps control eating and has been studied as a feeding center since the 1950s.

Rossi is an assistant professor at Robert Wood Johnson Medical School and a resident scientist at the Child Health Institute of New Jersey.

He has spent years on one group of those cells, the ones that release the signaling chemical glutamate. Switching those cells on makes a mouse eat less, so the team calls them a brake.

Lu recorded from single cells in thin slices of mouse brain, using a glass pipette fine enough to seal onto one neuron and read its electrical activity.

Pushing a small current into each cell showed how readily it fired.

On a normal diet, the brake cells in females fired more times for the same push than the cells in males did. Their electrical spikes were also narrower.

Female cells also received inhibitory signals from neighboring cells more often, so they were both easier to fire and more often held back.

The baseline recordings came from 40 cells in three male mice and 51 cells in three female mice.

The team also recorded cells from other mice after one, two, or seven days on a high-fat diet.

Fat supplied 60% of the calories in the experimental diet, compared with 21% in the mice’s usual chow.

In males, one day was enough. The brake cells fired more for the same current, and they needed less current to fire at all. The change was there on day one, day two, and day seven.

In females, the firing rate was unchanged at the one-, two-, and seven-day measurements.

The team wrote that the male result surprised them, since the two-week studies had pointed the other way.

The signals arriving at the cells shifted too, but not in the same direction. In males, the excitatory inputs that drive these cells grew less frequent.

In females, it was the inhibitory inputs that dropped, and only briefly: by day seven they were back to normal.

The input recordings came from just two mice per group, which the authors list as a limit on what they can say about them.

A cell that fires more easily on fatty food could be part of the problem or part of the body’s defense.

To tell which, the team gave the brake cells a bacterial sodium channel called NaChBac, which keeps them in a more excitable state. Then they weighed the mice every day for a week.

Male mice with the extra excitability gained less weight than control males, on normal chow and on the fatty diet alike.

Female mice on chow were unaffected, which the authors think fits with their cells already being excitable. Female mice on the fatty diet lost weight.

The treated mice weren’t eating less. Over six hours with the fatty food in front of them, treated mice of both sexes ate more of it.

Their activity in an open arena didn’t change either.

The authors wrote that the weight difference most likely comes from the mice burning more energy, though they didn’t measure that directly.

Female mice tend to put on weight more slowly than males on a high-fat diet, and the authors see the brake cells as one reason why.

In males, the quick response may hold off some weight gain before the longer-term blunting sets in.

Raising the cells’ excitability, the authors wrote, may limit weight gain without reducing food intake.

Everything here happened in mice, and in a region of the brain where many kinds of cells are mixed together.

The recordings couldn’t separate the subtypes of brake cells, so the team can’t say which ones changed.

Nor can they say whether the shift in incoming signals came from the cells sending them or from the connections themselves.

Why the female cells start out more excitable is also open. Males and females often respond differently to fasting and overfeeding, and removing the ovaries from female mice makes them gain weight the way males on fatty food do.

The authors expect estrogen to be involved, but this study tested no hormone.

The biggest open question is how a brake that fires more easily on day one ends up blunted by week two.

The team suspects the cells recalibrate under sustained pressure, the way neurons across the brain adjust to a lasting change in their input.

No one has yet recorded the same cells across that whole stretch. The authors wrote that a study following them from the first day to the second week is what it would take.

The full study was published in the journal The Journal of Neuroscience.
