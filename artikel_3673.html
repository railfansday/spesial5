Meet Colleen Allen, the Future of American Fashion

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

This is the November 4, 2026 edition of Fit Check, your front row seat to the power players, peacocks, and deep pockets of fashion—and how they influence the rest of the world.

The United States has been overtaken by a blue wave following last night’s midterm elections. James Talarico flipped Texas to make it to the Senate, Chris Pappas of New Hampshire became the first out gay man to be elected to the Senate, and Keisha Lance Bottoms of Georgia is now the first Black woman to be elected governor in the history of the US. There were great memes about what this could mean for the country—largely, the comeback of woke—and some fashion moments worth discussing. Scroll down for a midterm-fashion verdict, including notes on the curious case of a seemingly AI-generated picture featuring Chanel and Valentino products supposedly spotted at a Michigan Democratic Party election night event in support of Abdul El-Sayed.

In other news, Ye is staging a comeback Yeezy show in Los Angeles tonight at the brand’s flagship—keep an eye out tomorrow for a report from my Angeleno colleague Maxwell Adler. Congratulations are in order for Schiaparelli’s Daniel Roseberry, who is having a great week after running the New York City Marathon on Sunday, posting a much-discussed thirst trap on Monday morning, and receiving the Leader in the Arts Award at the Smithsonian’s annual Hirshhorn New York Gala on Monday night. Congratulations as well to the winners and honorees at the 2026 CFDA Awards. More on that very curious evening below, including an interview with Colleen Allen, certified fashion darling and Emerging Designer of the Year winner.

Mentioned in this issue: Jon Ossoff and his tight suit, Colleen Allen, Ralph Lauren, Jill Biden, Melania Trump, Hasan Piker, Zohran Mamdani and Rama Duwaji, and Tim Burton and his new Loewe collaboration.

The CFDA Fashion Awards on Monday night brought some star power to the American Museum of Natural History: Keke Palmer hosted, and presenters included Matt Damon, Cole Escola, Lupita Nyong’o, Laufey, and Demi Moore. And yet the mood of the evening was somewhat tentative. The Council of Fashion Designers of America is under something of a renovation: Steven Kolb, its former CEO, resigned in September following an altercation with PETA protesters at New York Fashion Week. Serving in his stead is Hildy Kuryk, a comms expert who spent four years at Vogue as its executive director of communications following a career in politics. Kuryk is serving as interim CEO and heading up the search for Kolb’s permanent replacement. On Monday night, she stayed out of the spotlight, leaving most of the audience wondering—and gossiping about—who the new face of the CFDA could and should be.

Rachel Scott of Diotima took the main award of the night as womenswear designer of the year, and Anthony Vaccarello, who just left Saint Laurent, was presented with the International Designer of the Year Award by muses Zoë Kravitz, Hailey Bieber, and Rosé. Ralph Lauren was named menswear designer of the year, securing his place as the most awarded designer in CFDA history. Mr. Lauren himself made an appearance—a crowd-pleaser that also left me wondering if the American industry often puts too much emphasis on its past and not enough on its future. In that spirit, I called up Colleen Allen, who took the prize for emerging designer of the year, and asked her to introduce herself to those of you who haven’t met her yet.

Colleen Allen received her award from designer Gabriela Hearst and actor and writer Cole Escola.

José Criales-Unzueta: What would you say is the most important thing for people to know about your background?

Colleen Allen: I often lead with that I always was a menswear designer and was never intending on doing womenswear. That point of view has really guided my work and who I’ve become. I just love the process of making. I did menswear because there wasn’t an identity of women that I saw that I connected with, really, with brands when I was first going to school. And I just loved the craft of tailoring and learning the physical process of cutting a pattern. I think there’s such an ephemerality in making womenswear, in a way. Draping on a mannequin is quite abstract to me. A huge part of my identity is thinking like a tailor.

Where did you go to school, and where did you work before starting your brand?

I did half at Parsons, half at Central Saint Martins. I consider Parsons to be quite technical, and Saint Martins sort of opened up the world conceptually for me and gave me a lot of freedom and thinking about world-building. I was at The Row doing menswear for three years, and then I also interned at Vogue for a minute [and] Calvin Klein 205W39NYC was a very formative experience. When I was an intern there, I was full-time. I was there for eight months, but it was just so cool because it was all in the same room with Raf [Simons, now at Prada], Pieter [Mulier, now at Versace], Matthieu Blazy [now at Chanel]. You knew at that moment how special it was and how crazy it was. I think the world didn’t process it, but when you were in the room, you knew these people were all so important. And so I think being around that every day and absorbing the way that they worked together and they’re such world-builders, all three of them are very conceptual. I think that was a huge informative experience for me.

I think that people know me for color. The feedback that I get from people who buy the clothes is that when you’re wearing a big velvet coat in a color, or even just a dress or a top, people get stopped on the street just because it’s so visually impactful. Velvet is also a signature. That duality of color and texture, and also a bit of a historical Victorian sensibility.

Where do you think that interest in Victorian garments comes from?

There’s an emotional, aesthetic love for it. There’s a bit of a groundedness and a weight to it, and a bit of that period of time was quite spiritual thinking about the afterlife and death, but there was a sense of romance around that that I think really seeped into the clothes in a way that I don’t know if other periods of time really reflect. On the other side of it, it isn’t Victorian specifically, but a lot of historical dress for women is incredible tailoring—thinking about those bodice jackets that have a lot of tiny little panels with lots of little grosgrain finishing on the seams on the inside—and it’s all about molding to the female body. As a menswear designer, I love wearing men’s clothes and wearing a tailored jacket, but I think that the idea of having that sensibility and also reflecting femininity is the perfect storm for me. A lot of that just got lost in a certain period of time once women were able to wear pants, and in the transition of women being able to access menswear, women’s tailoring never got back to that sort of technical level of fitting the body or the female form.

You’ve never done a runway show, which is something that many young designers feel signals their arrival. Why is that?

I love fashion and wanted to work in fashion because I loved watching fashion shows. And specifically, I loved shows that were immersive. I always go back to [Alexander] McQueen [that signaled] fashion is more than product. It is this world-building, cinematic performance art, and you can take it just so much further emotionally. If I’m going to do a show, I want it to carry that level of weight. For me, at my scale, it just feels like the most manageable way to encapsulate a sense of emotion is to do it through a [photo] shoot and showroom appointments for now, just in truly a practical sense. But it is my dream, and I’m working towards having a show. But when I do it, I want it to feel special.

It’s a bit surreal, in a way. It’s especially cool that it’s voted by the industry, and it sounds maybe kind of cheesy, but I do just feel like that is the best reward. That all these people that I’ve worked with are acknowledging [me]. It feels solidifying, grounding. It feels like I’ve been doing this forever, and it’s also going by so fast at the same exact time. It lets me be present and acknowledge myself for how much work has been put into it and that I did it and it’s real and it’s something I dreamed about for so long.

Do you feel like a fashion-industry darling?

[Laugh] Oh my God, how does one answer that question? Gosh, I think that three years ago I was entering these rooms knowing absolutely no one... I think the showroom has actually really fostered real relationships and friendships in the industry, and a sense of community that just feels really good. I work as hard as I can to support whatever the stylists are doing or the editors, I’m always physically running around to make sure that they get the clothes because I love what they do. I love that I know the photographer and the stylist and all the people, and know that if my clothes are in it, it will be amazing for me and they made the effort to reach out to me, to include me in it. The word darling is such an ick word [laugh]. I feel like the word I want to say is camaraderie, because everyone just works so hard. Nobody is flouncing around being a darling. We are all hustling together, and if I can support your hustle and you support mine, that’s the best thing. And when it works, it’s just so satisfying.

Tim Burton, iconoclast filmmaker extraordinaire, has a new capsule with Loewe, which has recreated characters and vignettes from The Nightmare Before Christmas as bags and charms. These kinds of collabs were a staple when Jonathan Anderson was at the brand, and Jack McCollough and Lazaro Hernandez seem to be game to keep them coming with the same endearing whimsy.

In the age of artificial intelligence, the film has become a symbol of human craft and ingenuity. “The more it keeps going that way,” Burton told me yesterday, calling from London and answering a question about the proliferation of AI in film, “the more I appreciate [the human] side of it. I think it’s something that’s very important to keep in the world, the tactile, not being on a screen all the time and doing all that sort of thing. To me, [humanity] is very important to life and art and anything, really.” We also spoke about his collaboration with A$AP Rocky and whether he’d ever do a live-action remake of 2005’s Corpse Bride.
