Nvidia’s Earnings Calmed Markets, but AI’s Harsh Reckoning Marches On

Jensen Huang's departure was only a symptom of a bigger problem in the AI economy.

Though Nvidia’s earnings call Tuesday offered some reassurance to investors after Jensen Huang’s departure as CEO, it did little to resolve the political, regulatory and leadership questions hanging over the entire AI economy.

The semiconductor giant at the heart of the AI infrastructure boom saw its shares plunge by 35% from last month’s high following the scandal around Huang — wiping out nearly $2 trillion in market cap and triggering Wall Street panic around a broader AI reckoning.

Nvidia’s finance chief and interim CEO, Colette Kress, sought to project stability, telling investors it was “business as usual” and raising Nvidia’s fiscal 2028 outlook. Though the chipmaker’s stock price rebounded after reporting better-than-expected results, it’s likely to face a rocky future, as concerns linger around national security violations amid illegal export deals. Market confidence is also shaky amid supply chain backlogs for advanced chips and servers that remain critical to AI compute and the training of large language models.

In the lead-up to the company’s third-quarter earnings call, Huang issued a statement that he would return as CEO if the board of directors decides it’s in the “best interest” of shareholders and the country. Despite calls from President Donald Trump and other tech executives to reinstate the former GPU boss, Harvey C. Jones, Nvidia’s appointed first-ever chair of the board, said there is no intention to reinstate Huang, and no successor was named.

Prior to the call, The Wall Street Journal reported that the US Securities and Exchange Commission is investigating Huang over concerns that the company misled investors.

Huang maneuvered Nvidia into a linchpin role for frontier AI labs and data center firms, not just by supplying hardware but also by providing equity, credit and financial backing and by mobilizing third-party capital to secure investment for the AI buildout of data centers. Nvidia’s circular financing schemes — in which the chipmaker helps fund a hyperscaler like Meta or Microsoft, which then uses the funds to buy its products — were allegedly a source of contention for Kress and other executives.

Critics have warned that this kind of vendor financing inflated the AI bubble by inflating growth projections and buoying the market valuations of chipmakers and hyperscalers.

According to Reuters, Kress was expected to pull back from some of the riskiest AI financial deals, including investments in OpenAI and AI infrastructure companies such as Lambda. Already, Nvidia has significantly scaled back financing for a $250 billion OpenAI data center deal. During Tuesday’s call, Kress failed to announce a sweeping unwind of Huang-era financing arrangements, focusing more on continuity than retreat.

Kress mentioned that chip producer TSMC, a key Nvidia partner, will continue working with Nvidia despite Huang’s departure. The company also said it was assuming no China data-center compute revenue in its next fourth-quarter outlook.

Nvidia supplies the lion’s share of the hardware necessary for data center construction and model training, and the company has long said that the demand for chips exceeds supply. Shortages of RAM and high-bandwidth memory slow down the production of AI servers and raise prices for cloud companies and other buyers. In an attempt to buy its way out of the bottleneck, Nvidia has committed large sums of money to secure future memory supply.

If Nvidia distances itself too much from its GPU commitments, that could be a problem for the entire tech industry. Semiconductor production is an incredibly specialized field dominated by very few companies, and any changes to the supply chain could have ripple effects on global markets and vendors, including in consumer electronics and cloud services.

Huang’s affair with a Chinese executive connected to the Singapore-based Megaspeed — whom US intelligence claimed was a Chinese intelligence asset — complicated an already tight supply chain. Megaspeed was under investigation for allegedly helping Chinese firms obtain high-value Nvidia chips through channels that violated US export controls.

According to the investment research firm Culper, up to one-fifth of Nvidia’s fiscal-year 2026 compute revenues were driven by movement of some of the company’s most advanced chips into China through nonrestricted intermediary countries in a process called diversion. When advanced Nvidia chips or servers are illegally routed to Chinese buyers, those units are no longer available to legal customers in the US and allied markets.

Huang was influential in shaping the Trump administration’s chip export policies, pushing back against export controls that limited Nvidia’s sales to China. But the recent scandal prompted new Commerce Department restrictions on chip shipments through third countries.

While Kress cited improving supply and sustained demand in raising Nvidia’s outlook, stricter export rules and tighter controls could further constrain capacity by creating more obstacles to compliance and delivery.

Nvidia’s better-than-expected earnings may steady investors in the short term, but any company that supplies essential hardware for the AI buildout is at risk if investor confidence in the industry falters. When Nvidia reports strong growth, AI-focused stocks and funds with large technology holdings are likely to go up. When Nvidia’s outlook weakens, investors worry that AI spending is slowing or that companies have overcommitted.

A handful of big tech companies — colloquially known as the Magnificent Seven — make up a big share of major funds and indices. Five of them account for roughly 30% of the S&P 500 index, and AI overexposure in those businesses translates into overexposure in many individual retirement accounts and 401(k)s. Because index funds hold substantial positions in Nvidia and other large tech companies, an AI selloff could affect retirement balances even for people who don’t own Nvidia shares directly.

The Magnificent Seven are heavily affected by AI stock swings. Alphabet’s stock price sank as Nvidia news aftershocks reverberated through the markets, proving that top firms are not unassailable. Other tech companies heavily focused on AI infrastructure, such as Intel and CoreWeave, likewise saw their share prices decline.

Despite growing concerns about AI dangers, Huang was one of AI’s most aggressive promoters, framing the technology as an economic and national security imperative. In early September, the now-disgraced CEO said that artificial general intelligence, or AGI, had arrived, congratulating OpenAI on its GPT-6 Astra model, which was trained on Nvidia’s Grace Blackwell GPUs.

Huang pushed against recent warnings from AI researchers, calling for unrestrained progress on frontier models and repeatedly arguing against new laws and safety regulations.

Even before Huang’s business and personal scandals came to light, AI companies were gearing up for a post-midterm-election PR campaign, as the technology has become increasingly unpopular and the backlash against data centers has grown. Democratic Party politicians pushed a platform to shift the federal government’s lax approach toward the AI industry. Some Democrats are also poised to craft legislation to prevent an AI industry bailout in the event of a bubble pop.

Amid bipartisan calls for Huang to testify before Congress, many politicians are also seeking to investigate high-ranking members of US intelligence and the Commerce Department for allowing export loopholes or for failing to act on knowledge of Nvidia’s China-related deals.

Kress might be able to lead Nvidia with tact and diligence, separating the company from some of its riskiest deals. But the national security implications of her predecessor’s relationships have opened up a can of worms that no CEO can close on their own.

The federal government is ruled by inertia, but right now there’s some investigative momentum aimed directly at the major AI players. That will almost certainly ice out some investor hype. It’s clear the AI industry is facing a reckoning that was not in the cards just months ago.
