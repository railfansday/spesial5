A tiny remote controlled beetle robot named BeetleBot uses artificial muscles to explore narrow spaces and grab hidden objects

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Printed muscles that shrink when heated let this beetle-sized soft robot walk, grip an object and send back what it feels.

Shrink a walking robot and its motors and gears get harder to fit, not easier. Insect-scale machines have mostly solved this problem with tiny, rigid parts. BeetleBot has neither.

Its legs move because strips of printed plastic shrink when an electric current warms them, the way a muscle shortens when it fires.

The robot is about the size of a real beetle, and a person wearing a sensor patch on one finger steers it by bending that finger.

A camera on its head and a pressure sensor in its jaws send back what it sees and what it is touching. The operator can decide the next move without seeing the robot at all.

Inho Kim built it as a postdoctoral researcher at Caltech and is now an assistant professor of chemistry at Sungkyunkwan University. Sang Ouk Kim of KAIST and Wei Gao of Caltech led the work with a group at NTU Singapore.

The artificial muscles are made of a liquid crystal elastomer, a rubbery plastic with molecules that line up in one direction.

Heat it past a certain point and that alignment breaks down so the material contracts along the line of the molecules and pulls. Let it cool and it stretches back.

The team printed each muscle as a stack of layers with a laser-cut metal heating wire sandwiched inside. Running current through the wire warms the plastic.

At 3 volts, a free-standing muscle shortened by about 45 percent but returned to its full length once the power stopped.

Pulling force matters as much as travel. At their strongest these muscles pulled with 0.18 megapascals of stress. This is within the 0.1 to 0.35 range of a mammal’s skeletal muscle.

That’s why a sheet of printed plastic can carry a robot with a camera and a gripper bolted to it.

The placement of those muscles came from the insect. The team dissected and filmed Mecynorrhina torquata, a large flower beetle, and measured how its leg joints swung when its muscles were stimulated.

That relationship set where the artificial muscles went on the beetle robot’s own six legs.

Insects work their legs with muscles arranged in opposing pairs. One pulls a joint one way and the other pulling it back. BeetleBot copies the arrangement.

Its front legs lift and lower the body, bending about 20 degrees, while the back legs swing forward and back, bending about 12 degrees.

Walking consists of those two groups firing in turn. The front muscles lift the front of the body, which tips the rear legs onto the ground. The back muscles then fold and push the beetle robot forward. Heat one side only and the robot turns.

For the head, the researchers copied the mandibles a beetle uses to hold its prey. Two curved flaps close when their muscles contract, a simpler arrangement than the spring-and-air hand built for the same job. A capacitive pressure sensor sits on the gripping surface.

The sensor is two thin, metal-coated sheets with an air gap between them. Squeeze them closer and the electrical charge they hold changes. This is what the beetle robot reports as contact.

It registered a touch in 0.127 seconds, and its reading rose almost in step with pressure up to 50 kilopascals.

The gripper can also tell shapes apart. Gripping a small sphere, a large sphere and a cube produced three different signal traces. This means the operator learns something about the object without ever seeing it close up.

A perching drone reads a branch the same way, by touch, where a camera stops helping.

The control patch is a printed strip of rubbery polymer loaded with carbon nanotubes, tiny conductive threads that form a network through the material.

Bending the finger stretches the strip, breaks some of those connections, and raises its electrical resistance.

Four finger movements produced four clearly separated signals, which the system reads as forward, left, right and grip, then sends to the robot over a short-range, wireless link. The patch held its signal through 1,000 bending cycles.

“A key feature of this work is the bidirectional feedback, which links the information detected by the robot to the user’s next action,” said Inho Kim, the paper’s first author.

Information running both ways is what separates this beetle robot from a remote-control toy. Other soft machines go further still, thinking without electricity by building the logic into the material itself.

The person sends a gesture, the robot sends back a camera frame and a pressure reading, and the next gesture depends on what came back.

To test all of it together, the team set a target object behind an obstacle and had an operator drive the robot there using the camera feed alone.

It took eight separate movements, turns and forward runs in sequence, to get around the obstacle, reach the object, close the gripper on it and carry it.

The pressure signal jumped at the moment of contact, which is how the operator knew to grip. That is the whole loop in one run: a finger moves, legs move, a sensor answers, and the finger moves again.

“This study expands the potential uses of soft robots by connecting the movement of artificial muscles, sensing, and user control in a single system,” said Sang Ouk Kim.

The muscles only work hot. Full contraction needs the plastic above roughly 230°F (110°C), and surfaces on the robot climbed past 212°F (100°C) during walking.

A thermal camera showed that heat staying local to the working muscle rather than spreading through the frame.

Heat is also what sets the pace. Each step waits for the plastic to cool, so the walking cycle runs slower than the muscle’s own limit. Adding payload slowed it further.

Two other limits stand in the way of sending one of these somewhere real. The finger patch’s signals pass through a wired front end before they go wireless, and the course the robot crossed was a small, built setup, not rubble or a pipe.

KAIST said the actuation speed, the energy efficiency and the power supply all need work before soft robots like this one inspect industrial equipment or explore the environment.

Nobody has driven one of these through a space that a person could not enter. Inho Kim said he plans to make the muscles faster and more efficient, and to improve the control methods that use the robot’s own sensing.

The full study was published in the journal Science Advances.
