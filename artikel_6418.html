Three world champion drone-racing pilots lost to an autonomous drone that sees with just one camera

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

A drone racing AI from Delft beat three world champion drone pilots in Abu Dhabi, with one camera, a tiny neural network and borrowed hardware.

Beating a human champion at drone racing used to take a stereo camera and a lab fitted with motion-tracking equipment. In Abu Dhabi last year, however, a drone with one ordinary camera beat three former world champions in a row.

Its control network was small enough to run on the flight controller, and it flew on hardware and a track its builders didn’t design.

The team at Delft University of Technology in the Netherlands calls the system MonoRace. Stavrow Bahnam and Robin Ferede of the University’s MAVLab led the work, with Christophe De Wagter and Guido de Croon.

Their result suggests that fast, reliable control of autonomous robots doesn’t need more sensors and bigger computers, just better use of small ones.

In an interview with Earth.com, de Croon said the team had no idea beforehand whether its drone would even get close.

“Although we knew our drone was going very fast, we were still surprised that it was not only able to compete with a world-champion-level human pilot, but even to win,” he said.

The Abu Dhabi Autonomous Racing League and the Drone Champions League ran the race in April 2025 with a $1 million prize pool.

Every autonomous team flew the same organizer-built drone, with one rolling-shutter camera and the motion sensors on its flight controller. No GPS, no external tracking and no hardware changes were allowed.

The indoor hall measured 328 feet by 98 feet (100 by 30 meters), and the track ran twice through 11 square gates with openings of about 5 feet (1.5 meters).

One stretch was a split-S, a dive through an upper gate and straight back through a lower one the opposite way.

Human pilots, who fly with video goggles and need lightning-fast reflexes, raced in the same hall on the same dates. The best of them met the best autonomous drones in a knockout of quarterfinal, semifinal and final.

The Delft autonomous drone won all three rounds. Its fastest run took 16.56 seconds, and it reached about 62 miles per hour (100 kilometers per hour).

De Croon told Earth.com that in the first match, the human pilot spotted the Delft drone and reacted to avoid it. The team thinks that cost the pilot a little time.

“At the same time, our AI was oblivious of any other drones, just flying as fast as possible,” he said.

Most drones steer through layers of AI and conventional controllers, each translating a plan into smaller commands. MonoRace skips them.

A neural network of three layers with 64 neurons each takes the drone’s estimated position and motion and sends commands straight to the four motors, 500 times a second. A motor can go from idle to full power in two thousandths of a second.

The network learned to fly in simulation, by trial and error. The simulated drone’s properties were randomized by up to 50 percent so the real one would fall inside what the network had practiced.

On the fastest lap, the measured forces ran at the edge of a 30 percent band around the model and sometimes past it, and the drone kept flying.

More aggressive versions flew faster laps and crashed more. The most reliable one finished 38 of 43 flights.

“Instead of first planning a desired flight path and conventional commands, our neural network learns to directly control the motors,” Ferede said.

At 62 miles per hour, the camera’s images arrive blurred and warped, and a second neural network has to find the gates in them 90 times a second.

A model of how the drone moves fills in between frames. The drone then compares where it expects each gate to appear with where the camera actually finds it.

The comparison also replaced the missing tracking system. The camera’s flexible 3D-printed mount shifted after every crash or repair, so the team flew a lap and then tuned the camera angles until the predicted and observed gates matched.

De Croon said this is how people learn too, predicting what will happen and comparing it with what they see.

The competition hardware caused trouble of its own. Long camera cables flexed under hard acceleration and picked up interference, corrupting between 8 and 75 percent of the images in 10 recorded flights.

The drone still completed the full two-lap track with half its frames corrupted.

One flight, with three-quarters of its images corrupted, flew more than 82 feet (25 meters) blind, cleared one gate and hit the next.

The accelerometer maxed out at 16 times gravity in the sharpest turns, which threw off the position estimate and caused two crashes. The fix was to trust the drone’s own physics model instead of the sensor until the readings came back.

The system still relies on square gates to know where it is, and it can’t see other drones. In a four-drone race it took off first and pulled ahead, then flew into a competitor while lapping it and finished third.

The system also assumes it knows the map of the track in advance.

De Croon said the next step is to replace the hand-built middle of the pipeline, where the drone works out its position, with a neural network as well.

That would let the learning process improve the position estimate itself, and he expects that to make the drone more reliable first and faster later.

He also said that the same, small network could be trained to save energy instead of time. Most machines run on tiny processors with simple controllers that work but are far from optimal, and he thinks energy-optimal control on those chips could save a lot of energy.

The researchers wrote that the approach could carry over to self-driving cars, mobile robots and humanoid systems, though the study tested only the racing drone.

The full study was published in the journal Science Robotics.
