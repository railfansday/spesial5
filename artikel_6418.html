A wrist sensor worn at home tracked stroke recovery more precisely than clinic tests

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

A wrist sensor worn for a day at home scored stroke recovery with about half the measurement error of clinic tests, and could shrink trials.

After a stroke, the usual way to track recovery is a therapist watching you work through a set of tasks for half an hour. Most patients get that test before rehab and again when it ends.

However, these two scores, months apart, can’t tell a therapist whether a patient is responding in week three.

Researchers have now turned a day of readings from a wrist sensor worn at home into a stroke recovery score. It tracked the same changes with about half the measurement error of those clinic tests.

A clinical trial built on the score could also reach a reliable answer with more than 60 percent fewer patients, the team calculated.

Sunghoon Ivan Lee of the Manning College of Information and Computer Sciences at the University of Massachusetts Amherst led the work with his graduate student Ryan Wang. Colleagues at WashU Medicine, Shirley Ryan AbilityLab and Spaulding Rehabilitation joined them.

The team didn’t build a new gadget. They reanalyzed about 23,000 hours of wrist recordings already collected from 215 people, and wrote the software that turns those recordings into a score.

Researchers call a measure like that a digital biomarker.

In an interview with Earth.com, Lee said the size of the gain surprised him. “We found that our digital biomarker is almost twice as precise as current clinical standard assessments,” he said.

In the two standard tests, the therapist scores each task on a fixed scale. Each takes about 30 minutes and a trained specialist to run, so in practice it happens before therapy and after it.

Both tests also have a ceiling. A patient at the maximum score has, by that measure, nothing left to recover, even when the arm still doesn’t work the way it did.

“That means during that therapy process, neither the patient nor the therapist has a clear idea of how patients are responding to the treatments that they’re receiving,” Lee said.

The recordings came from three earlier studies in St. Louis. One followed 61 people through standard rehab in the first six months of stroke recovery. They came back for up to eight clinic visits over 24 weeks.

Those were the patients whose arms were changing fastest. Another followed 79 people at least six months past a stroke, through an eight-week arm therapy trial. The third enrolled 75 healthy adults.

After each clinic visit, everyone wore a small motion sensor, the kind inside fitness trackers, on each wrist for about a day at home.

The software reads only the stroke-affected wrist. It picks out the straight-line movements that make up most daily tasks, such as reaching for an object, and measures how fast, how abruptly and how variously the arm moved.

A model then combines those measurements into one number, matched to the clinic score, on a scale with no ceiling.

In the early-stage group, the score agreed closely with both clinic tests. Over 24 weeks, its average rose along almost the same curve as the therapists’ scores.

Its measurement error was 52 to 58 percent lower than that of the clinic tests, which is where Lee’s “almost twice as precise” comes from.

Then there were the patients who had hit the top of a clinic test. Their wrist scores were still lower than the healthy adults’ scores, a gap that the clinic scales can’t show because they have no room above the top mark.

Asked by Earth.com what else the data showed, Lee named that gap. “We discovered that our digital biomarker can differentiate stroke survivors who received a perfect clinical score from healthy subjects,” he said.

“This is significant because it shows the biomarker detects subtle motor performance differences that current clinical standards cannot.”

A less noisy measure means a clinical trial needs fewer people to show that a treatment works. The team reran two past trials in simulation, with the score standing in for the clinic test.

In a trial of robot-assisted therapy, an 80 percent chance of confirming the treatment effect took eight patients per group with the clinic test, and three with the score.

In a second trial, the number fell from 26 per group to seven. These are simulations built on statistical assumptions, the authors wrote, not real trial results.

“We can get a clear idea of the effectiveness of the intervention using a lower sample size and far fewer resources,” Lee said.

The long-term group is where the score’s limits show. Those patients barely changed over eight weeks of therapy on either measure, so the data can’t say whether the score picks up improvement years after a stroke.

The healthy adults were a little younger than the stroke survivors, and they were never tested in the clinic; the team assigned them perfect scores.

Each person wore the sensor for one day per visit, so the reliability figures come from splitting that day in half rather than from two separate days.

And the sensor records acceleration alone, which forces the software to estimate gravity’s share of the signal. The team wants a gyroscope added in future versions.

Lee has filed a provisional patent and is pursuing the technology through a startup, Lumid Health, so he stands to gain if it reaches patients.

“The hardware is already there; accelerometer sensors are standard in modern fitness trackers and smartwatches,” Lee told Earth.com.

“However, translation into clinical practice requires rigorous third-party validation on independent datasets and regulatory approval, which could take years.”

What the score would give a stroke survivor, he said, is a day-by-day read on whether the prescribed exercises are working.

The study measured how well the score tracks recovery. It did not test whether a therapist who sees that number every day treats a patient differently, or whether the patient ends up better off.

Lee’s partners are recruiting stroke survivors for a follow-up study at Spaulding Rehabilitation Hospital. Other groups will have to confirm the score on recordings this team never touched.

The full study was published in the journal Science Translational Medicine.
