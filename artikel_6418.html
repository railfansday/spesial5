Scientists Unlock a New Way To Photograph Events That Vanish in Trillionths of a Second

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Eleven carefully timed images can connect the early and later stages of an ultrafast event, offering scientists a way to investigate fleeting processes that are difficult to repeat.

A limited set of images can leave gaps in a laser pulse or shock wave’s story. Its first moments and later development may unfold on very different timescales, so taking snapshots at regular intervals can miss changes that connect one stage to the next.

Conventional imaging methods operate on microsecond, or millionth-of-a-second, and millisecond, or thousandth-of-a-second, timescales, usually capturing images at evenly or nearly evenly spaced intervals. Single-shot ultrafast imaging typically uses a uniform interval within a selected time window. With only a finite number of frames available, that choice can favor a detailed view of one brief stage over a record of the broader process.

A team led by Keiichi Nakagawa, an associate professor in the Department of Bioengineering at the University of Tokyo, developed an approach that gives researchers more control over where those frames fall. Called logarithmic ultrafast photography, it distributes a fixed number of images across time, concentrating them around moments when characteristic changes occur.

“We wanted to find a way to connect these very different timescales in a single measurement. In other words, we wanted to see both the first instance and what follows, without having to choose one or the other,” said Keitaro Shimada, a postdoctoral researcher in the Nakagawa Group and lead author of the study.

The team implemented the approach in sequentially timed all-optical mapping photography, or STAMP, a technique that combines ultrafast imaging with high image quality. To adjust the timing, they used band-resolved individual delay generation, or BRIDGE. It extracts wavelength bands, which are portions of the light spectrum, in sequence and allows their timing to be adjusted independently. Each band remains linked to a specific instant in the process being recorded.

That control lets researchers place frames at selected moments on timescales ranging from femtoseconds, or quadrillionths of a second, to nanoseconds, or billionths of a second. Instead of maintaining a regular interval, they can customize the gaps between images to resolve rapid changes and follow subsequent stages within the same recording.

The technique can capture key moments with 11 frames spanning five orders of magnitude in time, a 100,000-fold range. The report also describes this as equivalent to an approximately 260-frames-per-second exposure, although it does not explain the basis for that comparison.

“We are particularly interested in phenomena that are difficult to reproduce exactly. In those cases, recording the early and later stages of the same event can reveal relationships that might be lost when a sequence is reconstructed from separate experiments,” said Nakagawa, a co-author and principal investigator of the study.

The researchers’ targets include short-lived phenomena whose peaks are difficult to catch and connections between phases of a physical process that have remained unseen because they occur on different timescales. They propose using the technique in laser processing, material dynamics, plasma and shock-wave research, and biomedical applications.

Reference: “Logarithmic ultrafast photography with band-resolved individual delay generation” by 19 October 2026, Optica. DOI: 10.1364/OPTICA.610944
