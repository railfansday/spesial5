Scientists poured dye into a sinkhole at the bottom of a Florida lake, and it surfaced 35 days later at a famous spring 19 miles away

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

A pink dye test revealed an underground connection between Florida's Lake Jackson and five natural springs.

On September 19, 2017, scientists poured a pink tracer dye called Rhodamine WT into a sinkhole in the bed of Lake Jackson, about 5 miles (8 kilometers) north of Tallahassee, Florida.

Thirty-five days later, the main peaks of that dye turned up at Wakulla Spring, about 19 miles (30.6 kilometers) to the south.

Traces of the dye reached five separate natural springs in all. The results show a connection between the lake and those springs through underground channels in the limestone.

Only a small amount of dye seems to have made the full trip, and the scientists suspect most of it spread into the porous rock along the way.

Lake Jackson sits in a closed basin of 27,000 acres (10,900 hectares), or 42 square miles (109 square kilometers). Several large sinkholes open in its bed, including Lime Sink in the north and Porter Hole Sink in the south.

The lake’s original name, Okeeheepkee, means “disappearing waters.” Every so often it drains away through the bottom, in events known as dry-downs.

Major or partial dry-downs have been recorded in 1907, 1909, 1932, 1935, 1936, 1957 and 1982, and several times since 1999. In the final stages, the lake appears to drain rapidly, as though someone pulled the plug from a bathtub.

A dry-down can follow a combination of below-average rainfall, low groundwater levels and high evapotranspiration. Heavy storms can refill the lake quickly.

Water that leaves through the lakebed eventually enters the upper Floridan aquifer, according to the Florida Department of Environmental Protection.

Researchers think some of that water may travel to Wakulla Spring, though the agency notes that the timing and flow path still need investigation.

Seán E. McGlynn, technical director of McGlynn Laboratories Inc., carried out the study with co-author Alan W. Niedoroda.

They used fluorescent dye tracing to test whether a subterranean flow between Lake Jackson and Wakulla Spring could be positively demonstrated.

They injected the dye into the lake-bottom sinkhole for six and a half minutes.

Water-quality sondes at Wakulla Spring and nearby Sally Ward Spring recorded the main peaks after 35 days.

Charcoal packs, which absorb dye from passing water, were placed at those two springs and three others nearby. They also picked up dye within time windows that matched the peaks recorded by the sondes.

Each breakthrough event was brief, lasting from one minute to 100 minutes.

In slides for a Florida State University karst symposium, McGlynn put the peak travel time at 35.21 days over 19.1 miles (30.7 kilometers), or 2,864 feet (873 meters) a day.

The results point to a conduit-like system in the aquifer dominated by advection, meaning the dye was carried along by the moving water. On average, it traveled about half a mile (0.8 kilometers) a day.

Because dye showed up at five well-separated springs, the authors conclude that the conduit system is both braided and branching.

The paper describes a leaky conduit, in which much of the dye moving through the channels dispersed into the surrounding limestone matrix.

The study was exploratory, and its authors caution that it allows only conjectural statements about how much of the dye ended up where.

The study’s authors call Wakulla Spring a renowned first-magnitude spring. Its limestone holds many interconnected holes that feed a network of conduits, and it discharges about 300 million gallons (1.1 billion liters) a day, according to Florida State Parks.

Its cave system is the longest mapped underwater cave system in the United States, with conduits extending both north and south of the spring. More than 22 miles (35 kilometers) of passage lie deeper than 190 feet (58 meters).

Earlier dye traces in the Wakulla Spring watershed had already shown that water entering four major swallets later flows out at the spring.

The area’s many sinkholes are developed in the St. Marks Formation, a fossil-rich sandy limestone 20 to 23 million years old.

The 2024 study points to a leaky-conduit flow able to carry nutrients and pollutants rapidly over long distances. The same weakness leaves cave groundwater vulnerable to pollution in other karst regions.

In August 2021, the Florida Geological Survey announced a second trace at Porter Hole Sink, using green fluorescein dye, to confirm the earlier connection.

The dye release was timed to low water and the approach of Tropical Storm Fred, whose rain was expected to push the dye toward 23 monitoring sites. The agency said results could take weeks or months.

“Confirming this connection will help resource managers develop more informed strategies for managing our water resources,” said Guy Means, then acting director of the survey and Florida’s state geologist.

The upper Floridan aquifer supplies drinking water to Leon and Wakulla counties and feeds Wakulla Spring.

The study was published in the journal Journal of Cave and Karst Studies.
