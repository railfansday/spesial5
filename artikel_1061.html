‘Historic change’: what the 2026 US mid-term election results mean for science

The US mid-term elections swept the Democratic party to power in both chambers of the US Congress, raising hopes that lawmakers will now push back against disruptions to science introduced by the administration of Republican President Donald Trump.

The results will mark the end of nearly two years in which the Republicans controlled the presidency and both congressional chambers — and many scientists around the world are celebrating the change as an opportunity to protect the country’s research system.

“This is a big win,” says Megan Peters, a cognitive scientist who moved in 2025 from the University of California, Irvine, to University College London, in part because of worries about US science. “It’s a demonstration that US [voters] are dissatisfied with what the current administration has been doing.” Scientific research, she adds, “is America’s superpower, and it’s been cut at the knees”.

Those hopeful for change include researchers affected by the US Agency for International Development (USAID) being shut down early in Trump’s presidency. “I’m cautiously optimistic,” says Quarraisha Abdool Karim, an epidemiologist at the Centre for the AIDS Programme of Research in South Africa, which is headquartered in Durban and had partnered with USAID on clinical trials. “What we’ve seen in the past 24 hours is that democracy and democratic principles, with individuals turning out to vote, can make a huge difference.” But, she adds, “we will not see overnight changes”.

As of 2.15 p.m. EST on 4 November, Democrats had secured at least 237 seats in the 435-seat US House of Representatives and 54 seats in the 100-member US Senate, giving them decisive majorities in both, with votes still being counted in some states. These majorities will give them the power to craft legislation that counteracts Trump’s science agenda, although there will still be obstacles. For instance, given that Democrats haven’t won two-thirds of the seats in both chambers of Congress, the president can veto those bills.

Trump initially told The New York Times that he would accept the mid-term election results and that he would “look forward” to working with Democrats. Later, however, he posted an image of a red hat with the word ‘VETO’ on it to Truth Social, his social-media platform.

Since Donald Trump became president for the second time in January 2025, his administration has pushed for unprecedented cuts to science funding, politicized decision-making at federal science agencies and interfered with policies at US universities that receive federal funding.

When Democrats take charge of both chambers of the US Congress on 3 January 2027, they will be able to control congressional committees, including science committees; set agendas for hearings that investigate government officials and their science policies; and use legally binding subpoenas to compel the Trump administration to provide documents and testify in front of Congress.

On social media, Democrats promised to make use of that authority. Robert Garcia, a representative from California who will soon chair the House Committee on Oversight and Government Reform, shared a two-word statement aimed at the Trump administration: “Lawyer up.” In particular, some scientists are calling for scrutiny of Russell Vought, director of the White House Office of Management and Budget, who has drafted a plan that gives political appointees, rather than scientist peer reviewers, control of decisions about research grants, and who has delayed the distribution of congressionally approved science funds.

Scientists and policy specialists said they were hoping to see more congressional oversight of the actions of Robert F. Kennedy Jr, the head of the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS), who has questioned vaccine safety, and whose tenure has coincided with the country’s worst measles resurgence in decades. “For sure, secretary Kennedy will be put on the hot seat” to explain his unscientific actions, says Georges Benjamin, executive director of the American Public Health Association in Washington DC. “What I’m hoping is that Congress will really push this administration to have a much more proactive response to the measles epidemic.”

Zoe Lofgren, a Democratic representative from California, said she would campaign to become the head of the House Science, Space and Technology Committee, which oversees agencies such as NASA and the US National Science Foundation. “We will hold this administration accountable for the damage they have done to the American scientific enterprise,” she said in a statement.

Stand Up for Science, a science-advocacy group based in Atlanta, Georgia, that has organized protests against the Trump administration’s reshaping of US science, says that it will release a plan on 5 November with strategies to push Congress for change.

Whether Congress will be able to increase funding for federal science, rather than just hold it relatively steady as they have for the past two years, is doubtful, however. Susan Collins, a Republican senator from Maine and chair of the Senate Appropriations Committee, had protected science budgets, but she lost her re-election bid in these mid-terms. Large boosts to science spending could also just be difficult to pass, given that the budget must also be signed by Trump, who has tried to slash non-defence science spending.
