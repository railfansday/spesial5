At the Races: After the deluge

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

By Mary Ellen McIntire, Daniela Altimari and Niels Lesniewski

Tuesday’s rout by Democrats has led one group dedicated to electing more Republican women to Congress to warn of a “crisis-level decline” of female GOP members on Capitol Hill.

Danielle Barrow, the president of Women for Women Action Fund, drew parallels between this week’s results and the 2018 blue wave.

“After 2018, when Republicans lost 40 seats and elected only one new Republican woman, the party made a real commitment to recruiting and investing in women – and it worked,” Barrow said in a statement Wednesday, adding that women accounted for 11 of the 15 House seats the GOP picked up in the 2020 election. But she cautioned that the party needs to sustain that focus across election cycles.

“Republicans cannot only make this investment after a wipeout election and abandon it when things improve,” she said. “It is time for party leaders to treat this as a permanent strategy, not a crisis response.”

The ranks of House Republican women won’t plummet to as low as after the 2018 elections, when the caucus had just 13 female members. At least 18 Republican women have won House races so far, according to The Associated Press, and two others are leading as of Thursday afternoon. The conference is likely to see two new female members: Florida’s Sydney Gruters, the wife of RNC Chairman Joe Gruters, and Washington state’s Amanda McKinney, who holds a comfortable lead as votes continue to be counted.

Still, those figures are worlds apart from House Democrats, whose caucus will have more than 100 women next year.

“We have always known that when women run, women win,” Florida Rep. Lois Frankel, chair of the member-led Elect Democratic Women PAC, said in a statement Wednesday.

Those incoming new members span the ideological range of the party, including progressives from New York and California who scored big primary wins this summer, to more moderate Democrats who flipped seats in Virginia and Pennsylvania.

House Democrats had set a public goal this cycle to push their female membership past 100. Former Speaker Nancy Pelosi, who is retiring at the end of the year, said electing Democratic women had been her “passion” since she joined the House in 1987.

“We made a decision to make more room for women at the tables of power – and generations of candidates, organizers and supporters have carried that work forward,” she said.

Blue breakthrough: Democrats flipped the House and Senate this week, giving the party one of its biggest nights in decades. On the Senate side, they wrested seats from Republicans in Texas, South Carolina, Kansas, Iowa, North Carolina and Ohio, while finally flipping Maine. Meanwhile, on the House side, Democrats were on track to not just win every race rated a Toss-up by Inside Elections but also pick up seats in deep-red territory that weren’t considered competitive until late in the cycle. Mary Ellen and Daniela summed up their initial election night takeaways, including how Democratic moderates and progressives both cheered the results and why many of Trump’s picks flopped at the ballot box.

Not going anywhere: The top Senate Democrat, Charles E. Schumer of New York, is confident he will be elected majority leader by his caucus, especially after the party’s resounding midterm wins. Several of his incoming members have promised to support others, our colleague Savannah Behrmann reports, but the reality is there’s no real plausible alternative. Schumer’s own seat is up in 2028.

Redistricting rout: Republicans’ redistricting gambit hasn’t paid off as they’d hoped, the latest election results show, with the GOP appearing poised to flip only a handful of redrawn seats. Democratic incumbents who sought reelection from their reconfigured seats appeared poised to all win, although the race for Rep. Darren Soto’s Central Florida seat remained uncalled as of Thursday afternoon.

Taking the chair: Among the bruising defeats that North Carolina Republicans suffered this week was National Republican Congressional Committee Chairman Richard Hudson’s loss to Democrat Richard Ojeda, a former West Virginia state senator. Hudson is now the second party campaign chief to lose reelection this decade after New York’s Sean Patrick Maloney , who lost in 2022 while serving as the DCCC chair.

New senators: The incoming Senate Democratic class includes a returning senator, a former governor, two lieutenant governors and a congressman, a notable amount of governing experience. Meanwhile, five Republican House members are moving across the Rotunda after winning open Senate seats.

Member-to-governor pipeline grows: Five newly elected governors will be departing Capitol Hill to become the top executive of their state or territory, Savannah reports. Additionally, two former members who also served in the Biden administration were elected governor, though a trio of House Republicans fell short in their gubernatorial bids.

Comeback season: Roll Call’s Nick Eskow reports on the former members who are poised to return to the House or Senate next year, with at least seven winning comeback bids Tuesday.

The firsts: Several candidates made history this week with their elections to Congress or the governorships of their state. Roll Call’s Andrew Menezes rounds up the milestone wins.

D.C. in the House: Robert White, D.C.’s newly elected nonvoting delegate, told our colleague Nina Heller that he plans to lobby fellow Democrats next year to support D.C. statehood. A potential selling point: The party could tackle affordability concerns better with two Democratic senators from the deep-blue District, he said.

Keeping count: As of 4:30 p.m. Thursday, Democrats had flipped 42 seats in the House and seven in the Senate, according to the AP. Republicans have so far picked up two House seats, both through redistricting. Twenty-eight House contests and two Senate races (Alaska and Mississippi) remain uncalled as of publication time. Check out Roll Call’s results tracker for updates as the races are called.

Indie losses: Independent Senate hopefuls running in deep-red states did not find success on Tuesday, even in races without a Democrat on the ballot. In Nebraska, former union leader Dan Osborn came up short in his second Senate bid. In Idaho, former state legislator Todd Achilles failed to dislodge Sen. Jim Risch, while Navy veteran Brian Bengs couldn’t topple South Dakota Sen. Mike Rounds. In Montana, where the Democratic nominee did not drop out, former university president Seth Bodnar lost to Republican Kurt Alme. On the House side, Alaska GOP Rep. Nick Begich comfortably leads commercial fisherman Bill Hill, though below the 50 percent threshold needed to avoid a ranked-choice runoff. With votes still being counted, the AP had yet to call the race at publication time.

Not just Congress: The blue wave that broke Republican majorities on Capitol Hill also washed over state legislatures. Democrats are poised to hold new majorities in 12 legislative chambers, including the North Carolina House and Texas House, according to the Democratic Legislative Campaign Committee, although some results aren’t official yet. The party also won governing trifectas in Michigan, Minnesota, Nevada, Pennsylvania and Wisconsin. Meanwhile, in blue states, Democrats tightened their grip on government: In Rhode Island, Republicans are on track to hold just 10 seats in the entire 113-member legislature, while next door in Connecticut, the 36-seat Senate could have as few as three GOP members.

Blame game: As Republicans take stock of what went wrong for them in the midterms, Vice President JD Vance was quick to point to the Iran conflict as an insurmountable albatross. “The reason we lost is the war,” Vance said in a lengthy X post. “If you’d like to argue it was worth it, that losing the Senate for the next 6 years was worth it, then do so. … But everyone knows why we lost.”

But the tariffs: A pair of Republican former senators, Arizona’s Jeff Flake and Pennsylvania’s Patrick J. Toomey, both also urged their party to focus on the impact of Trump’s tariff policies. “Arbitrary and capricious tariffs directly raised costs for all American consumers,” Toomey wrote Wednesday on X. “Many voted for Democrats yesterday.”

Staying put: Pennsylvania Rep. Brendan F. Boyle says he will remain in the House, where he’s poised to become the next Budget Committee chair, rather than launch a Democratic primary challenge to Sen. John Fetterman next cycle.

Bayh Bayh Bayh: A third generation of Indiana’s famed Bayh family won political office this week, with Beau Bayh’s election as secretary of state. Bayh, a Marine Corps veteran, follows in the footsteps of his father, Evan Bayh, who won the same post 40 years ago before going on to serve as governor and senator, and his late grandfather, Birch Bayh, who won three terms in the Senate. Indiana Democrats had a breakthrough election this week, defeating GOP Rep. Victoria Spartz, winning a second statewide office, unseating the speaker and breaking the GOP supermajority in the state House.

A win and a loss: Nebraska Republican Sen. Pete Ricketts won reelection, but his wife, Democrat Susanne Shore, lost a special election for a seat on University of Nebraska Board of Regents, which governs the university system. Shore, who lost to a fellow Democrat in the officially nonpartisan race, was making her first bid for elective office.

Bans on the ballot: Voters in Arizona, Nebraska and Washington state have approved new measures barring transgender athletes from participating in girls sports. A similar proposal in Colorado also appears likely to pass, although the AP had yet to call it as of publication time.

Roll Call elections analyst Nathan L. Gonzales of Inside Elections shares 15 initial lessons from the midterm elections, from the president’s “political mortality” to candidate quality and the power of personal brands. And, Nathan writes, as bad a night as it was for Republicans, it could have been even worse.

As Maine goes: The New York Times caught up with with Republican Sen. Susan Collins, who lost her bid for a sixth term in Maine. Collins, who had survived tough cycles before, spoke of getting “caught up in the Trump tsunami” and how she finally had her “Margaret Chase Smith moment.”

“These are extinction-level numbers”: Texas Monthly sat down for an interview with former state House Speaker Dade Phelan, who anticipated a down-ballot GOP rout but not the full extent of it. “MAGA needs to be dead,’’ said the Republican, who led the push to impeach state Attorney General Ken Paxton in 2023 and then was targeted for defeat by Trump. He opted against reelection this year.

Corruption a top issue: The Associated Press reported that its 2026 voter poll found corruption at the top of the list of concerns for voters who helped sweep Democrats into power on Capitol Hill.

Tar Heeled: Among the states where Republicans suffered their biggest defeats Tuesday, perhaps none was more stunning than North Carolina, where they’ve so far lost five U.S. House seats and are trailing in one another and lost control of the state House. The (Raleigh) News & Observer looks at how maps “designed to deliver strong Republican majorities [failed] so spectacularly at that goal.”

Support and discord: Michigan Sen.-elect Abdul El-Sayed’s sharply critical views toward Israel roiled the Jewish community in Michigan. But exit polling suggests most Jewish voters backed him, albeit in smaller numbers than they might have had a different Democrat run, Religion News Service reports.

That’s how many House districts won by Trump in 2024 have been called for Democratic candidates by the AP as of 4:30 p.m. Eastern time on Thursday.

Twenty-eight additional Trump-won districts remain uncalled at publication time, and Democrats are currently leading in eight of those races.

The figures stand in sharp contrast to the start of the cycle, when there were just 13 House Democrats in districts that backed Trump in 2024.

Since then, several Republican-led states redrew their congressional maps in an effort to increase the number of Trump-won districts and improve their party’s chances of holding the House.

In large part, this effort appears to have backfired in 2026, with the Democratic wave cresting high enough to wash over dozens of Trump-won districts. Twenty-one of the 52 Democratic wins so far have come in states that redrew their congressional lines: Texas (six Trump-won seats), North Carolina (six), Ohio (five), Florida (three) and Alabama (one).

Congressional Republicans are holding leadership elections next week as they transition from the majority to the minority party. Speaker Mike Johnson is not seeking his conference’s top post, setting up a clash to be the next House minority leader. On the Senate side, South Dakota’s John Thune hopes to continue leading his party in the chamber.
