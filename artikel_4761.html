Zinc: The Missing Link in Anorexia?

Over more than three decades of treating children, adolescents, and adults with eating disorders—including anorexia nervosa—I’ve seen the same pattern again and again. A patient arrives with severe anorexia, and they turn out to be severely deficient in one essential mineral: zinc.

So, while anorexia is a complicated disease, one part of treatment is very simple: If you, your child, or your patient has anorexia, the first and best step toward recovery may be correcting a zinc deficiency.

1: Zinc deficiency looks remarkably like anorexia. Imagine I described someone with a poor appetite, weight loss, slowed growth, changes in taste and smell, depression, anxiety, problems concentrating, low energy, social withdrawal, and digestive complaints. Would you think I was describing anorexia nervosa? You’d be right.

But I’d also be describing the symptoms of…zinc deficiency. Yes, many of the hallmark symptoms of anorexia are also classic symptoms of zinc deficiency. That doesn’t prove zinc deficiency causes anorexia. But it raises an obvious question: Could at least some of what we’ve traditionally thought of as the symptoms of anorexia actually be driven—or made worse—by a deficiency of this one nutrient?

2: Zinc deficiency is extremely common in people with anorexia. When researchers measure zinc levels in people with anorexia, they consistently find that a substantial percentage are deficient. Depending on the study and the methods used, at least half—and in some studies many more—show evidence of inadequate zinc status.

3: Remove zinc from an animal’s diet—and it begins to resemble someone with anorexia. In several studies, when scientists fed laboratory animals a diet lacking zinc, the animals ate less and lost weight. Growth slowed. They became less active socially. Females stopped reproducing. In short, they displayed features of anorexia nervosa.

Zinc is involved in hundreds of biological processes throughout the body; it helps regulate the activity of more than 300 enzymes, and it influences thousands of proteins that control growth, repair, metabolism, and communication between cells. In terms of our understanding of anorexia, zinc is crucial for:

Appetite regulation. Zinc participates in the complex network of hormones and signaling molecules that help regulate hunger, satiety, and energy balance. When zinc levels fall, that finely tuned system begins to malfunction. Appetite often declines. Food becomes less appealing. Eating can begin to feel like an obligation rather than a pleasure. This creates a cruel biological cycle.

A person who is already eating too little develops a zinc deficiency. The zinc deficiency further suppresses appetite. Reduced appetite leads to even less food intake. That, in turn, worsens the zinc deficiency.

I’ve seen this cycle happen so many times that I now think of it as one of the central biological engines driving anorexia. Breaking this cycle is one of the reasons correcting zinc deficiency can be so important.

Intra-brain communication. Zinc helps regulate the activity of several neurotransmitter systems that have long been implicated in anorexia, including glutamate and GABA, while also influencing serotonin and other chemical messengers involved in mood and anxiety.

You may be thinking: The biological connection between zinc deficiency and anorexia certainly sounds convincing. But does correcting the deficiency actually help people recover?

For more than four decades, studies on zinc and anorexia point in the same general direction: When people with anorexia receive supplemental zinc, they tend to improve. They gain weight more readily. Their appetite and sense of taste may return. Their mood becomes more stable. Physical development resumes. And, importantly, zinc has generally proved safe and well-tolerated.

One of the most impressive clinical trials enrolled 35 women who had been hospitalized with anorexia. Each patient was randomly assigned to receive either 100 milligrams (mg) of zinc daily or a placebo. The women continued taking zinc or a placebo until they achieved a 10% increase in body mass index (BMI). The results were striking.

The women receiving zinc increased their BMI at twice the rate of those receiving the placebo. Think about what that means. Both groups were hospitalized. Both were receiving expert care for anorexia. Both were working to regain weight. The principal difference between the two groups was that one also received zinc. And that group recovered significantly faster.

Writing in The International Journal of Eating Disorders, the researchers concluded, ‘‘The use of zinc supplementation should be considered in the treatment of AN patients.’’ I agree.

You’ll find citations to many other similarly impressive studies in the references at the end of this blog. The overall message from the research is that correcting zinc deficiency can improve recovery from anorexia nervosa.

To complicate matters, normal blood zinc does not always mean normal zinc status—because only a tiny fraction of the body’s zinc circulates freely in the blood, and the body works hard to maintain adequate zinc in the bloodstream.

For these reasons, laboratory results must be interpreted in context. A zinc value near the lower end of the reference range may be particularly significant when it appears alongside anorexia, restrictive eating, poor appetite, altered taste, slow healing, frequent infection, anxiety, depression, or other signs associated with deficiency.

Once zinc deficiency has been identified—or is strongly suspected—the next step is correcting it. I generally recommend beginning with therapeutic doses as part of a comprehensive program of targeted nutritional therapy. My protocols are the following:

For adolescents, the typical starting dose is 15 mg of zinc twice daily, for a daily total of 30 mg.

For adults, the typical starting dose is 30 mg twice daily, for a daily total of 60 mg.

After approximately three months, these doses are generally reduced by half—to 15 mg daily for adolescents, and 30 mg daily for adults. This level establishes ongoing maintenance of zinc levels during recovery.

These recommendations should be individualized for the patient. The protocol should also be supervised by a qualified healthcare professional knowledgeable about nutritional medicine.
