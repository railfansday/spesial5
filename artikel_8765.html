The Peptide Door Is Cracking Open, Even If The Risks Aren’t Going Away

Recently, an interesting thing happened. An athlete could read the World Anti-Doping Agency’s prohibited list and conclude that using certain peptides was a good way to lose a medal and win a ban. Then he could read a new federal announcement about those same compounds and conclude that Washington was opening the door to them. Same molecule. Two government-adjacent messages that seem to be saying two entirely different things. This could lead to quite a headache if you don’t bother reading the fine print.

On Oct. 30, the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) published a draft interim policy involving six experimental peptides: BPC-157, TB-500, KPV, MOTS-c, epitalon, and Semax. The idea is to use enforcement discretion while regulators decide whether these substances belong on the list of ingredients that certain licensed pharmacies and physicians can use in compounding. In plain English, HHS says it does not intend to pursue enforcement against qualifying compounders who follow a set of conditions. That is not the same thing as approving six new drugs.

Compounding is when a licensed professional prepares a medication for an individual patient rather than dispensing a mass-produced, approved product. The proposed policy calls for patient-specific prescriptions, traceable ingredients, quality documentation, reporting of serious adverse events, and other safeguards. It’s a long way from some internet chemist shipping you a vial labeled “research use only” with a complimentary discount code from a guy who benches four plates on Instagram.

I can see the argument for this. People are buying these things anyway. If a person is determined to use an experimental peptide, there’s an obvious appeal to getting it through a licensed professional instead of trusting an anonymous supplier whose laboratory might be a shack in a Chinese suburb. HHS says the policy is intended to steer people away from risky, unregulated sources. Fair enough. But a cleaner supply chain is not the same as a molecule that has been proven to work.

That’s the part the peptide evangelists tend to skip. BPC-157 has a reputation for repairing practically everything except a a broken heart. TB-500 gets talked about like it comes with replacement tendons in the box. But the clinical evidence supporting these sweeping claims in humans is thin. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) scientists raised concerns about the lack of convincing clinical data and about product safety.

An advisory committee nevertheless supported broader compounding access for six substances. Disagreement among experts is not proof that either side has discovered a miracle. But, the empirical data is unavoidably overwhelming. It’s a clear case of not believing the dyno numbers – the car feels faster. Such reports have dialed up to the point where the usual culprits smell dollar signs. However thin the clinical data, its being drowned out by an ever growing cadre of gym bros who swear it works, the list is prestigious too. Now all of a sudden the government tosses a glimmer of light down the hallway?  Out of nowhere? Isn’t it usually the government that’s slamming the door shut?

Note that the draft doesn’t authorize supplement companies to dump these compounds into pre-workouts and doesn’t establish that a product is safe or effective. The federal notice is open for public comment, and the policy can be revisited if safety or quality problems emerge. There are also state-level rules to contend with. Anyone announcing that peptides have been “FDA approved” has either misunderstood the document or is counting on you not reading it.

Now comes the part where competitive athletes have to pay close attention: BPC-157 and TB-500 remain prohibited under the World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA) rules used by many tested sports. A change in how federal regulators view compounding peptides does not rewrite the WADA rulebook. A prescription is not automatically permission to compete with a substance in your system.

The U.S. Anti-Doping Agency (USADA) has already sanctioned athletes over these compounds. Proof that the obstacle is here to stay.  What the outlaws will have to contend with is that while there are banned peptides that can already be detected directly in an athlete’s urine or blood, others present a much tougher analytical problem. And a ban doesn’t require WADA to have a practical routine test for the substance before they ban it. So, that right there is the loophole. And trust me when I tell you, it’s going to be exploited. Why? Because peptides work.

I’m all for serious research. I’m also for admitting when thousands of testimonials cannot all be wrong. If the new policy leads to better manufacturing controls and useful clinical data, good. If it merely replaces the sketchy online vial with a more expensive sketchy vial wearing a white coat, we haven’t accomplished much. There’s an important difference between making something easier to obtain and proving it’s worth obtaining. And if you compete in a tested sport, there’s a third question: whether using it costs you your career. Don’t confuse the three. And remember, if an athlete is prone to cheating, chances are he will cheat. And with what will he use to cheat? The stuff that works. Peptides are just now showing up in force and its a force to be reconned with.
