What the VA Gets Right About Death

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

— Its palliative care model protects the final, fragile moments families have together

On November 11, 1991, I called my father, a taciturn 24-year senior non-commissioned officer, to wish him a happy Veterans Day. I was a newly minted second lieutenant, and his pride in my becoming an officer and first-generation college graduate was palpable. That call evolved into an annual tradition spanning 34 years.

As I mourn him this Veterans Day, I'm grateful for what the system got right during the terminal months of his illness. It is easy to parrot the critique that the Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) is a broken, bureaucratic mess. But at its best, the VA healthcare system is a model of compassion that guards the dignity of those who served.

As a trauma surgeon and critical care specialist, I saw how the VA gave my father something I can't guarantee my own patients: superb hospice care that allowed him to die with dignity in the comfort of his own home. This dignified, holistic approach is a national model of excellence that proves why threats to privatize the system are a dangerous mistake.

This level of care is by design. In 2003, the VA did something revolutionary and became the nation's first health network to launch a systemwide palliative care mandate. That policy required every VA medical center -- whether a massive urban hospital or a small regional clinic -- to establish interdisciplinary teams dedicated to the physical, psychological, and spiritual needs of dying veterans and their families. As the largest integrated health network in the nation, the VA transformed the interdisciplinary model from a localized specialty into a universally funded standard of care.

The VA is often derided, and some of the criticism is valid: Long wait times for routine care and inconsistent quality have fueled the push to privatize the system. But those same criticisms apply to the many ivory-tower healthcare systems where I have worked: Insurance coverage gaps, delays caused by prior authorization reviews, and a national shortage of palliative care specialists are all barriers to care in the private sector.

In my three decades of medicine, no system where I've worked surpasses the VA for end-of-life care. The system operates on a massive scale -- nearly 86% of veterans access hospice or palliative care in their final 6 months of life -- and provides superb quality. Families consistently rate the VA's care higher in satisfaction than civilian alternatives. By working in tandem with local hospice agencies, the VA manages a patient's physical pain while wrapping the entire family in bereavement support. In practice, this model elevates end-of-life care for our veterans in three critical ways.

First is speed when it matters most. While a veteran might wait months for a routine specialty appointment, the system moves with impressive urgency at the end of life. Palliative and hospice consults bypass the notorious waitlists. When a veteran is dying, red tape is stripped away. End-of-life care is triaged as an acute, time-critical medical need.

Second is the shield against bureaucracy. End-of-life care in the VA is managed by dedicated interdisciplinary teams -- physicians, nurses, social workers, and chaplains -- who act as fierce advocates. They handle administrative heavy lifting, insulating families so they can focus on their loved ones. By removing the burden of navigating a complex medical system, these teams give families the invaluable gift of uninterrupted time together.

Third, the VA offers the advantage of concurrent care -- this is where the VA truly outperforms the private sector. Under standard Medicare rules, patients are usually forced to forgo life-prolonging treatments to qualify for hospice. The VA allows veterans to receive disease-modifying therapies, like palliative radiation or dialysis, while also receiving hospice support.

To die peacefully at home with autonomy is a blessing many Americans are denied. In this respect, we are doing right by our veterans. Were it not for the VA, my father could have been languishing in a hospital bed -- the fate of too many of my own dying patients. Instead of gutting the VA through privatization, Congress must fully fund it. Even better, civilian Medicare should adopt the VA's humane concurrent care model for all Americans, a move that would reduce the costly emergency department visits and lengthy intensive care stays I see in my practice.

Ultimately, the VA's superior palliative model protects the final, fragile moments families have together. Because they handled the bureaucracy, my final memories aren't of fighting insurance companies, as many of my patients' families do, but of just being his son.

There are so many things I wish I could do with my father one last time: fish with him off the base pier, listen to him belt off-pitch to Prince's "Purple Rain," or take our annual birthday trip to Vegas for the breakfast buffets, blackjack, and steak dinners he loved.

Still, knowing he was granted the dignity he deserved at the end, I would trade all of that for one more chance to pick up the phone today, call home, and hear his raspy laugh when I say, "Happy Veterans Day, Dad."
