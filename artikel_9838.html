My friends, has anyone considered the old standby straight-up denial? There's this famous study on which learning tasks people will take if given the option to improve their skills. One task is advertised as majorly effective, the other as so-so. It turns out the researchers could easily influence which participants chose which task. See, in the ineffective task, people could try and fail privately, and the highly effective one involved practicing in front of the group so everyone saw your mistakes. Clearly different paths with different risks and outcomes, and you all might have a sense of which group you would tend towards. How would a researcher influence you to pick one over the other? They gave people reading material about the malleability of the particular skill, debating in this case. One group read that debate is mostly an innate skill, the other that anyone can do it. It just takes training. It takes practice. The people who were in the inborn skill group, they did not participate in the activity that would help them improve, because then everybody would see that they lacked the skill.

The trainable skill group people were like, ok, let's go. Who cares if other people see that I can't do this yet? Showing people you're ignorant of something today can and should be super low stakes. It goes along with believing in neuroplasticity. This study is out of Carol Dweck's research about growth mindset, which had a whole Silicon Valley trend moment. I think it's not as trendy now, but the research is still solid. People who believe that their traits and abilities can significantly change are more likely to engage in behaviors that improve them. One problem that I ran into face first, like a very clean plate glass door one, is that when you start paying attention to whether you have a growth mindset and realize that without the researcher's priming that you would definitely be in the group that's like, better make sure no one finds out I'm a terrible debater.

Part of my identity has become someone who learns out loud, and I've modeled learning out loud in public. The first time a principal engineer on the Photoshop team wanted to show me how to use a new AI tool to bolt a tricky feature onto our existing architecture, I recorded our screen share session and posted it for the rest of the team because seeing a tool used in a real-world context is always more instructive than the demo-worthy vacuum. Especially because, like all my comrades who brought their kids to weekend morning soccer practices, we each come to AI and use AI in completely different ways. My favorite take on this is from Dr. Cat Hicks. She is a research psychologist studying software teams and gets riled up in a very data-driven way when people talk about things like measuring team effectiveness. She emphasizes that none of us are having the same experience.

"When you have a highly adaptive, dialogical tool," which means the user is in dialogue with the tool, "where the user is shaping the context and wielding progressive disclosure to probe very different parts of its capabilities, different people will continue to get very different answers. Until we get real about the personalized, adaptive nature of this." People are each running their own individual experiments with new tools, and if we don't tell each other what our own tool uses look like, no one will know that you can put your kid to bed already wearing their football kit. This is not the time to experiment alone until your output seems to resemble other people's. Plus, even if I just complain about a tool, I usually get some really good alternative suggestions. Even though we've all transcended onto this higher plane of mental abstractions, there is still value in showing your actual footwork. Where did your tool fall over? Why did you keep using it anyway?

Now you're in a group of people sharing information and refining ideas before they've fully calcified, and you make each other better. Another aspect of bringing people with you. A few months ago, I used an AI agent to help me plan out decluttering my basement.
