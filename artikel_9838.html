The Boutique PR Firm Is Having Its Moment

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Public relations follows a familiar professional-services model. Large clients hired large agencies. Big accounts have been staffed with big teams. Senior people sold the work, layers of specialists executed it, and scale itself was treated as evidence of capability.

Law went through a similar period. Then sophisticated clients began realizing that some of their hardest problems were better handled by smaller firms built around a handful of deeply experienced practitioners. Boutique law became a serious category in intellectual property, litigation, technology, venture capital, labor and other fields where judgment mattered enormously. Statista notes that boutique law firms have become increasingly significant in the U.S. legal market since the Great Recession.

Public relations may be entering its boutique-law era.

This has little to do with celebrating small businesses for being small. And let’s be clear, many a small professional services firm has attempted to call itself boutique just as a fancier way of saying they are small. They’ve claimed that it’s been by intent.

A ten-person mediocre PR firm remains a mediocre PR firm. The boutique model becomes interesting when a firm concentrates experienced people around a defined set of problems, keeps those people directly involved in the work and builds an operating model around judgment rather than staffing volume.

And boutiques have often been justified as a concentration of people with expertise in a specific niche, thereby arguing that while they are small, they have more scale in that particular niche. But in today’s environment, a more powerful boutique model may be expertise across industries, not over-concentrated in one silo.

That structure may be unusually well suited to the communications market taking shape right now, becuase we have at least one phenomena rolling across all industries at the same time, artificial intelligence.

Let’s start with an apparent contradiction. Demand for communications is healthy. PRovoke Media estimates that the global PR agency business grew roughly 5 to 6 percent in 2025. More than two-thirds of its top 300 firms grew, and more than one-third posted double-digit growth. The top 250 generated more than $18.25 billion in combined revenue, up from $17.4 billion the previous year. Revenue per employee across nearly 250 firms also crossed $200,000 for the first time, reaching slightly more than $205,000.

Inside agencies, however, the picture is considerably tighter. Davis+Gilbert's 2025 industry study surveyed 181 PR firms. Half projected annual revenue below $5 million, another 32 percent expected revenue between $5 million and $25 million, and only 18 percent projected more than $25 million. Just 50 percent expected revenue growth in 2025, down from 57 percent the previous year, while 35 percent expected revenue to decline, up sharply from 22 percent. Only 44 percent anticipated profit growth.

Those numbers describe a market in which clients still value communications while becoming much tougher about how they buy it.

That matters for boutiques because an agency designed around experienced practitioners can have a very different cost and staffing structure from a firm that needs multiple organizational layers to deliver an account. The client can buy the people doing the thinking.

That sounds obvious until you consider how much of the traditional agency industry was designed around leverage. Again, senior people win accounts. Teams execute them. Junior employees accumulate experience. Managers manage the process. Scale allows an agency to serve many clients simultaneously.

There is nothing inherently wrong with that model. It simply becomes less compelling as technology changes the economics of production.

Muck Rack's 2025 State of PR study offers a useful window into the speed of change. In its survey of 911 usable respondents, 77 percent of PR professionals said they already use tools such as ChatGPT in their workflow, while 59 percent expected AI and automation to become more significant during the next five years.

Think about what communications agencies produce every day, transcripts, research summaries, media lists, first drafts, coverage reports, briefing documents, competitive scans, background research, social copy and meeting notes. AI can already accelerate much of that work.

If technology reduces several hours of routine production to 30 minutes, clients have less reason to pay for the organizational structure historically required to produce it. The value migrates toward deciding what matters, understanding the client's business, identifying the right reporter, recognizing what will become a story, knowing when to push, knowing when to stay quiet and connecting communications activity to an actual business objective.

Those are judgment-intensive activities. The boutique model concentrates its economics there.

A three-, five- or ten-person firm with very experienced practitioners and strong technology can now command capabilities that once required a much larger operating structure. The result resembles what happened in other professional services, technology increases the leverage of individual expertise.

The unit of value becomes judgment per person. But just because a person has been in an industry for a long time does not mean their judgement is superior. Rather it means junior folks in the industry profit less by doing automatable tasks, and more by getting more opportunities to exercise judgement. And the more “at bats” they get, they can outpace more expensive older employees who are resting on years of service rather than the speed with which they can adapt and turn that experience into the expertise needed now.

The best theoretical explanation for why this model works may come from a fighter pilot, not a management consultant. In his 1987 briefing, Organic Design for Command and Control, John Boyd argued that complex, rapidly changing environments cannot be run through top-down bureaucratic control. They require an organic network of semiautonomous units operating with a high degree of local initiative.

In addition to being a maverick who hounded Pentagon leaders and worried giant defense contractors, Boyd is best known for the OODA loop, that is observe, orient, decide, act. If that process seems obvious, it’s because it is. His insight was not that the steps happen in sequence. It was that the side that cycles through them at a faster tempo forces its opponent to keep re-observing and re-orienting until the opponent loses coherence. And you don’t have to increase the speed of all elements, a subset can do, and amplify the others.

Apply that to an agency. When a small team has to report upward, wait for a decision and then receive instructions, the decision cycle slows. A journalist's interest shifts, a competitor announces, a news event opens a window. By the time the approval chain responds, the moment has changed. A small unit led by someone with deep domain knowledge can observe the change and act on it almost immediately.

Boyd also borrowed the idea of entropy to describe organizational friction. Information distorts as it travels up and down a hierarchy. If events move faster than the command structure can process them, headquarters ends up solving yesterday's problem while reality has moved on.

Decentralized units minimize that delay, which is much of what a boutique offers a client.

Boyd did not argue for chaos. He identified three conditions, drawn more than ironically from German military doctrine, that make decentralization work. Einheit is mutual trust and a common outlook, built through shared experience, so that leadership trusts the team to form a sound local plan and the team trusts that leadership's objectives are justified.

Fingerspitzengefühl, or fingertip feeling, is the intuitive understanding of a situation that lets an experienced person act without hesitation. Schwerpunkt is the main effort, leadership supplies a clear intent, and the unit decides how to achieve it.

And to address the obvious question, the words are German. But given World War II and the holocaust’s very appropriate place in cultural discourse, it’s worth noting that American battlefield tactics and democratic culture won against German approaches during that period when American troops did a better job taking these ideas and running with them and German command and control stepped back from them on the battlefield and off.

In the business world, those three ideas read like a description of a well-run boutique. The client supplies the objective. A small group of experienced practitioners who trust one another and the client understands the terrain and chooses the method. No one has to wait for a layer of management to approve a pitch to the right reporter.

Boyd even redefined the vocabulary. In an effective network, command means leadership, direction and shaping, and it should be highly visible. Control means assessment and verification, and it should stay largely invisible so that it does not interfere with local initiative. Good client counsel works the same way, clear direction up front, measured results afterward, and little process in between.

Boyd's conclusion was that agility and comfort with uncertainty outperform mass and centralized power. His work helped shape Marine Corps maneuver warfare doctrine and is widely cited in agile product development. Public relations and other over-consolidated industries now facing a version of the same lesson.

Boyd's ideas raise an uncomfortable question about the word at the center of this argument. “Boutique” suggests something small, refined and a little precious. It describes size and style. It does not describe how the work gets done.

A better description of the best small communications teams today may be Mission Command Units, compact groups of people with agency who obtain a clear objective, understand the terrain and are trusted to decide how to achieve the result. This year, the 250th anniversary of the American Revolution, offers an unusually apt example.

Colonel John Glover's Marblehead Regiment, the 14th Continental Regiment, is recognized by modern military historians as a premier historical example of a robust Mission Command Unit, more than two centuries before Boyd articulated the theory. It was composed largely of sailors, fishermen and merchants from coastal Massachusetts and beyond.

It benefited from the bonuses diversity gives with black, white, Native American, Jewish, and Spanish troops. They were not drilled in a conventional barracks but were highly organized despite that. They were raised in the multi-cultural proving ground and team sport of maritime survival and enterprise. That is Einheit in its ironically purest form. Washington got over his own different experiences in Virginia, and trusted Glover, and Glover's men trusted one another's skills, which let them operate as a single organism without a steady stream of orders.

In August 1776, with the Continental Army trapped in Brooklyn, Washington's intent was to evacuate roughly 9,000 troops across the East River in secrecy. There was no playbook. In darkness, fog and wind, the Marbleheaders wrapped their oars in cloth, used silent hand signals to coordinate boat traffic and moved the army under the noses of British warships. Washington set the objective. The regiment worked out the how.

On Christmas night 1776, a blizzard and ice floes threatened to stall the crossing of the Delaware. The Marbleheaders read the river and moved some 2,400 men, horses and heavy artillery across, preserving the surprise that made the victory at Trenton possible. In effect, they sped up Washington's OODA loop when conditions were working against him.

Earlier at Pell's Point that October, the intent was to delay the advancing British so the main force could retreat. Glover used about 500 men to stage a decentralized delaying action, placing small groups behind stone walls and shifting positions as the terrain demanded. That is the Schwerpunkt, a clear main effort, with local judgment about how to serve it.

The Marblehead Regiment was built on shared capability, local resourcefulness and unified intent. Its example is not mainly about being small. It is about trust, expertise and autonomy applied to a clearly defined mission, that allowed them to be small, and became an example of smallness at scale.

That is the standard a modern communications team should be held to. A firm does not become a Mission Command Unit by describing itself as boutique. It earns the description when the client sets the objective, experienced practitioners who trust one another decide how to reach it, and the result can be measured. By that test, some boutiques qualify and some large agencies do not. The label that matters is not how many people are in the room, but how much judgment they are trusted to exercise.

There is another development pushing PR in this direction. It has become harder to earn journalists' attention.

According to Muck Rack's 2025 State of PR report, 72 percent of practitioners cited low journalist response rates as a challenge and 62 percent reported shrinking media lists in the beats relevant to their clients. Only 2 percent said earning media coverage had failed to become harder.

At the same time, PR remains deeply embedded in journalism. Muck Rack found in 2025 that 84 percent of journalists said at least some of their stories originate with PR pitches. Yet 86 percent disregard pitches unrelated to their beat. In its 2026 survey, the figure saying at least some of their work started with a PR pitch rose to 86 percent.

PR still has enormous access to the front end of the journalistic process. That access becomes valuable when practitioners understand exactly whom they are approaching, what that journalist covers, why the story matters now and what evidence makes it worth pursuing.

Volume offers diminishing returns in that world. Specificity becomes economically valuable.

Where journalism goes in this model is not clear yet. Muck Rack found in 2025 that 34 percent of journalists independently publish news, commentary or other material outside a traditional newsroom. Among these creator journalists, roughly half have been self-publishing for more than five years. Eighty-two percent said at least some of their content begins with PR pitches, while 72 percent said most of the pitches they receive are irrelevant.

Influence now moves through major newspapers, trade publications, podcasts, LinkedIn, newsletters, independent journalists, conferences and communities organized around relatively narrow areas of expertise. A communications firm has to understand how those pieces interact around the audience its client actually needs to reach.

The number of possible outlets has exploded while the number of genuinely useful outlets for a particular story can still be quite small. That rewards people who can read the tarrain, and know their way around maps.

In the 2024 B2B Thought Leadership Impact Report from Edelman and LinkedIn, nearly 3,500 management-level professionals were surveyed across seven countries. Seventy-three percent of B2B decision-makers said an organization's thought leadership provided a more trustworthy basis for evaluating its capabilities than conventional marketing materials and product sheets. Ninety percent said they were moderately or very likely to become more receptive to outreach from a company that consistently produces high-quality thought leadership.

The newer environment makes that even more pronounced. Reporting on Edelman's 2026 research says 82 percent of buyers believe thought leadership is becoming more valuable, while 89 percent say high-quality thought leadership makes a company feel like a long-term partner worth choosing. Thought leadership remains highly valuable for B2B buyers

A CEO explaining an emerging market in Bloomberg, a founder writing intelligently on LinkedIn, a portfolio executive speaking at an industry conference and a company appearing accurately in AI-mediated discovery all contribute to the body of evidence buyers encounter before somebody schedules a sales call.

For a boutique firm, that creates an opportunity to work on the client's actual commercial problem. Who needs to know us? What should they know us for? Whose credibility matters to them? What evidence will persuade them? Where do those people get information? What measurable result are we trying to produce?

Boutique law firms became formidable because their clients could buy concentrated expertise. A company facing a complex patent case does not choose counsel by asking which firm can put the largest number of lawyers on the billing sheet. It looks for the lawyers it wants handling the case.

The latest data suggests the market is rewarding that instinct. In the 2026 Am Law 100, which reflects 2025 results, the most productive firms were not the largest. Wachtell, Lipton, Rosen & Katz, with 272 lawyers, led the ranking at $5.085 million in revenue per lawyer. Susman Godfrey, with just 226 lawyers, came second at $2.876 million. Kirkland & Ellis, with 4,145 lawyers and more than $10.5 billion in revenue, generated $2.547 million per lawyer.

Profits tell the same story. Wachtell became the first firm in the ranking’s history to pass $12 million in profits per equity partner, up 34.5 percent, while the Am Law 100 average rose 14 percent, according to LawFuel. Wachtell does this with a single office, a lean team structure and no large pool of junior leverage. And it is not alone in showing that smaller, elite, tightly focused teams can outperform much larger competitors. And those are still fairly large law firms. Many even smaller and more nimble firms have been underway.

Grove Law LLP offers a small-scale example of the model. The firm marked its first anniversary in mid-2026, and it was built by founding partners who are still the ones doing the work for clients, in complex litigation, internal and government investigations and regulatory advising. Its stated mission is “optimal client service through authentic lawyering, rooted in integrity,” and it describes its approach as looking at the whole system around a legal problem, including operations, people, economics and reputation, rather than the narrow matter alone. That is a boutique defined less by its headcount than by how its experienced lawyers are trusted to exercise judgment and shape the result.

Meanwhile, the supply of such teams is growing. JDJournal reports a rising number of senior Big Law partners leaving to launch boutiques in high-stakes litigation, white-collar defense and regulatory work, citing lower overhead, direct partner involvement and flexible pricing as advantages. Large firms still dominate on total revenue, and some of the strongest evidence above comes from lean firms inside the Am Law 100 itself. But the pattern is consistent, concentrated expertise, not headcount, increasingly drives the economics.

Communications buyers are increasingly able to make the same calculation.

Who will actually work on my company? Have they dealt with this market before or alternatively can they connect dots others who are overly siloed can’t? Do they understand the journalists? Can they challenge our positioning? Can they sit with the CEO? Can they turn technical expertise into an argument people outside the company understand? Can they tell us that our proposed announcement isn't news on its own but help us develop the context that raises it to a new level? Can they show what happened because of the work?

That final question deserves particular attention. Muck Rack found that 67 percent of PR professionals say producing measurable results is the best way to increase the perceived value of PR.

Boutiques should be easier to hold accountable because their engagements can be built around defined results rather than the utilization of a large team. Coverage in a targeted publication, engagement with a defined group of journalists, increased executive visibility, movement in search authority, speaking opportunities, inbound conversations and introductions to prospective customers can all be counted.

The world's largest communications firms exist for good reasons. You’ll note that I’m citing surveys that come from large communications firm whose name you’re more likely to recognize. PRovoke Media Global Top 250 PR Agency Ranking 2025 shows businesses such as Edelman, Burson, Weber Shandwick, FleishmanHillard and Real Chemistry operating at enormous scale, with several generating hundreds of millions of dollars in annual fee income. A multinational company coordinating dozens of markets, managing large consumer campaigns or supporting communications across many business units may genuinely need that infrastructure.

That being said, a founder or intrepreneur navigating a new category, a private-equity firm trying to become known for a specific investment thesis, a healthcare company introducing an unfamiliar technology that is moving beyond incremental improvements, a manufacturer who persists despite market cycles but misses opportunities because the broader market doesn’t understand why, or an executive trying to build authority around a changing market often needs a small number of expert people with the autonomy and agency to work out that problem every day until it’s solved, and do it on an accelerated basis.

Technology is making that model more within reach, if used effectively. Changes in journalism are making expertise more valuable. Tougher agency economics are forcing clients to scrutinize what they are buying. AI is lowering the cost of production while increasing the premium on original thinking and credible experience.

Law has already shown what can happen when experienced specialists leave structures built around scale and organize themselves around expertise, client access and consequential problems. Public relations now has many of the same ingredients.

Boyd would recognize the pattern, and so might John Glover. More are recognizing it now.
