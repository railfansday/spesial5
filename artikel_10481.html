Why Nvidia brought Jensen Huang back as CEO despite his scandals

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

It took just 11 days for Nvidia to reverse course on Jensen Huang, its totemic CEO, who is returning to his position as president and CEO, as well as a member of the board.

The decision also appears to have received the White House’s tacit approval, according to Nvidia’s surprise announcement of Huang’s return. That’s despite an ongoing independent investigation into whether Huang was targeted by Chinese intelligence through a woman with whom he has acknowledged having an affair. And the controversy extends beyond that relationship: Huang has also faced allegations of at least one, and possibly two, other affairs with subordinates. During his brief absence from the company, he also faced criticism over his reportedly toxic management style.

“I am honored to return to NVIDIA and lead one of the most consequential technology companies in the world at the beginning of a new industrial revolution,” Huang said in a statement.

His return to power echoes Sam Altman’s brief sojourn outside OpenAI—an episode that marked its three-year anniversary last week—and highlights just how important individual leaders have become to the success of companies like Nvidia and OpenAI.

“It’s a stunning return,” says Kristy Loke, a research fellow at MATS studying China’s place in the broader AI world—and Nvidia’s role in the global geopolitics of AI. “It doesn’t necessarily mark the end of the chip debates in D.C., but it speaks to the centrality of Jensen as the chief strategist for Nvidia and as the force that’s holding the many pieces of the AI ecosystem together.”

That centrality was evident in the groundswell of support for Huang’s reinstatement across Silicon Valley over the past week. A veritable who’s who of tech executives spoke up for him. Elon Musk was among those calling for Huang’s return on November 14, warning that many of Nvidia’s most important supply-chain deals rested on personal relationships with Huang rather than written contracts. Uber co-founder Travis Kalanick also backed him, arguing that his mistakes did not warrant removing him from the company he built. By November 16, a petition calling for Huang’s reinstatement had attracted more than 180,000 signatures, including those of Epic Games CEO Tim Sweeney, Replit CEO Amjad Masad, and more than 8,800 people identifying themselves as Nvidia employees.

Nvidia’s board appears to have reached a similar conclusion about Huang’s integral role in running the company, owing in large part to his hands-on management style. “This is a board that reversed itself because the alternative, a founder-less Nvidia at the start of the Rubin cycle with a CEO search running into next year, was the bigger risk to shareholders,” writes Dan Ives, principal analyst at Yorkville Ives & Co.

Nvidia is partway through launching its Rubin chip platform, with updates due later this year—a process Huang will likely want to oversee closely, given that the platform has already generated $20 billion in its first quarter of production. “It chose to pair the reversal with the governance architecture investors will want to see around him,” Ives adds.

Ives has attempted to put a price on Huang’s importance. In his estimation, Nvidia is worth $300 a share with Huang back in charge and its governance troubles resolved, versus $225 without him—a difference of a third. Even so, he has maintained his formal price target of $225 while the investigation continues.

Investors also appear confident in Huang’s return, with Nvidia shares surging nearly 30% in the first few minutes of trading following the announcement.

Huang’s 11 days in the wilderness were more than twice as long as Altman’s absence from OpenAI, but his grip on Nvidia appears no less significant. As Fast Company previously reported, the skills and responsibilities any permanent replacement would need to master make for a daunting list. They include balancing the competing interests of the White House and Beijing while managing a complex web of informal relationships with Taiwanese suppliers—relationships that have helped cement Nvidia’s seemingly unassailable lead in the AI chip race.

It’s Huang’s relationship with Washington that may have proved most important, reckons MATS’ Loke. “Trump’s presidency in his second term is very much an AI presidency,” she explains. “The same goes for the American stock market. Huang is the key node, through circular financing, support for the open model ecosystem, and also being a key supplier of AI hardware.”

“He is, it seems in hindsight to some,” Loke adds, “in fact irreplaceable.”
