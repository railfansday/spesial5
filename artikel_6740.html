What Does Transgender Mean? Everything You Need to Know, According to Experts

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

From gender identity to transition, here's what the term actually means.

The word "transgender" gets used a lot these days, but that doesn't necessarily indicate that everyone understands what being transgender actually means. Between headlines, social media debates, and ongoing conversations about gender identity, it's easy to feel like you're missing a piece of the puzzle or even worry about using the wrong terminology altogether. Fortunately, understanding what it means to be transgender doesn't have to be as complicated as the internet sometimes makes it seem.

"Being transgender means your gender identity doesn't align with the one you were assigned at birth. That's the whole heart of it," says licensed marriage and family therapist Adrienne Michelle, LMFT. "It doesn't tell you anything about how someone looks, what they've explored medically, or how far along they are in any journey. It's simply about your authentic sense of self being different from what you were told you were at the start."

Ultimately, gender is deeply personal, and there's no one "right" way to be transgender. Whether you're talking with a friend, supporting a loved one, or simply trying to better understand the world around you, having a basic understanding of transgender identity can make the conversation feel a whole lot less intimidating and a lot more inclusive.

The term "transgender" is an identity label that refers to people whose gender identity doesn’t match the sex they were assigned when they were born, explains therapist and LGBTQ+ educator Zoe Stoller. You'll also often hear transgender shortened to simply trans. The prefix trans- means "across" or "on the other side of," reflecting that a person's gender identity differs from the sex they were assigned at birth.

"Gender is expansive, and 'trans' is the umbrella term that often holds all of that expansiveness," says Michelle. "It isn't a single identity; it's a beautifully wide category that makes room for so many people."

The transgender umbrella includes trans men, trans women, and many nonbinary identities, though not everyone whose gender identity differs from the sex they were assigned at birth identifies as transgender. And that's completely okay. The right label is the one that feels right to the person using it.

Although they're often used interchangeably, sex and gender aren't the same thing. Sex refers to the biological characteristics someone is labeled or assigned at birth, while gender refers to a person's internal sense of who they are. You may also come across the terms AFAB/AMAB (assigned female at birth/assigned male at birth), which describe the sex someone was assigned when they were born, not their gender identity. And while babies are often labeled male or female at birth, sex itself isn't always binary, as intersex people exist too.

Once you understand that distinction, a lot of the terminology starts to make more sense. Terms like nonbinary, genderfluid, genderqueer, and gender nonconforming all describe distinct experiences or expressions of gender, but they aren't interchangeable, and they don't all automatically mean someone is transgender. (P.S. If you're looking for a deeper breakdown of those identities, check out our complete gender identity glossary.)

"Gender nonconforming is about not feeling aligned with the normative, performative ways gender is 'supposed' to show up—the expectations around how a man or woman should look, dress, or move through the world," says Michelle. "It's about expression, not identity. You can be gender nonconforming and cisgender, and you can be gender nonconforming and trans. The two sometimes overlap, but they're answering different questions: transgender is about who you are, gender nonconforming is about how you show up."

As Stoller explains, the transgender umbrella includes both binary identities (such as trans men and trans women) and many nonbinary identities, though not everyone whose gender differs from the sex they were assigned at birth identifies as transgender. Like every other aspect of identity, everyone's relationship with gender is unique.

First things first: Despite the headline right above this, there's no one way to "look" transgender.

As Stoller explains, being transgender isn't defined by surgeries, medical procedures, or any other external milestone. The only thing someone needs to "count" as transgender is feeling that the label accurately reflects who they are. For many people, that identity is deeply affirming regardless of whether they ever choose to transition.

Transitioning simply refers to the process of aligning someone's life more closely with their gender identity. "It’s important to note that transition isn't a checklist," says Michelle. "Some people explore hormones or surgery, some focus on legal or social changes, some pursue none of it, and every one of those paths is whole and valid."

Some people choose to align their outward appearance with their gender identity because it's an affirming—and sometimes life-saving—way to feel seen as their authentic selves, Stoller says. Others don't transition at all or may be unable to do so for personal, financial, or safety reasons. Regardless, their gender identity is no less valid.

Now that you know what transgender means, it's worth talking about how to actually use the term. According to Michelle, "transgender" is an adjective, not a noun. "You'd say 'a transgender person' or 'transgender people,' never 'a transgender' or 'the transgenders,'" she explains. "It works just like any other descriptive word; you wouldn't call someone 'a tall,' so the same care applies here."

As Stoller explains, it's also important not to assume someone identifies as transgender unless they've shared that information with you. Even if you think someone's gender identity technically falls under the transgender umbrella, it's ultimately up to them to decide which labels feel the most accurate and affirming. When in doubt, use the language someone uses to describe themselves.

One of the most common terminology mix-ups is the difference between a trans man and a trans woman. "A trans man is someone who was assigned female at birth and now identifies and lives as a man. A trans woman was assigned male at birth and identifies and lives as a woman," explains Michelle.

It's an important distinction because these terms describe who someone is, not who they used to be. In other words, trans men are men, and trans women are women. The word trans simply provides additional context about their gender journey—it doesn't change their gender.

As Stoller notes, some trans men and trans women identify fully within the traditional gender binary, while others may also feel connected to nonbinary experiences. Like every other aspect of gender identity, there's no one "right" way to experience or describe being transgender.

The short answer? Use the pronouns someone asks you to use. It's really that simple.

As Stoller explains, people—whether they're transgender or cisgender—can use whatever pronouns feel the most affirming to them, including multiple sets of pronouns or neopronouns. Although people often associate certain pronouns with certain genders, pronouns don't always tell you someone's gender identity.

If you aren't sure which pronouns someone uses, it's okay to politely ask. And if you accidentally get it wrong, apologize, correct yourself, and move on.

Another common misconception is that being transgender somehow determines who someone is attracted to. It doesn't.

Gender identity and sexual orientation are two completely different things (and both are different from romantic orientation), Michelle explains. Gender identity describes who someone is, while sexual orientation describes who they're attracted to. That means transgender people can be straight, gay, lesbian, bisexual, pansexual, asexual, or identify with any other sexual orientation—just like cisgender people.

Like gender identity, sexuality is deeply personal. Labels can be helpful, Stoller says, but they don't always capture the full picture of someone's experience, so it's always best to use the language someone chooses for themselves.

Being a good LGBTQ ally doesn't require having all the answers. It starts with listening, staying open to learning, and treating transgender people with the same respect you'd want someone to show you.

Use the name and pronouns someone asks you to use, and don't make assumptions based on their appearance or gender identity. As Michelle explains, a trans man might use he/him pronouns, a trans woman might use she/her, and a nonbinary person might use they/them—or another set of pronouns that feels most affirming to them. If you aren't sure, it's okay to politely ask.

You may also come across the word transsexual, particularly in older books, articles, or medical literature. While some transgender people have reclaimed the term for themselves, Stoller notes that it's generally considered more outdated and medicalized than transgender, and many people find it offensive. Unless someone has specifically told you that's the label they use, it's best to avoid it.

Above all, remember that every transgender person's experience is different. Listening, respecting the language people use to describe themselves, and remaining open to learning will go a long way.

If you've made it this far because you're wondering whether the word transgender might describe you, know that you don't have to have everything figured out today. Gender identity isn't a quiz you pass or fail, and there's no deadline for understanding yourself.

As Stoller explains, there's no single way to experience being transgender because every person's relationship with gender is different. Some people transition socially, medically, or legally, while others never transition at all. None of those experiences make someone's identity any more or less real.

If you'd like support, consider talking with an LGBTQIA+-affirming therapist, connecting with trusted friends or loved ones, or exploring resources from organizations like The Trevor Project can be a good place to start. You don't have to navigate questions about your identity alone.

As Michelle puts it, "Gender is expansive, full stop." The most important thing isn't fitting neatly into someone else's definition; it's giving yourself the space to discover what feels authentic to you.
