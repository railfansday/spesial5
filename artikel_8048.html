Iran Executed a Mountaineer for Alleged Violence in Anti-Government Protests. Hundreds More Face the Same Fate.

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Ali Sina Mostafavi (Photo: Courtesy of Iran Human Rights)

Ali Sina Mostafavi dreamed of climbing Everest. In 2019, he trekked to its Base Camp. Under a photo of him pointing to the summit, he wrote on Instagram in English: “Top of the world. I promise to come back to you.”

Mostafavi never fulfilled his promise. On October 14, he was executed by the Iranian authorities in Kerman Central Prison.

The 38-year-old is one of at least 39 people to have been executed in connection with Iran’s anti-government protests last winter, and one of six athletes among them.

Dr. Zohreh Abdollahkhani, an ice climber and co-founder of the Norway-based Human Rights for Sport, told Climbing, “From a human rights perspective, every single aspect of this case is an absolute violation. The state has not made any credible, independently verifiable evidence public.”

Born in 1987 in Bam County, southeastern Iran, Mostafavi was a professional mountaineer and a member of the Speeco mountaineering club in his home city of Kerman. He called mountains ‘the love of his life,’ according to Abdollahkhani.

Mostafavi climbed two of Iran’s highest peaks, Damavand (5,610m/18,410ft) and Alam-Kuh (4,850m/15,910ft). In 2016, he ascended Mount Kazbek (5,054m/16,581ft) in Georgia.

In 2019, Mostafavi traveled to Nepal and summited Island Peak (6,189m/20,305ft) and trekked to Everest Base Camp. In 2022, he attempted Ama Dablam (6,812m/22,349ft), reaching 6,600m/21,654ft.

Mostafavi was married and had a 13-year-old daughter, Respina. Alongside his climbing career, he was an accomplished musician who played the tar, a traditional Persian lute. Friends spoke of his sensitive, artistic spirit, and his deep love for mountains, music, and animals—notably, his beloved cat and dog.

On his Instagram account, @everest_coldhell, his bio reads: ‘Mount Explorer. Love the nature. Musician.’

In December 2025, Iran’s economic crisis sparked street protests that developed into nationwide demonstrations against the Islamic Republic’s repressive regime, which enforces compulsory hijab laws restricting women’s autonomy, conducts violent crackdowns on protests, issues arbitrary arrests, and employs the use of torture.

In an attempt to suppress dissent, authorities used lethal force, killing thousands and arresting tens of thousands of protesters. The human rights organization Amnesty International called this “the deadliest period of repression by the Iranian authorities” in its decades of monitoring the country.

Following the assassination of Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei by US-Israeli airstrikes on February 28, Iran’s government intensified repressive crackdowns on dissent under the pretext of “wartime conditions.”

By October, at least 106 people arrested during the January protests had received death sentences, according to documentation by human rights organization Hengaw.

Iran carries out the world’s second-highest number of executions after China. In 2025, its government executed at least 2,159 people, the highest figure recorded by Amnesty International since 1981.

On Instagram, Mostafavi expressed support for Iran’s former Crown Prince Reza Pahlavi, an exiled dissident who calls for a secular, democratic, and liberal Iran, describing him as “the only saviour of the homeland.” Two sources claim that Mostafavi’s family were set to move abroad before the 2022 ‘Woman, Life, Freedom’ protests began, but he ultimately chose to remain to fight for Iran’s future.

One obituary claims that he had been arrested twice in recent years for political activism and had spent time in Kerman Prison. This January, the mountaineer joined protests alongside millions of Iranians.

IranWire, an independent pro-democracy outlet, reported that Mostafavi was arrested three months later on April 25 alongside his wife, his sister, and his sister’s fiancé, while his mother’s home was also raided.

Mostafavi was reportedly beaten during his arrest and was forced to sign confession papers to release his family members. It is not known whether he had access to an independent lawyer, a right frequently denied to defendants in political and national security cases in Iran.

The Iranian judiciary’s account of the case was published by state-aligned media outlets including Mizan News Agency, Tasnim News Agency, the IRIB, and Tabnak. The allegations have not been independently verified.

The Judiciary of Iran claimed that Mostafavi had participated in the widespread anti-government protests in January, and that he was a member of the “Immortal Guards,” an armed wing of exiled dissident Reza Pahlavi’s group.

The judiciary also alleged that Mostafavi had shot and injured two civilians, fired at security forces, and possessed firearms, ammunition, and Molotov cocktails. It further accused him of communicating with foreign media and sharing footage of missile strike locations during the US-Iran war.

Mostafavi was convicted of the capital offense of moharebeh, or “enmity against God,” alongside several charges relating to alleged violence, weapons possession, and communication with hostile media. The evidence against him has not been made public.

The judiciary reported that the fiancé of Mostafavi’s sister received a prison sentence of 12 years and seven months for alleged cooperation, while he, his wife, and his sister were also sentenced to seven months in prison for posting content deemed as propaganda against the regime.

An appeal against Mostafavi’s death sentence was rejected. IranWire reported that he was transferred to solitary confinement the night before his execution and that family members were summoned. The judiciary announced that he was hanged at dawn in Kerman Central Prison on October 14. According to IranWire, Mostafavi asked the prison guards to pass along a message to the judge: “I did not wage war against God, nor did I commit moharebeh that you should charge me with it; my fight was with you.”

That evening, state-aligned outlet Tabnak published footage purporting to show Mostafavi’s arrest, weapons allegedly found in his possession, and a confession to a planned attack on Kerman Airport. IranWire reported that the confession appeared to have been made under duress and spliced together with unverified footage. Amnesty International has also identified signs of forced confessions in 18 videos of Iranian prisoners arrested between February and May 2026, during the same period as Mostafavi’s arrest.

Shortly after his death, a video of Mostafavi’s young daughter crying on her father’s grave, accompanied by their cat, circulated on social media.

Human rights groups have voiced concerns about Mostafavi’s case in the context of widespread forced confessions and sham trials lacking due process in Iran.

“The complete absence of forensic evidence or transparent court records confirms that his conviction rested entirely on forced confessions extracted under coercion rather than any objective proof,” Abdollahkhani said.

She explained that the arrest of Mostafavi’s wife, his sister, and his sister’s fiancé fits an established pattern of authorities using family members as leverage.

“The Islamic Republic routinely arrests close relatives and uses threats of torture or sexual violence to force athletes into making false confessions in a desperate bid to save their loved ones,” she said.

According to Human Rights for Sport, at least eight other mountaineers’ lives are at risk.

Maryam Hadavand Siri, a 42-year-old mother of two, led the Mountaineering Board of Pakdasht County in Tehran Province. She was arrested during the January protests, transferred to Evin Prison, and sentenced to death in April, without access to an independent lawyer. Following an appeal, her case was referred to the Supreme Court for review.

Social media activist and mountaineer Fatemeh Jami was arrested at her home on June 30 by Cyber Police officers. She was transferred to Gorgan Prison, where she remains detained without access to a lawyer or medical assistance.

Tara (Shirin) Masoudi, a member of the Razhan Mountaineering Group in Mahabad, was arrested on August 19. She was transferred to a detention centre and later released on bail. She is awaiting a final verdict.

Mohammad Hakimpour, Mostafa Ghaderi, Mohammad Saleh Hosseini, Hossein Abdollahi, and Khaled Rahimi—all members of climbing clubs in Saqqez—were arrested on October 8. According to Human Rights for Sport, they were subjected to physical abuse in detention and have been denied access to legal counsel, family visits, and telephone calls.

Abdollahkhani urges climbers to raise awareness of Mostafavi’s execution and the status of the eight other mountaineers, and to contact national federations, sports governing bodies, the International Climbing and Mountaineering Federation (UIAA), and the International Olympic Committee (IOC).

“How is it possible that a member of the climbing community is at risk of execution on alleged political charges, while the global climbing world stays silent?” she asked.

On October 30, Human Rights for Sport published an open letter to the UIAA executive board and athlete commission. In the letter, Abdollahkhani asks the UIAA to appeal directly to Iran’s national mountaineering federation, along with its Ministry of Sport and National Olympic Committee, to stop further executions and advocate for the release of imprisoned climbers.
