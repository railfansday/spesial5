Majority of people who have been in US prisons were also victims of violent crime, study finds

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Report based on survey of 20,000 Americans says 84% of people who are incarcerated were assaulted, shot or stabbed

The majority of people who have spent time in a US prison or jail have, at one point in their lives, been a victim of a violent crime, according to a new study. Researchers with Just Safe, a victims’ advocacy non-profit, found that 84% of people who had been incarcerated reported being assaulted, shot or stabbed before their first time in jail or prison.

The report is based on a nationally representative survey of 20,000 Americans. Of those who have been incarcerated, half of them had been hurt by a caregiver as a child and one-third were victims of sexual or intimate partner violence.

Lenore Anderson, president of the Oakland, California-based Just Safe, said this type of research can push the public and elected officials to reframe the politics of crime. Instead of simply focusing on punishment, the findings show the necessity of bolstering trauma recovery resources.

“It’s meant to share this very different viewpoint that most people don’t think about: the collateral consequences of victimization,” she said. “We can focus all of our time on how long the sentences are and how challenging the prison environment should be or we can flip the script to ask: ‘What happened and did people get help?’”

The report also lays out the different ways people who have been harmed and don’t get trauma recovery services cope with the lingering pain. Instead, some people turn to drugs and alcohol, while others start hurting people in the same ways they had been hurt. Both of these paths can lead people to cycle in and out of the justice system and put them at higher risk of violence in the future, the report said.

“Hurt people hurt people,” Anderson said. “This data is also screaming out that hurt people hurt themselves first. They’re showing up in our justice system for things related to substance abuse.”

These findings are no surprise to Jemaine Hunter. He grew up in Fresno, California, where as a child and young teen he faced violence from some of his family members. By the time he was 13, he would get into fights with young men from his neighborhood.

“I was probably a victim in every situation possible. Things have been stolen from me, I got into fights that I didn’t win,” he said. “But it wasn’t my fault that things happened to me.”

In addition to being harmed at home, Hunter watched people being shot and killed when conflicts went sideways. So he decided that he would do what he could to avoid the same fate.

“Hearing those stories made me say, ‘I don’t wanna be a victim.’ It was the homeboys being killed that made me feel like I needed to keep a gun and be ready to use it all the time,” Hunter said.

At 24, after being in and out of juvenile detention as a teen, he was convicted of attempted murder and sentenced to 34 years to life in prison. He spent 23 years in California prisons, including San Quentin in northern California, before being released in December 2025. He now works as a counselor for Sunset Youth services, a San Francisco-based non-profit that supports young people who have been repeatedly arrested or are on probation.

While in San Quentin, Hunter co-founded Arms Down, a first-in-the-nation support group for people convicted of gun offenses. Hunter heard stories of men who were jumped and abused, then joined a gang and carried firearms to feel safe. Another commonality between him and the men he served time with was a lack of protection from violence, a space to help process what happened to them and mentorship that could steer them away from repeating the same violence they faced.

“As a kid I needed that superhero and someone who would stand between me and that fear. Someone to say: ‘Hey man, back up. Don’t touch him’ and bring that ear and shoulder to lean on,” Hunter said.

The report’s authors lay out recommendations that can help turn victims of violence away from crime. Among them are training first responders, nurses and teachers on how to refer people to assistance services, and for municipalities to invest more money in trauma recovery while removing bureaucratic barriers to victims’ compensation.

“If we’re committed to public safety we have to ask what stops the cycle and that looks different from what our current criminal justice system does,” Anderson said. “It means ensuring that what happened never happens again through accountability measures that allow people to address underlying drivers.”

To help prevent future violence, Hunter has been teaching the Arms Down curriculum to youth in San Francisco who carry guns. Many have been jumped before or lost a friend to violence. Hunter said he is working to give them what he needed when he was a teenager and he hopes to see the model that Sunset Youth Services has developed be duplicated in cities across the country.

“What’s going on in San Francisco is beautiful and a blessing for these young men and women who are going through trauma,” Hunter said. “These community-based organizations deserve a round of applause and they’re doing something that the country can mimic.”
