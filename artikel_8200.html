The Horrific-Smelling Piece of Gear No Hiker Needs to Carry in 2026

There are smells I’ve experienced that still give me nightmares: The box of mail-order lobsters UPS once delivered to my parents two weeks late. The freezer filled with fish and ground beef that I had to clean out after someone unplugged it and left it outside in the summer sun. And, sliding into third place after those, the time that my hexamine fuel tablets got loose in my pack.

I was competing in the Grand Traverse, a roughly 40-mile ski mountaineering race from Crested Butte to Aspen, Colorado. Because of the ruggedness of the terrain, the organizers required that each team carry a full set of survival gear, including a stove. In an attempt to keep our kit as light as possible, my partner and I packed a tiny solid fuel burner and a few fuel tablets for a total of about 5 ounces—lighter than a 100-gram fuel canister. I double-bagged them and stuffed them in my beanie, which I hoped would stop them from jostling around in my pack.

If you’ve never used hexamine fuel tablets, the first thing to know about them is that they smell bad. And not bad in the way you’d expect: Instead of the petroleum twang of white gas or the skunky smell added to propane-butane mixes, the tablets have a distinct marine odor. Users have compared it to rotten shellfish or dead carp.

Halfway through the race, I started to notice hints of that smell when I opened my pack. But it wasn’t until after we crossed the finish line that I realized what had happened: At some point during the race, one of the bags had jostled open. The beanie I had packed it in was the main casualty; I put it on and gagged. It took multiple cycles in the washing machine and about a month of airing out before it stopped smelling like a Red Lobster during a power outage.

You, too, can have this experience, and you don’t need to spend much: Esbit stoves, the best-known brand of solid-fuel burners, can be had for as little as $12, fuel included. They’re cheap, simple, and at least notionally light. But whether you’re considering one for your ultralight kit or emergency winter use, I would buy literally anything else.

Solid-fuel stoves are old tech: The first hit the market in 1936, when Esbit, a German company, introduced hexamine fuel tablets and the compact burners designed to use them. Demand for them boomed during World War II, when Esbit’s burners became standard issue for the Wehrmacht; the Allies developed their own versions as well. Today, solid-fuel stoves still have more currency among European backpackers than American ones, who are less likely to have used them and, if they live somewhere where fire restrictions are common, often can’t, as land managers generally ban any stove without an on-off switch alongside campfires.

Fire hazard aside, there are concrete reasons why so few hikers nowadays are springing for solids. While the stoves are light, their fuel is not: It takes almost 2 standard tablets, or nearly an ounce, of hexamine fuel to bring a liter of water to a rolling boil, compared to about 0.3 ounces of fuel for a pressure-regulated canister stove like the Soto Windmaster. While someone using a canister stove does have to tote the weight of the metal container the fuel comes in, the difference in efficiency means that for any trip longer than a day or two, the solid-fuel stove’s advantage is questionable at best; at the very least, it means starting out with a heavier pack. They’re difficult, though not impossible, to light in the cold and glacially slow at melting snow, real disadvantages for an emergency stove that you may have to spark up with numb fingers. And then, of course, there’s that pungent smell.

If they’re inefficient and smell bad, why do solid-fuel stoves continue to hang on? I’ll give them this: They’re simple. Like anything with moving parts, a canister stove can break. Filters clog and piezo igniters burn out. There’s essentially no way to break a solid-fuel stove; step on it, and you can just bend it back into shape. Even when it comes to simplicity, though, I’m not convinced it’s the best choice: While they have their downsides, an alcohol burner is as cheap and light as a hexamine stove, smells better, and the fuel is easier to find. (Lest it seem like I’m trashing Esbit, I’ll note that they make a solid alcohol-burning model that doubles as a storage bottle for its fuel.)

I’m not a hater, or at least I try not to be: Every backpacker has their gear quirks, and if you’re an old-schooler who’s been using a solid-fuel stove since your scouting days, I think that’s great. But if you’re one of the newcomers on any given backpacking forum pondering whether you should buy that ultralight titanium solid-fuel burner, the answer is probably no. For about $5 and 12 grams more—that’s about the weight of a AAA battery—you could pick up a BRS-3000T, a canister stove that will give you far less grief. And as a bonus, it won’t make your pack smell like a mermaid with B.O.
