The Art of The Terrible Startup Name

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

When thinking of startup names, the usual question is how one identifies a great one.

But perhaps equally crucial is the reverse consideration: What makes for a truly awful startup name?

It’s a somewhat subjective exercise. Nonetheless, branding experts and occasional writers on the subject can agree on a few basic parameters. These include hard-sounding names, brands with negative connotations, and a certain ubiquitous suffix that could soon sound irrelevant.

Below, we look at each of these categories in more detail

One branding principle is that startups should avoid names that are unpleasant to pronounce. This includes words that sound too harsh or guttural, or are awkward in the native languages of potential users.

Unfortunately, it’s a principle often ignored.

For Athol Foden of naming consultancy Brighter Naming, the most prominent unpleasant brand name of recent quarters is Grok, the chatbot of Elon Musk’s SpaceXSI. Foden hates it so much he devoted an entire essay to what’s wrong with it.

“I still want to throw up when you say that name… and it is even worse in foreign languages,” he said in an interview. In his essay he called the brand a cross between “a suppressed swear word and a throat condition your doctor would prescribe antibiotics for.”

It’s not an anti-Musk stance. Foden is a fan of the entrepreneur’s Starlink as a name choice for the global satellite Internet service. It’s memorable and evocative in a positive way.

For other examples, we didn’t want to pick on current startups for their naming choices. So we looked instead to archives of startups past for other cases of harsh naming choices that didn’t pan out or are no longer operating under that brand.

Our shortlist included Quibi, a heavily funded short video startup with famous founders and a famously short shelf life. Others that have either folded or no longer operate under the original brand include Doostang, Gtrot, and Xobni (inbox spelled backwards, but not terribly clear how to pronounce.)

Another naming tactic to avoid is selecting a brand that evokes negative imagery or connotations.

“We always say: This is something you should be proud to wear on a hoodie,” said Bruno Benedini, co-founder of branding consultancy Taillight. If not, then reconsider.

Following this notion, one recently funded company name that rubbed both Benedini and your author the wrong way is Warhound, a startup focused on breeding dogs for roles in the military, law enforcement and emergency response that recently raised $200 million.

Sure, it’s memorable. But it sets an awfully aggressive tone, especially for a pet-pampering culture in which other startups promote offerings like dog spas, canine couture, and custom meals. While there’s a role for well-treated service dogs, it’s preferably far from any battlefield.

Or, as Benedini put it, “I’d be embarrassed to have Warhound on my hoodie.”

For other examples of less-than-stellar naming picks, we looked through the archives of startups past. One dud was Flud, which combines awkward spelling and imagery of a natural disaster. Boo.com, a defunct fashion site that was the iconic flops of the dot-com era also comes to mind, evoking fright more than the desire to shop. Another popular but questionable pick is Fire (sometimes alternately spelled Fyre), the chosen name of at least a dozen entries in the Crunchbase dataset.

It may still be in vogue to give your company a one-word name followed by AI. But don’t try to get a branding consultant to sign off on it.

Both Foden and Benedini advise against adding AI to the company name for multiple reasons. One is that it will likely sound dated going forward. Another criticism is that since virtually every startup incorporates AI to some extent, adopting a name with “AI” isn’t particularly meaningful.

If one does insist on an AI name, the minimum next step is to make sure it still works with those two letters on the end. Lots of companies — including Amazon (formerly Amazon.com) and Apple (initially Apple Computer) — have altered their names to keep up with the times and done just fine.

Despite all the advice on how to avoid an awful name, a part of me can’t help hoping more startups will just ignore it all. Then we could live in a world where startups sought funding with names like “Gruesome Grok AI” or “Festering Boil Labs.”

That said, there’s a reason startup naming is a serious endeavor, and bad choices often have equally bad consequences.
