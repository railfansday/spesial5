Rheinmetall reins in growth ambitions as Europe's rearmament boom hits hurdles

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Rheinmetall is adopting a more "cautious" approach after the German arms maker lost a major contract to build F126 warships for the country's military, in what would have been Rheinmetall's biggest order since World War II.

The tank and ammunition maker now expects sales of between 33 and 37 billion euros (between $36.2 billion and $40.6 billion) by 2030, down from a previous forecast of about 45 billion euros, it told investors at its Capital Markets Day on Friday.

That guidance incorporates a "more prudent approach" for its large vehicle systems and weapons and ammunition businesses.

CEO Armin Papperger said a 2-billion-euro reduction in the 2030 sales target for its vehicle systems business was a strategic decision to build in a "buffer" against potential project delays and supply chain disruptions, rather than a reflection of further lost contracts or weakening demand.

It follows a full-year 2026 guidance cut earlier this month, which sent shares down 16% as the company cited delays to military contracts and ammunition deliveries.

"The opportunity has not changed; the way we plan for it has," Rheinmetall said in a company presentation.

"We are becoming more cautious whilst retaining our agility in a dynamic market," it said, highlighting a "new normal level of conservatism and recalibrated ambition" in the mid-term.

Shares rose 4% in mid-morning trading amid a European defense sector firmly in the green.

Rheinmetall and the wider defense industry have benefitted massively from European governments' efforts to rearm amid the war in Ukraine and uncertain security guarantees from the U.S. As governments ramp up military spending to meet NATO's hiked spending targets, defense companies see swelling order books but also struggle to meet soaring demand.

While shares of Rheinmetall have averaged triple-digit gains between 2022 and 2025, they are down 49% year-to-date. Addressing capital markets specifically, Papperger said he understood the disappointment, but that the loss of the major F126 was "a political thing."

"The government made this decision, and the minister made that decision," he said.

Other headwinds for Rheinmetall in 2026 were an explosion at an ammunition factory in Murcia, Spain, in January, and delays to Germany's Arminius project, a major military procurement program for Boxer armored vehicles, which is now expected to be commissioned next year.

Papperger said delays were due to price negotiations with the government. "I could have the contract immediately if I would reduce the prices, he said.

"We are fighting hard to get this contract, and we are fighting hard, not only to get the contract, but to make it also profitable."

RBC Capital analysts called the lowered mid-term targets a "widely expected reset" with "a general dose of conservatism."

"[The] emphasis on digitalisation offerings and the broadness of the portfolio should also help address sentiment around Rheinmetall being an "old-tech" name in a "new-tech" paradigm," the analysts added.

Some investors are now questioning what role legacy defense contractors like Rheinmetall, BAE Systems, and Leonardo will play in an evolving military context, increasingly focused on technology, autonomous systems, and drones.

Papperger pushed back on the idea that drones will render traditional military equipment obsolete, arguing that militaries must still replace their aging fleets and maintain the ability to transport troops on the battlefield.

"Don't believe what a lot of people [are] telling you in the narrative 'we only need drones.' Forget about that," Papperger said. "The market potential is still there."

Rheinmetall on Friday also warned that Europe faces mounting security threats beyond conventional warfare, pointing to an increase in hybrid attacks ranging from industrial sabotage and cyber espionage to airspace disruption and satellite jamming.
