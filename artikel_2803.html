Big Blue View mailbag: Play calling, Malik Nabers, Dominic Zvada, more

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

Ben Fitzsimmons asks: I know Matt Nagy is the OC and is the one calling plays. I don't fully understand how Greg Roman fits into it. We've seen the Giants run it between the tackles at least 400 million times on first down this year. We've seen that terrible play from the infamous JJ Package show up a few times (still hasn't worked once btw). We've seen the Giants attempt and fail miserably to run the clock out at the end of games multiple times this year. We've seen a lack of creativity in the run game as they mostly just run behind Ricard and hope it works (it usually doesn't).

What I'm curious about is how much of that is Greg Roman and how much is Matt Nagy. Is Roman designing the run plays and Matt Nagy is just calling them? Is Roman just providing advice and vibes while Nagy draws up the plays? Does Roman have any impact on how run plays are called each game (I would assume not, but I've been wrong many times before)?

Ed says: Ben, Greg Roman's title is 'Senior Offensive Assistant'. Nagy runs the offense and makes all play-calling decisions. He makes final determination, with John Harbaugh's input, on the game plan. Roman is acknowledged as one of the best run-game designers in the game today. To my knowledge, Roman is responsible for designing much of the Giants' run game.

Harbaugh has explained that much of the vertical passing game comes from Nagy's background. As I've said, Roman's specialty is the run game.

Nagy's job, as he has explained it, is to take the menu of options he has in front of him and decide what to use and when to use it. Roman, who has been an offensive coordinator multiple times, sits in the booth with Nagy on game days. Of course there will be some sharing of information and ideas. The decisions, though, are Nagy's to make.

We see the warts. Sometimes, you make the wrong call. Sometimes, you make the right one and players don't execute it. The Giants are 14th in the league in score at 22.6 points per game. That's pretty good, considering that they are on their third quarterback and have struggled mightily to block in the run game.

Bob Donnelly asks: Following the Commanders' game you have questioned some of the offensive play calls, especially not running the ball in a few key situations. The OC makes the play call yet much of the blame/criticism seems to be directed towards the HC and his lack of confidence in his team's ability to run the ball in those situations.

In your view how much play calling influence does our head coach exert in critical situations?

Ed says: Bob, the reality is that the head coach gets credit when things go right, and everything that goes wrong also lands at his feet.

Harbaugh does not call the plays. He can set the direction, though. Does he want the ball run? Thrown? What personnel does he want in the game.

Tom Donlan asks: Malik Nabers can make spectacular catches and carry the passing game with a high volume of targets and catches. We've also seen drops and a costly fumble against Washington, as well as what some view as immature or disruptive behavior with poor body language on the field. What do you think he needs to do to become a more consistent No. 1 receiver, and do you know how the Giants view the way he voices his frustrations publicly?

Ed says: Tom, Malik Nabers has a drop rate this season of 3.5%, per Pro Football Reference. That isn't exactly terrible. He has fumbled three times in 185 career touches, one basically every 62 touches. Again, that isn't awful. Drops happen occasionally. No one who has the ball a lot goes through a career without fumbling at times.

I'm not getting into the body language debate. As far as becoming a more consistent No. 1 receiver, how about getting some consistent reps with the quarterback. Nabers barely had any time with Jaxson Dart last year. Nabers' reps were limited all summer as he came back from his knee injury, then Dart went down for the season in Week 2. Now, Nabers and J.J. McCarthy have worked together for a month.

My educated guess is the Giants would like him to choose his words more carefully. But, he was encouraged to speak his mind freely as a rookie, and he is still a 23-year-old kid learning to grow up with a much different coach than the one who drafted him.

One other thing I will say is that I still believe Nabers has to learn to do a better job fighting to protect his quarteerback. I'm thinking about that interception at the end of the Washington game. The receiver simply has to put up a fight there to make sure the ball gets knocked down and not intercepted.

Joel Story asks: I read an article saying that after the Giants/Eagles game on Nov. 8th, Kayvon Thibodeaux was fined $11,941 for taunting, Jeremiah Trotter was fined $6,428 for tripping, Zack Baun was fined $6,750 for illegal use of the helmet, and Andrew Mukuba was fined $9,041 for a hit on a defenseless player. Are these amounts correct? If so, do you know why the NFL/NFLPA have chosen to fine players more for taunting than for the other three actions which could potentially end other players' seasons or even their careers?

Ed says: Joel, yes those amounts are correct. The fine schedule, which you can see it for yourself, is collectively bargained.

Taj Siddiqi asks: JH is being criticized for keeping Dominic Zvada and let Jason Sanders walk. Sanders is performing like a pro while Zvada is struggling. Some people think one of the causes for his firing at Baltimore was his decision to stay with the kicker, whose name I can't recall. Is JH over confident regarding his ability to evaluate kickers? Is the guy at the Baltimore still there or is he even in the league?

Ed says: Taj, John Harbaugh has been around long enough to fully understand the ups and downs, and the risks, of choosing to ride with a rookie placekicker.

Harbaugh had a rookie placekicker in Baltimore last season. Tyler Loop was very good for the Ravens in 2025. He made 30 of 34 field goals (88.2%). Harbaugh got fired because the Ravens missed the playoffs and the organization felt like his tenure had run its course after 18 years. It just so happens that Loop missed a 44-yard field goal on the final play of that game that would have gotten Baltimore into the playoffs. It was his only miss of the year between 40 and 49 yards.

How does anyone criticize a coach for riding with a kicker who went 30 of 34? Loop is still with the Ravens, but he is injured and on IR.

Overconfident in his ability to evaluate kickers? Well, he and the Ravens drafted Jordan Stout in the fourth round in 2022. Stout is probably the best punter in the league. Sam Koch punted in Baltimore for 16 years, 14 of those for Harbaugh.

Justin Tucker was the best field goal kicker in the NFL for more than a decade, and was bombing 60+ yard kicks long before there were a bunch of other kickers in the league capable of doing it. I would say Harbaugh knows good kickers when he sees them. If Harbaugh ends up being wrong about Zvada, that will be the first kicker or punter he has been wrong about in his time as a head coach.

As for Jason Sanders, I saw a number of those practices in the spring. A day or two before he got cut I watched him try to kick a 50-ish yard field goal on a windy day, and I watched the ball basically die and go straight down a couple yards short. I didn't think he could handle the MetLife Stadium conditions, either.

Francis Castros asks: Can you provide the rehabilitation status of the following players and are they required to rehab at the Quest Diagnostics Training Center?

– Tyrone Tracy Jr. — RB (Hamstring)– Tremaine Edmunds — LB / MLB (Chest / Shoulder)– Braxton Berrios — WR (Foot – surgery)– Chauncey Golston — DE / Edge (Neck)– Jaxson Dart — QB (Knee – Meniscus)– Brian Burns — LB (Knee – ACL)– Roy Robertson-Harris — DE (Achilles)– Calvin Austin III — WR (Knee)– Rico Payton — CB (Undisclosed)– Jarrick Bernard-Converse — CB (Knee)– Gunner Olszewski — WR (Achilles)– Thaddeus Dixon — CB (Achilles)

Ed says: Francis, I really don't have much. The team is not required to, and does not, share information about players on IR.

I can tell you Tyrone Tracy should be back at some point in the next few weeks. I saw Brian Burns in the locker room after the Washington game. He is still on crutches. Jaxson Dart has been seen at the facility a couple of times by media, though I have not seen him myself. I have seen Roy Robertson-Harris around a couple of times.

Players are not required to rehab at the team facility. Many do, though, spend at least some of their time doing that.

Wesley Protheroe asks: I have a question regarding the utilization of the Giants' tight ends:

While Isaiah Likely has been outstanding, the Giants' "conventional" tight ends have been largely ineffective. Johnson is an unreliable pass catcher and only a fair blocker. Manhertz is a better blocker but has only one target all season and defenses don't have to account for him. Fidone has been mostly a healthy scratch but has shown good hands in the pre-season and caught his one target for 29 yards. Shouldn't the Giants consider replacing Manhertz with an interior lineman or at least throw to him periodically to keep defenses honest? Additionally, why not give Fidone a chance to play over Johnson. He is definitely the more natural receiver. Your thoughts?

Ed says: Wesley, the Giants are not going to intentionally throw to Chris Manhertz when they can throw to Malik Nabers, Isaiah Likely, Malachi Fields, or Darnell Mooney instead. Still, if he runs out in a pass pattern he does have to be covered.

I'm not replacing Manhertz with an offensive lineman who isn't used to being out on the end of the line of scrimmage. Honestly, though, while I can't point to specific plays it does feel to me like neither Manhertz nor Patrick Ricard are having the impact on the run game the Giants were hoping for.

In reference to Johnson vs. Fidone, I am curious if Fidone could play a bigger role. It is pretty clear, though, that the coaching staff prefers Johnson. Maybe part of the reason is that Johnson has also become a fairly significant part of the special teams.

David Silver asks: A lot of articles this week were taking a look at Abdul Carter, trying to make sense of his game. By any measure but sacks, he has been a destructive, disruptive force who makes quarterbacks uncomfortable, including a third-best speed to the QB of 2.97 seconds. It seems when he's not getting us a holding call, the QB is unloading the ball when they see him destroying another lineman. My question is, how often do the rush Carter from the blindside? I mean, if he is one of the fastest rushers to the QB in the league, what happens when you can't see him coming? More sacks, right?

Ed says: David, I don't have access to data on exactly where each of Carter's pass rushes come from. I'm sure it exists somewhere, but not in anything I have available.

What I can tell you is that Carter has played 503 defensive snaps, and has been used in a multitude of positions. He has been used 167 times at right outside linebacker, and 157 at left outside linebacker. That leaves 179 snaps where he has been aligned in a variety of other places, from nose tackle to middle linebacker.

From that, you have to extrapolate that defensive coordinator Dennard Wilson is already doing a good job moving Carter around.

Bruce Frazer asks: Donte Whitner on ESPN described JJ McCarthy as a one read quarterback, not ready to elevate the giants to a higher level. He showed a number of plays highlighting the problem showing JJ constantly keying on his first read and throwing to that receiver. Do you feel this is an issue that the coaches are aware of this.

Ed says: Bruce, c'mon now! This has been McCarthy's reputation since his Michigan days. It's hardly a revelation. He threw far less in college than the other quarterbacks in that draft class. He's played in 15 NFL games — less than a full season. This is a common issue with young, inexperienced quarterbacks. Of course the coaching staff is aware of it.
