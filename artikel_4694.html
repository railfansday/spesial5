Could Upskilling Save Your Marriage?

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

Posted November 15, 2026 | Reviewed by Jessica Schrader

Two weeks before he turned 50, David Levin was in counseling trying to save his marriage. But just a couple of minutes into the second of three sessions they'd agreed to try, his wife said she didn't want to do this anymore and wanted a divorce.

In the years since, he found himself using skills that, he says, arrived one marriage too late. "I had real time to ask who I was and what made me happy. I became a more present father, because my time with the kids suddenly felt finite."

He also noticed his divorced friends felt the same: "That's when it clicked. None of this actually required a divorce. It just required the time and a push."

He's put his experience, research, and insights from his own therapeutic sessions (pre- and post-divorce), into writing his first book: A Divorced Dad’s Playbook for the Still-Married: Hard-won secrets so you can be a better partner, parent & person.

Certified divorce coach Justin Milrad says, "A marriage doesn't fall apart overnight. It's a gradual accumulation of resentment, disconnection, unmet expectations, conversations that could've, should've, or never happened."

Milrad read Levin's book just as he was getting married for the second time: "Even though I've done the work, it reminded me of what I need to do better, what I need to focus on, and how I can show up authentically for myself and for my new wife."

Levin describes friction as the slow wear of a marriage: "A lot of households run on mind-reading. One partner quietly holds the whole family's inventory in their head, and the other has no idea that inventory exists."

On the other hand, Levin cautions, crisis is when the foundation itself is cracked: you no longer share core values, or one of you doesn't want to be there. "Ours was a foundation problem. The marriage had probably ended years before anyone said it out loud."

"A good tell for friction is that you still want to be in the room together, but you mostly talk about logistics," says Levin. "A tell for crisis is that you've stopped talking about the marriage at all."

He believes that most marriages that feel like they're failing don't actually have a cracked foundation; the couple just has skills problems that look like foundation problems. "Your marriage is probably fine. That's not a compliment, and it isn't an insult. It's where most of us live."

Upskilling in specific areas can strengthen a marriage. Levin speaks from experience when he says that skills can be learned but almost nobody works on them until it’s too late. He outlines 71 skills in his book, but he sees couples struggling the most with these:

Presence. Being in the room for real, not just physically.

Saying the good thing. One specific appreciation, out loud, every day. Most of us save our charm for strangers.

Making the invisible visible. One shared calendar and a 15-minute weekly check-in, so nobody has to read minds.

No tag-outs. Each partner able to run the whole household alone, so neither one becomes the default parent.

Not keeping score. Own your part first, and make a proposal instead of a case.

Courting. Putting yourselves back on the calendar, and treating date night like a date, not a logistics meeting.

Levin says one of the main reasons he wrote his book was to help normalize therapy for people who've never tried it: "I think therapy is amazing and everyone should be in it."

Much of the experiences Levin includes in his book came out of his own therapy, including Gottman's Four Horsemen and Nonviolent Communication: "The book is one layperson explaining to another what therapy can actually feel like. My therapist, Lauren Demby, LCSW, comes up a lot in the book, and she's a big part of why I'm such a believer."

Milrad agrees: "I never, in a million years, thought I'd be divorced or be a divorce coach, [but] I saw the opportunity to reinvent my life. What I offer is complementary to therapy. It's radical acceptance: tell me about the life you want to live, and then we figure out how to create that life."

"When you're going through a divorce, if you're doing it right, in my opinion," says Milrad, who also hosts The Conscious Divorce Podcast and is the author ofYou 2.0: Divorce, a Better Way Forward and co-author of The Co-Parenting Playbook. "You're working on yourself: about what went well, what could I have done better, if I were to do it again next time, what would I change?"

He says divorce can provide a chance to step off the "hamster wheel of life" and figure out how to create systems that are effective in another relationship.

Both Milrad and Levin stress that, sometimes, divorce is where you end up. And they want to reduce the stigma that it's somehow a failure or that the relationship was a mistake.

"It's OK to get divorced, and it can be one of the greatest blessings if you can have an amicable relationship with your ex, and if your kids are in a good place, and you find a happier place to be," assures Milrad. "And you should want the same for your ex, as well."

After doing the work to understand himself, Levin says, "I'm happier now than I've been in years."
