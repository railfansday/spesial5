The Three Most Common and Costly Things People Miss When Buying a Used Bike

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

Before buying a secondhand bike, inspect these potentially costly trouble spots first.

The customer came in right before a race because there was an issue with their shifting on the training ride. Yes, there was an issue with cable tension, but that wasn’t the biggest problem. The missing chip of carbon where the top tube met the seat tube overshadowed anything going on with the rear derailleur. When I asked the customer if he knew the chip was there, he said, “I bought the bike used and the seller said it was just a chip in the paint.”

Unfortunately, I’ve been on that end of the conversation with a lot of customers who brought in used bikes over the years. I get why someone would buy a used bike. Even when I ran shops, I would suggest used bikes as a way people could save money on a new (to them) bike. But there were too many times someone brought a used bike in for us to run through and we would find things like frame damage or wheels that needed to be replaced. Prices of new bikes have gone up dramatically over the last few years. Used bikes have their place for so many riders, but be aware of what you’re buying. Sometimes between the price of the used bike and then the parts and labor needed to make it ride-ready, you’ll be toeing the price of a new bike. Many of those new bikes also come with things like one free service or warranties from the manufacturer.

If you’re in the market for your next bike, it does make sense to consider used options. There are plenty of deals to be had on the used bike market. Here, I’ll give you the three most common mistakes I see people make when going the used route and give you my best practices when considering that used bike.

The biggest mistake I’ve seen people make is missing damage on the frame. I’ve had customers bring in used bikes where the frame had chips or cracks in the carbon or irreparable damage to aluminum frames. While there are shops that offer carbon repair, there are not a lot of reliable options for frame repair.

Let’s compare some pricing—an entry-level road bike is around $1,200 depending on the brand. Many times this will include some type of warranty, at least one free service or check on the bike, and at a minimum a free floor fit for you on the bike.

If the used bike’s frame needs to be replaced, then step one will be finding a frame compatible with as many existing components as possible. Many times parts like cables and housing, bottom brackets, and handlebar tape will need to be replaced when swapping to a new frame. Once a new frame is found, the parts listed above are added, and service gets put on the ticket, you are at or above the price of what that new bike would have cost.

This can be avoided by doing a thorough inspection of the frame when looking at a used bike. Do a thorough check of the frame—even the hard to get to parts like where the tires hide and under the frame. If I were buying a used bike I would bring a shop rag to wipe any dirty spots to do my due diligence. I wouldn’t recommend buying a damaged frame, unless you like it for wall art.

Wheels can be the second biggest money pit on a used road, gravel, or hybrid bike. When I was a service manager, we had to replace many wheels on newly purchased used bikes because the existing wheels were either damaged beyond repair or because the parts and labor to repair them outweighed the price of a new wheel.

The pricing was always surprising to people. Most shops I’ve worked in priced wheel builds around $100. If we were going to pull the hub (center of the wheel) and other components from the old wheel to install on a new wheel, there was a small, but additional charge. For people getting a used entry-level road, mountain, or hybrid bike, the labor of a wheel build, not including parts, could outprice the cost of a new wheel.

Reasons we would need to rebuild or replace a wheel were things like cracks in the rim at the spoke nipples, severe knocking in the hub (where the wheel develops too much lateral movement), or when the wheel was significantly out of true. Wheels can sometimes be straightened out (truing a wheel), but there are points where it can’t be brought back.

When looking at a used bike, there is a relatively easy way to check all of these wheel issues. First, look at the base of the spokes where they meet the rim. If you see cracks on the rim at any of these points, the wheel will need to get replaced. Once you check the base of the spokes, then hold the frame in one hand and the top of the wheel or tire in the other. Test for lateral movement in the wheel by holding the frame still and trying to move the wheel side-to-side. If it doesn’t move laterally, that’s another box checked.

Finally, spin the wheel while it is on the bike. Sometimes it can be hard to tell if the wheel is straight if the used bike has a flat tire. Try using your thumb as a guide. Hold your thumb a set distance from the rim surface (at least an inch). This technique will allow you to see if there is a severe wobble in the rim. Remember to keep your fingers far enough away from the wheel to not get them stuck in the spokes of the wheel as it spins. A small wobble can usually be fixed for under $50 at most shops.

This can be the hardest thing to see on a used bike because it can be really hard to tell how worn a drivetrain (chain, cassette or gears, and chainrings or crank) is with the naked eye. When someone is committed to buying a bike, a chain checker is one of the tools that can save you hundreds of dollars in the long run. A chain checker is a small, relatively inexpensive tool that checks how worn a chain is. By checking how worn a chain is, you can tell what other parts will need to be replaced. Many shops will recommend replacing an 11, 12, or 13 speed chain when a chain checker reads over 50 percent worn, replacing a chain and cassette or freewheel (gears on the back wheel) over 75 percent worn, and replacing chain, cassette or freewheel, and chainrings or crank (gears in the front near the pedals) over 100 percent worn.

If you don’t have the tool there are ways to check visually too. Sometimes you can see wear on the gears on the rear wheel (called the cassette or freewheel). When the teeth on the gears on the front or back start to become uneven and resemble shark teeth that is another quick visual check.

One fairly reliable trick to see if the chain is stretched is to try to pull the chain straight up and away from the gears by the pedals (called the chainrings). If it easily lifts up and away, and especially if there is a significant gap between the chain and chainring, there is likely a significant level of wear in the system. Having the chain checker is an easy way to tell just how much.

Pricing to get this fixed can easily exceed a few hundred dollars when all three components of the drivetrain (chain, cassette, and chainrings) need to be replaced. If the bike needs any additional labor that price can climb quicker than people expect.

When I ran a shop, I would always suggest customers get at a minimum a safety check or base-level tune-up on a used bike. This allows an experienced bicycle technician to inspect the bike, torque all the bolts and parts on the bike, and adjust things like shifting, brakes, and tire pressure. Even if the bike is a new bike that the seller said they just never rode, you never know who built it initially. I’ve had bikes in the stand that were in this category and the handlebar spun around with the slightest bit of pressure.

If you don’t want to take it to a shop, then consider options like local bike clubs, local bicycle nonprofits (our local example is the Coalition for Appropriate Transportation), or even an experienced cycling friend. Anyone who can run through the bike with an experienced set of eyes can help get you on the right path.

I used to suggest having the seller meet the potential buyer at my shop. This would allow both parties to be there when a service technician or adviser inspects the bike for an impartial assessment of the condition of the bike. Before using your local shop as the meet-up, give them a call to see if this is something they can offer.

This list includes the most common mistakes I would see specifically in the road, gravel, and hybrid categories of bikes. It does not touch on mountain bikes because that is an entire list in and of itself. Parts like suspension forks, rear shocks, dropper posts and pivot bearings are all things to be cautious about with used mountain bikes.
