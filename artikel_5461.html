Sovereignty is the AI story everyone mentions and few have planned for ahead of 2027

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

AI infrastructure economics are entering a more sober phase as enterprises move past standing up AI factories and start asking how to run them profitably. Durability, real demand and long-term value are now the tests separating lasting providers from the froth.

That shift rewards cloud providers with an operating track record rather than a single speculative wager. Vultr has extended its global cloud to AI with chips from Nvidia Corp. and Advanced Micro Devices Inc. Enterprises are now making a flight to quality toward stable, cost-efficient platforms, according to Kevin Cochrane (pictured), chief marketing officer of Vultr.

“We’re seeing now not speculative bets in the buildout of AI infrastructure. We’re seeing real bets based on proven economics, proven ROI that people are looking to achieve,” Cochrane said. “What I think is going to wind up happening is a maturation of our industry leveraging this open stack.”

Cochrane spoke with theCUBE Research’s John Furrier and Rebecca Knight at SC26, during an exclusive broadcast on theCUBE, SiliconANGLE Media’s livestreaming studio. They discussed how AI infrastructure economics favor a global footprint, why sovereignty requirements reach well beyond national governments and how fiscal conservatism shapes Vultr’s answer to market turbulence. (* Disclosure below.)

Vultr now operates 34 cloud data center regions, the latest a new region in Chennai, India. That footprint was built to keep cloud-native applications close to users, and inference follows the same logic, Cochrane explained. Agents make fast turns back to reasoning models, so CPUs and GPUs must sit together near the edge, he noted.

“This is kind of what Vultr was built for, which is one global platform, not isolated islands of data centers that are serving a bespoke customer. It is a true global cloud platform,” Cochrane said.

Sovereignty adds another constraint, and it reaches well beyond national governments. A global insurer tuning an open-source model for credit risk needs training clusters in each country because the underlying data cannot cross borders, Cochrane noted. Regulators are already tightening the rules, as the U.S. Commerce Department’s new curbs on remote chip access show.

“The way you have to post-train a model for credit risk in the U.K. versus Germany versus Singapore versus the United States, it’s going to be different,” Cochrane said. “It uses different data.”
