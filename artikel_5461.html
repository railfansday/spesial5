Inside a Plan To Convert Shipping Containers From the Border Wall Into Affordable Housing

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Flying over the Arizona desert one day in 2023, Beau Phillips looked out his plane window and spotted thousands of shipping containers—remnants of a dismantled border wall—scattered across the arid landscape.

That chance glimpse sparked an idea: convert the abandoned steel boxes into hundreds of affordable housing units and build a whole new community in the Tucson area for some of the 40,000 low-income residents waiting for housing assistance.

"These were built to keep people out, and now we're welcoming people in," Phillips tells Realtor.com®. "There is a poetic justice there."

Phillips, a semiretired MTV Networks and radio marketing executive, first encountered the containers in late 2022, when he took part in a demonstration protesting then-Arizona Gov. Doug Ducey's order to install a border barrier made up of more than 2,000 double-stacked steel shipping crates, topped with wire, near Yuma and in the Coronado National Forest.

After the Biden administration and environmental groups successfully sued, arguing that the 10-mile wall of containers was illegally placed on federal and tribal land, the state took the barrier apart in early 2023.

Some 2,200 steel boxes were left in the desert with no plan for their disposal, until Phillips saw them from a plane and pitched state officials on a new use for them.

To bring his vision to life, Phillips founded a nonprofit, Boxes of Hope, and teamed up with La Frontera AZ, a major affordable housing developer and behavioral health provider, to create a village made up of 350 to 450 shipping containers transformed into rental homes.

This project is aimed at singles, couples, veterans, seniors, and anyone priced out of Tucson’s rental market, where the median asking rent in October was $1,133, according to the latest data from Realtor.com.

"We're really opening it up wide to just whoever needs it, and we will make sure that the rent that is charged is affordable," says Phillips.

The proposed 25- to 30-acre community will take shape in Southeast Tucson, within 2 miles of the containers' current resting place to minimize transport costs. The site also sits along a bus line and within a half-mile of shopping.

Phillips, who freely admits that he knows "nothing about swinging a hammer," tapped Tucson native Ryan Egan, a veteran container-conversion expert, to lead construction on the ambitious building project, which is currently in the fundraising stage.

It takes about $59,000 to turn a 4-ton crate into a comfortable 300-square-foot residence insulated against Arizona's brutal 115-degree summers.

"It's about half the price that the city of Tucson now pays for-profit builders to build affordable housing, so they're thrilled," notes Phillips.

Each unit will be air-conditioned, with a private bedroom and queen bed, full bathroom, kitchenette with a refrigerator and a two-burner stovetop, storage, new flooring, windows, and sliding glass doors.

"It would be almost like someone who lives in an RV park," explains the Boxes of Hope founder and CEO.  "You have your unit that you live in, and you connect to utilities on site."

The exteriors will be sandblasted and decorated with colorful murals celebrating Tucson's culture.

Beyond providing a roof and four walls, Phillips and his project partner, La Frontera CEO Dan Ranieri, envision a holistic neighborhood complete with a park, laundry facilities, a community kitchen and dining hall, behavioral healthcare, transitional housing for the homeless, and on-site training in building trades like carpentry and plumbing,

"It's really a 360-degree solution to get people not just a place to live, but a place where they can thrive," says Phillips.

Boxes of Hope already has backing from Sen. Mark Kelly's office, the Arizona Department of Housing, and Tucson city leadership. The nonprofit is currently meeting with potential donors and pursuing government and private housing grants to move the project from planning into construction.

To any locals harboring "not in my backyard" concerns about the future affordable rental community, Phillips stresses that his team aims to be good neighbors from the start.

"We're going to meet with neighborhood groups. We're going to explain our vision," he says. " We want to listen to people, but we also want them to understand that we're trying to do something good here. We're trying to take lemons and turn them into lemonade."

He points to La Frontera's track record of managing affordable housing, which includes the development of nearly a dozen low-income tax credit projects since 2008.

Boxes of Hope plans to unveil its full development plan and the village's exact location site in January 2027, after signing a letter of intent with the landowner.

"He loves the concept so much that he dropped his price in half and said, 'I want to help you,'" Phillips recalls.

Despite the project's ambitious scope, the construction timeline moves quickly. Turning a raw container into a finished home takes about two weeks, and once production ramps up, Phillips expects the team to complete 12 to 15 conversions per month.

Site development is set to get underway in spring 2027, with the first tenants poised to move in by the second half of next year.

Beyond Tucson, Phillips believes that Boxes of Hope could serve as a blueprint for tackling America's broader housing affordability crisis.

" Last year, 25 million shipping containers arrived in the U.S. along the West Coast and never left because America doesn't export anywhere near as much as we import," he says. "Most of them are still sitting all along the West Coast in Seattle and San Diego and San Francisco and Portland."

That need for well-priced rental inventory is felt acutely nationwide. According to the latest Realtor.com monthly rental report, the median asking rent in October rose year over year for the first time since 2023, driven by a sharp decline in multifamily construction,

However, Realtor.com senior economist Jiayi Xu points out that container conversions have inherent limitations.

"A container can be converted into a studio fairly easily, but it is hard to build larger units with multiple bedrooms that meet the needs of families," she says.
