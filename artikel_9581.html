Motorcycle Helmets Are Becoming Giant Airbags. Could Helmets that Inflate like Airbags Work?

Imagine riding a motorcycle when a car pulls out in front of you. Before your head strikes the road, your helmet expands into an enormous protective cushion. That is the promise behind an emerging category of airbag-equipped head protection.

The latest attention centers on Chinese manufacturer Lanxi Yema Motorcycle Fittings, described in November 17 reporting. But Italian helmet maker Airoh and Swedish safety supplier Autoliv unveiled a related prototype in 2022. Sweden’s Hövding sold inflatable bicycle head protectors for years before going bankrupt, while Britain’s Ventete sells a helmet inflated before use.

Lanxi Yema Motorcycle Fittings is an established helmet manufacturer in Zhejiang, China. Its website claims a large factory and substantial conventional helmet production capacity. Those figures should not be mistaken for sales of its airbag design.

A Chinese utility model, CN224761381U: A Helmet Airbag Arrangement, was published September 18, 2026, following a November 2025 application. The patent supports the existence of a development project, not commercial availability or independently demonstrated protection.

The concept adds inflatable protection to a conventional helmet. A sensor and triggering system would need to detect a crash, activate an inflator and expand cushioning before head impact. I could not verify retail availability, a price, independently certified testing, an original manufacturer demonstration video or sales figures for this specific product.

At the 2022 EICMA motorcycle show, Airoh and Autoliv unveiled what their official announcement called a helmet concept with an integrated airbag. The airbag was designed to deploy around the crown of a conventional helmet, retaining the underlying helmet as protection even without deployment.

The British Motorcyclists Federation’s description explains how shell sections open to allow inflation. Autoliv’s technical brochure described preliminary impact research and estimated that, under modeled conditions, a serious skull-fracture injury probability could fall from about 60% to 30%. This is not a demonstrated halving of motorcycle deaths or a universal crash-risk estimate.

The project drew on earlier academic research, including work associated with Sheffield Hallam University. Yet I could not verify a commercial launch or unit sales for the integrated helmet. Autoliv remains active in motorcycle airbags: its 2026 partnership with RS Taichi concerns an airbag vest, and Cycle World reported on a motorcycle-mounted system intended to protect the rider’s upper body and head. Neither is proof the integrated helmet has shipped.

Hövding’s device was a collar worn by cyclists. Motion sensors attempted to identify a crash, triggering an inflatable hood around the head. The company described inflation in approximately 0.1 seconds and substantial development involving staged accidents. Its documentary announcement discusses the testing process; watch the documentary.

Unlike the motorcycle prototypes, this product reached a sizable market. A 2022 research publication from Germany’s Institute for Motorcycle Safety reported approximately 425,000 units sold since 2013. That is a historical count, not a confirmed lifetime total.

In 2023 Swedish authorities questioned the reliability of Hövding 3, imposed sales restrictions and initiated a recall. The company contested the action, but filed for bankruptcy in December 2023. Bikerumor’s account describes the legal and commercial fallout. A successful inflation demonstration did not settle questions about real-world reliability.

Austria’s iSi Group subsequently acquired the technology and intellectual property, according to the bankruptcy administrator. The Hövding website describes a forthcoming Hövding 4 with redesigned electronics and crash-detection software. It is intended for cycling, not motorcycling, and I could not verify a firm launch date or preorder availability.

British startup Ventete takes a simpler approach: inflate the helmet before riding, then deflate it for storage. Its aH-1 uses interconnected inflatable structures rather than a crash-triggered gas generator. A Financial Times profile reported years of development and an initial release that sold out quickly, without establishing cumulative sales.

The official product page lists versions priced at £185, £200 and £225, depending on the pump. Ventete’s FAQ explicitly says the helmet is not for motorcycles or mopeds. Its official video links include an inflation demonstration.

This distinction is essential: a pre-inflated bicycle helmet does not need to recognize an imminent crash. It instead must reliably maintain pressure, resist damage and protect the rider across relevant impact conditions.

Bumpair is another project focused on inflatable, compact bicycle head protection. An engineering case study describes work on skull fit, pressure retention and manufacturing, as well as a 2022 road-safety innovation prize. I could not verify large-scale sales or a certified motorcycle-specific version.

The underlying physics are plausible. Increasing the distance and time over which a head decelerates can reduce peak impact acceleration. But that does not automatically translate into lower brain-injury risk in every crash. Deployment timing, rotational forces, neck loading, and the behavior of the helmet without inflation all matter.

At 60 mph, a motorcycle travels about 2.7 meters in 100 milliseconds. A system that needs that long to inflate must detect danger early enough to finish deployment before the relevant head impact. An algorithm that misses a crash may provide no additional airbag protection; one that deploys unnecessarily introduces different risks.

Crash types also vary: direct impacts, glancing blows, sliding, tumbling and collisions with obstacles are not interchangeable. A peer-reviewed study of motorcycle helmets under oblique impacts emphasizes the importance of rotational injury measurements and impact location. A larger protective surface is not necessarily safer in every configuration.

The neck matters too. A 2023 biomechanics study involving airbag and helmet protection explored reductions in certain impact measures alongside potential neck-loading tradeoffs. It would be premature to generalize its results to all inflatable helmet designs.

Certification is another hurdle. A crash-triggered motorcycle helmet should be evaluated for ordinary helmet protection, successful deployment and failures to deploy. The Bicycle Helmet Safety Institute’s analysis of airbag headgear explains the regulatory complications faced by unconventional bicycle protection. Motorcycle approval requires its own applicable standards and testing.

Motorcycle-mounted airbags and wearable airbag vests already exist. Honda introduced an airbag-equipped Gold Wing in 2006, and suppliers have developed tethered and electronically triggered wearable systems. The Insurance Institute for Highway Safety discusses motorcycle airbag approaches and the limits of available real-world effectiveness evidence.

But a vest protecting the torso does not validate an inflatable structure around the head. Head injury biomechanics and crash timing create different requirements.

For Yema, the meaningful next milestones are independently published impact data, an applicable certification, an official demonstration with measured deployment timing, and confirmed customer deliveries. For Airoh and Autoliv, the unanswered question is whether the integrated helmet concept will become a retail product. For Hövding, the test is whether its new ownership can relaunch with dependable crash detection and regulatory acceptance.

The industry needs comparable testing across impact speeds, angles, surfaces and accident sequences, including linear and rotational acceleration, neck loading, and deployment failures. A slow-motion promotional video cannot substitute for those results.

Airbag helmets challenge the assumption that head protection must stay the same shape throughout a ride. Inflatable structures could add impact-management space only when needed. The concept is credible, and several companies have invested real engineering effort in it.

Yet the strongest evidence is still fragmented: a Chinese patent, a European prototype with promising modeled results, a once-successful bicycle product that collapsed amid regulatory concerns, and a commercial bicycle helmet inflated before use. None of that establishes that an integrated crash-triggered motorcycle airbag helmet has achieved proven, mass-market protection.

The future may involve helmets that transform in a crash. Until independent testing and certification support a particular product, riders should rely on appropriately certified conventional motorcycle helmets rather than an experimental airbag promise.
