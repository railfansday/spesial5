Vija Celmins, the Ultimate Artist’s Artist, Gets Honored by LACMA

Tanggal: 2026-11-11

On Wednesday, I walked down Crosby Street and stopped at an easy-to-miss building where, high above the cobblestoned street, is the studio of Vija Celmins, who’s been there for almost 50 years. It’s a classic gigantic SoHo artist loft, the kind you’ll be shocked to hear still exist—but they do. In the 1970s, the previous tenant put a Buddhist temple in the front room. Not finding anything particularly wrong with that, Celmins lived in the temple for a while.

“Then I finally thought, gee, maybe I could put a couch in here,” Celmins told me as I walked in.

It’s been decades since the galleries moved from SoHo to Chelsea and got replaced by boutiques. But among this warren of cast iron buildings, some of the artists who moved in during the pioneer days remain. Among them is Celmins, the masterful pop-landscapist who paints in infinite textures, taking on the universal with an obsessive tenacity. The 88-year-old is a living link to a long-gone generation, and someone always in the running when we talk about the best artists who are both still with us and who began their careers before the JFK assassination.

Approaching nonagenarian status, Celmins is still incredibly quick, with a generous, buoyant voice. She immediately hopped down the stairs of her old studio and took me over to the windows.

“I used to be able to see down the street before they built this ugly pink building—all the way to the bridge, because this was a parking lot,” she told me, referring to a building erected 21 years ago.

She walked past a print she made some years ago: a depiction of the ocean, her most famous subject, installed on the wall. She admired it in a refreshingly objective way.

“It’s so loose. Usually I tighten everything down,” she said. “I mean, it’s such a relief. I had forgotten that. And what did I do? I only made 10 of them, which is interesting, in ’16. I must have done this at… It looks like it’s a drypoint—doesn’t it look like drypoint? I mean, I did a fucking lot of oceans, as you know.”

Though not a household name, in certain circles, Celmins has attained near-mythic status. The very few people I told about this interview ahead of time were shocked she was having anyone over to her studios in Manhattan and Long Island. She works incredibly slowly, and thus has shown incredibly infrequently. This has started to change in the last few years. She had a retrospective at the Met Breuer in 2019, and in the last few years, a few shows of new work at her gallery, Matthew Marks—the blue-chip commercial concern that represents a disproportionate number of the greatest American artists.

Next year, Celmins will have a show at Dia Bridgehampton. Tomorrow, she’s the guest of honor at the LACMA Art+Film Gala, a big deal in American museumdom. It comes with a gigantic bash presided over by Leonardo DiCaprio and Eva Chow. The gala’s also honoring Denis Villeneuve, which Celmins is excited about. She’s seen the Dune movies and, like Villeneuve, is a big fan of the desert.

During my visit, she opened up a random drawer under a drawing table. There was stuff everywhere, decades of sketches and notes and Post-its. She has a constellation of postcards taped to the wall, with paintings as inspiration, ones that look nothing like Vija Celmins—Picasso, Balthus—along with photos of her heroes and her friends with pets. There’s Jasper Johns with his dog, Borges with his cat. She’s got framed artworks from friends who are mostly dead now, like a Ken Price drawing of some undulating cups—“You know Kenny Price?”—and a very kooky, absolutely amazing H.C. Westermann of a wolf with its heart throbbing in its chest, wandering through the desert. Westermann made it for her. She made Westermann a wolf drawing in return. She doesn’t know what happened to her wolf.

There are drawings of globes, sculptures of globes, her tablet paintings, more prints of the ocean, a paperweight from an insurance company in Kansas City—near where she grew up in Indiana after leaving Latvia—a wrapped-up canvas labeled “ED MOSES” on the side, and plenty of drawings of Saturn.

She moved into the SoHo place after two decades working in Venice Beach, California. While in LA, she honed and refined, and inarguably mastered, a painstakingly laborious practice of applying graphite to paper in slow, methodical marks, rendering the passage of time in hyperreal landscapes. She now mostly works in a studio on the eastern end of Long Island, where she's had a place for the last few decades. But it’s here at the SoHo studio, in the erstwhile cauldron of cutting-edge American art, where she loves to be.

“The studio out in the country is more like where I went back to painting, really,” she told me. “And here I did all these weird things.”

This wasn’t the first time we had met. A few weeks back, I was admiring the very cool Gary Hume show that Marks had up in his gallery on 24th Street when gallery director Jacqueline Tran tapped me on the shoulder and asked if I would like to meet Vija Celmins. Vija Celmins! At the public opening!

We chatted for a few moments. She seemed spry, fit, with an elastic mind. Then she invited me over for lunch—not in the city, but at her place on farmland out east. On an October morning I hopped on a Jitney, then an Uber to Celmins’s part of the hamlet, which is essentially rural, with small clapboard homes encircled by crop formations.

I arrived at the same time as my fellow visitors: Michael Govan, the director of LACMA, and his wife, the fashion executive Katherine Ross. Celmins welcomed us in and introduced us to her cat, Raymond, who immediately pounced on my cup of takeout coffee.

“Down, Raymond! He’s very friendly,” Celmins said. “He’s my son.”

Out here, in the two-room studio attached to her house, she’s been able to conjure a vision of landscape perhaps more potent than the breakthroughs she came to so many years ago. In her studio, she showed us how the raindrops on her window, as captured in her work-intensive flattened plane panorama, could take on epic qualities.

We sat down for lunch—quiche and soup from a local bakery—and Celmins started telling stories about California. She hadn’t lived there in a number of years, though the topography of the state is what willed her artwork into being. She drove out to the desert with James Turrell, with Doug Wheeler, and spent years—decades—arguing with Robert Irwin, who taught her at UCLA. Irwin called Celmins his “star student.” I’ve always liked this Bob Irwin quote about Celmins: “Vija has perfect taste. She doesn’t like anything.”

Govan told her about the museum, LACMA, and the new building that had finally been completed earlier this year after two decades of planning and fundraising. Celmins was quite curious about it all. She has a few plans for when she’s in town for the LACMA gala. She seemed intrigued about meeting her fellow honoree, director Villeneuve, and watched the Dune movies in preparation. (I heartily recommended she watch Sicario and Arrival as well.) She’s going to visit her old studio at 701 Venice Boulevard, which she hasn’t seen in decades.

“I was going to go out in the desert, but I’m not going to have time to do it,” she said. “I might have to make another trip. I really like the Death Valley desert because it’s so vast, so terrific. And they have airplanes zooming from the Air Force around really fast. I used to wave at them. They go really low, and they just go like, vvvvvrrroooooom! Right above, following the outline of the mountains.”

She’s also going to see Riko Mizuno, her Los Angeles art dealer, who is currently 94 years old. But most of her friends are gone.

“A lot of them have died, I mean, I didn’t have that many friends, but some died, and some are dying right now,” she said. “And then they moved away. Ed Moses, I knew pretty well. Billy Al Bengston. Well, Kenny Price, I loved Kenny.”

Then she mentioned Robert Therrien—“You know Bob Therrien?”—the late artist who has become quite famous for oversized sculptures that are astronomically popular on Instagram. A retrospective at The Broad this year became something of a sensation. I can’t imagine an artist more different than Celmins—but as it turns out, they had a deep mutual respect for each other.

“I met him once, and he was a very unusual person,” Celmins said. “He was really tall, and everything in his studio was like—he had a ceiling that was maybe like 25, 28 feet tall. And he had this little ladder that went up this side to this kitchen, which was this kitchen he remade from Chicago or someplace where his family used to live. I’ll show you one little thing. He gave me this.”

She got up from her chair and found the Therrian artwork on a shelf. It was a snout, bobbing up and down in a terrifying manner, attached to a curved metal rod. Just the inner part of the face of a canine, floating forever.

“It’s so disorienting. Where’s the rest of the dog?” I asked.
