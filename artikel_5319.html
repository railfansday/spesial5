If Indonesia wants 8% growth, it needs to give investors confidence that contracts won’t change on them

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Indonesia faces a test of investor confidence. A volatile stretch for the stock market, pressure on the rupiah, and concerns over fiscal discipline and policy uncertainty have raised questions about the direction of Southeast Asia’s largest economy—questions sharpened by MSCI’s proposal to reclassify Indonesian equities to “standalone” status, outside the benchmark emerging-market indexes that guide global portfolios.

Yet despite these wobbles, the long-term investment case for Indonesia remains strong. I saw it firsthand in Jakarta, where I recently led a business delegation of 33 American companies—investors, employers, and partners in developing Indonesia’s workforce. Across engagements with senior government officials and business leaders, one takeaway was clear: The U.S. business community remains committed to the Indonesian market.

But confidence cannot be taken for granted.

President Prabowo Subianto has set an ambitious target of 8% economic growth by 2029. Indonesia’s economy expanded 5.47% year-on-year in the third quarter of 2026, and the government is targeting 6% growth in 2027. Indonesia has the resources, talent, and market to pursue these ambitions. The challenge is creating the conditions that turn that potential into sustained investment.

Our discussions reinforced a simple point: Indonesia’s next wave of investment need not come only from new entrants. It can come from companies already operating there—businesses that have built factories, developed suppliers, trained workers, and established deep roots in the economy.

The question is what will encourage them to expand.

First, investors need confidence in the rules. Businesses can manage commercial risk. What they cannot easily manage is uncertainty over whether established agreements will be honored or regulations applied consistently. Concerns over changes to existing contractual terms and shifting regulatory requirements have unsettled investors in several sectors. Predictability, consistency, and fair treatment are fundamental to long-term investment decisions.

Energy offers a clear example. Indonesia wants to boost domestic production and strengthen energy security. American oil and gas companies have long supported these goals, helping meet domestic energy needs, develop local capabilities, and strengthen Indonesia’s competitiveness in global energy markets. These investments are made over decades, not quarters. Indonesia’s broader push for greater national control over natural resources has raised questions about the long-term security of investments. In oil and gas, regulations allowing changes to production-sharing contracts, including cost recovery and tax provisions, illustrate why contractual certainty remains an important issue. Sustaining this partnership requires respect for the agreements already signed.

This principle is widely recognized. The OECD, which Indonesia aspires to join, emphasizes the sanctity of contracts and the importance of parties fulfilling their obligations. The World Bank’s Business Ready framework puts a similar premium on reliable contract enforcement.

The new Oil and Gas Bill, currently under consideration in Parliament, offers an opportunity to strengthen that confidence. The proposed legislation would overhaul the management of upstream oil and gas operations and clarify certain investment provisions. Ensuring that the final law protects existing commitments, provides competitive fiscal terms, and improves regulatory certainty would help unlock investment in domestic production. Expanded U.S.-Indonesia energy cooperation could also strengthen supply security and deepen our economic partnership.

The principle extends well beyond energy. In manufacturing, changes to import licensing requirements have previously left thousands of containers stranded at Indonesian ports, disrupting access to essential materials and components. In technology, overlapping data localization requirements and evolving rules for cross-border data transfers can complicate investment in cloud infrastructure and digital services. These uncertainties carry real costs. Predictable policies give companies the confidence to plan, invest, and grow.

Second, Indonesia must make it easier to do business. Capital is mobile. Across Southeast Asia, governments are competing to attract investment, develop industries, and create skilled jobs. Indonesia cannot rely on the size of its market alone.

Consider manufacturing. Companies need timely access to equipment, components, and materials that may not yet be available domestically. Delays at the border can stall production and raise costs. In healthcare, duplicative testing and certification requirements can slow access to medicines and medical technology. While these regulations serve legitimate goals, including safety and quality assurance, requirements that duplicate internationally recognized standards without improving outcomes impose avoidable costs. Removing such barriers would benefit Indonesian businesses and consumers as much as foreign investors.

Equally important is meaningful consultation with the private sector before major laws and regulations are introduced. That means engaging businesses early, allowing adequate time to review proposals, and incorporating practical industry feedback before rules are finalized. Such dialogue can help policymakers anticipate unintended consequences, improve implementation, and give investors greater certainty.

Third, investment must build Indonesia’s own capabilities. President Prabowo’s emphasis on strengthening domestic industry is an objective American businesses share. The most successful investments create opportunities well beyond the companies making them.

Strengthening domestic industry also requires access to foreign technology, equipment, and materials that cannot yet be produced competitively at home. Local content requirements that exceed domestic production capacity can delay projects. A more effective approach would pair realistic requirements with supplier development, workforce training, and technology transfer.

The digital economy offers another opportunity. Clear, practical rules for artificial intelligence, cloud computing, and cybersecurity can help Indonesian businesses adopt new technologies, improve productivity, and create skilled jobs. American companies can be partners in that transformation.

These are not competing priorities. A stronger investment climate and a stronger domestic economy go hand in hand.

I left Jakarta encouraged by the government’s willingness to engage and its determination to deliver economic growth. But the real measure of progress will be what follows those discussions. Dialogue matters. Implementation matters more.

Indonesia’s recent challenges underscore the importance of that work. Market confidence can be fragile, and political uncertainty carries economic costs. Investors are watching not just Indonesia’s growth prospects, but the consistency and credibility of its economic policies as well.

American companies are not short-term speculators. We are long-term investors and partners. Our commitment to Indonesia has endured through economic downturns and political transitions. We intend to build on it.

Indonesia does not need to convince us of its potential. We already believe in it. The opportunity now is to translate that belief into more investment, more jobs, and lasting prosperity for the Indonesian people. That begins with a simple principle: consistent and fair treatment of investors, backed by policies they can trust and commitments they can count on.
