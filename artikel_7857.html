"Original Meaning and the Freedom of Speech and Press": "Ambiguous Evidence: Enjoining Repetition of Libels"

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

I'm serializing my new Notre Dame Law Review article, "Original Meaning and the Freedom of Speech and Press: Some Illustrative Examples"; here's the third and last Part:

Throughout much of American history, the dominant view has been that courts can't enjoin libels. The first precedent on this was Brandreth v. Lance (1839), an 1839 New York trial court decision. This was followed by State ex rel. Liversey v. Judge (1882), an 1882 Louisiana Supreme Court decision. Subsequent decisions helped establish this as a firm rule, seemingly culminating in Near v. Minnesota ex rel. Olson.

Yet since then, many state courts have allowed permanent injunctions against libels. By my count the court systems of thirty-four states have either so held or have in practice been so acting, with only six states plus D.C. continuing to categorically bar such injunctions. {See Eugene Volokh, Anti-Libel Injunctions, 168 U. Pa. L. Rev. 73 (2019).}

This trend seems to have accelerated with the rise of the internet. Historically, a libel has been a short, sharp shock—typically, a one-time article in a newspaper that is printed and can't be removed—with the only possible remedy being damages or criminal prosecution. But the internet can turn libel into a steady drip, drip, drip of damage, with more readers seeing a libelous web page every day. Injunctions thus seem a more useful remedy.

The internet also makes it easier for judgment-proof defendants to cause real damage; an injunction can thus be useful in a way that a damages remedy might not be. Criminal libel is theoretically available in about ten states, and there are indeed likely a couple of dozen criminal libel prosecutions in the United States each year. But in most states the only way to deploy criminal remedies in service of protecting reputation is through getting an injunction that can be enforced through the threat of criminal contempt.

Are these injunctions contrary to the original meaning of the First Amendment? The answer turns out to be complicated, especially if we focus on permanent injunctions, entered to forbid the repetition of material found to be libelous in a final judgment (with the defendant having had an opportunity to call for a jury trial).

It's true that early sources often view the freedom of the press as precluding prior restraints: To quote William Blackstone, "The liberty of the press is indeed essential to the nature of a free state: but this consists in laying no previous restraints upon publications, and not in freedom from censure for criminal matter when published." Yet this doesn't quite speak to the modern question whether the law may restrain repetition of material for matter found to be criminal (or tortious).

Nor does it speak to whether the law may require removal of such matter after a trial: Removal just wasn't practically an option in the print era. In such situations of injunctions against repetition of an already published libel or ordering its removal, "[e]very freeman" would retain the "undoubted right to lay what sentiments he pleases before the public" (again quoting Blackstone) at least in the first instance.

Nor is there a "restrictive power of a licenser" or "control of an inspector" in the sense that the Framers likely understood licensers, which is to say officials administering a licensing system. (To quote St. George Tucker, under that system "all books printed were previously licensed by some of the great offices of state, or the two universities, and all foreign books were exposed to a similar scrutiny before they were vended.") The injunction is imposed by a judge, often following a jury verdict, administering justice between two parties, not running a traditional licensing system that "subject[s] all freedom of sentiment to the prejudices of one man, and make[s] him the arbitrary and infallible judge of all controverted points in learning, religion, and government."

Likewise, early sources stressed the importance of allowing a jury to resolve what is true and false in libel cases. But, as I noted, the modern permanent injunction in libel cases is entered after a final judgment on the merits, which at least in principle allows a defendant to demand a jury trial. If the defendant has so demanded, then any injunction would be based on the jury verdict.

This stems in part from the twentieth-century merger of law and equity, since equitable remedies, such as injunctions, can now be issued following the use of the legal procedure of a jury trial. (Historically, one argument against injunctions in libel cases has been that "equity will not enjoin a libel," a statement about the limits of the equity courts more than about constitutional protections for free expression.) Indeed, the Delaware Court of Chancery, which operates in a state system where law and equity remain largely separate, has continued to hold that it generally can't enjoin libel. But it expressly left open the possibility that, once the Delaware Superior Court (the law court) concludes—after a jury trial, if the parties don't choose a bench trial—that a statement is libelous, an injunction can then be issued.

In practice, anti-libel injunctions are often parts of default judgments, or follow bench trials, and thus don't involve factual findings by juries. In both those situations, though, the defendant can be said to have waived his right to a jury trial. To be sure, temporary restraining orders and preliminary injunctions are entered before any opportunity for a jury trial—but for that reason many courts continue to reject them on First Amendment grounds.

And, as the California Supreme Court held in allowing such post-final-judgment permanent injunctions, the reasoning of Near v. Minnesota ex rel. Olson expressly sets aside injunctions in traditional civil libel disputes:

The high court was careful to point out … that the statute being considered was "not aimed at the redress of individual or private wrongs. Remedies for libel remain available and unaffected." … But most significant is that the court … clarified that it was not addressing "questions as to the extent of authority to prevent publications in order to protect private rights according to the principles governing the exercise of the jurisdiction of courts of equity." In a footnote, the court cited [an] … article by Roscoe Pound that observed that English courts permit "an injunction in case the libel was repeated or publication was continued after a jury had found the matter libelous." Therefore, Near expressly did not address the issue posed in the present case.

The Supreme Court hasn't confronted such permanent injunctions against repetition of proven libel since Near, but it has authorized injunctions against obscenity (which Near had anticipated) and against commercial speech that proposes illegal transactions. The one case in which it granted certiorari as to anti-libel injunctions, Tory v. Cochran (2005), largely disappeared when the plaintiff, famed lawyer Johnnie Cochran, died while the case was pending. None of these cases discussed the original meaning of the First Amendment, and the lower court cases dealing with the matter have largely not discussed it, either.

So here we have a fairly rich body of originalist evidence related to libel law generally. We have a considerable body of evidence from before and shortly after the Framing that talks about "previous restraints." And we have some nineteenth-century sources that seem to take the view that all injunctions in libel cases are unconstitutional. But how exactly do the Framing-era sources apply to a permanent anti-libel injunction issued by a court following the resolution of a libel case? Hard to know for sure.
