The Best Cross-Country Ski Boots, Apparel, and Accessories of 2027

What to know: This guide includes the best cross-country ski boots, apparel, and accessories for frigid to warm winter days on the skin track. Testers in Maine and the Midwest named the Fischer RC5 BOA Skate as the best skate-ski boot for its power, warmth, and comfort. Testers also voted the KinetiXx Sol XWarm Trigger as the best hybrid ski glove and mitt.

In cross-country skiing, every limb is in a constant state of motion. Thus, it’s rare to feel cold when moving on the trail. The only sensation worse than being too cold is becoming a sweaty, overheating mess halfway into the ski, sapping energy and increasing your risk of hypothermia. That’s why it takes time and patience to learn how to manage your inner furnace with the exterior conditions and dial in the best cross-country ski gear, from your boots to your hat.

To find the perfect kit, our Midwest testing team spent four months classic and skate skiing in variable conditions—everything from a heavy first-season snow in December to sub-zero temps in January to an early, warm thaw in March and more snow that pushed the season into April. Meanwhile, the team in Maine spent as many months on snow testing gear.

Combined, we put dozens of products through the paces and ranked these 11 pieces of gear as the best cross-country ski boots, apparel, and accessories.

Pros and Cons + Snug fit with BOA + Powerful, warm, and comfortable – Too wide for narrow feet – Sizing tool isn’t accurate

This beautifully constructed boot is stiff enough for racing but also so comfortable you can cruise all day with warm and happy feet. And at $379, the RC5 is still a few hundred dollars less than the World Cup-level race boots, even with the addition of the BOA closure system—a feature normally found only in Fischer’s Speedmax line.

Our testers loved the BOA for two reasons: It tightens the boot with more precision than laces, eliminating sloppy fits. And the dial on the boot’s exterior is easy to use with a glove on. More to love: The plush, thermally formable foam liner can be heated in the oven and contoured to your foot for a custom fit. A three-layer waterproof membrane lining wicks sweat and blocks snowmelt, while an exterior zipper keeps out snow and slop. Testers also liked the stiff plastic cuff, which provided ample ankle support without creating hot spots or blisters. Underfoot, a hard exterior chassis helped efficiently transfer power from skater to ski.

Skiers, especially women with narrow feet, may find the medium-volume boot a little too wide. And one more thing: Be wary of the AI-powered, app-driven sizing tool on Fischer’s website. For two different testers, it recommended a size two sizes too small.

Pros and Cons + Warm and cozy in frigid temps + Stable and solid underfoot – Too wide for narrow feet

This Norwegian-made boot has a trifecta of layers that make it the ideal classic boot for full-day forays. First: a furry fleece lining. In the middle is a super lightweight, low-density, highly porous insulation called Solarcore, used by NASA and mostly composed of air and silica gel that traps heat. The third warming factor is a Gore-Tex membrane that keeps snow out but allows sweaty moisture to escape.

Our longest test was 20 kilometers on a crisp 17-degree day. On that trek and on subsequent shorter days, one tester proclaimed the Alfa the warmest boots she’s tested. What it isn’t, she added, is a nimble, form-fitting, ultralight race boot.

With a wider last and a hiking-boot-like shoelace as the only mode for tightening, the Alfa felt roomy on our tester’s narrow foot, but its warmth and stability were second to none, especially while going slow and taking in the scenery on days with blasts straight from the Arctic.

Weight: 2 lbs 15 oz (Outlander) and 2 lbs 8 oz (Eve) Sizes: 36-49 (Outlander) and 35-42 (Outlander Eve)

Pros and Cons + Secure heel fit + Very comfortable – Soft toe compromises ski control

Given the variability of foot shapes and widths, it’s rare for all testers to praise the same boot for fit and comfort—yet the Outlander (and women’s Outlander Eve) took top scores from everyone who tried this boot. “It snugged my heel in place without pinching,” said a tester who wore it while touring from Maine’s Poplar Stream Hut.

Cushioning within the boot envelops the Achilles to prevent slippage, while an exterior panel of rigid TPU wraps around the back of the heel to improve lateral control. Additional support comes from the footbed, which proved leagues better than the flimsy ones that ship with most boots: Its anatomic, arch-supporting platform uses breathable open-cell PU foam. That, plus the mid-volume interior with a cinch-cord closure wrapped narrow feet and broad stompers alike with zero blisters or hot spots reported—even after one 27-kilometer slog. Low-bulk Thinsulate insulation kept testers’ feet cozy in 15 to 35 degrees (colder than that, and the chill penetrated within 45 minutes). But the boot’s soft, foot-conforming comfort compromises some control: The pliable forefoot and toe didn’t pressure skis as commandingly as other boots we tested.

Weight: 3 lbs 8 oz (per pair) Sizes: 36-48

Pros and Cons + Precise and powerful ski control + Impressively warm – Heavy

The Rottefella Xplore binding wrings more control and stability out of any ski it’s paired with—but not every Xplore-compatible boot lives up to the binding’s power and capability. This boot does. The XP12 masterfully translates skier inputs to the binding and unlocks impressive maneuverability and precision from skis. Steering accuracy comes, in part, from a customizable fit made possible with two separate cinch-cord systems that snug the forefoot and ankle separately. “The lacing eliminates gaps around my foot that other systems can’t always adjust,” explained one tester. The boot’s sturdy shell (reinforced with injected rubber on the exterior and an internal heel counter) also provides abundant torsional stiffness to command ski edges.

This is also the warmest classic boot we tested, keeping toes comfy through seven-hour tours and 15-degree conditions. The low-volume interior fits slender feet best, but because the boot is heat-moldable, a bootfitter’s alterations can achieve a comfortable wrap on wide or irregular shapes. But superior comfort, warmth, and control come with a weight penalty: At about 1.75 pounds per boot, it’s heavier than many Xplore-compatible boots. Still, for high-mileage tours, testers recommended them because their feet felt happier.

Pros and Cons + Lightweight + More affordable than some carbon poles – Strap system can be finicky

Many Nordic poles offer a strap system that can quickly detach and reattach to the pole, all the better to adjust boots, handle fitness apps, or take phone calls. Ski racers complain, however, that these systems can backfire during competition when the strap separates from the pole and the skier loses precious seconds trying to reattach it.

For skiers who aren’t chasing a podium, Leki’s PRC 650 entry-level pole has a soft cork grip, a stiff but lightweight graphite shaft, strong carbide tips, and an oblong basket that keeps the pole from plunging into deep snow. All that and a durable detachable strap system Leki likes to call “The Shark.” On the pole, a plastic fin sticks straight up near where the thumb sits. A small cord attached to the top of the grip loops around the fin, which attaches it to the pole and is further secured by a plastic lever. To detach the grip from the pole, a “quick-release button” operated by the skier’s thumb activates the lever, allowing the loop to slip up and off the shark fin. Complicated? Maybe. But this mechanism is one of the more reliable systems we’ve tested.

Resist the urge to burrow into a too-warm jacket on a bitingly cold day and instead opt for the lightly insulated Mammut Rime. The hoody, built for a wide range of winter mountain sports, is the ideal weight for high-aerobic output. Plus, the cut is more form-fitting than other jackets we tested, which made it feel less bulky and especially well-suited for Nordic skiing.

Recycled polyester insulation on the front and back is body-mapped to keep the core warm. Layered on top is a wind- and water-repellent treatment, which deters biting temps from creeping in. Two stretchy side panels that continue up the length of the inner arm provide movement and additional breathability.

Our tester took this out for a 20-kilometer skate in 19 degrees and again on a 16-kilometer classic ski on a windy, 25-degree day with only a base layer underneath. She was thrilled to end both skis feeling like Goldilocks—“just right.” A snug hood adds additional warmth on the coldest days, two front zippered pockets are roomy enough to fit climbing skins and extra layers, and an interior vertical pocket on the left breast keeps a phone warm.

At first glance, the Bergtagen 60 Insulated Vest looked too roomy and long to be comfortable for cross-country skiing. But on a damp and biting 10-degree day, one tester threw it over her regular lightweight ski jacket for extra warmth, and it saved the day.

With a shell and lining made from a wind- and water-resistant recycled Pertex and high-loft, loose-fill recycled polyester insulation, the vest adds warmth without bulk. The fit is relaxed and falls to mid-buttocks in the back, offering extra warmth for the derriere. The short collar, however, flapped against testers’ necks and chins in the wind.

Elasticized armholes, two spacious pockets, and a zipper pull that’s easy to tug with gloves reinforce its role as warmth insurance on days when one jacket just isn’t enough. Best of all, if you overheat, it squishes down to the size of a softball, small enough to throw into a drink belt.

Pros and Cons + Buttery soft + Retains shape after washing + Pants come in full and ¾ lengths – Expensive – Very little compression

Many base layers are simply too thick for high-aerobic activities—but not the Woolies Pro Tech. Made from 85 percent superfine 17.5-micron merino wool and 15 percent nylon, this ultra-lightweight crew and its matching bottoms are soft against the skin, slightly stretchy, and paper-thin in a good way. Despite their minimal bulk, both pieces deliver impressive warmth: One tester wore the crew with just a lightly insulated jacket on a 13-degree ski day, while the bottoms fit easily beneath ski pants yet provided a beefy barrier against the cold in temperatures as low as 11 degrees.

Raglan shoulder seams and gusseted underarms in the top and a gusseted crotch in the bottoms allow for unrestricted movement with minimal chafing, while the bottoms’ wide elastic waistband stays comfortable through rapid-fire activity. Even after sweat-inducing workouts, both pieces came back dry and stink-free. The biggest downside? Price.

Every year Skida comes out with a host of new prints from bold primary colors to feminine floral pastels that offer a surprising hit of motivation to get out there and crush it on the trails, even on the coldest days.

Our best advice is to start in a qualified shop with employees dedicated to Nordic skiing—especially for ski boots and poles. There you’ll have access to a full range of brands and expertise from skiers who have devoted their lives to this sport and who can explain why a pole’s “swing weight” matters (see below for the short explanation).

For Nordic ski clothing, there’s no need to go hog wild on a new wardrobe, especially if you’re already a winter endurance athlete, like a runner or ski mountaineer.

If you’re new to cross-country skiing, resist the urge to rush out and buy a new kit. Try what winter gear you already have and see how it works, then slowly fill in the holes. On the other hand, if you’re new to a winter aerobic sport, keep in mind that with cross-country skiing, less is more, with the exception that it might be slow going (and hence, colder) at first because it takes time to master technique. The key is breathable layers. But the one non-negotiable piece we can’t live without is an extra cozy puffy to throw on after skiing.

Hot or cold? Some people sweat profusely; others barely break a sweat. Tune into your own physiology and dress accordingly. There’s a narrow sweet spot between underdressing and overheating.

Hat or headband? Some skiers prefer full ear coverage from a headband, while others use a hat and neck gaiter to cover their ears and neck. Either way, start with a gaiter you can shed as you warm up.

Hydration pack, hip pack, or belt? Staying hydrated is essential, but carrying water while skate skiing can be unwieldy. Belts are minimalist, waist packs hold more, and hydration packs make drinking easiest—but each comes with trade-offs in bulk, comfort, or accessibility. We tested several systems and found enough finicky issues, from leaky caps to uncomfortable fits, that we left them out of this year’s review.

How to protect fingers and toes? Cross-country skiing requires streamlined gloves and snug boots, which can make it tough to stay warm. Fingers and toes can go numb before your body warms them back up—and sweaty socks and liners can leave them freezing during breaks. If you run cold, start with hand and foot warmers.

Comfort is king with boots. Even some lifelong elite skiers we know never buy the most expensive race boots because the lack of insulation and cut to shave weight can make for miserably cold feet. Most boots are unisex and feel wide for skiers with narrow feet, but some manufacturers are beginning to offer boots in a narrower width. If the boot that fits the best still feels too wide or too flat, many skiers buy a custom insole.

Ski poles are more complicated than they look.

One thing to consider is swing weight, which refers to the pendulum motion of each pole stroke. Weight near the tip of the pole requires more energy from the skier than weight near the top of the pole. It matters because the average ski racer makes about 30 to 45 pole plants per minute. If poorly distributed, swing weight can fatigue arms over time. While that’s not so important to beginner skiers, what is important is a pole’s weight-to-strength-to-stiffness ratio, and carbon poles almost always win on that front.

Grip is also key—pay close attention to how the grip is attached to the pole and read reviews about whether it accidentally deploys. Also consider the grip material—cork tends to absorb vibration and resist moisture, while foam tends to be more comfortable.
