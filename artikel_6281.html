United Airlines Bans AI Booking Agents, But Who Is It Really Protecting?

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

In a November 11 announcement, United said it is restricting unauthorized third-party personal AI agents across its digital platforms and through travel agencies and aggregators.

The airline warns that sharing credentials with these tools could expose sensitive information or lead to unwanted purchases and account changes. It also raises regulatory concerns, pointing to the acknowledgment passengers must make concerning hazardous materials during check-in.

Those concerns deserve consideration. But they do not adequately explain why an assistant should be prohibited from making a change that I understand, authorize, and could make myself.

United has added the following to its prohibited ticketing practices:

“The use of AI Agents to make, modify or service a Ticket, and/or to purchase any service or product.”

The definition encompasses autonomous and semi-autonomous assistants, including those whose actions a passenger supervises or approves.

We already have tools that monitor airfare and notify us when prices change. I use fare alerts myself. But receiving an alert still leaves me with work to do. I must examine the offer, check the restrictions, and determine whether changing the ticket actually leaves me better off.

Imagine an assistant doing that work continuously, then completing an authorized change whenever the savings justify it based on the parameters I input at the start of the process…something I would happily use.

Consider a hypothetical $800 ticket whose rules allow you to rebook at a lower fare and retain the difference as a future flight credit, with no change fee.

The price drops to $650. Your AI assistant identifies the same flight and fare conditions, completes the permitted change, and secures a $150 credit.

Later, the price drops to $575. It does the calculation again and captures another $75.

You still take the flight you intended to take, but now have $225 available toward future travel.

That is not a cash refund on a nonrefundable ticket, and the actual opportunity depends on the fare rules. But evaluating those complications is precisely what would make the technology useful. Tell it what you want to preserve and when it may act. Let it calculate whether the lower price represents actual savings.

Airline revenue management benefits from passengers behaving differently. Some book early, some wait, and some keep checking after buying. Many understandably consider the transaction finished once the confirmation email arrives.

An assistant that keeps shopping changes that calculation.

A brief fare reduction that I would otherwise miss becomes an opportunity and a small saving that would not justify 20 minutes of my time may become worthwhile when software handles the work. Multiply that across millions of reservations and you can understand why an airline might become uncomfortable.

It would put serious pressure on the ability to retain a higher fare from someone whose ticket permits a lower-price exchange. The airline could still set its prices and restrictions, but fewer customers would leave available savings unclaimed merely because they were busy living their lives.

I suspect United understands this very well. This strikes me as a preemptive move to control who can manage a reservation before automated consumer tools become an ordinary part of travel.

These new rules do not suggest that United intends to eliminate every fare alert: the more consequential restriction is preventing the assistant from acting on what it finds.

I would not hand any application unrestricted control of my MileagePlus account and yes, I do not want software canceling an important trip because it misunderstood an instruction. But those are reasons to establish permissions and accountability.

United’s hazardous-materials example is particularly weak as a justification for prohibiting reservation management. The federal passenger-notification rule requires disclosures and passenger acknowledgment in specified circumstances. Reading that requirement, I do not see why an assistant could not stop and ask the traveler to complete the necessary acknowledgment personally.

More to the point, checking whether my ticket can be exchanged for a lower fare does not require an assistant to make a declaration about what is inside my suitcase. A responsible system should require approval where needed and limit what software can do. United’s restriction still encompasses an assistant whose actions I supervise and approve.

So no, I do not accept the customer-protection explanation.

Scott Kirby offered a more revealing observation at a Chicago event, according to The Wall Street Journal:

“To be honest, I don’t want to create another middleman.”

I understand why United would prefer to control the customer relationship, but my interests are not identical to United’s. I may want someone working for me, comparing the options and continuing to look after my reservation once United has collected payment.

I recently examined Kirby’s argument that technology helps make United the best airline in the world. I agree that its app is excellent. Better technology has made my United travel easier. But I also want technology that helps me spend less. An assistant that understands my preferences and captures an eligible fare reduction would be doing exactly that.

United should explain what secure, authorized access would require. Simply blocking an AI assistant carte blanche protects the airline’s control much more clearly than it protects my interests.

United says its restrictions on third-party AI agents protect customers. I think they also–and primarily–protect United from a future in which passengers can monitor and manage reservations far more effectively than they do today.

Just as airlines use complex algorithms to extract the highest fare fro Mme, I want to use technology to help capture savings without having to babysit every reservation.

To me, this looks like a cheap shot dressed up as customer protection. United is entitled to secure its systems, but I am far less sympathetic to using that explanation to block an authorized assistant from helping me obtain a better deal.
