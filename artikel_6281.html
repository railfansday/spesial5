New FAA Meltdown Report Points To Disturbing Oversight Changes Under Trump

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

The more we learn about the nationwide FAA ground stop, the less convincing the effort to blame old equipment and previous administrations becomes. New reporting points to an oversight policy changed under the current administration and a technician whose role apparently ended after letting a contractor through the door. Aging infrastructure required careful management. Instead, we appear to have received less of it.

A CNN investigation describes a troubling gap between the oversight the FAA says occurred and what sources say actually happened.

The issue concerns work by L3Harris at a radio communications facility in El Dorado, Arkansas, before the November 13 ground stop. According to CNN, an FAA technician provided access to the building but understood that his responsibility ended there.

One source described the technician this way:

“He was only there to unlock the door…there was no monitoring.”

FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford had said the installation was monitored. An agency spokeswoman subsequently explained:

“The escort is not there while the technician is doing work”

Those accounts require reconciliation, but strike me as irreconcilable. Opening a door and overseeing an installation are very different responsibilities.

A March 2025 FAA memo changed the process for contractor maintenance. Work previously submitted as requests requiring approval became notifications that could proceed without affirmative FAA approval. The policy retained a review role for FAA liaisons, but a small group was tasked with handling thousands of notifications.

This is why I find the continuing effort to blame Biden or Buttigieg so absurd. Neither man was running the department when this policy changed or when this installation occurred. I have already written about the contractor questions surrounding this failure. The new reporting makes those questions more urgent.

Decades of deferred investment explain why modernization is necessary. They do not excuse weakening the supervision of the modernization itself. If anything, working with old infrastructure should make the government more careful about introducing new equipment, not less.

The staffing issue deserves scrutiny too, also highlighted by One Mile At A Time.

In February 2025, the FAA fired hundreds of probationary employees during the administration’s government-cutting campaign. Duffy said no controllers or critical safety personnel were among those dismissed. The Professional Aviation Safety Specialists union later announced the reinstatement of the 132 terminated employees it represented.

That means we cannot necessarily a direct causation from those firings to this outage, though the broader approach remains fair game. An administration that celebrates eliminating positions needs to account for the work those people perform. If that work still needs doing, someone else must absorb it, a contractor must perform it, or it goes undone, a particular concern at an agency already struggling with staffing.

In its statement on the ground stop, PASS says the FAA’s May 2026 workforce plan called for at least 750 additional certified professional technicians by 2030. The union says little progress has been made toward hiring them.

We can certainly agree that unnecessary government spending should be eliminated. But a smaller payroll does not demonstrate greater efficiency if the remaining organization cannot adequately supervise the work entrusted to it…the cost of a nationwide aviation disruption must enter that calculation too, especially now.

Meanwhile, Musk is distancing himself from the political movement he helped finance.

Speaking at the DealBook Summit on November 18, he said:

He described himself as politically moderate, rejected blind adherence to Republican policies, and expressed regret over his election spending:

“I feel pretty foolish about having spent so much money and not seeing much for it”

We joke about United Airlines CEO Scott Kirby adjusting his political posture with the prevailing winds. Apparently, he has company. I would welcome a sincere reassessment from Musk. But distancing himself from a label does not erase his role in promoting the administration or DOGE.

It also does not establish that he now recognizes the problems with the personnel policies he championed. Regretting an unsuccessful political investment is different from regretting how the government was managed. Still, when even Musk wants distance from MAGA, I think it is fair to ask whether the brand has reached its expiration date.

My reading of the election is that Americans are tired of the performance. They want competent government and explanations they can believe. Being told that every success belongs to the president while every failure belongs to his predecessor wears thin and it goes well beyond right/left or Republican/Democrat divisions.

To be fair, Bedford has now offered something we have heard too little of. As ABC News reports, he said Wednesday:

“I’m the guy they hired to get this done. So blame me. If we need to blame somebody, blame me.”

I appreciate that. He also promised a transparent accounting of the technical findings. But accepting blame must lead to an explanation of the decisions that preceded the failure. It cannot simply shield Duffy or end the inquiry.

The incoming Congress should audit the maintenance policy, staffing records, risk assessments, and contractor communications. It should establish what oversight was required, what actually occurred, and whether the agency had enough qualified people to carry out its responsibilities.

Duffy wants another $50 billion. Perhaps the modernization program needs that money. But Congress should understand how the existing process failed before assuming that a larger appropriation will fix it…and maybe remind Duffy that instead of going on reality TV road trips he should have done the job he was hired to do.

Accountability must become something more useful than another round of political theater that both sides use to score political points. Trump remains president for two more years. The new Congress will have to work with him to repair the damage and modernize this system.

I find his vindictiveness and self-absorption deeply troubling. In his recent 60 Minutes interview with Ross Douthat, he said:

“I do hate some of my enemies and I always will.”

That is a miserable disposition for governing a country whose citizens will always disagree. Even so, waiting for him to become a different person is not a strategy. Congress should pursue practical agreements that restore adequate staffing and contractor oversight while funding necessary upgrades.

If Trump needs to claim credit for a functioning aviation system, let him. I care far more about the result than denying him another chance to stoke his vanity. Congress can scrutinize the administration’s failures while negotiating legislation that begins to correct them. If the goal is to make things better, then we need to borrow the line from Ronald Reagan: “There is no limit to what a man can do or where he can go if he doesn’t mind who gets the credit.”

The latest reporting points toward an oversight failure under the current administration. The exact technical cause and the contribution of staffing decisions still require investigation, but blaming old equipment is plainly insufficient.

Modernizing a system this complex will involve difficulties. I would be much more understanding of an administration that acknowledged those difficulties, explained its mistakes, and corrected them. Instead, passengers have received conflicting accounts and political blame. The new Congress needs to insist on answers, then work with the president on repairs that outlast the next news cycle.

Americans need to be able to trust the people running their aviation system…to some extent, the past is in the past and today is a new day and a chance for course correction.
