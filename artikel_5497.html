Winter produce and vitamin C: 12 fruits and vegetables worth knowing

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Free daily briefing on global business news.

These 12 winter fruits and vegetables high in vitamin C, from navel oranges and kiwi to broccoli and kale, offer cold-month ways to get a nutrient the body can't make

Humans cannot make vitamin C. Every bit of it has to come from food, and that need continues after summer berries and garden tomatoes leave the markets. Cold months change what is fresh, but they do not empty the produce aisle of good sources.

Winter is citrus season in the U.S. Navel oranges are in season, and grapefruit, clementines and mandarins share the same shelves. Lemons, used mostly for juice and zest, round out the group. In the U.S., kiwi is often at its peak in winter, with California fruit available through the cooler months. Pomegranates, eaten for their arils, are another winter fruit. On the vegetable side, cool-season crops such as broccoli, Brussels sprouts and cauliflower carry much of the load. Kale is a cold-hardy leafy green. Red and green cabbage store well through winter.

Vitamin C, also known as ascorbic acid, does several jobs in the body. It is needed to make collagen, the protein that gives structure to skin, tendons and blood vessels. It works as an antioxidant. It also helps the body absorb non-heme iron, the type of iron in plant foods such as beans, lentils and leafy greens.

How produce is handled in the kitchen also matters. Vitamin C is water-soluble and breaks down with heat. Long cooking in water draws it out of vegetables and into the pot. Eating produce raw, or steaming it instead of boiling it, preserves more.

This guide covers 12 winter fruits and vegetables high in vitamin C. It is a selection, not a ranking, and the slide order is editorial. Red bell peppers are a very rich source, while pomegranate contributes a more modest amount. In winter, red bell peppers are generally grown in greenhouses or imported rather than picked from local fields. Each slide explains what the food offers, how to prepare it and any cautions worth knowing, such as grapefruit's interaction with certain medications.

Navel oranges are in season in the U.S. during winter, and they belong to citrus, the best-known dietary source of vitamin C. A whole fruit needs no preparation beyond peeling.

The fruit takes its name from the small, button-like formation at its blossom end, which resembles a human navel. That bump is a second, undeveloped fruit growing inside the first.

Navel oranges are seedless. As a result, the trees cannot be grown from seed. Growers propagate them by grafting budwood onto rootstock. The variety traces back to a mutation on an orange tree in Brazil in the 19th century.

Trees from that line reached Riverside, California, in the 1870s. California remains the center of U.S. navel orange production, with a harvest that generally runs from late fall into spring. Florida's orange industry, by contrast, concentrates largely on juice varieties such as the Valencia.

Navel oranges suit eating whole better than juicing. Their juice turns bitter after it sits for a while, a problem the citrus trade calls delayed bitterness. The main compound behind it is limonin. The thick peel comes away easily, so the fruit is practical to segment and eat out of hand.

The Cara Cara is a navel variety with pinkish-red flesh. It originated as a mutation found in Venezuela in the 1970s. It shares the seedless structure and winter season of the standard navel. Its flavor is sweeter and less acidic.

When buying navels, a fruit that feels heavy for its size usually holds more juice. Whole oranges keep longer in the refrigerator than on the counter.

Vitamin C has a long-standing reputation as a cold remedy. Regular vitamin C supplementation does not reduce how many colds people in the general population catch. That finding concerns supplements taken routinely. It does not change the role of oranges as an everyday food source of vitamin C during winter.

Grapefruit is a winter citrus fruit that supplies vitamin C. It also carries a caution that sweet oranges and clementines do not: it can interact with certain prescription medications.

In the U.S., grapefruit fills store shelves in the cooler months, when groves in Florida and Texas are harvesting. Supermarkets usually stock white, pink and red varieties. The pink and red flesh gets its color from lycopene, the same pigment found in tomatoes and watermelon.

Grapefruit is a relatively recent addition to the citrus family. It is a hybrid of the pomelo and the sweet orange. It was first described in Barbados in the 18th century. Growers were cultivating it in Florida in the 1800s.

The medication issue sets grapefruit apart from other winter citrus. The fruit contains compounds called furanocoumarins. These block an enzyme in the small intestine known as CYP3A4. That enzyme normally breaks down part of some drugs before they reach the bloodstream.

When the enzyme is blocked, more of the drug enters the bloodstream than the prescribed dose is meant to deliver. Higher blood levels of a medication can raise the risk of side effects. Affected drugs include some used to treat high cholesterol, high blood pressure and irregular heart rhythm. Not every drug in a given class is affected. A blanket rule for a whole class of medication does not work.

The interaction applies to the fresh fruit as well as the juice. Its effect can last a day or longer after eating grapefruit. Spacing a glass of juice and a pill a few hours apart does not reliably avoid it.

A pharmacist can check a specific prescription for a grapefruit interaction. Medication labels and patient information leaflets often state whether the fruit should be avoided.

For people not taking an affected drug, grapefruit is a straightforward way to add vitamin C to winter meals. Halves can be eaten with a spoon. Segments work in salads with avocado or fennel. Juice is another option, though whole segments keep the fruit's fiber.

Clementines and mandarins are small winter citrus fruits that supply vitamin C and peel by hand. They need no cooking or preparation before eating.

The peel is the practical difference. The rind comes away with fingers alone. No knife, cutting board or juicer is required.

Larger citrus is often sliced or sectioned before eating. With a clementine or mandarin, the whole process happens in the hand.

Their small size matters too. A single fruit fits in a coat pocket, a lunch bag or a desk drawer. It travels without a container and leaves only a peel behind.

That portability makes these fruits a ready snack at work, at school or in transit. They can be packed in the morning and eaten hours later with no extra steps.

Size also shapes the portion. One fruit is a modest serving on its own. A second or third adds more, so intake can be adjusted one piece at a time.

Once the peel is off, the segments separate cleanly. A single fruit is easy to split between people or hand out to children.

As winter citrus, clementines and mandarins are in season during the cold months, when the range of fresh fruit narrows.

Lemons provide vitamin C, but they are mostly used for their juice and zest rather than eaten whole. That keeps portions small, so a lemon usually adds a modest amount to a day's intake rather than serving as a main source.

The fruit's sharp, sour flesh explains much of that pattern. Few people eat a lemon the way they eat an orange or a clementine. A serving of broccoli is likewise eaten as food in its own right, while a lemon usually works as a seasoning.

Lemon juice goes into salad dressings, marinades, sauces, hot tea and glasses of water. The zest is the thin, colored outer layer of the peel. Grating it removes that layer and leaves the bitter white pith underneath. Cooks add zest to baked goods, grain dishes and rubs for fish or chicken. In each case, the amount used is a spoonful or two, not a full serving of fruit.

Repeated use is how lemons contribute. The human body has no way to produce vitamin C on its own. Diet is the only supply. Small additions spread across several meals count toward the total, alongside larger servings of other produce.

That dependence on diet has a clear consequence. Severe vitamin C deficiency over a long period causes scurvy. Steady intake from a range of fruits and vegetables prevents it.

Lemons belong to the citrus group that fills produce sections in the colder months. Among those fruits, they are usually treated as an ingredient rather than eaten as a snack.

Kiwi is one of the best-known fruit sources of vitamin C. In the U.S., it is often at its peak during winter. California-grown kiwi stays available through the cooler months. That makes it a cold-weather option alongside winter citrus.

The fruit most shoppers picture is the green kiwi. It has fuzzy brown skin, bright green flesh and a ring of small black seeds around a pale core. Hayward is the dominant green variety in commercial production. Golden kiwi comes from a closely related species, Actinidia chinensis. It has smooth, nearly hairless skin and sweeter yellow flesh.

Kiwi is native to China, where it was once known in English as the Chinese gooseberry. Growers in New Zealand developed it as a commercial crop in the 20th century. They marketed it under the name kiwifruit, after the country's national bird. Italy, New Zealand and China are now among the largest producers.

The winter timing in the U.S. follows the Northern Hemisphere growing cycle. Imports from the Southern Hemisphere, including New Zealand and Chile, keep kiwi on store shelves in the warmer months.

The vitamin C in kiwi plays a direct role in building collagen. The body needs the vitamin to make this protein, which forms the framework of skin, tendons, ligaments, blood vessels and bone. Collagen is the most abundant protein in the human body.

Kiwi continues to ripen after harvest. A ripe fruit gives slightly under gentle pressure. Placing firm kiwi in a bag with an apple or banana speeds ripening. Those fruits release ethylene gas, which triggers the process.

The skin of green kiwi is edible, though many people halve the fruit and scoop out the flesh with a spoon. Golden kiwi skin is thinner and easier to eat whole.

Raw kiwi contains actinidin, an enzyme that breaks down protein. That enzyme keeps gelatin desserts from setting when fresh kiwi is mixed in. It also lets pureed kiwi work as a meat tenderizer in marinades.

Pomegranate arils supply vitamin C, but the fruit is not among the richest sources of the nutrient. In a winter diet, it works as one contributor among several rather than a primary source.

A pomegranate is a round fruit with a tough, leathery rind. Inside, hundreds of arils sit packed in chambers divided by a bitter, spongy white membrane. Each aril is a small seed wrapped in a sac of tart-sweet red juice. The aril is the part people eat, seed and all.

The pomegranate tree is native to a region stretching from Iran to northern India. It has been cultivated around the Mediterranean for thousands of years. In the Northern Hemisphere, the fruit reaches markets in fall and stays available into winter. In the U.S., California grows most of the commercial crop.

Vitamin C acts as an antioxidant in the body. It helps neutralize free radicals, which are unstable molecules that can damage cells. Pomegranates also contain other plant compounds, including polyphenols such as punicalagins. Anthocyanins, another group of compounds, give the arils and juice their red color. These substances are separate from vitamin C. Their presence does not make the fruit a richer source of it.

Getting the arils out takes some work. One common method starts by scoring the rind and breaking the fruit into sections in a bowl of water. The arils loosen under gentle pressure from the fingers and sink to the bottom. Pieces of white pith float to the surface, where they are easy to skim off. Working underwater also limits splatter. Pomegranate juice stains fabric and wood.

Arils go into salads, grain bowls and yogurt, or onto roasted meats as a garnish. They are a common ingredient in Persian and other Middle Eastern cooking. Pomegranate molasses, a thick syrup made by reducing the juice, adds a sour note to stews and dressings across the same region.

Whole arils and bottled juice are not interchangeable. Eating the arils keeps the edible seeds, which carry fiber. Pressing the fruit into juice leaves most of that fiber behind.

Broccoli is one of the strongest vegetable sources of vitamin C available in the colder months. As a cool-season crop, it stays in the produce aisle alongside winter citrus after summer vegetables have gone.

The way broccoli is cooked shapes how much of that vitamin C reaches the plate. Vitamin C is water-soluble. It dissolves into whatever liquid surrounds the vegetable during cooking.

That makes boiling the method most likely to pull it out. When broccoli sits in a pot of hot water, the vitamin moves out of the florets and stems and into the water. If that water is poured off, the dissolved vitamin C goes with it.

Heat causes a second kind of loss. Vitamin C breaks down when it is heated, and longer cooking destroys more of it. A long boil combines both problems — prolonged heat and a large volume of water.

Two adjustments limit those losses. Shorter cooking times reduce heat exposure. Using less water leaves less room for the vitamin to leach out.

The cooking liquid itself holds part of the answer. The vitamin C that leaves the broccoli ends up in the water. Dishes that keep that liquid, such as soups, retain some of what dissolved.

Cooked briefly in little water, broccoli keeps more of its vitamin C.

Brussels sprouts are a good vegetable source of vitamin C, and cooking method affects how much of it reaches the plate. Brief steaming keeps more of the nutrient than long simmering in a pot of water.

The sprouts are a cool-season crop. They belong to Brassica oleracea, the same plant species that produces cabbage, broccoli, kale and cauliflower. Each sprout is a small bud that forms along a tall, thick stalk. Its leaves wrap tightly, like a miniature cabbage head. In the U.S., they reach markets from fall into winter, and some grocers sell them still attached to the stalk.

The name refers to Brussels, Belgium, an area long associated with growing them.

Vitamin C breaks down with heat, and longer cooking means more loss. Boiling adds a second route for loss. The longer sprouts sit in hot water, the more vitamin C passes into the cooking liquid. That liquid is usually poured away.

Steaming avoids submerging the sprouts. They cook above the water rather than in it. Halved sprouts are usually tender within several minutes. That shorter contact with heat and water keeps more of the vitamin in the vegetable.

Size and cut affect timing too. Whole sprouts take longer to cook through at the center than halved or quartered ones. Cutting them first shortens the time the outer leaves spend exposed to heat. Sprouts of similar size cook evenly. None have to stay on the heat while larger ones catch up.

Overcooking carries a second cost. Brussels sprouts contain sulfur compounds called glucosinolates. Prolonged cooking breaks these down into compounds that give off the strong, sulfurous smell many people associate with the vegetable. A sprout that is bright green and just tender has spent less time on the heat than one that has turned olive-drab and soft.

To keep more vitamin C, trim the sprouts and cut them to a uniform size. Then steam them until a knife slides in with slight resistance.

Kale is a cold-hardy leafy green that contains vitamin C. It holds up through frost and stays in the produce lineup after more tender greens have finished for the year.

Kale belongs to the same species as broccoli, Brussels sprouts, cabbage and cauliflower. All of them are forms of Brassica oleracea, bred for different parts of the plant. Kale was selected for its leaves, broccoli and cauliflower for their flower heads, and Brussels sprouts for their side buds. Cabbage was bred for its tightly packed head of leaves.

Cold weather can also change how the leaves taste. After a frost, kale often tastes sweeter. The plant converts some of its stored starch into sugar as temperatures drop.

Several types of kale reach grocery shelves. Curly kale has ruffled, tightly frilled leaves. Lacinato kale, also called Tuscan or dinosaur kale, has long, flat, dark blue-green leaves with a bumpy surface. Red Russian kale has flatter, fringed leaves and purple-red stems.

The vitamin C in kale also matters for another nutrient in the same meal. It helps the body absorb non-heme iron, the form of iron in plant foods. Heme iron, which comes from meat, poultry and fish, is absorbed more readily than non-heme iron.

Kale contains non-heme iron of its own. That places the iron and a nutrient that aids its absorption in the same leaf.

Kale also pairs with other iron-containing plant foods. Cooking it into a dish with chickpeas, tofu or quinoa adds vitamin C to a meal whose iron comes from plants. The same idea applies to grain bowls, soups and stews built around plant ingredients.

For people who eat little or no meat, plant foods supply most or all of their dietary iron. Pairing those foods with a vitamin C source such as kale is one way to make use of that absorption effect.

Red and green cabbage both contain vitamin C, and both store well through the winter. That long storage life keeps cabbage available as a vitamin C source after more delicate produce has gone.

The shape of the vegetable is part of the reason. A whole cabbage is a firm, tightly packed head of leaves. Leafy salad greens and soft fruit tend to wilt or bruise within days. A whole head holds up much longer in cool storage.

That durability changes how cabbage fits into a week of cooking. A head can sit in the refrigerator crisper until a meal calls for it. Cooks can cut off what they need and keep the rest for later meals.

The two common colors are handled the same way in the kitchen. Green cabbage is the familiar pale variety used in many everyday dishes. Red cabbage is usually deep purple and adds strong color to a plate.

Cabbage also suits raw preparations, which keep it away from cooking heat entirely. Slaw is the best-known example. It is made from thinly shredded raw cabbage tossed with a dressing.

That dressing can be vinegar-based or creamy. Either way, the cabbage itself is not cooked. Red and green cabbage can be shredded together in the same bowl.

Raw shredded cabbage works beyond slaw as well. It can be layered into sandwiches and wraps for crunch. It can also be folded into tacos or scattered over grain bowls.

Cauliflower is a cool-season crop that provides vitamin C. It grows in cooler weather, unlike many warm-weather crops that drop out of season in winter.

The main thing to watch with cauliflower is total heat exposure. The longer it stays on the stove or in the oven, the more of its vitamin C is lost.

Raw cauliflower involves no heat at all. Whole florets can go on a plate with a dip. Chopped finely, they can be mixed into a grain or leafy salad. Neither approach calls for cooking.

Roasting sits at the other end of that range. The florets stay in a hot oven until their edges brown. That extended exposure costs some of the vitamin C. The browning that gives roasted cauliflower its flavor comes from that same long heat.

For cooked cauliflower, the way it is cut changes the cooking time. Small, evenly sized florets cook through faster than large or uneven pieces. A shorter stretch on the heat leaves more of the vitamin intact.

Red bell peppers are a very rich source of vitamin C, and they stay on supermarket shelves through the winter. In colder months, though, most of them are greenhouse-grown or imported rather than picked from nearby fields.

Bell peppers are a warm-season crop. The plants do not tolerate frost, so outdoor growing in cold regions ends before winter arrives.

A red bell pepper bought in January usually comes from one of two places. It may have been raised in a greenhouse under glass or plastic, where growers control heat and light. Or it may have been shipped from a region with a warmer climate. In places with hard winters, it is not a local field crop at that time of year.

The color has its own explanation. Green, yellow, orange and red bell peppers all belong to the same species, Capsicum annuum. A red bell pepper is typically a fruit left on the plant until it fully ripened. A green bell pepper is the same kind of fruit, picked earlier.

Red peppers spend longer on the plant before harvest than green ones. That extra growing time is one reason they often cost more at the store.

Bell peppers also differ from their hot relatives. Chili peppers get their heat from capsaicin. Bell peppers carry a recessive gene that leaves them without it. That is why they taste sweet rather than spicy.

In the kitchen, raw red bell pepper slices work as a snack or for dipping. Diced, they go into salsas and grain salads. In a stir-fry, they can go in near the end so they stay crisp. Their mild sweetness pairs with sharper flavors such as onion, garlic and vinegar.

Whole peppers keep best unwashed in the refrigerator crisper drawer. A cut pepper should be wrapped and used within a few days.

For winter shoppers, the red bell pepper works as a year-round option rather than a seasonal one. In the cold months, it usually grows indoors or travels a long distance to reach the store.
