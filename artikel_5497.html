Hedge fund pressures Invitation Homes to speed up home sales

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Land & Buildings sent an open letter to Invitation Homes shareholders arguing the single-family rental REIT trades about 40% below the value of its homes and has underperformed major benchmarks for years. The activist investor is urging the company to sell homes individually at retail prices and commit to returning at least $2 billion a year to shareholders via buybacks or special dividends, while sharply reducing acquisitions and development. The clash could reshape capital allocation strategies in the institutional single-family rental sector and, if the plan gains traction, would push more homes from rental portfolios into the for-sale market.  AI Summary

Activist investor Land & Buildings Investment Management is pressing single-family rental giant Invitation Homes to accelerate home sales, sharply increase capital returns and scale back growth plans, arguing the company’s stock trades at a steep discount to the value of its houses.

In an open letter to shareholders released Monday, the Stamford, Connecticut-based hedge fund said it believes Invitation Homes is “among the most undervalued companies in the REIT sector” and criticized the board and management for what it called “complacency” and “half measures” in addressing the gap.

Land & Buildings, which owns an undisclosed stake in Invitation Homes, is led by Jonathan Litt, a longtime activist in the real estate investment trust sector. Invitation Homes (NYSE: INVH) is the nation’s largest owner of single-family rental homes, with more than 80,000 properties concentrated in Sun Belt and other high-growth markets.

Land & Buildings said the public market values Invitation Homes’ properties at roughly $270,000 per home, compared with an average sale price of about $460,000 on homes the company has sold since December 2025. That implies a discount of about 40%, according to the letter, which was disseminated via GlobeNewswire.

The firm said it separately valued more than 90% of the company’s wholly owned portfolio, “address by address,” and arrived at an estimated average value of approximately $425,000 per home. That analysis underpins its net asset value estimate of about $45 per share, which Land & Buildings said is more than 65% above Invitation Homes’ current trading price even after assuming 6% selling costs on every home.

“REITs with sound fundamentals and no distressed assets do not trade at such a discount indefinitely. Something has to give,” the firm said in the letter.

“Invitation Homes’ Board of Directors and management team are committed to acting in the best interests of the Company and its shareholders to deliver long-term, sustainable value,” says a prepared statement from the company. “Our focus is on strengthening our foundation and enhancing the resident experience that sets us apart. This focus has resulted in solid operational and financial performance in recent quarters, supported by consistent demand, strong retention, and continued execution across our core markets.”

“We are always open to hearing and considering the perspectives of our shareholders. Members of our Board and management team have had numerous discussions with Land & Buildings over the past several months to better understand its views, as we would with any shareholder. While we are disappointed that Land & Buildings has chosen to make its issues public at this time, we remain open to further engagement.”

Land & Buildings has highlighted what it described as sustained underperformance. Over the trailing one-, three- and five-year periods, Invitation Homes has lagged the S&P 500, the broader REIT index, its closest peer American Homes 4 Rent and the proxy peer group the board uses for benchmarking executive compensation, the letter said.

Over the past three years, Invitation Homes ranks second to last in that compensation peer group, trailing the group average by more than 35 percentage points, according to Land & Buildings. Shareholders who held the stock for five years have lost more than 20% of their investment, including dividends, the letter said, even as U.S. home prices have continued to climb.

The firm noted that Invitation Homes’ shares now trade at an implied capitalization rate of about 7%, roughly 60 basis points wider than American Homes 4 Rent, and at a lower multiple of adjusted funds from operations. The stock trades only modestly above its $20 IPO price from early 2017 while national home prices are up nearly 80% over that period, according to the letter.

Land & Buildings blamed Invitation Homes’ strategy for much of the gap. The company was a net buyer of homes in every year from 2022 through 2025 despite “a poor cost of capital,” and earnings have “repeatedly fallen short of expectations,” the firm said.

The letter also pointed to legal and regulatory costs. Land & Buildings said Invitation Homes has paid more than $79 million to resolve four legal matters related to resident treatment and business practices, including a $48 million settlement with the Federal Trade Commission in 2024. At the same time, the board approved more than $25 million in special retention awards in March 2026 for the CEO and three other executives, according to the firm.

“This troubling misalignment and lack of accountability is deeply concerning,” Land & Buildings said.

The activist laid out a three-part plan it says could unlock value and narrow the discount to net asset value:

Land & Buildings estimated that a multi-year, programmatic asset sale and capital return program could deliver “substantial annual accretion” to both earnings and NAV per share, supported by what it pegged as nearly $40 billion of gross asset value.

The letter criticized what Land & Buildings characterized as incremental steps by Invitation Homes, including recent home sales and share repurchases, arguing those moves have not been sized or communicated in a way that would change how investors value the company.

“Half measures appear to be this Board’s modus operandi: do just enough to relieve pressure while preserving the status quo,” the firm wrote.

Land & Buildings also objected to the board’s Nov. 11 appointment of Colin Connolly, CEO of office REIT Cousins Properties, as a new director. The firm said the board had previously told it that refreshment was not needed and argued that adding another sitting REIT chief executive provided “duplicative skill sets” rather than shareholder alignment and capital allocation expertise.

The activist said independent research firm Green Street wrote less than a year ago that Invitation Homes should “shrink its portfolio, return capital to shareholders, and redirect the bulk of its energy internally on operations until its valuation improves.” Land & Buildings said it had urged management to outline a clear, multi-year capital return plan at REIT conferences in September but came away with “little more than lip service.”

Invitation Homes did not immediately respond to a request for comment on the letter or the firm’s proposals. The company has previously defended its strategy of balancing internal growth, acquisitions and select dispositions as it manages a large, geographically diverse portfolio in a tight housing market.

The campaign underscores how corporate decisions at large institutional single-family landlords can influence for-sale and rental supply in already constrained markets. Land & Buildings’ proposal would require Invitation Homes to sell thousands of homes a year to owner-occupiers or directly to existing tenants at retail pricing.

For homebuilders and real estate agents, a large program of individual home sales by Invitation Homes could add incremental existing-home inventory in high-demand metros where resale listings remain scarce. The suggested 6% down-payment rebate for tenants could create a new pipeline of first-time buyers but might also draw scrutiny from policymakers and tenant advocates who have criticized investor ownership of single-family homes.

For other single-family rental REITs and institutional owners, the dispute highlights growing pressure from investors to prioritize buybacks and capital returns over portfolio growth when shares trade at discounts to estimated property values. If Invitation Homes embraced or was forced into a multi-year shrink-to-grow strategy, it could reset expectations for capital allocation across the sector.

Land & Buildings said it prefers to work “constructively” with Invitation Homes but “will not hesitate to take whatever steps we believe are necessary to maximize value,” a signal that a proxy fight or board challenge is on the table heading into the 2027 proxy season.
