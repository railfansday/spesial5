Microsoft 365 outage credits: 7 things businesses should know about SLA claims

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Microsoft 365 outage service credits exist, but businesses buying through a Cloud Solution Provider claim them via that provider, for the affected service only, prorated by outage length

A Microsoft $MSFT 365 outage can earn a business a service credit. Microsoft's partner guidance describes that remedy as a credit and does not call it a cash refund. The way to claim it depends on who sold the subscription.

For companies that buy through Microsoft's Cloud Solution Provider program, known as CSP, the process is set out in Microsoft Learn documentation for Partner Center. The page is titled "How and when to request a credit from Microsoft" and was last updated on June 16, 2026. It is written for partners, not end customers. That means the business that lost access to a service does not file the claim itself. Its provider does.

The guidance covers products bought in the legacy commerce experience as well as the new commerce experience, which Microsoft abbreviates as NCE. It applies the service level agreement policy to services sold through CSP. The scenarios that qualify for a credit are listed in a separate document, the Online Services Consolidated SLA. The Partner Center page points to that document rather than restating its terms.

Credits are also limited in scope. A credit attaches to the service that went down, not to every app in a bundled plan. Its size is prorated according to which service was affected and how long the disruption lasted. Requests for Azure credits follow a different filing deadline from requests for all other services.

Microsoft's Partner Center page also handles credits that have nothing to do with downtime, such as credits for accidental purchases and duplicate orders on legacy products. Those follow their own rules and are not remedies for an outage.

The direct-purchase path is a separate question. The Partner Center guidance does not describe how organizations that buy straight from Microsoft claim SLA credits. The steps below should not be read as covering them.

The seven slides below are a selection of points about Microsoft 365 outage service credits for CSP-sold services, not a full rulebook. They cover who can file, how credits are scoped and prorated, and the deadline split. They also cover the evidence a request needs, where to find an incident ID and how advisory incidents differ from outages.

Only Cloud Solution Provider direct providers and indirect providers can submit a service level agreement credit request for a Microsoft $MSFT 365 outage on services sold through CSP. Indirect resellers are not permitted to file these requests.

That rule comes from Microsoft's Partner Center guidance on Microsoft Learn, which sets out how partners request credits from Microsoft. The page is written for partners in the CSP program, not for end customers. Its outage rules apply to services sold through that channel.

The CSP program has two partner tiers that matter here. A direct provider buys Microsoft cloud services from Microsoft and sells them straight to customers. An indirect provider supplies those services to indirect resellers, who then sell them to business customers. Under the credit rules, the right to ask Microsoft for an outage credit sits with the provider in both models. An indirect reseller cannot file one on a customer's behalf.

For a business customer, the practical effect is that an outage claim starts with whichever company sits on the provider tier of the relationship. A customer that buys from an indirect reseller depends on the indirect provider behind that reseller to file the request.

Inside the provider, the task falls to a specific role. Partner Center is the portal Microsoft partners use to manage customers, orders, billing and support requests. The Admin agent role gives partner staff administrative access to the customer tenants that partner manages. A person in the Admin agent role handles credit requests. The request goes to Microsoft as a support ticket filed in Partner Center.

The rules are not limited to newer subscriptions. Microsoft's guidance applies the SLA outage policy to products bought under the legacy commerce experience. It applies equally to products bought under the new commerce experience, known as NCE. A customer with a mix of older and newer CSP subscriptions follows the same provider-led route for both.

A Microsoft $MSFT outage credit covers only the service that went down, not the full subscription. Microsoft's Partner Center guidance gives an example: an Office 365 suite customer whose only outage was in SharePoint can receive service level agreement (SLA) credit for SharePoint alone.

The credit does not reach the rest of that customer's plan. Microsoft decides credits according to which services an outage affected.

An Office 365 suite bundles several online services under one subscription, and SharePoint is one of them. In the SharePoint example, the services that kept running stay outside the claim. Only the service that failed is part of it.

The guidance refers to affected services in the plural. That wording ties a credit to each service an incident touched, not to the subscription as a whole. A disruption confined to SharePoint produces a claim for SharePoint and nothing else in the bundle.

The way a business pays and the way a claim is assessed do not line up. The suite arrives as one subscription on one bill. An outage claim is still weighed service by service, and the bill does not set its boundaries.

The example comes from Microsoft's page on how and when to request a credit. That page is written for partners in the Cloud Solution Provider (CSP) program, and it applies its SLA policy to services sold through CSP. It does not describe how customers who buy directly from Microsoft claim credits. The SharePoint example belongs to that CSP context.

For a business reviewing an outage, the starting point for any claim is the specific service that failed. The plan the business holds does not set the scope of the credit.

Microsoft $MSFT prorates SLA credits for services sold through its Cloud Solution Provider (CSP) program. The size of a credit depends on two things: which service went down and how long the outage lasted.

Proration means the credit is a proportional amount rather than a flat payment. A qualifying outage does not produce a fixed sum that is the same for every customer and every incident. The amount moves with the details of the disruption.

The first factor is the affected service. Microsoft's Partner Center guidance on credits ties the calculation to the product that suffered downtime. It looks at the service that failed, not at the full subscription that service belongs to.

The second factor is duration. Under the same guidance, the length of the outage feeds into the size of the credit. A short interruption and a long one on the same service are not treated as the same event.

The two factors apply together. Two outages of equal length on different services need not produce matching credits. Two outages on the same service need not match either if one ran longer than the other. Neither factor alone settles the amount.

Whether an outage qualifies at all is a separate test. Microsoft lists the qualifying scenarios in the Online Services Consolidated SLA, its service level agreement for online services. The Partner Center guidance states that this document also applies to services sold through CSP.

The Online Services Consolidated SLA is also where the detailed terms sit, including credit percentages, downtime thresholds and uptime commitments. A business weighing a claim can read the section of that document covering the service that went down. That check shows whether the disruption meets a qualifying scenario before a request is filed.

Microsoft $MSFT applies one deadline to outage credit requests for Azure and a different deadline to requests for all other services sold through its Cloud Solution Provider (CSP) program. Microsoft's Partner Center guidance on requesting credits places Azure in its own category for timing.

A business that buys both Azure and Microsoft 365 through a provider therefore works with two request deadlines, not one. An Azure outage is claimed under the Azure deadline. A Microsoft 365 outage falls under the deadline that covers every other service.

The split also applies when one disruption affects both Azure and a Microsoft 365 service. Each affected service is claimed under its own category's timing. An organization that runs workloads on Azure alongside Microsoft 365 apps cannot treat one request window as covering both.

The business does not submit the request itself. A CSP direct provider or an indirect provider files it as a support ticket in Partner Center. Indirect resellers are not permitted to file. A customer that wants a credit for downtime has to bring the outage to its provider. The provider's filing then has to land within the deadline for the affected service.

That handoff adds a step between the outage and the claim. A customer that reports an incident late leaves its provider less time to prepare and submit the ticket. Raising the outage with the provider soon after it happens helps keep the request inside the window.

Meeting a deadline does not by itself make an outage eligible. Whether a period of downtime qualifies for a credit is set out in Microsoft's Online Services Consolidated SLA, not in the deadline rules.

A Microsoft $MSFT 365 SLA credit request must include identifying details. Among them are the customer's tenant GUID, the outage ID and the customer's email address.

Microsoft's Partner Center guidance lists these as items a Cloud Solution Provider partner supplies with an outage claim. They connect the request to one customer and one recorded incident.

The tenant GUID is the globally unique identifier assigned to a customer's Microsoft 365 tenant. It points the claim at a single organization's environment.

The outage ID identifies the service incident behind the claim. It links the request to a specific event Microsoft recorded, not to a general report of downtime.

The customer email gives a contact address tied to the affected business.

These details go into the credit request, which a CSP direct provider or indirect provider submits as a Partner Center support ticket. The end customer does not file it.

The guidance asks for further items beyond these three. A business that buys Microsoft 365 through a provider can keep its tenant GUID and the relevant outage ID on hand. The provider then has them ready when it opens the ticket.

The outage ID that a Microsoft $MSFT 365 SLA credit request needs is listed in the Microsoft 365 admin center. Each incident ID shown there includes an abbreviation for the affected service.

Microsoft's Partner Center guidance names the outage ID as a required part of a credit request. The ID ties a claim to one specific recorded incident rather than to a general report of downtime.

The abbreviation inside the ID shows which service the incident involved. That label connects the incident to the specific product a credit would cover.

For services sold through the Cloud Solution Provider program, the provider that files the claim puts the outage ID in its Partner Center support ticket. A business that buys Microsoft 365 through a provider can find the incident in the admin center and pass its ID along to that provider.

Microsoft $MSFT's Partner Center credit guidance treats an advisory incident as a separate category from an outage. It also sets out what follows once a credit request is approved.

Both points apply to Microsoft 365 services sold through the Cloud Solution Provider program, known as CSP. The guidance applies its service level agreement policy to those services.

The credit process in the guidance is built around outages. Advisory incidents sit outside that outage category. Anyone preparing a claim should therefore confirm which type of incident they are dealing with before they file.

An approved request produces an SLA credit for the affected service. Microsoft's guidance presents that outcome as a credit.
