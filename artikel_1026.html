Chao Liu has driven up and down the US East Coast — from Florida to Massachusetts — before detouring to France. His latest stop is at IFREMER, where he will continue to pretend to be a real scientist until someone finally catches on.

The machine sat between the deep-fryer truck and a three-legged goat, housed inside a canvas tent that smelled heavily of kerosene and stale popcorn. It was a massive, rivet-popped iron boiler known officially as the Differential Analytical Oscillator, although the hand-painted sign out front simply read: THE DAO BOILER — KNOWS ALL, TELLS ALL. FIVE CENTS.

By late afternoon, the fairground was a soup of churned mud and diesel exhaust.

“It don’t do lottery numbers,” the operator said, not looking up from the greasy carburettor he was scraping with a butter knife. He was a small, sour man named Silas, wearing a leather apron stiff with soot. “And if you’re gonna ask if your brother’s a bastard, save your nickel. The answer is always yes.”

Jerry shifted his boots in the sawdust. He was 19, wore a stained canvas jacket, and had spent the past three days trying to look tragically heartbroken on his front porch. “I don’t care about the lottery. I want to know about Clara.”

Silas sighed, spitting a dark stream of tobacco juice into a bucket of scrap iron. “The butcher’s girl? She left on the 4.10 rail to Lowmarsh. What’s to know? She wanted indoor plumbing and a man who didn’t smell like fertilizer.”

“No,” Jerry said, his jaw tightening as he dropped a heavy brass nickel onto the tool chest. “She said it wasn’t that. She said there was a … a fundamental misalignment in our vectors. I want to know what that means. I want the real, exact reason.”

Silas looked at the coin, then at Jerry’s face. He let out a dry, rattling chuckle. “The exact reason? All right, kid. But the tub don’t do poetry. You want the low-voltage ribbon or the full squeeze?”

“Give me the whole thing,” Jerry said, crossing his arms. “Don’t spare the details.”

Silas shrugged, picking up a heavy, lead-lined copper helmet attached to the boiler by a thick bundle of rubber-coated cables. It looked like an old diving apparatus, dripping with mineral oil. “Put it on. Full throttle means you get the absolute breakdown. Don’t say I didn’t warn you.”

Jerry lifted the helmet. It was incredibly heavy, cold against his ears. He lowered it over his head, plunging himself into absolute darkness.

For three seconds, there was only the smell of hot grease and the rhythmic thump-thump-thump of the internal pistons. Then, the engine engaged the clutch.

The Universe didn’t open up with grand spiritual enlightenment. It opened with a relentless, deafening avalanche of raw data.

Jerry didn’t see Clara’s face, or a vision of her on the train. Instead, a cold, mechanical voice began reading a molecular ledger directly into his auditory cortex. It informed him that on Tuesday at 3.14 p.m., the atmospheric humidity had caused a 0.04% swelling in the leather of Clara’s left boot, which altered her gait by three millimetres. This structural variance, combined with the gravitational pull of a passing manure spreader, had shifted her centre of gravity exactly enough to cause a microscopic tear in the cotton fibre of her socks.

The machine kept going. It explained that the chemical composition of Jerry’s afternoon sweat contained an excess of sulfur molecules that reacted poorly with the zinc coating on his porch railing, creating a localized scent profile that her olfactory nerves categorized under Sub-Class 4: Decaying Organic Matter. It gave him the exact thermodynamic equation of the breeze that had carried the smell to her nose. It gave him the precise atomic mass of the dust particle she choked on when she tried to say goodbye.

It went on for what felt like an eternity, resolving her ‘broken heart’ into a massive, agonizingly boring list of friction coefficients, cellular degeneration rates and barometric pressure shifts. There was no love. There was no betrayal. There was only a three-hour maths problem about why a girl walked towards a train station.

The helmet was violently yanked off his head.

Jerry stumbled backwards, blinking against the harsh light of the tent. The machine was smoking, its violet glow dying back into a dull, rusted grey. Silas was standing over him, holding the helmet, looking thoroughly bored.
