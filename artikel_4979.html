James Gray on <i><em>Paper Tiger</em></i>, Adam Driver, and Why Great Leading Men Need Danger and Vulnerability

The filmmaker talks ambition, masculinity, and why he’s always trying to mine his own life for the truth.

ONCE YOU’VE GONE to outer space with Brad Pitt and almost gotten eaten in the Amazon, where else is there to head but home? That’s the question filmmaker James Gray asked himself after wrapping the World War I-era adventure epic The Lost City of Z and the celestial adventure Ad Astra. His answer: “I guess the conscious desire was to go back to what I know.”

That resulted, first, in 2022’s Armageddon Time, a largely autobiographical story of a young Gray growing up in Queens with his imperfect parents (Anne Hathaway and Jeremy Strong) and loving grandfather (Anthony Hopkins). His latest film, Paper Tiger, was originally conceived as a sequel to that one, but eventually settled into a separate mostly autobiographical and intimate tale of the director’s youth.

The new film once again features characters explicitly based on his parents, this time with the bespectacled father, Irwin, played by Miles Teller and the expressive mother, Hester, played by Scarlett Johansson. Naive Irwin gets drawn into a sketchy business plan by his brash brother Gary, a former cop and ambitious entrepreneur played by Adam Driver—and things only go south when Irwin proudly decides to show his new venture off to his sons and sees something he should not have.

Paper Tiger also marks a return to Gray’s roots as a teller of crime stories that will stop you in your tracks. (Longtime fans will remember he made his directorial debut with the Tim Roth-led hitman thriller Little Odessa in 1994.) But what could be just another crime drama in the hands of a lesser filmmaker becomes a grounded human story about the downside of ambition with Gray. His films are arresting because they feel like they could happen to you, too.

This new film, then, allowed the 57-year-old director to continue doing what he’s done his whole career: tell stories that, while lightly fictionalized, are still, at their core, true—regardless of genre. “I don’t see the movies as ‘crime dramas,’ even though clearly that’s what they are, because that’s part of the fabric of my young life growing up,” he says. “In my father’s case, and in my family’s case, it’s in our DNA. And so, for me, what is simply a memory of some sort, becomes quantifiable as a crime drama. Really, what you’re seeing is me trying to be very sincere with you about my own experience.”

It’s a story that’s been deep within him for a long time—and one he’s glad to have finally purged. “Now that I’ve gotten out of my system a little, I’m ready to expand the palette again,” he says.

As Paper Tiger expands to a nationwide release in theaters, Gray spoke with Men’s Health about finding great leading men, mining his life for stories, and the key to making everything personal.

Plus—read Gray’s take on his former Lost City of Z star Robert Pattinson as James Bond, from just a couple of days before the casting became official.

MEN’S HEALTH: You’ve worked with stars like Brad Pitt, Joaquin Phoenix, and Charlie Hunnam, and now Paper Tiger adds Adam Driver and Miles Teller to that list. So, what makes a great leading man?

JAMES GRAY: To be completely candid, some aspect of what you’re asking is unknowable until you see it on screen. It’s funny, I was reading about the casting of Peter O’Toole in Lawrence of Arabia. He had never done anything on screen, and all of a sudden they put him in the movie, and there’s magic. There are other people who you try to put in movies over and over again, and it never really clicks, for whatever reason. There’s an intangible quality to a great actor that we almost never can put our finger on.

That said, I’m looking for an ability to convey two things at once: danger and vulnerability. A sensitivity. Often, what you find is it comes from intelligence. I’ve never worked with a great dumb actor. It does not exist. If the actor is marvelous—and I’ve been lucky enough to work with almost exclusively great actors—they are all brilliant in an emotional way. Does it mean they can quote Proust? No, but that doesn’t matter. They have an emotional awareness, and they can read a room, and they have a shrewdness. All of this stuff goes into the work, because if you have a close-up of an actor, you can immediately see what they’re thinking, if they are thinking. I’m looking for both danger and vulnerability, in kind of equal measure. And you see it over and over in great performances. In fact, I can only think of one performance in all of cinema history, really only one, that is both great and has no danger in it, and that would be what Jeff Bridges does in The Big Lebowski. It has no danger at all, but it’s still a great performance. I’m not exactly sure how he pulled it off—it’s a real achievement. Maybe because he’s so sensitive and lovable in it that you fall for him, you know? But almost always, you have to have that aspect.

MH: When you’re casting for movies like Paper Tiger and Armageddon Time, which are ostensibly based on your own life, do you feel more of a pressure to make sure you get it right, as opposed to a story you’re perhaps a layer removed from, like The Lost City of Z?

JG: It’s funny, I’m not sure how much difference there is. I know Lost City of Z is about a guy in Edwardian England going into Amazonia, but when I was conceiving of that movie, I never tried to distance myself from Percy Fawcett at all. I tried to, in fact, invest myself in him. He was a dreamer, someone who felt slighted by the system, and he wanted to prove himself over and over again. He didn’t have medals. He wanted to be celebrated, and he finally gets celebrated, and he needs more. All of that is in us.

So, your question is a very good one, but in all truth, I’m trying—and maybe you don’t succeed, of course—in all cases to put myself into the work. That’s what I would say is making something personal. That’s very different from making something autobiographical. Autobiographical adheres to the facts of your life, and it is true—these last two pictures have been quite autobiographical. But that’s just so that hopefully I have an easier time in getting at what it is that I’m thinking about. They’re different modes, but you’re still trying to do the same thing, which is to get at your interiority.

MH: Miles Teller plays a character based on your father. I’ve never seen him do anything like what he does in Paper Tiger. What sold you on him?

JG: I had seen a bunch of his work, and I’d also spoken to my esteemed assistant director, a wonderful, beautiful man named Doug Torres, who’d worked on a movie with Miles Teller. I knew about him through him, and also the people that had worked with him. I had seen a film called Bleed for This, where he plays Vinny Pazienza, and his performance has what we were just talking about. He’s a boxer, he has a toughness, but it’s a very vulnerable performance. I didn’t want to lean into the nebbish, schlub aspect to the character at all. I wanted the character to have a certain fortitude, because then the vulnerability can really come through. In other words, if you’re just casting a movie and the guy is a nerd or whatever, and you’re watching a nerd be nerdy, it’s not that interesting. What I found was that Miles has an alpha quality, but to see a tenderness inside of that was the thing that really interested me. And I saw it in that film.

MH: Adam Driver is a sensational actor too. What was the appeal and experience of working with Adam?

JG: Adam has a very powerful onscreen dark quality, but he’s also open. He does completely different things from take to take. He’s always trying to find something new, something inventive. And he has tremendous craft. You can tell he’s really studied, and so you have both the craft and also he’s not in a rut. It’s just small things sometimes that you fall in love with. You’ve seen the film—he comes into the house, he brings Peter Luger’s, and he grabs the lamp, you know? A piece of set dressing. And he holds it under his face, and he says, “Tonight, Luger’s is coming to us.” So, all of a sudden, he’s like a showman. It’s these little touches, where he understands character. And again, I know I’m repeating myself—this comes from intelligence. He’s incredibly intelligent. He sees bullshit a mile away. That is a very good thing for an actor. He is a truth-teller. Great actors are truth machines. They will not let you lie in a scene. They are always open to the moment.

MH: That character is fascinating to me, because in other movies like this, he might turn into a villain. But your story plays with such shades of gray, and it really stands out.

JG: The idea was to set up a scenario where you see the guy and your immediate impression might be “Oh, he’s a cop, he’s bringing food over. He must be a jerk, or he’s trying to scheme, or something.” But in fact it turns out the exact opposite; he had nothing but very pure intentions, and winds up sacrificing himself. That was very much part of taking the audience’s expectations and subverting them. Now, when you do something like that, you’re going to have, hopefully, a small percentage of the audience, whining and complaining, telling you What are you doing there? How could he be naive? But you’re also going to have a large percentage of the audience that’s going to embrace the fact that it’s not a cliché. I was leaning into the sense Adam has as an actor, saying, “Yes, but we’re going to trick you.” And in the end, he’s going to have a generosity and a nobility to his character.

MH: Adam’s character is really brash and confident—it seems like he’s used to getting what he wants and isn’t afraid to kick some ass. Miles’s character, on the other hand, is a lot more reserved and sometimes naive. How did you go about designing the story around these two guys who each carry a very different brand of masculinity and see the world in different ways?

JG: It’s interesting you bring that up, because the operating principle of the movie… I was up very late one night, and I was watching—as one would—C-SPAN, I guess to try to put myself to sleep. And sometimes they televise the speeches that CEOs give at Davos, or that kind of thing. And I saw the CEO of a large corporation, and he was giving a speech, and he said “95% of people don’t know there’s a game.” He said, “Of the remaining 5%, 4% know there’s a game and don’t know how to play it, and then the top 1% know how to play it, and will win.”

I think his percentages are off—I think there are a lot more people who know there’s a game but don’t know how to play it, and that’s why we have a lot of anger in the world. But I thought of the story this way. You have Gary, a character who knows there’s a game, and knows how to play it, but he won’t make the ethical and moral compromises to win. You’ve got Irwin, who knows there’s a game, but has no idea how to play it. He’s smart—he’s an engineer. But he’s not shrewd. It’s a very different intelligence. And then you’ve got Scarlett, who probably knows there’s a game and maybe even knows how to play it, but fate has a different plan for her. So, the movie was organized along these principles. That this idea that our society always rewards the smartest and best, that it’s a meritocracy, is not really true. It rewards a certain kind of smarts, along with a certain kind of willingness to compromise. I saw Adam and Miles in this framework.

MH: Do you often find the germ of an idea in mundane places like watching C-SPAN in the middle of the night?

JG: One of the horrible things about creativity, and it’s also one of the truths, is that it is a very mercurial, impatient, sometimes too patient, thing, and sometimes ideas come to you in the most random of ways. The biggest obligation you have to your imagination is your willingness to make the random idea connect. In other words, the essence of creativity is your ability to make connections. So, sometimes, you get inspiration from totally bizarre sources that you’d never imagine. It’s up to your imagination to be open to the randomness of these connections, and to try and pull from them the source of your idea. It can sometimes be totally different, like, you’re in nature, you get an idea, or you hear someone, a homeless man, say something—that’s an idea. It can come from anywhere. And then we have the idea, of course, of myth, to give it some kind of structure. But the random idea is of tremendous value. It can come from C-SPAN, or it can come from a baby crying. It’s all part of the soup.

MH: With this movie, as you have so often throughout your career, you’re mining from your own life. I’m sure many of these memories are painful, so how do you protect yourself emotionally and mentally to avoid falling into a dark and difficult place?

JG: It’s an excellent question, and the answer is that you kind of can’t. I wish I could give you a glib response—the way that I protect myself is X—but what I owe you, really, is something that is honest, that is sincere. And I don’t owe you the idea that I should make myself look good, and that you should like me. I owe you my own version of the truth. So, if I’m working, it’s not fun all the time. Sometimes, you have to do things that are uncomfortable, that are unflinching, that are honest. And it’s not always pleasant. We recreated a fight between my parents, when my mother hit my father. Scarlett and Miles created it almost exactly as I recall it. This is my job. That’s why I do it. I do it to give it to you. And through that, you find the air of compassion and sympathy for a collective experience.

In other words, you’re not presenting a fantasy that has no relationship to honest emotion. And, by the way, when I say ‘fantasy,’ I don’t mean Star Wars can’t do it. Star Wars can do that. When he says, “No, Luke, I am your father,” that is actually connecting to something real, in the guise of a fantasy. When I say ‘fantasy,’ what I really mean is the filmmaker is trying to get you to like him or her or them, and that’s not my job. Sometimes you go to these unpleasant places, and it’s my job to cope.

MH: You’ve mentioned expanding your palette now that you’ve gotten these stories out of your system—do you have anything specific in mind? Is there anything you haven’t done yet that you want to try to tackle?

JG: I would love to do a Western. I would love to do a horror movie. I can’t believe I’m going to say this—I would love to do a musical. I don’t know what kind of shape the musical would take, but all of these genres are different forms of expression of the same ideas. I find the Westerns of John Ford unbelievably powerful to this day, because they’re always expressing something that’s inside Ford. Especially the films he made after World War II, which have such a thread of loss in them. And they’re very powerful to this day. You see John Wayne struggling with moral and ethical catastrophes in The Searchers, and you see a kind of post-war reckoning with what was a catastrophe of moral proportions in World War II, and you see it in John Wayne. You see the person of Ford coming out in that way, and using it in that canvas, the American West. That’s beautiful, and a way into the dark side of our personalities. Really, it becomes about me trying to find a story that will accommodate that, and that’s the difficult thing.
