Fraudsters Used Fake Crypto Exchanges to Steal $37 Million, SEC Alleges

The Securities and Exchange Commission has charged eight entities with running a sprawling scheme that used fake crypto trading platforms and sham investment advisers to steal more than $37 million from U.S. investors.

The case targets eight Colorado-registered entities that the SEC says were likely operated by individuals overseas.

According to the complaint, filed in Colorado federal court, the defendants lured victims into believing they were working with a legitimate investment adviser and crypto trading platform, when both were entirely fake, “simply fronts to steal investor money.”

The mechanics will sound familiar to anyone who has followed the rise of “pig butchering” scams. The SEC said the fraudsters advertised on social media to funnel investors into group and private chats, where operators posing as advisers first coached them to trade stocks on real U.S. markets before steering them toward a bogus crypto platform and pressing them to send as much money as possible. Victims believed they were buying real crypto assets; in reality, the funds were stolen.

To fake legitimacy, the operators went to unusual lengths, the SEC said, fabricating phony SEC certificates and even making filings with the SEC, FINRA and FinCEN, then pointing skeptical investors to those documents as proof they were real.

The scheme ran from August 2024 through May 2026 across four adviser-and-platform pairs, with the perpetrators abandoning one pair and spinning up the next every few months.

“The SEC continues to seek out and expose fraudulent investment scams that prey on U.S. investors,” said Laura D’Allaird, chief of the agency’s Cyber and Emerging Technologies Unit, urging the public to keep reporting such schemes.

The charged entities, carrying officious-sounding names like Convergent Wealth Advisors and Essential Crypto Exchange, face allegations of violating federal antifraud provisions, with several also accused of breaching the Investment Advisers Act.

The SEC is seeking injunctions, disgorgement and civil penalties. It credited cooperation from regulators in Hong Kong, Singapore, China and Switzerland, underscoring the cross-border nature of the fraud.

The case reflects a persistent threat, as fake platforms and social-engineering scams continue to drain billions from victims worldwide.

Chainalysis and others have documented how such operations, often run by organized crime from Southeast Asia, have scaled with the help of AI-generated personas and scripts, turning romance and investment cons into one of crypto’s costliest categories of fraud.
