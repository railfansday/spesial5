Builders are looking for a bottom. 2027 plans can’t wait that long

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Builders are balancing incentives, margins, and cash recovery as resale inventory rises and rental options improve. Key signals include a 5.5% drop in existing-home sales, 5.1 months of supply, and widespread builder discounting.  AI Summary

We’re at that agonizing moment in a housing market downturn when finding “the bottom” becomes the first next thing to hope for.

The trouble is, market bottoms tend to reveal themselves only in the 20/20 clarity of hindsight, not in any set of measures we can confidently decipher in the present.

For America’s homebuilders, residential developers and investors, this creates an uncomfortable predicament. They’re not just competing with the clock. They’re competing with other parts of the residential continuum –rentals and resales – that are also competing with the clock. They must make decisions that will determine their performance in 2027 before they know whether the worst is behind them.

Decisions, for instance, like how much to build, where to commit capital, what prices will move homes, and what sales pace will sustain profitability.

The evidence accumulating as 2026 nears year-end offers little or no assurance that the housing market has found solid footing. Weakening household demand, rising competition in the resale market, increasingly attractive rental alternatives, and persistent uncertainty about employment, incomes, and home values are making it harder to balance pricing and sales pace sustainably.

The question for builders is not whether they can predict the precise moment the market turns. It is whether they can adapt their businesses to current conditions, preserve financial and operating flexibility to withstand further weakness, and position themselves to benefit when household demand eventually stabilizes.

The latest evidence points to a market that is still losing momentum

Thursday’s report from the National Association of Realtors offered another indication of the challenges facing the housing market. Existing-home sales fell 5.5% in October to a seasonally adjusted annual rate of 3.75 million, down 8.8% from a year earlier and the weakest reading since August 2010.

The number of existing homes for sale increased to 1.59 million, up 4.6% from October 2025. At the current sales pace, that represented 5.1 months of supply, compared with 4.4 months a year earlier and the highest level since November 2015.

The national median existing-home price, meanwhile, rose just 0.4% from a year earlier to $416,700. That combination of declining transactions, rising months’ supply and relatively stable reported prices illustrates a housing market in which buyers and sellers have yet to agree on home values under prevailing financing and economic conditions.

The numbers reveal a critical point of distinction. A rising months’ supply does not necessarily mean homeowners are flooding the market with listings. It can result from sales falling faster than inventory is being absorbed. Both dynamics are relevant to builders, whose homes compete with an increasing selection of existing properties for buyers.

The latest data offer little encouragement to builders expecting a quick rebound in household demand. The National Association of Home Builders reported that September new-home sales fell to a 676,000-unit annual pace, 5.3% below the year-earlier level, while the supply of new homes for sale remained elevated at 8.4 months.

The October NAHB/Wells Fargo Housing Market Index fell to 29, its lowest level since June 2012. Forty-three percent of builders reported cutting prices, while 65% continued to offer sales incentives.

Those findings point to a persistent problem for homebuilding companies: Builders continue to make meaningful concessions to stimulate sales, but households’ response remains insufficient to establish confidence in a sustained recovery.

Mortgage rates remain an important constraint, but the industry’s demand problem is increasingly difficult to explain through financing costs alone.

Cotality Chief Economist Dr. Selma Hepp points to the cumulative financial pressures facing prospective buyers. Elevated mortgage payments, tariff-related construction costs, insurance premiums, property taxes, utilities, and everyday household expenses are affecting households’ ability and willingness to take on the obligations of ownership.

“For housing, the key question is not simply whether mortgage rates decline, but whether they fall enough to meaningfully improve purchasing power and bring more buyers and sellers back to the market,” Hepp says.

Her observation highlights a sharp difference between qualifying for a mortgage and deciding that buying a home makes financial sense.

A household may technically qualify for a mortgage yet still find that the monthly payment, down payment, and ongoing ownership costs leave too little room for savings, unexpected expenses, or other financial commitments. For households concerned about job security or declining home values, waiting may seem increasingly prudent.

The broader economy complicates that calculation. Higher-income households with substantial savings, accumulated home equity, or financial assets may be able to absorb elevated housing costs, whereas entry-level and middle-income households have far less flexibility.

That unevenness helps explain why a national decline in mortgage rates might boost sales only modestly. Some prospective buyers need lower monthly payments. Others need greater confidence that their incomes will remain secure, that their other living expenses will stabilize, and that the home they purchase will retain its value. These concerns are not necessarily resolved by a builder’s offer of a temporary mortgage-rate buydown.

The Federal Reserve’s latest Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey underscores the demand problem. Banks reported weaker demand across all residential mortgage categories in the third quarter, even as lending standards remained largely unchanged for most mortgage products. Banks also reported weaker demand and tighter lending standards for construction and land development loans.

The implications extend beyond individual homebuyers. When expected sales absorption weakens, the financial justification for developing additional lots, opening communities, and committing construction capital becomes more difficult.

One of the most important changes in homebuilding’s competitive environment has been the reversal of several conditions that supported new-home demand during the pandemic and its aftermath.

During that period, builders benefited from an unusually tight existing-home market. Homeowners who had secured historically low mortgage rates had little incentive to sell, limiting resale inventory and directing prospective buyers toward new construction.

At the same time, elevated rents in many markets made homeownership relatively more attractive. Builders with available lots, construction capacity, and mortgage financing relationships were positioned to capture buyers with fewer alternatives.

The appeal of renting is not limited to households that cannot afford to buy. For some households, renting lets them keep their savings, preserve geographic flexibility, and avoid the risk of buying a home before local prices have stabilized.

Existing homes are also becoming more formidable competitors. In markets where resale listings have increased and homes are taking longer to sell, prospective buyers can compare new construction with a wider selection of existing properties. Sellers who need to relocate, settle estates, or complete other transactions may be increasingly willing to negotiate.

Builders therefore face a three-way competitive challenge that was less pronounced during the pandemic-era housing boom. They must compete against other builders, existing-home sellers and rental operators, each offering a different mix of price, location, amenities, monthly cost and financial commitment.

This competition is especially intense in markets where builders have substantial inventories of completed homes and developed lots. A resale seller may have the financial flexibility to postpone a transaction, whereas a builder must continue to carry land, construction, financing and overhead costs until inventory converts to cash.

This pressure can prompt builders to adjust their effective prices sooner than many existing-home sellers. Yet lower new-home prices can also influence resale sellers’ expectations, triggering another round of competitive adjustments. The process may continue for some time before either market reaches a sustainable balance.

For builders, the most immediate challenge is determining how much pricing flexibility is needed to maintain sales velocity without sacrificing the profitability required to sustain their businesses. Reducing prices and offering incentives may increase orders, but those measures can also erode gross margins and reduce returns on capital already committed to land and construction. Maintaining prices may protect reported margins on individual transactions, but it can leave completed homes on the market longer, increase carrying costs, and delay the recovery of invested capital.

Neither approach necessarily produces a better outcome without understanding the relationships among sales pace, effective pricing, inventory costs and cash generation.

The problem becomes more difficult when household demand continues to weaken. Pricing decisions that seem sufficient to generate sales in one month may need to be reconsidered the next as competing builders adjust incentives, resale sellers lower expectations, or rental operators offer more attractive terms.

The industry’s challenge is therefore to find a lower price at which buyers will purchase homes and to determine whether homes can be produced, marketed, and sold at that price while still generating acceptable returns. Not one or the other, both. That distinction directly impacts 2027 budgets.

A community that achieves an acceptable gross margin with three sales per month may generate inadequate returns if absorption drops to one or two sales. Conversely, a lower-margin community that turns inventory quickly and requires less capital may outperform one with stronger nominal margins but slower cash recovery.

These tradeoffs will vary by market, product type, land basis, construction costs, and financing structure. A single national assumption about mortgage rates or home-price appreciation cannot resolve them.

The industry’s search for a bottom should not become a reason to postpone decisions about how businesses will operate if demand remains weak. Instead, the uncertainty should drive builders to test their 2027 plans against a broader range of outcomes, including scenarios in which conditions deteriorate further before improving.

The first priority is a more precise understanding of household purchasing power and competing housing alternatives in each community’s trade area.

Builders need to know not only which prospective customers qualify to purchase, but also what they are willing to pay when comparing a new home with available resales and rental properties. That analysis should include the full monthly cost of ownership, the incentives required to attract buyers, and the potential impact of additional competing supply.

The second priority is reassessing land commitments, community-opening schedules, and construction starts.

Projects approved under stronger demand assumptions may no longer meet their original return requirements. Builders should evaluate whether development schedules can be phased, whether lot deliveries can be adjusted, and whether to defer capital commitments until sales absorption justifies additional investment.

The third priority is to maintain the financial and operational flexibility to respond when conditions change.

That means closely monitoring completed-home inventory, construction cycle times, cancellation rates, financing costs, and cash recovery. It also means identifying opportunities to reduce product and construction costs rather than relying indefinitely on incentives to bridge the gap between household purchasing power and home prices.

These are practical operational decisions, not substitutes for a stronger housing market. They can, however, determine how much financial strain a builder faces while waiting for demand to stabilize. They may also determine which companies are positioned to invest as better opportunities emerge.

Hepp identifies the upcoming spring selling season as an important test of whether housing activity can begin to recover.

“Until then, I expect activity to remain relatively subdued, making the spring homebuying season an increasingly important test of whether the market can finally turn a corner,” she says.

Homebuilders cannot control when household demand stabilizes. They can control how quickly they recognize market changes, how effectively they adjust their cost structures and capital commitments, and how well they prepare their businesses for conditions that may remain difficult longer than expected.

The bottom will become clear in hindsight. The work of getting through it – and of emerging stronger – waits for no one.
