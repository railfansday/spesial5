10 flu shot myths and what the CDC said about them

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

Free daily briefing on global business news.

A 2011 gallery paired 12 flu vaccine fears with CDC answers. Here are 10 of those myths and what the agency said.

Flu season hands millions of Americans a small, personal choice. The decision takes a moment at a clinic, yet it rests as much on what people believe about the vaccine as on anything a doctor says. A person who trusts the shot books an appointment. A person who doubts it postpones the visit or skips it. Multiply those private calculations across the country, and beliefs about a single injection shape how families face the weeks ahead. The outcome touches more than the person in the chair. Coworkers, classmates, and relatives breathe the same air through those weeks.

Three worries keep surfacing as people weigh the shot. Some people fear the vaccine could trigger autism. Others suspect the shot delivers the illness it promises to prevent. A third group questions whether the injection accomplishes much of anything. Each fear carries a price. A parent worried about autism may keep a child unvaccinated. Someone convinced the needle brings on sickness may go without protection. A skeptic who sees no benefit has no reason to make the trip. Hesitation can also push the question down a crowded to-do list, where it can sit untouched. Answering these fears means setting each claim beside what public health authorities said about it, one at a time.

CBS News took on these objections in a photo gallery posted Dec. 6, 2011, as influenza made its yearly return. The gallery placed 12 widely held notions about influenza vaccines next to the position of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. Each entry pairs a short statement of the myth with a brief rebuttal. Its corrections cite research in general terms and name no researchers, trials, or sample sizes. The evidence here reflects the agency's stance as the outlet relayed it. This article examines 10 of the gallery's 12 myths. Every answer that follows describes what the agency said in 2011, and readers should check current guidance before acting on any of it.

Flu shots contained only inactivated viruses in the 2011 season, and the CDC held that particles in that condition could not infect anyone. People who expect the needle to deliver the disease it targets misjudge what goes into the syringe. A dose of disabled virus has no way to start a case of influenza.

Salt-water placebo shots exposed only two complaints unique to the flu vaccine, redness where the needle entered and a sore arm. Researchers gave one group the vaccine and another an injection of salt water, then compared what each set of people reported. Both differences stayed confined to the injection site, and neither resembles a bout of flu. By this measure, the shot's distinct toll amounted to a tender, reddened patch of skin.

The CDC reports that needle-delivered flu vaccines rely on either inactivated viruses that cannot infect or proteins drawn from an influenza virus. The agency states that a flu vaccine cannot cause flu.

Flublok, a recombinant vaccine, comes from an insect cell line and uses no influenza viruses or eggs, according to the CDC. Flucelvax begins with influenza viruses grown in cell culture, which undergo inactivation and leave the finished product unable to give anyone influenza. The agency rules out influenza from either product. The two take separate paths to the same safety, one disabling the virus after growing it and the other never using it. Whatever the method of manufacture, none of these injected vaccines contains a virus able to cause disease.

Influenza that surfaces soon after a shot traces to a virus caught from another source. A person who catches the virus ahead of vaccination, or during the window before the body builds its protection, can fall ill. The CDC explains that the vaccine plays no part in such a case. Someone who gets sick after a flu shot has more reason to retrace recent exposures than to blame the vaccine.

A belief common in 2011 held that a flu shot lost its point once November ended, and the CDC's answer rejected that cutoff. Vaccination after that month kept paying off as long as influenza moved through communities. The myth turned a missed appointment into a reason to skip the whole season. Circulating virus set the real deadline.

Experts urged people to get immunized as soon as the vaccine became available that year. Early beat late, and late beat never. The advice named an early target without closing the door on later appointments. Anyone who missed the first weeks of supply had not lost the winter. The guidance treated a delayed shot as a fallback worth taking, and it gave no one grounds to pass on protection over a slow start.

Flu activity in the U.S. can begin climbing in October and hold on until May, according to the CDC. The CDC reports that activity tends to crest between December and February. Measured against that calendar, the heaviest stretch of illness arrives after the myth's supposed deadline has passed. Anyone who abandoned the shot under that logic would face the entire peak window unprotected.

The CDC repeated the point in its guidance for the 2022-2023 season, telling people who could not get vaccinated until November or later to go ahead at that point. The agency tied that advice to flu activity that can stay heavy into May. The agency's stance from 2011 held up in that later advice, and November offered no excuse to stay unvaccinated.

Early doses gave the fullest coverage, and later doses bought defense for the months when flu reached its height. A tardy start cost some weeks of protection without wiping out the payoff. By the myth's logic, the people most in need of a push, those already running behind, were the ones told to give up.

Last season's flu shot offered no promise of protection in 2011, according to the CDC position the gallery relayed. The gallery aimed that correction at people who believed a single shot keeps working for years. Flu viruses mutate from one year to the next, and each round of change leaves the previous formula a step behind. As a result, the vaccine got a fresh update every year. Anyone counting on a 2010 shot to carry them through the winter relied on a formula built for viruses that had since changed.

The slow pileup of small genetic mutations in influenza viruses goes by the name antigenic drift. The CDC's Pink Book identifies drift as the chief reason a person can catch influenza more than once. The same process explains why the makeup of flu vaccines faces review and revision each year. Each new recipe answers a virus that refuses to hold still.

Immunity from any one shot fades in less than a year for practical purposes, according to the Pink Book. Two forces drive the decline. The antibodies a vaccine prompts the body to make wane, and the circulating viruses keep drifting away from the strains the shot aimed at.

Strain match governs how much benefit a shot delivers. The payoff varies with the resemblance between vaccine strains and the strains spreading in a given season. Three other factors shape the result: the recipient's age, the recipient's health, and the type of vaccine. A shot designed around an earlier season's viruses starts behind on strain match. The strains it targets have had another year to evolve.

Yearly reformulation and the brief life of vaccine immunity turn the flu shot into a seasonal appointment. Each season resets the question, and the only shot that answers it is the one built for the viruses circulating that winter.

Government scientists in 2011 urged vaccinated people to keep up the everyday steps that stop germs and viruses, including those behind influenza, from moving between people. Under that guidance, a flu shot excused no one from ordinary hygiene. The myth treats vaccination as the last step anyone needs, and the 2011 position answered it by keeping those steps in place after the shot.

Tiny drops of fluid make the case for those routines. Experts believe the main way flu travels between people is through drops an infected person releases by coughing, sneezing, or speaking. Those drops can land in the mouth or nose of someone close by, who may breathe them into the lungs as well. A second, less common route runs through objects. Someone touches a surface holding flu virus and then touches the mouth or nose, and in some cases the eyes.

Feeling healthy offers no proof of being harmless to others. A person with flu may be able to pass the virus along before realizing they are sick, as well as during the illness. Silent spread gives daily safeguards a job on days a vaccinated person feels fine.

A few plain practices close off those paths. Covering the nose and mouth during a cough or sneeze might shield people nearby from illness. Keeping distance from others can lower the odds of handing a respiratory virus along. Regular handwashing protects against germs, and an alcohol-based hand rub fills in when soap and water are out of reach. Clean hands target the object route, where germs reach the eyes, nose, or mouth on a person's own fingers.

Government scientists asked parents to teach the same habits to their children, a request that extended the recommendation past the vaccinated adult and into the rest of the household. A shot works inside one body. Covered coughs, distance, and washed hands interrupt the trips flu takes between bodies, and the 2011 advice relied on both layers at once.

Holding off on a flu shot until people nearby fell sick opened a gap of about two weeks, the time the vaccine needed to deliver full protection under the CDC's position as of 2011. The myth cast vaccination as a response to someone else's illness. Its believers figured they needed a shot only once the flu had reached the people around them. Under that logic, a symptom-free social circle served as proof that the needle could wait.

Sick neighbors make a poor alarm. Waiting for others to fall ill was likely to mean acting too late, the CDC held. By the time a cough moves through an office or a household, the virus is circulating within arm's reach, and the person who held off has no head start. A visible case marks a moment when exposure may already have occurred. Symptoms in others tell a person about the past, and a vaccine plan built on them trails the virus.

Antibodies that guard against flu need that same stretch to form after vaccination, according to the CDC. A shot taken after a local wave of illness begins leaves its recipient unshielded during the window when the virus moves through family, friends, and coworkers. The body spends those days building its response while the virus keeps moving.

The myth also misreads what a flu shot does. Vaccination works by preparing the body before a threat appears. A flu shot behaves the way a smoke detector does, useful only if someone mounts it before the fire starts. Treating it as an emergency response turns a preventive tool into a gamble on timing.

The cost of delay lands on the person who waited. Each day spent watching others for signs of illness eats into the lead time the vaccine requires. Timing the vaccine to a local outbreak reverses the order that matters. Immunity has to be in place before exposure. The decision to get a shot belongs ahead of the first sniffle in anyone nearby.

Babies younger than six months can catch influenza, yet in 2011 the CDC considered them too young to receive a flu shot. The myth assumes anyone at risk can roll up a sleeve. Infants in their first half-year break that assumption. They face the virus with no vaccine of their own to stop it. Age alone kept the shot from them. Nothing in the CDC's answer suggested these babies resisted infection.

The CDC's 2011 remedy moved the defense one step outward. Its best safeguard for these infants was immunization for every other member of the household, along with the caregivers who tend the child. Parents, older siblings, and babysitters all fall inside that circle. A grandparent who watches the baby on weekends belongs in it as well. Each vaccinated person closes one route the virus could travel to reach the baby.

The guidance turns a personal health choice into a family one. An adult who skips the shot leaves a gap in the barrier around a child who cannot get one. With no shot to fall back on, the infant relies on coverage among the grown-ups to set the level of safety in the home. The circle can defend the baby no better than its weakest member allows.

Daily habits shield infants from flu alongside the vaccine. Keeping babies away from sick people and disinfecting surfaces both help. Covering coughs and sneezes with a tissue, wearing a mask, and washing hands the right way add more layers. The adults carry out all of those steps. A baby cannot wash its own hands, wear a mask, or step away from a coughing visitor. Even the protection that comes without a needle depends on the people nearby.

Each adult's decision about the vaccine in 2011 counted twice in a home with a young infant, once for the adult and once for the baby.

A flu shot could lower a person's chance of catching influenza by as much as 90%, according to the CDC position the gallery relayed in 2011. The agency paired that figure with a concession. The vaccine does not succeed every time. The two statements fit together. Studies put 90% at the ceiling of what the shot can achieve, and some recipients land below it. A person weighing the shot was buying a lower risk, with no guarantee attached.

Older adults and young children gain less from the vaccine, the CDC said in 2011. Age is one of several traits that shape the shot's results. Flu vaccine effectiveness can shift with a person's underlying medical conditions, past bouts of influenza, and earlier vaccinations. A healthy adult and an older relative with chronic illness can receive the same dose and come away with unequal defense.

Serious flu complications remain a target the shot can hit in cases where it fails to block infection, according to the 2011 guidance. An older adult or young child who comes down with the flu despite vaccination may avoid the worst turns of the illness. The benefit narrows in those groups without disappearing. A better match between the vaccine and the viruses spreading in a community raises the odds of strong protection against influenza illness and its complications.

Everyday conditions outside the controlled setting of a clinical trial can trim the payoff a vaccine provides. A figure drawn from a season of routine clinic visits can fall below the gallery's upper mark without contradicting it.

The myth grades the vaccine by the cases it fails to prevent. Judged that way, the shot looks weakest among older adults and young children, the same populations where the 2011 guidance credited it with heading off dangerous aftereffects. A tally of cases alone leaves out half the job the CDC assigned the shot for those patients.

Thimerosal, the ingredient behind the autism fear, showed no connection to the disorder across many studies, according to the CDC's position in 2011. The compound is a mercury-containing preservative, and some flu vaccines carried it that season. Critics had blamed it for a range of health problems and counted autism among them. The accusation pinned autism on a single component of a single shot, which handed researchers a narrow target to test.

Small doses of thimerosal caused no harm beyond redness and swelling at the injection site, the CDC's position held. Mercury on a vaccine ingredient list can alarm a parent, yet the amounts in a flu vaccine were small. The usual reactions stayed local, confined to the patch of skin around the needle. A few people may be allergic to thimerosal, though the CDC describes such reactions as rare. Critics described harm extending past the arm, and the evidence the agency relayed kept the common effects at the spot where the needle went in.

The no-link finding has outlasted the 2011 season. The CDC's vaccine-safety guidance concludes that research has turned up no connection between thimerosal in vaccines and autism. The agency points to numerous well-designed investigations that ruled out any role for the compound in causing the condition. Across the years between the 2011 gallery and the agency's current guidance, the answer on autism has held steady.

The fear hangs on one ingredient, and the findings left that ingredient with no case to answer. Thimerosal appeared in some 2011 flu vaccines and not in others. Versions without the preservative existed that season as well. A parent uneasy about mercury had a route to protection that left the compound out. A person who skipped vaccination over autism worries gave up immunity against influenza to avoid a danger the investigations never found. Of the two threats weighed in that choice, only influenza carried any evidence behind it.

Children between six months and eight years old who had never received a seasonal flu vaccine needed two doses at least four weeks apart in 2011, under the CDC's guidance at the time.

One vaccine covered the entire 2011 season, and most people needed a single dose of it. The gallery used those facts to puncture the idea that one shot leaves a person exposed. The myth takes a real rule and stretches it across the whole population, when the requirement applied to a narrow slice of young children.

The two-dose rule hinged on two conditions holding at once, the child's age and a blank vaccination record. An eight-year-old who had received a seasonal shot in a previous year fell outside it. So did a nine-year-old receiving a first flu shot. Age alone put that child past the cutoff.

The required spacing turns a young child's first flu season into a two-visit commitment, with the return trip landing a month or more after the first. Every week of delay on the opening shot pushes the follow-up back by the same week. A family that waits for a convenient moment to start the series also delays the day the child completes the course.

The CDC describes the first dose as a primer that readies the immune system, with the second dose delivering the actual protection. A child who needs both shots but receives one can end up with weaker protection or none, according to the CDC. Skipping the follow-up wastes much of what the first visit set in motion.

The CDC reports that children who have received two doses at any point need a single dose in the current season. The present-day test counts completed pairs of doses. The gallery's 2011 wording turned on whether a child had ever received a seasonal shot. Completing the pair in a child's first season sets up every one-shot season that follows.

Tamiflu and Relenza, two prescription-only antivirals, served as a backup against flu in the CDC position relayed in 2011, with the shot holding the front line. These drugs come as pills, liquids, and inhaled powders, and they treat the symptoms of an infection underway. Neither one can replace a vaccine that builds defenses before the virus arrives.

Speed decides whether these medicines help. In 2011, the guidance held that their benefit depended on a patient starting them within the first day or two of illness. Prompt treatment can ease the harshest flu effects and shorten the illness by an average of one day, according to the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases. The institute describes antiviral medicines as a way to help head off severe complications, including bacterial pneumonia. The early cutoff restricts their value to patients who seek care at the first signs of sickness.

A doctor's prescription stands between a patient and either drug. Pharmacies cannot sell flu antivirals without one. The medicines block influenza viruses from copying themselves inside the body. They act on influenza alone and offer nothing against other viruses that cause similar complaints. A patient with a flu-like illness who obtains a prescription may see no improvement from the drug if a different virus caused it. The visit needed to get that prescription can consume part of the brief period when the drugs work.

The CDC calls a yearly flu vaccine its first and most important step against flu and lists drugs to treat flu illness as another option. The agency's stance matches the order the 2011 gallery described, with antivirals waiting in reserve behind the shot. The vaccine aims to keep flu from taking hold. The medicines try to blunt a case in progress against a ticking clock. Anyone who skips the shot and counts on a prescription is betting on spotting the illness in time, reaching a doctor, and having influenza and not some other virus.
