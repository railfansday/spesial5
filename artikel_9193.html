crafted from anti-bird spikes and 3D-printed scaffolds, nests reveal a posthuman architecture

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

bird nests built from human debris and experimental structures point toward a model of shared authorship across species.

Birds have been building with human-made materials for a long time. Wire, plastic, fabric and other discarded scraps routinely find their way into nests, where they are put to work again and are given new life. In recent years, the examples have become stranger. Magpies and crows have constructed nests from metal spikes installed specifically to keep birds away from buildings. Along the front line in Ukraine, birds have woven fiber-optic cables discarded by military drones into their nests.

These structures show us a model for posthuman architecture that’s quite literal, as their authorship belongs across species. Humans manufacture the materials and alter the landscape, while birds gather, select and reorganize what they find. The nest takes shape somewhere between the two.

Historian Matthew Holmes examines this relationship in his 2026 paper Feathered Architects: Collecting Histories of Anthropogenic Material in Birds’ Nests (read here). Holmes argues: ‘Looking at birds’ nests — and the items within them — is occasionally rewarded with a snapshot of the present state of the Anthropocene.‘

Human consumption and waste become entangled with animal construction, and leave traces of changing technologies inside structures built by birds. He notes that the phenomenon stretches back well beyond contemporary plastic waste. During the Dust Bowl era, American crow nests made from barbed wire pointed to an agricultural landscape stripped of usable vegetation.

The materials tell different stories these days, with some instances showing synthetic materials to actually be the more desirable choice. Amsterdam coots have accumulated layers of plastic into nests that persist across multiple seasons. This means that a building cycle could be extended, when it once depended on vegetation that decomposed between uses. Researchers studying two canal nests found layers of plastic sourced over decades.

Perhaps the clearest reversal appeared in European cities, where carrion crows and Eurasian magpies began tearing anti-bird spikes from buildings and incorporating them into nests. A magpie nest discovered in Antwerp used hundreds of spikes across its outer structure, with the sharp points facing outward.

Researchers suggest the metal may serve much the same purpose as thorny branches, and helps defend eggs and chicks from other birds. An object designed to exclude birds from human architecture had become bird architecture itself.

This exchange now moves in the opposite direction too. In Chicago, the ongoing exhibition Best Nest: Design for Nature and Culture examines how bird construction has informed architect Jeanne Gang and her studio’s design process. Drawings and models sit alongside objects and more than seventy ceramic nesting vessels originally developed for The Living Orders of Venice at the 2025 Venice Architecture Biennale.

The exhibition approaches the nest as architectural intelligence. Different species build according to different bodies and environmental pressures, producing structures whose geometry responds directly to inhabitation. Studio Gang carries these observations into projects at building scale and into habitat structures made specifically for birds.

Studio Gang demonstrates this building-scale interpretation of nest logic in in New York with its Richard Gilder Center at the American Museum of Natural History. Its organic and cavernous interior is carved out with bridges and passages which stitch more than thirty connections into the existing museum. The team notes that the interior feels assembled through occupation and movement rather than imposed as a single object.

In Best Nest, Studio Gang places the Gilder Center as one monumental example within its broader study of nesting structures, where built space grows through the accumulation of spaces around one another. The building also draws from geological erosion and the movement of water, but seen through this lens, its network of cavities and connections begins to read like another kind of nest built at civic scale.

MIT-LUMA Lab’s Bird+House furthers this idea even still. Developed by Hiroshi Ishii, Lucy Li and Hye Jun Youn with input from the Cornell Lab of Ornithology, the 2026 project creates 3D-printed nesting structures for barn swallows whose traditional nesting sites are disappearing alongside barns and other familiar structures.

The team begins by 3D-scanning existing swallow nests and studying their dimensions before printing open scaffolds from locally sourced biomaterials. The most important detail is that these structures stop halfway. It’s been found that barn swallows often reject artificial nests that are fully completed, so Bird+House gives them a framework and leaves the final construction to the bird. The swallow chooses how the nest is completed by adapting the scaffold and adding its own material.

It’s a small gesture, but it shifts a familiar relationship between human and feathered builders. A birdhouse usually arrives as a finished, human-made object awaiting an occupant. Here, occupation continues the act of construction. The architect determines enough to make coexistence possible, then another species takes over.

Across these examples, the nest becomes interesting far beyond its form. Birds turn plastics and defensive hardware (see here) into building material. Architects study avian structures as legitimate forms of design knowledge. At MIT, the human-made object deliberately remains incomplete until its non-human inhabitant modifies it.

Posthuman architecture, seen through the nest, therefore has little to do with copying organic curves or borrowing the appearance of animal structures. Its more radical proposition concerns authorship. Buildings exist inside landscapes already occupied and continually altered by other organisms, whether architects acknowledge those relationships or leave them out of the drawing.

The birds building with anti-bird spikes make that shared world difficult to ignore. Human architecture attempted to dictate where they could land. The birds took part of that architecture apart and built something else with it.
