John Wayne And John Ford's Best Movie Is The Searchers

Director John Ford and actor John Wayne are one of the most prolific film-making duos in cinema history. The two collaborated on 14 movies over their 50-year friendship and professional partnership. They are particularly well known for their powerful influence on the Western genre, producing nine such films together. In 1956, Ford and Wayne released their greatest Western of all time—The Searchers.

This iconic duo met when a young Wayne—known as Marion Morrison at the time—worked a summer job on the Fox lot. Ford, who was already an experienced director at the time, took a shine to the kid and helped propel his early career (via PBS). Following various walk-ons and side roles in Ford films, Wayne had his first lead role in the 1939 Stagecoach. Considered among the best Westerns ever made, this classic movie launched Wayne’s name into Hollywood stardom and cemented the actor-director duo.

Wayne then led a plethora of iconic Ford Westerns, and five non-Western films, such as The Quiet Man (1952). The two made consistently competent movies and helped forge the Hollywood Golden Age. Wayne and Ford made their greatest masterpiece of all time in 1956.

From Warner Bros. Pictures, The Searchers stars Wayne as Ethan Edwards, a Confederate veteran searching for his niece who was taken by a Comanche tribe. The anti-heroic figure fights not to save his niece, but to kill her so she will not assimilate into the Native American culture. The gritty Western is a seminal and influential classic.

The Searchers is widely considered the best Western movie of all time, with Stagecoach not trailing far behind. It is a clear standout among Ford and Wayne’s 13 other movies, despite fierce competition.

Starting with Stagecoach, Ford generally cast Wayne as the standard Western hero with an individual but strong moral compass. Wayne’s Hollywood stardom was synonymous with his good guy persona. In The Searchers, Ford casts Wayne as his grittiest and most complex character yet, perhaps of all time.

Playing against type, Wayne gives a visceral performance as the racist and vengeful Edwards. Throughout the film, Edwards is driven by an obsession for revenge and an outdated, warped understanding of the world. The lone rider becomes so out of touch with civilized standards that, by the end, he cannot return home and is left wandering the rugged wilderness.

Wayne appears to delight in this meaty role and delivers a career-topping performance. Both Ford and Wayne are at their best in The Searchers.

The Searchers transcends the Western genre to become one of the most influential movies of all time. It is number 12 on AFI'S 100 YEARS...100 MOVIES list, above Star Wars, which is inspired by The Searchers. The film is both groundbreaking and timeless for its masterful story, characterization, and thematic cinematography.

The narrative follows the classic “hero’s” journey formula of The Odyssey, in which a battered man attempts a long quest home and uncovers new inner-truths along the way. In the case of Edwards, he returns home only to realize that he has lost touch with humanity and no longer belongs in a civilized home space—relegating him to life as a wondering lone rider.

This classic formula, reimagined and repopularized by The Searchers, has influenced cinema for the past five decades. Film critic Stuart Byron once proclaimed, “in the same broad sense it can be said that all recent American cinema derives from John Ford's 'The Searchers’” (via The Washington Post).

The Searchers is credited as one of the first revisionist Westerns, paving the way for modern gems like No Country for Old Men. It also introduced ideas such as the central anti-hero that would gain traction in Spaghetti Westerns of the 1960s. Outside the Western genre, The Searchers has directly influenced a variety of modern movies, including Martin Scorsese’s 1976 crime-drama, Taxi Driver.

The influential cinematography in Ford's film cannot be downplayed either. The 1956 Western impressively used visual language to express and highlight narrative themes. This is particularly true in the film's iconic final shot, which communicates Edwards' inability to return home by framing his body through a doorway as he wanders into the distance.

While Ford and Wayne’s storied professional partnership has produced a plethora of stellar films, The Searchers remains their greatest accomplishment.
