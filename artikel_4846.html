The pros I chatted with say the two most important factors to look out for when buying wrinkle creams are your skin type and the active ingredients in the formula. Here are some shopping pointers.

First, you must pair your wrinkle cream with sun protection—up to 90% of visible skin aging is from the sun. "UV exposure from the sun speeds up the aging process by breaking down collagen and elastin, and it can cause pigmentation (such as sun spots and melasma)," says dermatologist Nazanin Saedi, MD.

Retinoids are a class of vitamin A derivatives (like the OTC version, retinol) that are "the single most proven anti-aging ingredients, after sun protection," says dermatologist Hadley King, MD. They boost cell turnover, collagen and elastin, and minimize discoloration, says Dr. King. It's also a fantastic ingredient for treating adult acne and those with oily skin.

Here's the catch: You'll need to start off using a pea-sized amount to avoid irritation, and pregnant women can't use it, says Dr. Spearman. You can also use the sandwich method, which involves adding a base layer of moisturizer, adding the retinol on top, and following up with another layer of moisturizer. Thankfully, there are some very effective retinol alternatives such as plant-based bakuchiol which do a lot of the same great things as retinol, but are safe for pregnant women or those with very sensitive skin, says Dr. Spearman.

Using these proven ingredients isn't enough. "Just as on food labels, ingredients are listed in order of their amounts," says Dr. King. Your safest bet is to use wrinkle creams with the active ingredients listed earlier on in the label instead of at the end for the most effective, anti-aging results. While the order of ingredients does give some information, it's not really enough to know the concentrations unless these are also listed—some brands that pride themselves on transparency do this, but most don't, says Dr. King.

Our skin type also informs our ideal wrinkle cream. For example, those with oily, acne-prone, or combination skin will appreciate a lightweight wrinkle cream, while those with dry skin should look for a richer formula that offers ample hydration. "Heavier creams tend to work better for more mature, more dehydrated skin,” says Dr. Spearman. “These are often too much for those who are acne prone or have oily areas of skin." Generally, brands will list on the box which skin type will benefit from using its creams.

There are many processes that the body goes through when aging, and one of those is the production of less collagen, which can make your skin more susceptible to wrinkles, Dr. Yadav says. Collagen is what helps the skin maintain its elasticity and resilience.

As you age, cellular turnover also decreases, which causes dead skin cells to build up on the surface of the skin, making its texture look rougher and uneven, and exacerbating the appearance of wrinkles, Dr. Yadav says.

When you opt for wrinkle creams that fight these two things, they can promote stronger, firmer, smoother, and more even-looking skin, Dr. Yadav says. "Additionally, adding moisture into the skin helps to plump up the look of fine lines, albeit temporarily," she adds.

There's really no specific age that's best for working a wrinkle cream into your skincare routine, since people can form wrinkles at different ages depending on their lifestyle, sun exposure, and heredity, Dr. Yadav says.

On the other hand, Dr. Jhin suggests people can start using a wrinkle cream as early as their 20s. "Even though many people start using anti-aging products in their 40s or 50s when signs of aging have become more prominent, your skin’s natural collagen production actually starts to decline around age 25," she says.

Dr. Jhin often tells her patients to think of skincare as a marathon—not a sprint. "There is no 'quick fix' for aging," Dr. Jhin says. "Like regular exercise and a well-rounded diet help you maintain a healthy body, continued, regular use of anti-aging products will help your appearance in the long run."
