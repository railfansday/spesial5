Rare Marie Antoinette ‘Breast Bowl’ Hammers for $2.47 M. in Paris, Surpassing Top Estimate

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

One of the last known “Breast Bowls” still in private hands that once belonged to Queen Marie Antoinette has just hammered for €2.25 million ($2.47 million) at the Coutau-Begarie & Associés evening auction in Paris’s Hôtel Drouot, beating its high estimate of €2 million ($2.19 million).

Legend has it that this series of 18th-century porcelain bowls, made by the Royal Sèvres manufacturers, was modeled after the ill-fated French queen’s real breasts. While no documented proof of such a legend exists, the so-called “jatte-téton” or “nipple bowls,” painted in fleshy light pink with an areola and nipple at the tip, do have a surrealistic, convincingly realistic quality, despite a too-perfectly circular shape. Made in 1787, the bowl in question was in fact inspired by ancient Greek mastos, or breast-shaped drinking cups used at banquets dating back to the 6th century BC. It is one of four highly prized jatte-tétons delivered that year and in 1788, destined for the royal “pleasure dairy” in Rambouillet, near Versailles, that King Louis XVI gifted to Marie Antoinette. There, per the fashion of the time, nobility would gather to socialize and taste an assortment of fine milk, cream, and cheese delicacies prepared for them.

The rare breast bowl comes from the collection of French dealer Claude Meyer, who passed away in November 2025. His children happened upon it while going through boxes of storage, and learned that their late father, whose Paris gallery closed in the 1990s, had never offered it for sale. It is unclear how Meyer acquired the piece, which is known to have once belonged to the French Marquise de Chanaleilles.

Three of the four breast bowls made in 1787 and 1788 for the queen’s royal dairy dishes are housed in public collections today, including two at the Musée de Sèvres and one at the Museo Duca di Martina in Naples. The bowl that sold tonight is the last of that specific series. They are “the most celebrated and the most extravagant shape of the porcelain service,” by the royal Sèvres manufactory for the queen’s dairy, notes the auction house in its description.

With the bowl comes an intricately carved tripod stand decorated with goat heads in relief, as seen above. Indeed, the breast bowls require a pedestal to hold them upside down, as it were, so as to contain the milk and cream they were meant to serve.

Speaking to Le Journal des Arts about the bowl, auctioneer Alexandre de La Forest Divonne observed: “It has become something of a symbol of the queen; it is truly a totem.”
