Major study argues we need immediate rules to protect young people from the harms of digital media

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

Lancet experts say digital media harms to young people come from app design and weak rules, not lack of willpower. They urge governments to act now.

When teenagers can’t stop scrolling on their mobile devices, the easiest explanation is to label them as having weak self-control.

A group of public health experts has a different answer. The feed was built to keep that thumb moving, and the responsibility belongs to the companies that built it and the governments that let them.

In a three-paper Series in The Lancet, the experts said the harm that digital media does to young people comes from business models, design choices and weak rules. They want governments to act now, before anyone has proof of cause and effect.

If they’re right, the question is no longer how many hours a child spends on a screen. It is what the apps on that screen were designed to do.

Professor Ilona Kickbusch of the Digital Transformations for Health Lab in Switzerland chaired the Series. Dr. Jay Shaw of the University of Toronto led its first paper.

The caveat arrives early. Heavy, addiction-like use is linked to poorer mental health, sleep and physical activity, Kickbusch said, but the evidence isn’t conclusive. A link isn’t proof of cause, and the authors rate the evidence low in certainty.

In an interview with Earth.com, she said the size of the effects is only half the question. “If you consider how many children are now using digital media, even small adverse effects might have significant public health consequences,” she said.

“That is why we are calling for a precautionary approach to policy rather than reacting only after harm has occurred.”

Open almost any social app and the feed never ends. Designers built that bottomless scroll deliberately, Shaw and his co-authors wrote, and it has encouraged amounts of screen time already known to be harmful.

The companies behind those features have lobbied to hold off regulation while knowing about the harms, the authors wrote.

“Most digital platforms are designed by companies to capture attention and collect data, not to protect health,” Shaw said. He added that infinite scroll, autoplay and personalized recommendation feeds are deliberate design choices that draw attention.

Scale turns a design detail into a public health question. By one estimate the authors cite, people who were online in 2025 spent an average of 141 minutes a day on social media apps, about two hours and 20 minutes.

In a 2025 survey of more than 10,000 adults in the United States, the average was more than 6 hours a day on phone apps, which is most of a school day.

Shaw’s paper compares the technology industry with tobacco and ultra-processed food. Both of those industries worked to weaken regulation while their products harmed health.

Governments have allowed digital companies to regulate themselves for decades, the Series reports, and commercial growth has come before health.

“Technology companies are borrowing a playbook well known from tobacco and fossil fuels: manufacture doubt, discredit critics, frame regulation as a threat to freedom,” said Professor Barbara Prainsack of the University of Vienna, a co-author on the Series.

Missing regulation doesn’t mean a technology goes ungoverned, she added. It means the companies set the rules instead of public policy.

Shaw and his co-authors called today’s approaches to governing that system deeply insufficient to protect public health.

They also describe adolescence and early adulthood as periods when the brain and social life are especially sensitive. The idealized selves people build online are linked to body shaming and poorer mental health, especially in young people.

Asked by Earth.com what surprised her most in the evidence, Kickbusch named the gaps. “What was most surprising about the evidence on the health impacts of children’s digital media use was what it doesn’t tell us,” she said.

Most studies measure individual behavior, such as screen time, she explained. Researchers have paid very little attention to the bigger structures, such as content created by algorithms and targeted advertising.

Young people also care about the benefits of going online and about AI, she said, and researchers have studied both of these areas less.

Access to data is another problem. “The most valuable data on how these platforms actually affect users are hidden within technology companies, limiting independent scrutiny and slowing scientific progress,” said Louise Holly, a Series author at DTH-Lab.

Shaw’s paper lists eight recommendations for researchers. One is to audit and publish the practices of technology companies that harm health, using insider accounts from former employees and investigative reporting.

Another is to keep industry money out of the research itself, a lesson the authors take from tobacco and pharmaceuticals.

Single measures such as age limits aren’t enough on their own, so the Series calls for action at the company, national and global levels at once.

Companies would have to test new products and features for harm before releasing them. They would also share data on how those features affect users’ health with vetted public-interest researchers.

A global treaty through the World Health Organization would set baseline standards, so weak rules in one country can’t undo progress elsewhere. A dedicated international body would monitor AI harms to children and young people.

Kickbusch told Earth.com that governments must work on several fronts in parallel: reduce access, regulate individual technologies and address the commercial models and algorithms behind social media. They also need to cooperate across borders, she said. “They must accept this is a major public health issue!”

Lawsuits are part of the picture too. Courts can compel companies to disclose internal documents in a way regulators often can’t, Kickbusch said. She sees litigation as one route to accountability among several, not a substitute for regulation.

The authors also worked with a youth advisory group of six adults aged 23 to 30, who talked with them about the lived experience the research can’t capture.

The steps governments and companies need to take will take time, Kickbusch said, because they are politically contested. Families don’t have to wait.

“Meanwhile, parents must help children mitigate risks by significantly reducing their use of digital media and the access to smartphones. They must explain to children how digital platforms and algorithms work,” she said.

Families who understand the design features and the commercial motives behind the platforms can protect themselves better, she explained, even when cutting back leads to difficult conversations at home.

“And parents themselves must change their social media use and be an example for their children,” she added.

No one has proved that the design features cause the harms linked to them, or measured how large those harms are, and the authors say so plainly. The links are modest in size and rest on evidence they rate as low in certainty.

The scope is also wider than the kids with phones. The same industries shape the health of people who don’t use the technology themselves, Shaw’s team wrote.

By the International Telecommunication Union‘s count, 2.2 billion people had no internet access in 2025. Africa was the least connected region.

Shaw said the field needs much more research into how specific design choices change the way people use digital media, instead of only measuring how long they spend online. That research depends on data the companies now keep to themselves.

Until that data is shared, the most important measurements of how these platforms affect adolescent health stay inside the companies that built them.

The full study was published in the journal The Lancet.
