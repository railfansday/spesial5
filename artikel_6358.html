Official reports could be underestimating the amount of forever chemicals in drinking water by at least half

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

A model of 1.8 million EPA water tests estimates that reporting below-limit PFAS as "zero" misses over half the estimated PFAS in US drinking water.

Seeing “not detected” next to forever chemicals on a water report might seem like you have completely clean water. However, it actually means the lab just could not detect the chemicals below a specific cutoff point.

For the two most common forever chemicals, this cutoff point is exactly the same as the highest legal limit allowed.

Researchers have now modeled the EPA’s national test results with those below-limit values filled in. Counting them as zero, they estimate, captures less than half of the PFAS the model projects for community water systems.

The missing share is an estimate, not a measurement, and it doesn’t mean any one household’s tap holds twice as much PFAS.

PFAS, short for per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances, are the man-made forever chemicals that can persist in the environment for a long time.

Gehendra Kharel, an environmental scientist at Texas Christian University in Fort Worth, led the work with colleagues at TCU, the University of Florida and Oklahoma State University.

In an interview with Earth.com, Kharel said the size of the information gap stood out to him. “About 98 percent of the roughly 1.86 million individual PFAS results in the January 2026 dataset were below reporting limits,” he said.

“Those records still contain information: we know the concentrations were below specified thresholds, even though their exact values are unknown.”

The EPA requires public water systems to test for 29 PFAS. The January 2026 release held results from 10,297 systems, including a sample of small ones.

A reporting limit is the lowest concentration a lab can measure with confidence. Anything under it is logged as “less than” the limit, and when the EPA compares results with its health benchmarks, it counts those results as zero.

Counting them as zero makes sense for checking a benchmark. But the same rule, the team wrote, is the wrong tool for estimating how much PFAS is in the water overall, because it sets 98 percent of the results to zero.

For each PFAS detected at least once, the team fitted a statistical curve to all the results, measured and below-limit alike.

From that curve they drew 500 possible values for every below-limit record, averaged them, and added up all the chemicals for each water system.

Across 9,991 community water systems, weighted by the people each one serves, the zero rule gave an average of about 6.6 parts per trillion for the 25 PFAS combined.

The model’s estimate was about 15 parts per trillion. That difference is the whole finding.

A part per trillion is a nanogram in a liter of water, about one drop in 20 Olympic swimming pools. The standard way of adding up the results captured 44 percent of what the model estimated.

For the six PFAS that carry federal drinking water limits, the gap was narrower: about 2.6 parts per trillion under the zero rule, and an estimated 5.2 from the model.

Kharel told Earth.com that “more than half” describes the share of the model’s estimate that the zero rule leaves out.

“It is not a direct measurement of previously unreported contamination or a finding that every household’s water contains twice as much PFAS,” he said.

The gap was not the same everywhere. Among the 12 PFAS detected most often, the zero rule captured about 74 percent of the model estimate in systems that draw on groundwater.

In systems that use surface water or a mix of sources, it captured about 45 percent. Smaller systems showed a wider gap than larger ones.

Asked by Earth.com which systems most need more testing, Kharel said the study does not produce a priority list. The patterns suggest where more sensitive follow-up testing could be useful, he said, and they do not show that those systems have higher concentrations or greater health risks.

The EPA set a limit of 4 parts per trillion for PFOA and PFOS in April 2024. The monitoring program’s reporting limit for both chemicals is the same number.

Under the zero rule, 1,277 sampling locations were at or above the PFOA benchmark and 1,364 were at or above the PFOS benchmark.

The model added between 16 and 33 locations for PFOA and between 4 and 25 for PFOS, depending on which curve it used. No location where every result fell below the limit crossed the benchmark.

So a large gap in the national average barely changed which places are near the limit. “Better concentration estimates do not automatically mean more regulatory violations or greater health risk,” Kharel said.

The comparison is a screening exercise, and whether a system violates the rule is decided separately.

Lower reporting limits would close most of the gap. If labs reported PFOA and PFOS down to 1 part per trillion instead of the current 4, the zero rule would capture 84 to 92 percent of the model’s combined total.

Kharel’s advice for anyone holding a water report starts with the utility that issued it.

“Ask your utility what ‘not detected’ means in that report, which PFAS were tested, what reporting limits were used, and when samples were collected,” he said. “‘Not detected’ does not establish that the concentration is zero.”

Because the reporting limit for PFOA and PFOS equals the legal limit, a clean result says only that the water is somewhere below that number.

“A below-limit result does not tell us whether the concentration is close to that value or much lower,” Kharel said.

The size of the gap depends on the curve. The main model used the shape that fit the data best. Two other shapes gave smaller gaps.

For the 12 most-detected PFAS, they put the zero rule’s share at 65 percent and 76 percent, instead of 55 percent. The direction never changed; the size did.

The estimates cover only the systems in the EPA dataset, sampled where water enters the pipes. Private wells and the water at anyone’s tap are outside them, and the study does not estimate exposure or health risk.

What the water actually holds below 4 parts per trillion is still unmeasured. The records can say only that it is somewhere under the limit, and finding out means labs reporting lower numbers.

Until they do, the team has made its model available as a screening tool called PFAS-CAST. Utilities can run the same calculation on their own results and see where more sensitive testing would help.

The full study was published in the journal Cell Reports Sustainability.
