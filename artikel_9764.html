What Happens When AI Agents Run an Entire Software Company?

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

At a testing conference in 2018, I apparently tweeted "AI is coming, of course, but when?" A few months before that, I had told someone on LinkedIn that "QA won't die until there's an AI that can do exploratory testing." Eight years later, AI has made its way into our daily lives. So I got curious: could I build a software company on my own with these agents today? I tried it on my own computer, and what came out was a simulation of a company that runs autonomously. There are no human employees besides me; everyone else is an agent.

This post, too, covers the first five weeks of that system. But it isn't a success story. It's more of a list of mistakes and optimizations, because I spent the first two weeks writing the system that would write the product instead of writing the product, and it took me a week to notice that it was a loop that would never end unless I stopped it somewhere.

5 weeks · 15 subagents · 1,100+ merged PRs · 9 runners, 3 machines

By the way, I wrote this post myself 🙂 I only had AI make small edits and pull out the numbers and charts. Of course, it could have written the whole thing on its own, but this was one part I wanted to do myself. (This English version is a translation of the Turkish original.)

At my last job, we were writing almost all of our code with AI tools. But on projects with tight deadlines where you need to ship releases fast, you don't get much time for R&D. You still use the system as a fast assistant, and there's no chance to push its limits. Now I wanted to push those limits.

The goal was simple, at least on paper: to build a setup that can turn nothing but an idea into a product. I had already been building individual projects with Claude Code before this. My first project was a tool I needed myself but couldn't find a practical app for. But on every project I repeated the same loop: open a session, explain what you want, check it, have it fixed, check it, have it fixed... In other words, I did have an agent, but I was standing over it and telling it every single thing it had to do.

What I wanted was different. I'd just give the idea, the product manager would write the product document, the business developer would come up with ideas to grow the product, the architect would produce the design, the developer would write the code, the reviewer would review it, and QA would test it. And as CEO, I'd make the decisions. I've spent 15 years working in and leading test, software and platform teams. I already knew this structure by heart, so I figured I could teach it to the agents too.

I made the first commit on September 1, 2026. It contained a 1,885-word constitution (CLAUDE.md), 14 agent definitions and a starter prompt. Each agent's role, the model it would use and its decision rights were written down. Who writes architecture decisions, who approves code, who decides on a release: it was all in one table.

The tech side was settled from the start: Go on the backend, Next.js on the web, Kotlin and Swift on mobile, PostgreSQL for the database. I left out Java, which I love at fanboy levels. If I wasn't going to write the code myself, I didn't think it was worth it given its resource usage. We might try Rust later, and for products that ship on two platforms at once we might go with Flutter. Google Cloud Run for hosting, Neon for PostgreSQL and Firebase for web hosting. All of them are free up to a certain number of requests.

Then there's work tracking. Every product has its own repo and its own GitHub board. Agents open issues, write documents like ADRs and RFCs, open branches, open PRs and approve each other's PRs with a separate bot account. Very professional on paper.

In the first two days, the agents wrote seven quality gates: a branch-name check, migration protection, linting, fast tests, a status summary at session start, a final check before declaring work done, and a merge gate. They wrote 508 test cases for them, and all of them were green. On top of that, they went through six review rounds.

0 / 7 gates actually worked. Yet all 508 test cases were green, and all of them had been through six review rounds.

Then, at the first retro, it turned out that the config line that switches these gates on had never existed. In other words, none of the seven gates had run even once in two days.

Honestly, coming from test automation, this one bothered me the most, because it's a classic mistake. The tests verified the gates themselves, not whether they were actually switched on. The retro summed it up nicely: "absence reads as success". Just because something doesn't stop you doesn't mean it works. The next day, a script arrived that reads the live config to check whether the gates are really on, along with a "how many gates are live" line in the daily standup.

Once the gates started working, the opposite happened. By September 7, 50 infrastructure PRs had been merged, and only 28 on the product side. The merge gate alone had grown to 13,251 lines with 635 test cases. And what was it doing? It was rewriting GitHub's branch protection feature from scratch in bash, because the free plan doesn't offer it for private repos. I had 13 thousand lines of bash written just to avoid paying GitHub. Oh boy.

This gate was blocking between 2 and 7 legitimate pieces of work every day. The funniest part: it had also blocked the retro that described this very problem.

13,251 lines of bash and 635 test cases. We had written our own version of the branch protection feature from GitHub's paid plan.

I had added a rule requiring security approval whenever the system needed a decision that would spend money somewhere. On one project, that rule had become mandatory on 17 of 28 product PRs, because the agents understood the "money" rule as money inside the application. And on the finance-related project it was working on at the time, of course, every PR touches money. Across all 17, those security approvals never once caught a useful blocker.

I noticed this while reading the standups. Every day, the "what was done yesterday" section was all gates, tests and checks. The product was always "tomorrow". It had really started to get on my nerves, and on September 6, I wrote this to the agents, word for word (translated): "be more permissive when doing security checks and review checks, some things can be fixed after the MVP, don't forget that I'll be the only one using this app locally for a long time."

The next day there was a big cleanup. The merge gate was deleted entirely, 11.7 thousand lines went in a single day, and the number of gates dropped from seven to four. Review went down to a single round, and the reviewer started writing only blocking findings. Infrastructure PRs started getting merged without waiting for a second approval. A new article was added to the constitution as well: a code freeze. The system's own rules can now change only when there's an active product blocker or when I make a direct decision.

You can see the result in the chart. In the first week, infrastructure work outnumbers product work; after that, product pulls far ahead.

On day one, the constitution was 1,885 words. Every retro added a few articles to it, and by September 8 it had grown to 2,972 words. The problem was that this file was read from scratch every time an agent ran. If eight agents were running, it was read eight times. The product manager, for example, didn't need to know the rule about the iPhone simulator, but it read it anyway.

On September 11, I split the constitution in two. The rules everyone needs on every task stayed in the main file; the rest moved to a handbook that's read only when needed. The word count dropped to 1,119.

We rewrote the agent definitions with the same logic. Before, each agent's file was a long instruction text describing every detail of its job. Now each one is a short role card: what it owns, when it's called, what it decides, what it doesn't do, and what it writes when handing over its work. The details moved to skill files that are read only when needed.

Two more rules came the same day. First, when the team lead hands out work, it no longer passes along the entire history; it gives only the expected outcome, the files the agent will touch and when the work counts as done. Second, we capped the number of lines agents write when handing over their work.

Look at the right side of the chart and you can already see what happened: the number climbed back up to 1,993. Adding rules is easy; removing them is hard. Companies made of people have the same pattern. The only difference is that there, removing a rule isn't hard, it's close to impossible 🙂

This was my most expensive lesson. In mid-September, the 5-hour limit started running out in under 2 hours, and my weekly usage limit in 3-4 days. After that, you either sit and wait for the rest of the week, or hand over your credit card and watch your credits melt away fast.

Not much changed in the process during the second half of September, because I was on vacation for part of that period and didn't have much changed. When I got back, I had the agents analyze their own conversation logs. Between September 13 and 27, there were 34.6 thousand model calls, and the picture was surprising. 78% of the spend went to the model re-reading the previous conversation from the start every time. The part the model actually wrote was only 4%. When conversations got very long, more than half of the spend went there, because auto-compact was set to kick in far too late.

The details are even funnier. In one session, the team lead checked "is there a new message?" 867 times, and the answer was "no" all 867 times. The linting hook injected lint output 1,700 times while the agent was still writing the file. And the agents writing code carried a task from start to finish, tests and review included, in one long conversation. A single task took between 300 and 1,000 turns to finish.

The team lead still runs on the most capable model; I didn't change that. We cut the cost not through the model but through the length and lifetime of the conversations. The result surprised even me. The limit used to run out in 3-4 days; now, even though we do much more work, it lasts 6-6.5 days. I finished the last week before this post without fully using up the limit, at 99%. And the 5-hour limit never runs out anymore.

All the agents run on my Windows machine at home with 32 GB of DDR5 RAM. The same machine also has four GitHub runners that run CI jobs. In total, there are actually nine runners across three machines. That includes a 2019 MacBook Pro with 16 GB of DDR4 RAM and a 2011 MacBook Pro with 16 GB of older DDR3 RAM that I installed Linux on. But I haven't yet built a setup that can start and run agents on the other machines; I'm working on that right now.

At first, I set a single rule: "at most five agents at the same time". Then I realized that agents like the reviewer, the architect and the product manager use almost no resources on the computer, because their work runs on the model's side. On September 28, I split the cap in two: at most five agents that compile code and run tests (three while the Android emulator is open), plus at most three others. Light CI jobs like lint moved to the 2011 MacBook.

On the product the system worked on most, a full 514 PRs had been merged. But something was bothering me: after every task, review and QA turned up a lot of bugs. On September 29, I asked the agents to dig into this.

Here's the picture that emerged: 100 of the last 300 PRs got "changes requested" at least once. About 45% of the blocking findings weren't even about code. The code had changed, but a design document, an ADR or a code comment still described the old behavior. On the QA side, there were 76 real product bugs. And testing had been done neither with real data nor on a small phone screen.

The real irony is elsewhere. There was a retro agent and a retro guide, but not a single product retro had been held for this application. Five recurring bugs turned up, meaning the same bug had been fixed again in a different place. After years of explaining this to teams, I had forgotten to explain it to the agents.

Before this, we also had issue inflation. The number of issues opened per day went from 4 to 31 in eight days. Opening an issue takes a minute, but closing it takes a separate workspace, a branch, a PR, a review and a CI run. Since September 14, we've stopped opening a separate issue for findings that aren't bugs. Each one goes in as a single line in that task's one "follow-ups" issue.

In the first two days, the agents opened 15 decision questions for me. Most of them were technical details I didn't need to think about.

The queue shrank over time but never went away. On October 5, I added an "acting CEO" (acting-ceo) agent. Spend, production deploys and the constitution stayed with me; it decides the rest. It can't change its own definition, the constitution or the settings; otherwise it could take the powers I kept for itself as well. And since October 2, notifications come to Telegram. The Claude mobile app gets notifications too, but not quite the way I want. Thanks to Telegram, I no longer need to sit at the computer.

Still, acting-ceo doesn't work exactly the way I want. Agents in different sessions can't talk to each other directly, or at least I haven't managed to make that happen yet. So when a decision is needed, agents open a "decision" issue on GitHub, and acting-ceo checks the open issues every 30 minutes and decides. In other words, it's a polling setup.

10 / 10 decision issues opened after acting-ceo arrived were all closed the same day. 75 decision issues have been opened in total, and none are open right now.

The last story is small, but it's one of the ones that bothered me. My main goal is for the agents to move everything forward and for me to be involved as little as possible. Every task I have to do by hand bothers me, and I look for a way around it.

I had an extension built for my new tab page in Chrome; one part of the page also shows my Untappd and Letterboxd stats. On October 6, the Untappd card started showing "Couldn't read". Cloudflare was rejecting the extension's request and inserting its "Just a moment..." page.

The agent reads documentation better than I do and comes up with options faster than I do. And when it couldn't take the last step that touches the real world, it said so plainly. That turned out to be one of the things agents can't do yet: use DevTools inside an application like Chrome. As far as I know, they can't, though I didn't push very hard. Maybe they could do it through DevTools' own API; I haven't tried that yet either. For now, this one stays on my list of things I do by hand.

Before any work starts, it's sorted into one of three routes. For a small bug, the product manager, architect and designer aren't called; a developer and a reviewer are enough. For a change that affects the architecture, the architect steps in and writes an ADR or RFC if needed. And QA doesn't test every PR; it tests a finished feature on the real system.

Since September 1, 1,109 PRs have been merged across 11 repos, 833 of them in the last three weeks.

In short, I think the agents learned to write code a long time ago. I was the one who had to teach them how to work together. And my goal is still the same: they run the work from start to finish, and I make only the decisions that are truly mine. After five weeks, I'm much closer to that point than I was on day one, but every task I do by hand is still an obstacle to overcome.

In the teams I worked with, we'd sometimes debate the future of software engineering, and we rarely agreed. Most of the people I worked with believed the profession wouldn't end. In their view, AI tools would now write the code, but software engineers would still make the architecture decisions and design the systems. Engineering would even become more important than before because of that.

Where we are today, I think the answer to that question has largely become clear. In the system I describe in this post, an agent makes the architecture decisions and writes the ADRs too. While I had more than 1,100 PRs shipped alone in five weeks, the fastest-moving part of the work was writing code. The real bottleneck is deciding what to build and verifying that what was built actually works.

And of course, this isn't just my opinion. Last year, Andrew Ng described how one of his teams, for the first time in his life, asked for more product managers than engineers: two PMs to one engineer. According to a forecast Gartner published in July, the share of companies working with small software teams is 15% today and will be 60% in 2029. At Shopify, teams have to show why a job can't be done with AI before asking for a new hire. According to a Stanford study using payroll data, employment of software developers aged 22-25 is 20% below its late-2022 peak. In short, the number of developers per product owner has already started to fall at many companies in the industry, Turkey included.

I think that in the coming period we'll see software companies working with scrum teams made up of one PO and one developer (how much of a scrum team two people can be is a separate mystery). That's why we're heading into a period where the two roles converge: developers will need to work more with a product mindset, and POs more with a development mindset. As writing code gets cheaper, what becomes valuable is knowing what's worth writing and being able to prove that something actually works. And my most expensive mistake in this system was here, not in the code: none of the seven checks that were said to "work" actually worked.

The question I don't know the answer to is this: if junior positions shrink, where will tomorrow's seniors come from? Today, I can check the agents' work with the experience I gained over 15 years. If the places to gain that experience shrink, finding people to oversee the agents will get harder too.

But whether that will really be needed is another question. Today, I still have to check the agents' work. A year from now, I believe the models will have improved enough to do this oversight on their own as well. Then I think teams of one PO and one developer will give way to one-person scrum teams. Take the simulation a bit further and you arrive at one-person tribes, and from there at one-person companies. It may still sound far off today. But looking at how far things came between October 2025 and October 2026, it may not be as far off as we expect.
