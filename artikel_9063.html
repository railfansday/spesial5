SO–IL imagines a museum wrapped in shimmering glass scales in the norwegian forest

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

a translucent skin of glass scales shelters an intricate network of timber beams and elevated walkways within the norwegian forest.

Architecture studio SO–IL presents its design for the Kistefos Museum in Jevnaker, Norway, where an enveloping skin of translucent glass scales would take on the changing colors of the surrounding forest. The architects imagine a building with a swollen, almost organic silhouette that appears to float just above the forest floor. In the renderings, its pale green exterior takes on a silvery glow under the low Norwegian sun.

The proposal was developed as part of an international competition for a new museum at Kistefos, an art destination about an hour north of Oslo. The site occupies the grounds of a historic wood pulp mill and is already home to an extensive outdoor sculpture collection and BIG’s twisting, bridge-like gallery, completed in 2019.

The new museum was conceived as a permanent home for the collection of Norwegian art collector Christen Sveaas. SO–IL was among eight finalists in the competition, which ultimately saw Swiss architecture studio Christ & Gantenbein selected to develop the building in December 2025.

Much of the character of SO–IL’s architecture comes from its unusually deep facade. The architects call this space the ‘thicket,’ a timber-framed environment that occupies the perimeter of the museum. Custom glass elements are arranged in a repeating, scale-like pattern across its curved outer surface, with larger openings cut into the skin to expose the structure beneath.

From within the thicket, visitors would find themselves among a network of exposed wooden beams and elevated walkways. Metal staircases wind between floors, while platforms extend out toward the trees. There is enough room here for large sculptural works, some of which appear suspended within the timber framework.

The use of locally sourced wood recalls the industrial origins of Kistefos, where the production of wood pulp began in the late nineteenth century. Through the translucent glass, the forest would remain partially visible even as visitors move deeper into the museum.

The team at SO–IL organizes the interior around a central oculus, which visitors encounter after passing through a compressed entrance from the public plaza. From this point, several routes lead through the collection. Openings between floors introduce views into neighboring galleries, so visitors might catch sight of an artwork well before they reach the room in which it is displayed.

The exhibition spaces occupy two levels, with smaller galleries downstairs dedicated to craft and historic artworks. On the upper floor, rooms for modern and contemporary art vary in height and exposure to daylight. SO–IL’s renderings explore these differences in atmosphere, from a deep blue-green lobby beneath a curved skylight to expansive, pale exhibition rooms.

Throughout the proposal, opportunities to step away from the enclosed galleries are built into the museum’s architecture. A visitor could emerge from an exhibition onto a platform among the trees, where the glass overhead filters the sunlight and the sculptures of Kistefos’s outdoor collection are visible below.
