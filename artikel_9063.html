Amongst the wave of IEMs to come out in 2026, I don't think I've been more excited about the look of an IEM than with the ZiiGaat Seraphim. And the more I found out about these beauties, the more excited I got about their sound.

Tuned by the consistently excellent Jay Audio, and combining two dynamic and three Knowles balanced armature drivers, the Seraphim promises a flagship-tier listening experience without – at $259 – the accompanying destruction to your wallet.

At this price point, they're not going to make my list of the best budget IEMs under $100, but there's every chance that they make my buying guide to the best IEMs under $500. Read on and see if they sound like the right option for you.

Overall sound: There was online chat about the Seraphim being a mini-Storm, but that's actually a bit of a red herring. I heard the Subtonic Storm at CanJam London, and from memory, it has much less emphasis on bass, noticeably fuller mids, and a significantly more nuanced, airy treble presentation overall. Instead, the Seraphim offers an exciting, contrasty sound with strong bass and a well-balanced treble. The mids are a little lean for me, and won't be for everyone, but if you're looking for sub-bass impact, these are your boyees!

Bass: The Seraphim uses an isobaric dual dynamic driver configuration that is meant to be good at delivering deep frequencies. I have no way of testing whether that's true, but what I can tell you is the sub-bass hits like a truck. In a good way.

Rather than bleeding into the lower midrange, the mid-bass remains tightly controlled, prioritising accuracy over bloated warmth. I really like this, as it provides a sense of bounce and grip texture, where bass guitar lines from Dry Cleaning's MySoul / Half Pint feel satisfying, while electronic kicks and 808s sound authoritative, separated, and just a blast to listen to.

However, this separation of upper bass and lower mids, while eliminating bleed, brings its own issues to the tuning...

For all my headphone and IEM reviews I play them through my Luxsin X8 and Chord Mojo 2 desktop DAC/Amps, as well as several dongles. This ensures sufficient power for peak performance, and provides a transparent source for a clean, detailed signal. My music sources are Spotify Lossless and CDs.

Midrange: Again, if marketing materials are to be believed (they're not, they're not!!) the Seraphim's Knowles balanced armatures allow the midrange to lean toward a clean, analytical profile that slightly favours upper registers.

For me, the midrange has pros and cons. Higher register vocals, such as Neil Young on Tell Me Why, are forward and engaging, cutting through the arrangement with transparency. But the lower mids feel a tad lean, stripping some natural body and chest resonance from vocals, especially on the lower tones of Barry White on Can't Get Enough of Your Love.

Despite this minor honkiness on certain vocal tracks, the instrument separation is really good, making every piano plonk and acoustic guitar strum sharply defined. I think the upper mids are so balanced that overall the timbre of the Seraphim is good, and instruments sound 'right', on the whole (though this is definitely where the Seraphim falls down against the CrinEar Reference – see the comparison section below for more on that).

Treble: The treble response is probably the most impressive part of the Seraphim's tuning. It delivers a highly resolving, crisp presentation without harsh peaks. It just seems to be very well balanced, offering a well-extended presentation, with just enough air to bring sparkle but without the tiring glare that you often get with bright IEMs.

I hear plenty of micro-details and coupled with the massive sub-bass, you could call this a very exciting V-shape tuning. Now, there's no perfect tuning, and especially when it comes to treble, one person's subtle spotlight is another's midday sun aimed at your eyeballs, and I think the Seraphim's treble can get a little much. But that's only on music that's mixed bright, like Michael Jackson's Billie Jean or Lucinda Williams's Sweet Old World.

But on 80% of my music library, the treble is smooth and fatigue-free, allowing for long listening sessions.

Price: At $259, the Seraphim sits in a competitive mid-fi price zone where consumers rightfully expect premium technicalities, durable accessories, and engaging tunings without glaring flaws.

And you get all of that with the Seraphim! It offers good detail retrieval, a thunderous sub-bass response, modular cable terminations, and a quality metal build that justifies its relatively modest asking price.

But how does it compare to the competition?

This is slightly unfair, as the Seraphim isn't going for the same style of tuning as the neutral Reference. Yet it's worth comparing, because at $350 the CrinEar Reference offers what I would describe as a far more sophisticated sound. There are lots of fun, exciting, contrasty, V-shaped IEMs out there, like the Seraphim. At the time of writing, there's only really one truly exceptional neutral IEM: The Reference.

When placing the Seraphim alongside the Reference, it's impossible to ignore the visual similarities. Aesthetically, the Seraphim is definitely trying to steal Crin’s lunch, mimicking the sleek metallic faceplates and minimalist shell contours. And Jay does it better! The Seraphim looks so much more engaging and visually interesting than the Reference's bland logo faceplate.

As mentioned, the sound is totally different. Where Crinacle goes for strict neutrality, emphasising natural vocal timbre and a smooth yet full midrange, the Seraphim leans heavily into a more flash, fun, V-shaped profile. It aggressively pushes that thunderous ten-decibel sub-bass slam and analytical treble, trading tonal purity for excitement and flair. And sometimes that's what I want! Most of the time, I get plenty excited by the balanced brilliance of the Reference.

Another area the Reference wins in is comfort. For me, the longer, deeper fit means a better seal is achieved quicker than with the Seraphim.

Pitting the Seraphim against the Binary EP321 is more of a fair fight. First of all, at $310 the Binary EP321 is more comparable price-wise. It's also a visually interesting IEM with its multi-coloured, reflective faceplate.

Sound-wise, it's a bright IEM with enough sub-bass and mid-bass to justify the 'exciting' descriptor. I think it does mid-bass better than the Seraphim, and with its extra treble, there's more detail in that bass with the EP321.

Most important for me, the EP321 is less lean in the lower mids, injecting much-needed warmth and thicker vocals that the Seraphim can lack. As mentioned, the Binary EP321 provides more treble energy thanks to its MEMS driver (says the marketing), meaning ultra-high frequencies are achieved with blinding speed.

Ultimately, if you crave physical sub-bass impact, grab the Seraphim, but if you prioritise airy treble and natural lower mids, the EP321 wins out easily. For me, I'd always go for the EP321 over the Seraphim.

ZiiGaat has fully embraced sturdy metal shells, providing an understated faceplate design that feels solid without being heavy. Although I've read online that some people don't like the thin cable, I'm a fan of it! After all, when was it decided that thick = great quality?! It wraps easily and avoids annoying microphonics entirely, which are major plusses for me.

In terms of ergonomics, the robust nozzles create a rather noticeably shallow fit, so I couldn't use my favourite Juzear eartips, as I wasn't able to get a proper seal, and instead I used the new rounder tips that came with the Dunu Vitruvian IEMs, which are longer. I still had issues with achieving a seal when swapping them with other IEMs for comparisons, but I don't expect many people will be doing that, and once I got the seal, it stayed, so I don't want to overstate it.

The Seraphim is mid-sized and I find them very comfortable when fitted. This makes them a good choice for long daily commutes or extended listening sessions at your desk without any noticeable fatigue.

I'm confident that the ZiiGaat Seraphim will be many people's idea of perfect in an IEM. Their tuning is ideal for audiophiles wanting visceral sub-bass impact combined with pristine detail retrieval. EDM fans and gamers will get on with them, with its punchy dynamics and superior imaging. They also look stand-out-beautiful, and though that's not enough by itself, it's a hell of a big plus in my book. After all, my favourite IEM is the CrinEar Reference, but I far prefer the look of the Seraphim!

Listeners seeking lush, thick male vocals should look elsewhere, as the lean lower mids will prove frustrating. However, despite a few subjective quirks, it remains an incredibly competent hybrid monitor, and I really liked it for the vast majority of my music library.
