Led by Record-Breaking $190.4 M. van Gogh, Sotheby’s Impressionist and Modern Art Sales Rake in $777.8 M.

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

By Daniel Cassady, Sarah Douglas November 19, 2026 12:13am

It was an evening of superlatives at Sotheby’s, whose two auctions of Impressionist and modern art in New York tonight brought in a total of $777.8 million.

That total is the highest one for a single night at the auction house, edging past the $706 million achieved last November at the house’s inaugural evening double-header at the Breuer building. And while the 12 lots from the Blaquier-Arrieta collection came in at $412 million, just shy of their presale low estimate of $414.5 million, those dozen works still managed to set a record for a single-owner evening sale dedicated to Impressionist and modern art at Sotheby’s. (Unless otherwise noted, prices cited here are inclusive of fees.) Two lots in the Blaquier-Arrieta collection were the most anticipated paintings of the season: Vincent van Gogh’s Châtaigniers en fleurs (1890) and Paul Cézanne’s Arlequin (1888–90).

They both achieved eye-popping prices—$190.4 million for the van Gogh, the artist’s highest price to date and the highest price for any work of art ever sold at Sotheby’s, and $111 million for the Cézanne, the second-highest price for Cézanne at auction. But the bidding was anticlimactic.

Both works hammered below their pre-sale estimates. There was just a smattering of bids on the van Gogh. The Cézanne inched along with four bids alternating between Sotheby’s specialists Lisa Dennison, Chairman Americas, and Helena Newman, chairman of Sotheby’s Europe. It hammered to Newman.

Asked after the sale whether the publicity surrounding the restitution claim against the van Gogh had affected bidding, Sotheby’s CEO Charles Stewart said he didn’t think so.

“First of all, it’s a record price for the artist,” he said, pointing out that it was also the fourth-highest price achieved by any artist at auction. “I think you have to separate auction heat from auction results,” he said.

The liveliest bidding came for the final two paintings, both of them with estimates on the lower side, for this sale. Edith Eustis and Simon Stark on the phones, as well as several bidders in the room, went for Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec’s Femme assise de profil vers la gauche (1890). It sold to a man in the second row for $3.8 million. Then, Wendy Lin, Serena Hildebrandt, and Alina Davey battled on the phones for the Camille Pissarro landscape, Maison de Piette à Montfoucault, effet de neige, avec un homme portant un fagot (1875). It came down to Lin and Davey, and went to Davey for $2 million. It was bought by the same bidder who bought Edgar Degas’s Avant la course (ca. 1893) for $18 million a few lots earlier, bidding with Sotheby’s Ben Doller.

Things picked up considerably during the 47-lot, multiple-owner sale that followed, which saw lively bidding in the room and no fewer than 33 Sotheby’s specialists fielding bids over the phones. All told, this sale made $365.8 million, with all but one of the lots sold. It helped that the highest-priced lots came early in the sale and generated heated bidding wars.

The top price from that sale was the $47.3 million for Edward Hopper’s 1944 seascape The Martha Mckeen of Wellfleet, from the collection of Baroness Carmen Thyssen-Bornemisza. It’s far from Hopper’s record—$91.8 million for Chop Suey from the Barney Ebsworth collection at Christie’s in November 2018—but it is his second-highest price at auction. As dealer Lock Kresler pointed out on the Nota Bene podcast, the closest comparison is another Hopper seascape, Blackwell’s Island, from 1928, which sold at Christie’s in 2013 for $19.1 million.

The Martha Mckeen sparked a bidding war between four Sotheby’s specialists on the phone bank, with Scott Niichel, vice chairman and head of middle market for modern and contemporary art, coming out the winner. Accounting for fees, the work made $47.8 million.

Close behind the Hopper was the $45 million achieved by Rene Magritte’s 1964 painting L’Ami de l’ordre, which stars the painter’s famous man in a bowler hat. It came from the collection of Sydell Miller, who bought it in 1998 for a mere $5.3 million. The previous record for a bowler hat painting was for the one sold at Christie’s London in March 2024 for £33.7 million (about $43.2 million), Magritte’s third-highest price. Several bidders in the room went up against two Sotheby’s specialists, Jackie Wachter and Bame Fierro March. A bidder in the room was the winner, but no one seemed to be able to identify him.

Also adding sparks early in the sale was Aaron Douglas’s 1931 painting of Harriet Tubman. Even if it had merely sold near its pre-sale estimate of $2.5 million–$3.5 million, the piece would have handily broken the record for Douglas: $600,000, for a piece sold at Swann Galleries auction house in 2008. But it more than quadrupled that estimate to make $14.1 million, all of which will go to Bennett College, a historically Black women’s college, that sold the piece.

Some eight bidders went after the painting, beginning with Jackie Wachter going up against her father, George Wachter, who serves as Sotheby’s chairman of North America and South America. Several bidders in the room popped in before Sotheby’s head of American art Stefany Morris entered the war. In the end, it was Morris versus the room, with Morris the winner.

“We knew it was going to make a big price,” Sotheby’s vice chairman and head of contemporary art David Galperin said after the sale. “That’s one of the most exciting auction moments—when you have something that is so categorically the very best” by an artist whose work rarely comes to auction.

Halfway through the sale came another big moment: a painting by Mary Cassatt, Summertime (1894–95), from the Armand Hammer collection, sold for $13.3 million, a new record that nearly doubles the previous one, $7.5 million. Here the evening’s depth of bidding was again on display: specialists David Galperin, Caroline Seabolt, Lin, and Simon Stock all went after it, but the winner was an online bidder.

On the lower end of the price spectrum, a landscape by Chaim Soutine, Place à Céret (1921–22), estimated at just $800,000–$1.2 million, made $3.9 million. Five Sotheby’s specialists on the phones wanted the picture; so did bidders in the room. Jen Hua, deputy chairman of Sotheby’s Asia, cast the final bid.

Over the course of the evening, Hua and Lin, the Sotheby’s Asia chairman, were especially busy. Asian collectors bought works by Signac, Maurice de Vlaminck, Cézanne, Claude Monet, Renoir, Soutine, Egon Schiele, and Degas (including the Degas in the Blaquier-Arrieta sale for $18 million). They bid on works by Monet (the Blaquier-Arrieta picture that sold for $20.2 million), Picasso, Magritte, Cassatt, and Degas.

The sale was not without hitches. A Remedios Varo drawing failed to sell, and three lots—pieces by Magritte, Cézanne, and Matisse—were apparently withdrawn while the sale was in progress, given that the withdrawals were not announced prior to the sale, as is customary. (Two of the withdrawn lots–Henri Matisse’s La Jetée à Collioure (estimate $5–$7 million), and Paul Cézanne’s L’Oncle Dominique (estimate $3–$5 million)–are among the 11 in the sale that come from the collection of George Weiss, the hedge fund founder now in bankruptcy, as ARTnews revealed earlier this month.)

Every one of the Blaquier lots was guaranteed. For Phillip Hoffman, founder and chairman of the Fine Art Group, that brought up a question about Sotheby’s strategy.

“The guarantees were just too high,” he told ARTnews immediately after the Blaquier-Arrieta sale. “I think these guarantees are just pushing the limit.”

He said Sotheby’s had left buyers very little room to compete, and had been too confident in its pricing, with the result a string of lots that attracted little meaningful competition.

Calling it “a little bit of overconfidence” on the house’s part, he speculated that they might have lost as much as $50 million on guarantees.

Stewart, the Sotheby’s CEO, defended the house’s approach to the evening.

“Our estimates weren’t shy,” he acknowledged. “You can read into that whatever you like. We were happy with the result of everything.”
