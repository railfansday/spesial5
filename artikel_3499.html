Only 3 Movies Are More Entertaining Than The Lord of the Rings

The Lord of the Rings showed us how incredible it feels to follow characters through a massive adventure. We watched them grow through every danger, sacrifice, and impossible decision in three films. But some movies manage to deliver that same rush in just a couple of hours. They pull you into situations so intense that you almost forget you're watching a film. You find yourself holding your breath during a chase, worrying about someone you've only known for an hour, or desperately hoping a character makes the right choice before everything falls apart.

What makes the three films on this list unforgettable is how deeply grounded their stakes remain in human emotion. They prove that when a movie respects its characters' choices and allows their relationships to evolve naturally, even in the middle of total chaos, the audience stays entirely hooked. Their victories feel earned. Their sacrifices linger. Let's look at how these three films pull it off.

Raiders of the Lost Ark is fun because Indiana Jones (Harrison Ford) can be brilliant, frightened, stubborn, and unlucky within the same minute. In the opening temple, he measures the sand, swaps the idol for a bag of sand, and still loses the prize when the mechanism starts closing. Then he runs from a giant boulder and loses the idol to Belloq (Paul Freeman). Indy can know exactly what he’s doing and still find himself in a worse situation.

The map room is a pleasure, as Indy uses the staff and sunlight to locate the Ark while the Nazis do the digging for him. But the film keeps its people in view, too. Marion (Karen Allen) survives captivity, tries to drink Belloq under the table, and refuses to cooperate. When the Ark opens, Indy tells Marion to shut her eyes and holds her close. The movie fittingly ends its wildest spectacle with Indy protecting her.

Terminator 2: Judgment Day begins with a brilliant reversal. The machine that tried to kill Sarah (Linda Hamilton) in the first film is now walking toward John (Edward Furlong), while the new T-1000 (Robert Patrick) is hunting him. At the mall, John has to decide whether to trust either one. His order that the Terminator (Arnold Schwarzenegger) must not kill anyone becomes the first boundary John sets with the machine.

Sarah’s escape from Pescadero is the film’s hardest moment. She reaches Miles Dyson (Joe Morton) with a gun, sees his children, and cannot make herself shoot him. That hesitation gives John and the Terminator time to arrive, and the machine helps Dyson understand what his research will cause. The chases and gunfights are spectacular, but the true core of the movie is the machine learning that a promise matters. It refuses to kill, comforts John, and finally lowers itself into the molten steel because it understands why it must go. John’s face as the Terminator gives him the thumbs-up is heartbreaking. The machine has changed. It understands love. It learns how to say goodbye.

Mad Max: Fury Road keeps changing the rescue as the characters learn what it will cost. The story follows Furiosa (Charlize Theron), who smuggles the Five Wives out of the Citadel, and Max (Tom Hardy), who is chained to Nux’s (Nicholas Hoult) car as a blood bag when the pursuit begins. The pursuit is packed with detail at every turn. You see the War Rig, the Pole Cats swinging between vehicles, Nux trying to earn Immortan Joe’s (Hugh Keays-Byrne) approval, and Furiosa protecting the women without stopping to explain herself.

Then the plan changes. The Green Place is gone, so Furiosa and the others must turn back through the army chasing them. Furiosa finally faces Joe, and the chase ends with his death and his followers’ defeat. On the return, the Wives open the water gates and the Citadel’s people rise to meet them. The ending gives each victory a visible result. Joe is dead, Furiosa is carried upward, and water reaches people who were denied it, leaving a sense of exhilaration and relief for all involved.
