Yes — premium has moved past hype into standard U.S. airline revenue architecture, and it’s now at the crowded end of its growth curve. Premium cabins generated $188 billion, or 24% of global passenger revenue, in 2024, and U.S. premium capacity is up 33% since 2020 against 16% for economy. The open question is no longer demand. It’s whether every carrier scaling premium at once dilutes the yields that made it work. 12

The behavior behind it is capital, not talk. United ordered more than 250 aircraft with more business class and premium economy, Delta is spending $1 billion on new Delta One suites arriving in early 2027, American is flying retrofitted 777-300ERs, Alaska is adding business class on widebodies and select narrowbodies plus its first premium economy, and JetBlue is launching domestic first class 345.

The results follow the spend. Delta’s premium cabins hit 50% of passenger revenue in Q2 2026, up from 45% in Q1 2024, with premium revenue up about 57% over that stretch to $6.9 billion 6. Delta held a 9.5% operating margin through the fuel shock on fares, premium mix, loyalty, and non-flying revenue 7.

Premium is also being unbundled, which is the clearest sign it has become normal pricing infrastructure rather than a flagship product. United is selling domestic First and Business as Base, Standard, and Flexible fares, extending segmentation it already applied to Polaris and Premium Plus 8. United CFO Michael Leskinen said “all of society is becoming more premium,” citing hotels, cruise lines, and car service 9.

The front cabin keeps growing but stops being one product. Tiered business fares let carriers sell the seat to price-sensitive buyers without discounting the full experience, which protects yield as capacity rises.

Skift Research’s main caution is oversupply rather than demand weakness. If carriers scale premium simultaneously, especially long-haul, they compete for a finite pool of travelers willing to trade up and dilute the premium.

With jet fuel averaging $4.50 a gallon and domestic fares up about 32% year over year, carriers without premium and loyalty levers are the exposed ones. Deutsche Bank expects American, Alaska, JetBlue, and Frontier to lose money in 2026.

Premium demand in the U.S. is structural, built on a K-shaped income recovery and self-funded leisure travelers rather than corporate accounts alone. What’s cyclical is the return on it, and that depends on how much premium seating the industry adds from here.

No cut has happened, and none is imminent. The Credit Card Competition Act was reintroduced in January 2026 after a Trump endorsement but still has not had a floor vote, and the $38 billion Visa/Mastercard settlement compresses standard rates while leaving premium tiers — the ones funding travel rewards — largely untouched. The risk to travel’s co-brand economics is Congress, not the courts, and the industry is betting Congress never acts. 1

U.S. interchange — the merchant-paid fee on each card swipe — remains uncapped, unlike in the EU, UK, and Australia 1. That single condition underwrites travel’s most profitable business line. Delta collected $8.2 billion from American Express in 2025, about 14% of adjusted operating revenue, American took $6.2 billion from co-brand and other partners, and Morgan Stanley estimates co-brand operating margins at 35-50%. 12

Where caps landed, rewards shrank. The EU and UK capped credit interchange at 0.3% in 2015, and no European airline collects anything close to $8 billion from a card partner 1. Australia’s Reserve Bank cut its cap to 0.3%, effective October 2026 and said explicitly it was setting the level so issuers could no longer use interchange to fund rewards 1. The U.S. has run its own version: the Durbin Amendment capped debit interchange in 2011 and debit rewards largely disappeared 1.

The lobbying tells you what’s at stake. Airlines for America runs a “Protect Our Points” campaign, and the card and banking industry has spent roughly $200 million opposing swipe-fee reform 1.

Nothing in the current reporting points to a cut in the near term — the bill has been reintroduced three times without a vote, and the settlement spares the premium tiers. But the downside modeling the industry works from doesn’t contemplate reform at all: Morgan Stanley’s three paths for the travel co-brand market by 2035 ($100B, $60B, $40B) all assume the fee structure stays intact.
