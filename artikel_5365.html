11 flood safety steps, from the first alert to emotional recovery

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Learn the difference between a flood watch and a warning, and 9 more safety steps for before, during and after a flood.

Staying safe in a flood depends on moving early, because a whole community faces the same flood at the same time. Many people picture a flood as a private emergency at their own front door, judged by the hour when the water stands highest. In practice, neighbors reach for the same roads, the same supplies and the same help all at once. In a single Ottawa flood, the number of basements reported underwater topped 6,000, according to CBC News. The roads out fill up as well, and the CDC advises evacuees to expect heavy traffic on evacuation routes. A household that waits to see what everyone else does has already waited too long, so the useful work has to happen before the water rises.

The danger also lasts long after the water drains away. A falling waterline feels like the end of the emergency, and that is the moment many people relax. The harm keeps coming in the weeks that follow. People face contaminated water and spoiled food, mold in walls nobody dried in time, fumes from generators run in the wrong place, injuries during cleanup and the strain of living through all of it. The signals people rely on are easy to mistake for an all-clear. A flood alert, a line on a flood map and a depth at which water turns dangerous each show where danger starts, not where safety begins. A person on the safe side of any of them can still be at risk. Staying safe means acting before the official signal turns urgent and staying careful well after the flood looks finished.

The American Red Cross laid out flood safety advice that runs from reading the first alert to recovering emotionally afterward. That advice covers the difference between a watch and a warning, the mold that grows in a home left wet, the carbon monoxide that no one can see or smell, and the emotional toll that stays with people after the repairs are done. Taken together, the guidance forms a timeline that starts before the first raindrop and continues well past the cleanup. The reader's job along that timeline is to act sooner at the start and to stay careful longer at the end.

Many people hear "watch" and "warning" as two levels of the same alert, but the real difference between them is time. A watch means flooding is merely possible, while a warning means flooding is occurring now or soon, according to the American Red Cross. A household that knows which one is in effect knows whether it still has time or has to act immediately. The CDC says that people should evacuate when a flood warning is issued or when authorities direct them to leave. A warning is the moment to go. Anyone who starts getting ready at that point has already lost the hours the work required.

The watch stage sounds like permission to wait, but most of the preparation belongs there. During a watch, the CDC's National Center for Environmental Health advises households to fill their vehicle's gas tank, put essential documents in waterproof material and set aside enough water to last at least three days. A full tank lets a family drive out before anyone tells it to go. The papers and the water are already packed when it does. Flooding is only possible during a watch, but that is when nearly all of a household's preparation should happen.

Flood risk can change from one block to the next, so the question that matters is whether a particular house is exposed, not whether the region floods. The American Red Cross directs residents to FEMA's Flood Map Service Center, where anyone can search for a home's flood zone by its address. The answer is only as current as the map behind it, and Harris County, Texas, last gave its floodplain maps a comprehensive overhaul in 2007, according to the Houston Chronicle. A house labeled as outside the floodplain is not necessarily free of risk, and the label is where a homeowner's research should begin.

When officials redraw a map, the label on file can change for a great many homes at once. The Houston Chronicle that draft maps could shift more than 420,000 properties in the Houston area to a higher-risk rating. If those drafts take effect, the rating those owners have known would no longer describe their risk. The change would not be spread evenly. In Hunterwood, about 85% of parcels, 650 of 764, may be moved to higher risk, the highest share among Houston's super neighborhoods. An owner or buyer should therefore check how the drafts treat the surrounding neighborhood, along with the zone listed for the address.

When forecasters predict a river flood or a storm surge, many households decide to stay put and wait to see how high the water gets. The American Red Cross advises households to learn whether their area is prone to these floods and to review their evacuation plan so they can leave quickly. The first of those steps belongs well before any alert. A family that learns only on the day of the flood that a river or the sea can reach its home has already lost the head start the rest of the plan depends on. The second step sets the standard for readiness: a plan reviewed in advance turns leaving into a matter of minutes instead of a debate at the kitchen table. A household in a flood-prone area should treat departure as a real possibility and have it worked out before officials make the call. The real decision is made days earlier, and the hours before the water comes should go to carrying it out.

The American Red Cross offers a free phone app, the Red Cross Emergency App, that a household can install on its own before a flood arrives. The app costs nothing, so no household has to go without it because of price. The Red Cross also offers a Spanish version, and families who read Spanish rather than English can use the app as well. A household needs to install it before the forecast turns, while there is still time to act ahead of everyone else.

Red Cross help also covers the weeks after the flood, when people feel they should be coping on their own. The Red Cross lists the Disaster Distress Helpline, 1-800-985-5990, under taking care of yourself after a flood. The line is free and takes calls or texts, so anyone can reach help for flood stress from home, without waiting for an office to open or a shelter bed to free up. A household that saves the number when it installs the app will have both ready before anyone needs them.

The health risk from a flood stays behind after the water drains, in everything the water soaked. "Stay away from floodwaters. They may contain sewage, sharp items, and chemicals that can make you ill," the American Red Cross warned. For that reason, the CDC says that anything edible the flood or storm reached belongs in the trash. Contact alone is enough to make the food unsafe. After losing so much, people want to save what they can, but food the water reached is a loss, and no amount of cleaning brings it back.

A kitchen the water never reached can be lost as well. The CDC advises throwing out perishable food that sat unrefrigerated through a power outage, and for anything uncertain it gives the same rule as the Red Cross: if you aren't sure the food is safe, discard it. A refrigerator on a dry upper floor may therefore need emptying as surely as the flooded basement. With that standard, a household has to know food is safe before keeping it rather than prove it spoiled before tossing it. Anything nobody can vouch for after the outage belongs in the trash.

After a flood, people head for the nearest dry, enclosed space, and that instinct is where the carbon monoxide risk begins. The American Red Cross rules out the home itself as a place to run anything that gives off carbon monoxide. Getting back inside a house is therefore not the same as being safe inside it. A garage can feel connected to the outdoors, but it is on the same prohibited list, and a wide door does not make the air in it safe to breathe. Families who move into a camper while their repairs go on face the same rule, because a camper is named too. The home, the garage and the camper are the places a displaced family is most likely to shelter, and the rule covers all three.

An open window does not solve the problem either, and setting equipment outdoors near an open window is also prohibited. People cannot judge this danger for themselves. "Carbon monoxide can’t be seen or smelled, but it can kill you fast," the American Red Cross said. A person's own body gives the main signal, and anyone who begins to feel ill, lightheaded or weak may be breathing the gas. Those complaints are ordinary enough to pass for the tiredness of a long day of cleanup. The gas can kill quickly, so those symptoms leave little time to act. The safer course is to keep this equipment out of every place on the list, including the ground just outside an open window, from the first time anyone switches it on.

Authorities can also order people to leave, and once they do, a household has no decision left to weigh. "Never ignore an evacuation order," the CDC said.

A household that finished its work during the watch can follow that order in minutes, while one that waited spends the warning packing as the water rises.

A flood-zone line also determines what some homeowners must pay. Inside the 100-year floodplain, owners whose mortgages are federally backed are required to buy flood insurance. For those households, a change on a map becomes a new bill. The maps are drafts, and any single home's label could change before they take effect.

The sensible move is to look up the address today and plan as if the higher-risk label already applied, rather than waiting for a final line or treating the old one as protection.

Most flood safety advice assumes there will be some time between the first alert and the arrival of the water, enough time to understand the threat, weigh the options and move. A flash flood may come with little to no warning, according to the American Red Cross. People caught in one may have no time at all to decide where to go. That changes what preparation means. A household facing a flash flood can't count on an alert to start its planning. The decisions it made in advance are the only ones it can rely on once the water starts to rise.

The plan this threat calls for is simple: choose a spot of higher ground ahead of time, one the household can reach quickly. Speed is the test that matters most. When there is little or no warning, a hill or upper floor that takes too long to reach does nothing for the people who can't get there in time, however high it is. A family should judge each option by how fast everyone in the household could get to it on foot or by car, and pick the closest one that is truly above the water. That choice has to be made on a dry day, when people have time to look at the routes, compare the options and settle on a single place. Every person in the home should know where it is, so no one has to stop and discuss it when the time comes.

A household that already knows where it is going needs no warning time to start moving.

Leaving early also keeps people out of the water itself, and contact with floodwater carries risks that have nothing to do with its depth or speed. Most people judge floodwater by whether it can knock them over or sweep them away. But what is in the water is a hazard of its own, and the safer course is to stay out of it entirely rather than guess how clean or shallow it looks. Some coastal waters naturally carry vibrio bacteria, and a cut exposed to that water during a flood can become infected, according to the CDC. The risk weighs most on anyone who already has a cut, scrape or wound. For them, leaving before the surge arrives keeps broken skin away from water that may hold those bacteria.

A household that gets out ahead of a river or the sea keeps its members clear of the current and of whatever the water carries.

A flooded road rarely looks dangerous, because the water seems shallow and a car feels heavy enough to push through it. Yet a person can be knocked off their feet in 6 inches of fast-moving water, and 12 inches is enough to float a vehicle, according to the American Red Cross. The weight of a car gives no protection in water that shallow, and a driver who stays behind the wheel is inside the vehicle once it floats. The Red Cross places this advice among the steps for the middle of a flood, when the time for preparing has run out. At the water's edge, a driver faces one decision and has seconds to make it: drive in or turn back.

Those figures are not a limit to measure the road against. A number invites a driver to treat anything under 6 inches as safe to cross, and that reading is the trap. The CDC's National Center for Environmental Health sets no safe depth for moving water at all. Taken together with the Red Cross thresholds, that position shows what the figures really mark. They show how quickly floodwater turns deadly, not a level below which wading or driving is safe. The numbers are worth knowing only as proof that very little water is enough to kill.

Two separate safety agencies reduce this advice to a rule rather than a measurement. The Red Cross sums up the point in a short slogan, and the CDC uses the same four words. "Never drive in flooded areas—turn around, don't drown!" the CDC said. The word "never" leaves no exception for a driver who thinks a few inches can be crossed.

The decision that counts is made before the tires touch the water. Once a car is in the current, the driver has few good choices left.

Local recovery help through the Red Cross can come with a fixed end date and a spending limit. On July 15, 2026, Ottawa's city council voted to fund Red Cross recovery work after a local flood, but only for six months and with no more than $400,000 from the city, according to CBC News. Ottawa residents who expect that help to last as long as their own repairs may find it gone before the work is done.

A household that sets up the app and the helpline today, and plans its repairs without counting on local help to last, will be ready on both counts.

People need protection before they begin cleaning up after a flood, because the cleanup is where much of the harm happens. Once a house is dry enough to enter, many people go straight back in to clear it, as if the dangerous part were over. The property can stay hazardous long after the flood, with electrocution risk, falling trees and debris among the dangers, according to the American Red Cross. Clearing a home puts people directly among those hazards. "Many injuries happen during cleanup," the Red Cross said. Cleanup workers and volunteers should be up to date on tetanus shots, ideally before cleanup begins, according to the CDC. That protection has to be in place on the first day of work, and a tetanus check belongs on the to-do list before the cleanup weekend, not after someone gets cut.

After a flood, the wound to worry about is the one that gets dirty, even if it doesn't look serious at the time. The tetanus concern lies with wounds contaminated by floodwater, soil, waste or saliva, not with contact with the water itself, according to the CDC. Crushing damage also carries a higher chance of infection than a slice through the skin. A finger or foot caught under shifting debris therefore deserves more attention than an open gash that looks worse. Anyone hurt during cleanup should watch a dirty or crushed wound until it heals rather than dismiss it on the day it happens.

A household can finish clearing the house while a wound from that work is still at risk of infection.

A home the flood never entered can still have unsafe water. Surface water can contaminate groundwater, the CDC reports, and that can affect private wells as well as city systems that draw on groundwater. A household with a private well in a flooded area needs to have the water tested before using it, and the well owner is the one who has to confirm it is safe. Until then, suspect water is off-limits for far more than drinking: mixing baby formula, making ice, cooking, washing hands or dishes and brushing teeth all have to wait. People who would never drink a glass of that water can still be exposed through those routine tasks.

Owners have only hours to dry out a flooded home, and many use up that time before they reach the front door. Most people expect cleanup to begin once the water is gone and they are back inside with time to work. The American Red Cross says a house still wet after 24 to 48 hours should be treated as moldy. Families kept away by an evacuation or a blocked road who can't get back within that time should walk in expecting mold rather than hoping they can still prevent it. For a household that evacuated, those hours may pass before anyone has seen the damage.

The rule asks owners to act before they see any mold. The 24-to-48-hour mark is a reason to assume mold and act. It is not a confirmed finding that a particular home has mold, so a clean-looking wall after that point is no sign that the room is safe. Owners who wait for visible proof will act too late. The safer habit is to treat any part of the home that stayed wet past that mark as moldy. Before that point, owners should keep air moving through every damp room so it has a chance to dry in time. Some losses also have to be accepted early. Anything soaked that can be neither fixed nor dried out should go out with the debris. Keeping those things in the hope that they recover only delays the rest of the work.

When mold is measured in hours, airing out the house and clearing out what can't be saved belong at the top of the cleanup list.

A household can dry its walls and confirm that its tap water is safe and still not be finished with the flood. The people who lived through it may stay shaken long after the house is livable again, and that reaction is an expected result of the disaster, not a personal failing. "It's normal to have a lot of bad feelings, stress, or anxiety," the American Red Cross said. Anyone who feels that way is not overreacting and has no reason to wait until the strain gets worse before dealing with it. Disaster stress has its own dedicated source of help, the Disaster Distress Helpline, so reaching out is a standard part of flood care rather than a last resort. The helpline is free, which means repair bills that already stretch a household's budget are no reason to put off a call.

Recovery from stress starts with ordinary care of the body. Eating healthy food and getting enough sleep both help a person cope, and those basics deserve the same priority as drying out rooms or replacing spoiled food. They should not get only the time left over once the house is handled. The helpline number, 1-800-985-5990, belongs in a phone now, before the strain arrives, so that reaching out later takes a single step.

A flood isn't over for a household until the people in it have recovered too.
