U.S. kindergarten MMR coverage sits 2.6 points below the WHO target

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

The U.S. has lost the measles-free status it held for 26 years, according to a Trump administration official. The official said the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) delivered that decision to the administration in a letter.

PAHO sent the letter on Friday. The official described it to Reuters on Monday.

The official spoke on condition of anonymity because the decision had not yet been made public. PAHO was expected to announce the decision formally on Tuesday. Until that announcement, the account of the letter rests on the official's description.

The letter did more than deliver a verdict. PAHO also urged the U.S. to step up vaccination to halt the spread of the virus, according to the official.

U.S. kindergarten vaccination coverage was 92.4% for the 2025-26 school year, the CDC reported in August. That is 2.6 percentage points short of the 95% level the World Health Organization recommends.

The figure applies to one group: children entering kindergarten in the 2025-26 school year. It is not a measure of how many Americans of all ages are protected against measles.

The CDC number is a nationwide snapshot of a single school year. By itself, it does not show how coverage has changed over time.

What it does show is the distance between current kindergarten coverage and the WHO benchmark. The WHO recommendation calls for 95% of people to receive two doses of the MMR vaccine. A national kindergarten figure below that mark means fewer children starting school are covered than the benchmark calls for.

Two doses of the MMR vaccine are 97% effective at stopping measles from spreading. The World Health Organization recommends that 95% of people receive both doses to reach herd immunity.

The two figures measure different things. The 97% figure describes how well the vaccine works in a person who completes both doses. The 95% figure describes how much of a population needs that protection.

The WHO benchmark counts people who have received two doses. A single shot does not meet it.

Herd immunity is the protection that a highly vaccinated population gives to people who cannot be vaccinated themselves. Those people have no vaccine-derived protection of their own.

They depend on high vaccination among the people around them to keep the virus from reaching them.

Some individual schools in Spartanburg County, South Carolina, reported vaccination rates as low as 20% during the state's measles outbreak earlier this year. At that level, most children in those schools were not vaccinated.

A national average cannot show gaps like these. The countrywide kindergarten figure combines schools with high and low rates into a single number.

That means a national rate close to the herd immunity target can still include individual schools where coverage is a fraction of it. The Spartanburg County schools are one such case.

Walter Orenstein, a professor emeritus at Emory University, described the task in terms of reach rather than averages. "What we need to do is terminate the current transmission," he said. "To do that, we need to enhance immunization uptake in virtually all communities."

His wording sets the goal at the level of individual communities, not the country as a whole.

Public trust in vaccination dropped during the COVID-19 pandemic and kept dropping in the period after it. Josh Michaud, a public health analyst with KFF, said rebuilding that trust depends on time and on public health officials themselves.

Reuters lists this loss of trust among the pressures that coincide with falling immunization rates in the U.S. The decline it describes concerns trust in vaccination generally.

Michaud set out what recovery would require. "Regaining that trust takes time, and requires a consistent effort from the officials that are working in public health," he said.

By his account, the work of restoring confidence sits with the people inside the public health system. He described it as a sustained effort over time.

The largest U.S. measles surge in more than three decades has come alongside falling immunization rates and rising vaccine hesitancy under Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., Reuters reports. Several experts in that reporting said official messaging about MMR risks has made public concerns worse.

The connection between Kennedy and lower coverage rests on Reuters' reporting and on the views of those experts. Reuters describes the two trends as unfolding over the same stretch of time.

Kennedy's doubts about the shot predate his current role. Reuters reports that he has questioned the safety of the measles vaccine for years. The news agency adds that he has done so without scientific evidence.

The experts' criticism extends beyond Kennedy himself. They pointed to statements from both Kennedy and the White House about the risks of the MMR vaccine. In their assessment, that messaging worsened concerns the public already held.

Those concerns did not begin with the current administration. Trust in vaccination dropped during and after the COVID-19 pandemic, and the experts said federal messaging has added to that decline.

The CDC's official stance differs from the criticism the experts described. The agency says it supports measles vaccination.

The federal government has publicly pushed several states to loosen their school vaccine mandates, according to Reuters. It has also spent millions of dollars investigating vaccine safety.

These are two distinct lines of federal action. The mandate pressure targets the requirements that individual states set for children attending school. The safety spending directs federal money toward reexamining vaccines.

School vaccine mandates are set at the state level. That is why the federal push has taken the form of public pressure on states rather than a direct change to the rules.

Every measles case recorded in the U.S. over the past two years began as a virus imported from another country, according to Walter Orenstein, a professor emeritus at Emory University. He said Americans traveling abroad often carried the virus home.

In Orenstein's account, those imported infections did not stay contained. Travelers $TRV who returned with the virus went on to set off outbreaks in their local communities.

Orenstein said the U.S. needs to resume its support for global immunization efforts. He said that support was disrupted when the U.S. left the World Health Organization.

The Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) judged U.S. measles status by asking whether a single strain of the virus had circulated in the country for 12 months or longer. A senior Trump administration official, speaking anonymously, said the Utah outbreak, which began in June 2025, was key to that determination.

The test turns on how long one strain kept spreading, not on the size of any single outbreak. What matters is whether the same strain moved through the country without a break for a full year.

PAHO reviewed epidemiological and laboratory evidence supplied by the CDC to answer that question. Together, the two kinds of evidence let reviewers tie individual cases to a specific strain and place them on a timeline.

The week before the official described PAHO's letter, a U.S. independent advisory panel presented the CDC's measles status report to PAHO. The panel added its own assessment that the U.S. had likely lost elimination status.

Walter Orenstein, a professor emeritus at Emory University, served on that panel.

The panel's assessment and the official's account point in the same direction. PAHO's formal announcement was still expected when the official spoke.

To regain measles elimination status, the U.S. must show that transmission has stopped and has not been sustained for 12 months. It also needs a high-quality surveillance system that functions.

The two conditions are linked. Proving that no chain of spread has lasted a full year requires a system that can find and confirm cases. Without reliable detection, the first condition cannot be shown.

Jennifer Nuzzo, director of the Pandemic Center at Brown University, pointed to outbreaks that keep appearing in Pennsylvania, New York and Wisconsin. In her view, there is no clear evidence that national transmission has ended. She also said it is unlikely that every case is being counted.

Her two points bear on both requirements. Continuing outbreaks in three states work against the first condition. Cases that go uncounted point to a weakness in the second.

Two further public health tools are needed. The first is a stronger surveillance network that can identify the measles virus and test for it. The second is more epidemiological capacity. That means the ability to follow up on cases and break chains of transmission.

The two tools serve different stages of a response. Surveillance finds the virus. Epidemiological follow-up works to stop it from passing from one person to the next.

Dr. Ben Kasstan-Dabush, assistant professor of global health and development at the London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine, said the U.S. needs to raise vaccination across all groups. His emphasis is on breadth, with coverage rising in every group rather than in a single population.

CDC Director Erica Schwartz, who has held the job for three months, told Reuters last week that the agency would not change its response if elimination status is lost. She made that comment before PAHO's expected formal announcement.
