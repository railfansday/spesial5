10 things to know about filing an Army claim after a blast damages property

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

How an Army claim for property damage works after a military blast such as the lead azide detonation in Iowa, from the Federal Tort Claims Act and SF 95 to deadlines, exceptions and fee limits

A blast from a nearby Army range, demolition site or training exercise does not, on its own, entitle a property owner to payment from the federal government. Cracked plaster, broken windows and damaged foundations may all trace back to a military explosion. The government pays only when a claim meets conditions that Congress wrote into federal law.

The route to compensation is an Army claim for property damage. It is a formal demand for money presented to the Army, and it must come before any lawsuit. The main law behind these claims is the Federal Tort Claims Act. That law lets people seek money from the U.S. for property damage caused by the negligent or wrongful acts of federal employees acting within the scope of their jobs.

Army Regulation 27-20 governs how the Army handles claims. Army claims offices process them, and the U.S. Army Claims Service at Fort Meade, Maryland, sits at the top of that system. Two other laws, the Military Claims Act and the Foreign Claims Act, cover some cases the main statute does not reach.

Three requirements shape a claim under that statute. The claimant has to show fault, not only that damage happened near a federal operation. The claim has to reach the proper agency within a fixed deadline. It also has to name an exact dollar amount, and that figure limits what the claimant can later seek in court.

The law also shields the government in other ways. Claims based on discretionary government decisions are barred, even when the discretion behind them was abused. Claims arising from combatant activities during wartime are excluded as well. The danger of explosives or blasting does not by itself make the government liable.

We cover each piece of the process below: the laws that apply, the form used to file and the filing deadline. We also cover the dollar figure a claim must state and the evidence that supports it. We also cover the fault requirement, the exceptions that bar some claims, what happens after the Army receives a claim and the caps on what lawyers can charge. Each entry describes how the system works rather than offering legal advice for a specific case.

The Federal Tort Claims Act is the main law a property owner uses to seek money from the federal government after an Army blast. It allows money claims for property damage caused by federal employees.

The law is usually shortened to FTCA. Most of it sits at 28 U.S.C. section 1346(b) and sections 2671 through 2680 of the same title.

That range covers more than liability. It also holds the rules for presenting a claim to an agency, the limits on attorney fees and the exceptions that bar some claims. Those sit at sections 2675, 2678 and 2680.

Three conditions sit at the center of the statute. The damage must trace back to a federal employee. That employee must have been acting within the scope of the job when the damage happened. The scope requirement ties a claim to the work the government assigned, not to what an employee does on personal time. The act itself must have been negligent or wrongful. A claim that fails any one of these conditions falls outside the FTCA.

The FTCA borrows its standard of liability from state law. The law of the state where the act happened decides whether the government is liable. A claimant's case turns on how that state treats negligence and wrongful conduct, not on one national standard. A blast in Texas is measured against Texas law. A blast in Washington state is measured against Washington state law. Two similar incidents in different states can be judged under different rules as a result.

Army claims offices handle FTCA claims tied to Army activity. The U.S. Army Claims Service at Fort Meade, Maryland, sits at the top of that system. That puts the first stage of a claim in the hands of an Army claims office rather than a court.

Two federal laws besides the Federal Tort Claims Act can cover property damage tied to the Army. The Military Claims Act reaches certain claims from noncombat Army activities. The Foreign Claims Act covers damage caused by U.S. forces outside the U.S.

The Military Claims Act appears at 10 U.S.C. section 2733. Its reach is limited to certain claims arising from noncombat Army activities. It does not cover every incident that involves the Army.

This statute can apply where the Federal Tort Claims Act does not. A property owner whose claim falls outside the tort statute may still have a route to payment under the Military Claims Act.

Whether the law fits a particular blast turns on the activity that caused the damage. The question is whether that activity was a noncombat Army activity of the kind the statute covers.

The Foreign Claims Act appears at 10 U.S.C. section 2734. It addresses damage caused by U.S. forces outside the U.S. Under this law, the location of the damage is the starting point.

A blast tied to U.S. forces abroad raises different questions than one at a domestic installation. The overseas setting is what brings the Foreign Claims Act into play.

The two statutes sort claims by different tests. The Military Claims Act looks at the type of Army activity behind the damage. The Foreign Claims Act looks at where U.S. forces caused it.

Army Regulation 27-20 governs Army claims, including those brought under these two statutes.

Service members' own injury claims are treated differently and follow separate rules.

Standard Form 95, titled Claim for Damage, Injury, or Death, is the federal form used to present a claim under the Federal Tort Claims Act. A property owner seeking payment for blast damage from Army activity can file it with the Army.

The form is usually called SF 95. It is a standard federal form rather than an Army form. Claimants use it to present a Federal Tort Claims Act claim to whichever federal agency was involved. When the damage traces to Army activity, that agency is the Army.

The title shows the form's range. One form serves claims for damaged property, for personal injury and for death. A homeowner reporting a damaged roof after a blast uses the same document as someone reporting an injury.

The form is not the only accepted format. A claimant can also present the claim in a letter or another written document.

Any such writing must meet two conditions. It has to give the agency enough information to investigate the claim. It also has to demand a specific amount of money.

The first condition supports the review itself. An Army claims office examines a claim before deciding on it. A writing that leaves out what was damaged, where it happened or how it happened gives the office little to investigate.

The second condition rules out open-ended requests. A demand for fair compensation, or for whatever repairs end up costing, names no amount. A writing without a stated dollar figure does not meet the requirement.

Filing SF 95 or a qualifying letter starts the administrative stage of a federal tort claim. It does not decide the outcome. The Army still has to review the claim and decide whether to pay it.

A property owner seeking payment for Army blast damage under the Federal Tort Claims Act has two years to present a claim to the proper federal agency. Under 28 U.S.C. section 2401(b), a tort claim against the U.S. that misses that window is barred.

The two-year clock starts when the claim accrues. Accrual is the legal starting point for the period. The statute measures the deadline from that point. It does not run from the date an owner decides to seek payment.

The deadline applies to presenting the claim, not to filing a lawsuit. Presenting means delivering the claim to the agency responsible for the activity that caused the harm. For damage tied to Army activity, that agency is the Army. Army claims offices receive these filings.

The word barred carries real weight in the statute. A claim that reaches the agency after the two-year period closes cannot go forward under the FTCA.

The administrative claim is a required first step. An owner cannot bypass the Army and take an FTCA property damage claim straight to federal court. The claim must go to the agency first. Any lawsuit can follow only after that stage.

Section 2401(b) also sets a second, later deadline. That limit governs filing suit after an agency denies a claim in writing. An FTCA case therefore runs on two separate clocks. One governs reaching the agency, and the other governs reaching a courtroom. The two-year period covers only the first.

A Federal Tort Claims Act claim must name one specific dollar amount, known as a sum certain. That figure does two jobs. It tells the Army what the claimant wants paid. It also sets the most the claimant can later seek in court.

Under this requirement, a claim has to demand a definite amount of money. A property owner whose home, barn or fence was damaged by an Army blast cannot leave the figure open. The owner also cannot ask the agency to decide what the loss is worth. The claim has to put a number on the harm.

The requirement holds whether the claim arrives on Standard Form 95 or in a letter. A letter can describe the damage in detail. Without a dollar amount, it still falls short of a valid claim.

The ceiling matters most if the dispute reaches federal court. There, the claimant generally cannot sue for more than the amount stated to the agency. That limit comes from 28 U.S.C. section 2675(b).

The same provision allows two narrow exceptions. The first covers newly discovered evidence that was not reasonably discoverable when the claim was presented. The second covers intervening facts relating to the amount of the claim. For that second route, the claimant must allege and prove those facts.

The first exception looks back at information that existed but could not reasonably have been found at filing. The second looks forward to facts that developed after the claim was presented. Neither gives a claimant a general option to raise the number later.

For blast damage, this structure puts weight on the valuation done before filing. A known repair left out of the figure does not become newly discovered evidence because it was overlooked. The same applies to a damaged item the owner knew about but did not include.

The sum certain is the claimant's own valuation of the loss. It reflects the claimant's estimate of what repairs and replacements will cost.

A property owner who files an Army claim carries the burden of proving it. A well-supported file usually combines dated photographs and repair estimates with receipts, proof of ownership and records showing the property's condition before the blast.

None of this comes from a single checklist in the statute. It reflects general claims practice. The formal floor is simpler: a written claim must give the agency enough information to investigate. Evidence is what turns that floor into a claim an investigator can verify.

Dated photographs record the damage itself. Pictures taken soon after the blast capture cracked walls, broken windows or shifted foundations before weather, cleanup or repairs change them. A date on each image helps tie the damage to the time of the event rather than to an earlier or later cause.

Repair estimates translate visible damage into money. A written estimate from a contractor describes the work needed and what it should cost. Having more than one estimate gives an investigator a way to check whether a figure is reasonable.

Receipts do a related but separate job. They document money already spent, such as emergency repairs, a temporary cover for a damaged roof or a replacement for a broken item. An estimate predicts a cost. A receipt shows a cost that was paid.

Proof of ownership answers a threshold question: whether the person filing has a stake in the damaged property. A deed, a vehicle title or a bill of sale can establish that link.

Records of the property's condition before the blast fill a gap that post-blast photos cannot. Older photos, inspection reports, appraisals or maintenance records can show that a crack or leak was not already there. Without them, an investigator has no baseline against which to measure what changed.

Gaps in the record fall on the person who filed. Missing or undated documents weaken the claimant's case, not the agency's.

An Army blast that damages property does not create a right to payment on its own. Under the Federal Tort Claims Act, the claimant must show fault — a negligent or wrongful act by a federal employee — not simply that the activity was dangerous.

That fault requirement sits in the statute itself. The law permits money claims for harm caused by the negligent or wrongful acts of federal workers. It does not open the U.S. Treasury to every loss that follows government activity.

Negligence, in general legal terms, is a failure to use the care a reasonable person would use in the same situation. A wrongful act is conduct the law treats as improper. Either one can support a claim. Without one of them, the claim has no footing.

The Supreme Court took up this question in Laird v. Nelms, decided in 1972. The case involved property damage blamed on a sonic boom. At issue was whether the government could be held liable for that damage without proof of a negligent or wrongful act.

The court answered no. It held that the FTCA does not impose strict liability for ultrahazardous activity. Strict liability means responsibility for harm regardless of fault. Under the ruling, the act reaches fault-based claims, not claims resting on the danger of the activity alone.

For explosives, the consequence is direct. A claimant generally cannot recover just by showing that a detonation or blasting operation carried risk. The hazardous nature of the work does not stand in for proof of a careless or wrongful act.

State law still matters to these claims. The FTCA generally looks to the law of the state where the act happened. Laird v. Nelms means a state rule that imposes liability without fault does not carry over to a claim against the government under the FTCA.

A claim built on fault therefore points to conduct. It identifies what a federal employee did, or failed to do, that fell short of reasonable care and led to the damage.

The discretionary function exception, at 28 U.S.C. section 2680(a), bars claims against the federal government that rest on the exercise of a discretionary function or duty. The bar applies even when the discretion was abused.

Section 2680 is the part of the Federal Tort Claims Act that lists its exceptions, each in its own lettered subsection. Each one removes a category of claims from the law's reach. A claim that falls inside one of them cannot succeed under the FTCA, however much damage the property suffered.

The discretionary function language covers choices made by a federal agency as well as those made by an individual government employee. It also covers a failure to exercise or perform such a function. The same subsection separately bars claims based on an employee's use of due care in carrying out a statute or regulation, valid or not.

The wording of section 2680(a) does not turn on whether a decision was sound. A choice can be a poor one. It can even be an abuse of discretion. If the conduct counts as a discretionary function or duty, the exception still covers it.

That feature sets the exception apart from an ordinary fault inquiry. A property owner may be able to point to careless conduct behind a blast. The claim can still fail if the conduct falls within the exception.

Dalehite v. United States, decided by the Supreme Court in 1953, is the standard early authority on the discretionary function exception. The case is known as the Texas City disaster case.

A second carve-out sits at section 2680(j). It excludes claims arising from the combatant activities of the military or naval forces, or the Coast Guard, during time of war. This exception looks at the setting of the conduct rather than the kind of decision behind it.

Damage tied to wartime combat therefore falls outside the FTCA. Whether an exception applies is a separate question from whether negligence can be shown. A claim has to clear both.

If the Army has not made a final decision within six months of filing, the claimant may treat that as a denial and take the claim to federal court. A written denial starts a separate six-month clock to file suit.

The first window comes from 28 U.S.C. section 2675(a). It gives the Army a defined stretch of time to investigate and decide. The statute frames the deemed denial as an option for the claimant, not an obligation. A claimant who would rather keep waiting for the Army's answer is not forced to sue at the six-month mark.

The second window works differently. Under 28 U.S.C. section 2401(b), a claimant who receives a written denial has six months to file suit in federal district court. That clock starts on the date the denial was mailed. It does not wait for the letter to arrive. A suit filed after the window closes is barred.

Together, the two six-month rules mean the path to court opens either through the Army's silence or through its written refusal.

A denial does not have to end the administrative process. The Justice Department's regulations at 28 C.F.R. section 14.9 allow a claimant to ask the agency to reconsider a denial. That request keeps the dispute with the Army rather than moving it straight to a courtroom.

A lawsuit, if one follows, runs differently from many civil cases. Under 28 U.S.C. section 2402, a judge decides an FTCA case, not a jury. The claimant presents evidence and argument to the court. The judge then rules on both the facts and the law.

The money available in court is also limited. Section 2674 bars punitive damages against the U.S. A claimant can seek payment for the property loss itself. The court cannot add an award meant to punish the government for its conduct.

Federal law limits what a lawyer may take from a Federal Tort Claims Act recovery. Under 28 U.S.C. section 2678, attorney fees cannot exceed 20 percent of an administrative settlement. The cap rises to 25 percent for a judgment or for a settlement reached after a lawsuit is filed.

The two caps track the two stages of an FTCA claim. The 20 percent limit governs a claim settled at the agency level, before anyone goes to court. For property damaged by an Army blast, that means a settlement reached through the Army claims process.

The 25 percent limit applies once the dispute moves into litigation. It covers a judgment entered by a federal district court. It also covers a settlement the parties reach after the lawsuit has been filed.

The ceiling depends on when the case ends, not on the size of the payment. A settlement reached at the administrative stage falls under the lower cap. The same dollar amount agreed after a suit begins falls under the higher one. The two ceilings sit five percentage points apart.

The caps are written into the statute, so they apply to fee arrangements in FTCA matters. A claimant can compare the percentage in a fee agreement with the ceiling for the stage the claim has reached.

A lawyer is not required to file. A property owner can prepare and present an administrative claim personally, without hiring counsel. The claim goes to the same Army claims offices that handle submissions prepared by lawyers.

A claimant who files alone handles each step of the process. That includes describing what happened, gathering records of the damage and putting a dollar figure on the loss. No attorney fee comes out of the payment when no lawyer is involved.

The caps apply only to money actually paid through a settlement or judgment. They limit a lawyer's share of a recovery. They do not guarantee that any recovery will be paid.
