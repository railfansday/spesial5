Can bank deposits be affected in a cyberattack? 10 things to know

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Bank deposits can be affected in a cyberattack through blocked access, data breaches or account takeover, and FDIC insurance and Regulation E protect money in different ways.

A cyberattack on a bank and a theft from one customer's account are separate events, and U.S. rules treat them differently. The first targets the institution's systems. The second targets a specific balance, and it is the more direct path from a hacker to a depositor's money.

So can bank deposits be affected in a cyberattack? The answer depends on the kind of attack. Banks record balances in ledgers and keep backups. An attack on their systems does not by itself erase what customers hold. Ransomware or a distributed denial-of-service attack can still lock people out of online banking and apps for a time. A data breach can expose names, account numbers or Social Security numbers. That exposure does not move money on its own. It does give criminals material for later fraud.

Protection also depends on details that are easy to overlook. Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation coverage applies when an insured bank fails. It covers up to $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category. It is not built to repay money a criminal takes from an account at a bank that stays open. Credit unions carry comparable coverage through the National Credit Union Share Insurance Fund, run by the National Credit Union Administration. The standard limit is the same $250,000.

Recourse for theft comes from elsewhere. Regulation E, which implements the Electronic Fund Transfer Act, limits consumer liability for unauthorized electronic transfers. How much a customer can lose depends partly on how quickly the problem is reported. Credit cards fall under a separate federal law, the Fair Credit Billing Act. Business accounts generally sit outside Regulation E. They rely largely on the Uniform Commercial Code and the account agreement.

Scams add another layer. When a criminal tricks a customer into sending money, the payment is generally not treated as an unauthorized transfer under Regulation E. That distinction can shape what a bank is required to do.

The 10 slides that follow cover ways cyberattacks and fraud can reach a deposit account, along with the protections that apply to each. They run from insurance limits and reporting deadlines to account takeover, ransomware and data breaches. The last slide covers everyday habits, such as transaction alerts and stronger login security. Those habits help catch fraud early and make stolen credentials harder to use.

A cyberattack on a bank's own systems does not, by itself, wipe out the balance recorded in a customer's account. The larger risk to an individual depositor is a criminal getting into a specific account and moving money out of it.

A bank hack and a theft from one person's checking account can sound like the same event. They work through different mechanisms and lead to different remedies. Knowing which one happened determines how a deposit was exposed and which protections apply.

A deposit balance is a record. It is the bank's accounting of what it owes a customer. Banks keep that record in ledgers and back it up. When a bank's computer systems go down, the obligation does not disappear. The money a customer deposited is still owed, even if the screen showing it is temporarily unavailable.

Cyberattacks reach depositors along three broad paths. The first is disruption. An attack can knock out online banking, mobile apps or transfers for a period. Customers may not be able to see or move their money while systems are restored. Losing access for a time is not the same as losing the balance.

The second path is data exposure. A breach hands criminals personal and account details, but stolen data does not move money on its own. What it provides is material for later impersonation or for opening accounts in a customer's name.

The third path is account access. This is where deposits are most directly at risk. A criminal with working login credentials can start transfers that pull real funds out of a real account. Unlike the other two paths, this one can reduce a balance directly.

Each path calls for a different response. Disruption mostly means waiting until service returns. Data exposure calls for watching for follow-on fraud. Unauthorized access calls for contacting the bank as soon as the activity is spotted.

Which rules apply after a loss depends on what happened. The answer turns on whether the institution failed and whether the transfer was authorized. It also turns on whether the account is a consumer or business account, and how quickly the problem was reported.

FDIC insurance protects deposits up to $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category. It pays out when an insured bank fails. A hack affects this coverage only if the bank itself fails.

The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation is an independent agency of the U.S. government. Congress created it through the Banking Act of 1933, after a wave of bank failures in the early 1930s. Its insurance is backed by the full faith and credit of the U.S. government. Coverage is automatic. A depositor does not need to apply for it or buy a policy.

The trigger is the failure of the institution, not the cause of the trouble. A bank could close because of bad loans, a run on deposits or, in theory, damage tied to a cyberattack. In each case the insured amount is the same. The FDIC generally either arranges for a healthy bank to take over the failed bank's deposits or pays insured depositors directly.

Ownership category sets the effective ceiling. The FDIC separates accounts by how they are owned. Common categories include single accounts, joint accounts, certain retirement accounts such as individual retirement accounts, trust accounts and business accounts. Each ownership category at the same bank carries its own $250,000 limit. A person with a single account and a share of a joint account at one bank can be insured for more than $250,000 in total.

The coverage applies to deposit products. Those include checking accounts, savings accounts, money market deposit accounts and certificates of deposit. It does not extend to stocks, bonds, mutual funds, annuities, crypto assets or life insurance policies. That holds even when those products are sold through an insured bank. The contents of a safe deposit box are not covered either.

Credit unions use a parallel system. The National Credit Union Administration insures member deposits through the National Credit Union Share Insurance Fund. Its standard limit is also $250,000. As with the FDIC, the fund pays out when an insured credit union fails.

A depositor can confirm whether a bank is insured through the FDIC's BankFind tool. The NCUA offers a similar search for federally insured credit unions.

FDIC insurance pays depositors when an insured bank fails, not when a criminal takes money from an account at a bank that stays open. If a fraudster drains a checking account at a healthy bank, deposit insurance is not the tool that restores those funds.

Deposit insurance has a single trigger: the failure of an insured institution. When a bank fails, the FDIC steps in so insured depositors can reach their money. An account emptied by a hacker does not meet that condition. The bank is still solvent and still operating.

Credit unions work the same way. The National Credit Union Administration insures member deposits through the National Credit Union Share Insurance Fund. That fund also responds to the failure of an insured credit union. It does not reimburse a member whose savings a criminal moved out of an account at a credit union that remains open.

The two situations raise different questions. A bank failure asks whether an institution can meet its obligations to all of its depositors at once. Theft from one account asks who bears the loss, the customer or the bank. Deposit insurance was designed to answer the first question, not the second.

For theft, recourse comes from three other places. The first is the bank's own obligations to its customer. The second is federal law on electronic payments. Regulation E, which implements the Electronic Fund Transfer Act, sets rules for unauthorized electronic transfers on consumer accounts. The third is the account agreement, the contract a customer accepts when opening the account. It spells out how disputes, errors and security responsibilities are handled.

A balance well under the insurance limit can still be stolen. The size of a deposit says little about protection from fraud. What shapes the outcome after a theft is the type of account, the type of payment and how fast the customer tells the bank.

Regulation E, which implements the federal Electronic Fund Transfer Act, limits how much a consumer can lose when someone moves money out of an account without permission. The rule sets liability in tiers, and each tier is tied to a reporting deadline.

The rule applies to electronic fund transfers on consumer accounts. These are accounts held by individuals for personal, family or household purposes. Debit card purchases, ATM withdrawals and online transfers out of a checking or savings account all fall within its scope.

An unauthorized transfer is one started by someone without the account holder's permission. The account holder also receives no benefit from it. A thief using a stolen debit card fits that definition. So does a criminal who logs in with stolen credentials and sends money elsewhere.

The first liability tier is the narrowest. It applies when a debit card or other access device is lost or stolen. If the consumer tells the bank within two business days of learning about it, liability is capped at $50. If the unauthorized transfers before that notice total less than $50, the consumer owes only the smaller amount.

Waiting longer raises the ceiling. Reporting after the two-business-day mark can push the consumer's liability up to as much as $500. The clock starts when the consumer learns of the loss or theft, not on the day the card went missing.

These figures are legal maximums, not amounts a consumer automatically owes. Bank or card network policies can be more generous than Regulation E requires. The account agreement spells out which terms apply to a given account.

Regulation E also requires banks to investigate errors that consumers report. Unauthorized transfers count as errors under the rule. A prompt, specific report gives the bank what it needs to begin that review.

The protections have limits on who they reach. Business accounts are generally not covered by Regulation E. Disputes over unauthorized transfers on those accounts are governed largely by the Uniform Commercial Code and by the terms of the account agreement. A company and an individual customer at the same bank can face different rules for the same type of fraud.

A consumer who misses the 60-day deadline to report an unauthorized electronic transfer can face larger losses on later transfers. Under Regulation E, the clock starts when the bank sends the statement that shows the problem.

This deadline is separate from the rules about a lost or stolen card. It does not depend on the customer knowing that a card is missing. It turns on what appears on the periodic statement, whether that statement arrives on paper or electronically. Once the bank transmits a statement showing an unauthorized transfer, the consumer has 60 days to flag it.

The rule carries the most weight when nothing physical goes missing. A criminal can copy card details at a compromised terminal, steal an account number in a data breach or log in with stolen credentials. The customer may still hold the debit card and have no reason to suspect anything. The statement then becomes the first record of the theft.

The larger exposure applies to transfers that happen after the 60-day period ends and before the consumer reports. For those later transfers, the consumer can be held responsible. To hold the consumer liable, the bank must show that the transfers would not have occurred if the consumer had reported in time.

The logic is about prevention. A thief who goes unnoticed for months can keep moving money out of an account. Regulation E places part of the cost of that delay on an account holder who had a statement in hand and did not act on it.

The regulation allows the time limit to be extended for extenuating circumstances. It names extended travel and hospitalization as examples. In those situations, the bank must extend the period to a reasonable time.

The window covers electronic activity on consumer accounts. That includes debit card purchases, ATM withdrawals, online bill payments and electronic transfers between accounts.

Reviewing every statement, or the transaction history in a banking app, keeps the deadline from coming into play. Contacting the bank by phone or in writing as soon as an unfamiliar transaction appears gives the bank notice. Notice ends the consumer's exposure to further losses under this rule. Keeping a record of the date, the person contacted and what was said creates evidence of when that notice was given.

Federal law caps a consumer's liability for unauthorized credit card charges at $50. Fraudulent credit card charges also draw on the card issuer's credit line, not on money sitting in a bank deposit account.

That difference matters most for anyone worried about their deposits. A stolen credit card number gives a criminal access to borrowed money. The charges show up as a balance owed to the issuer. The cash in the cardholder's checking or savings account stays where it is.

The $50 cap comes from the Fair Credit Billing Act. The Federal Trade Commission and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau both describe that limit in their consumer guidance. Card networks and issuers can also set policies that are more generous than the legal minimum.

Debit cards work differently. A debit card is tied directly to a checking account. When a criminal uses a stolen debit card number, the money leaves the customer's own account. The customer then has to dispute the charges to recover those funds.

That difference has practical consequences. While a debit dispute is being resolved, the money is missing from the account. A lower balance can cause other payments, such as rent, bills or automatic transfers, to be declined. Depending on the account's terms, it can also trigger overdraft fees.

Debit card disputes fall under Regulation E, which implements the Electronic Fund Transfer Act. Under that rule, the customer's share of a loss depends on how quickly the problem is reported. The credit card cap under the Fair Credit Billing Act does not rise in tiers based on reporting speed.

For online shopping and other card-not-present purchases, the type of card determines where the exposure sits. A stolen credit card number puts the issuer's credit line at risk. A stolen debit card number puts the cardholder's own deposits at risk.

Both protections still depend on the cardholder. Unauthorized charges have to be spotted and reported to the bank or card issuer.

Account takeover happens when a criminal gains working login access to someone's online banking and then moves money out as if they were the customer. The attack usually starts with stolen credentials, gathered through phishing, reused passwords or malware on the victim's device.

Phishing is the most direct route. A message poses as the bank, a delivery company or a payment app. It sends the target to a fake login page. Whatever the person types there goes straight to the attacker.

Reused passwords open a second door. When one website leaks its login data, criminals try the same email and password combinations on banking sites. This tactic is known as credential stuffing. It works only when a customer has used the same password in more than one place.

Malware takes a quieter path. Keyloggers record what a user types. Some banking trojans can capture login details or display fake screens inside a real session. The customer may see nothing unusual while the software collects what the attacker needs.

Once inside, the criminal tends to act fast. Common moves include adding a new payee, changing the contact email or phone number on file and sending transfers to accounts the attacker controls. Changing contact details can divert alerts and verification codes away from the real account holder. That buys the attacker time to move money before anyone notices.

On a consumer account, a transfer started by a criminal who logged in without the customer's permission is generally an unauthorized electronic transfer under Regulation E. The customer did not initiate the payment and did not agree to it.

A different scam can look similar on a bank statement but is treated differently. In an authorized push payment scam, the criminal never takes over the account. The customer is persuaded to send the money personally. The person doing the persuading often poses as a bank employee, a government official or a romantic partner.

Because the customer initiated the transfer, these payments are generally not treated as unauthorized under Regulation E. The central question after a loss is who actually started the transfer — the account holder or someone who broke into the account.

Ransomware or a distributed denial-of-service attack can shut off online banking, mobile apps or transfers for a time. Losing access is not the same as losing money. A disruption alone does not erase the balances a bank has recorded.

The two attacks work differently. Ransomware is malicious software that encrypts a target's files and systems, locking the owner out of them. The criminals behind it typically demand payment for a decryption key. A distributed denial-of-service attack, usually shortened to DDoS, does not break into systems at all. It floods a website or app with so much traffic from many sources that legitimate customers cannot get through.

For a depositor, the visible effect of either attack can look similar. A login page may fail to load. A banking app may show errors. Transfers, bill payments or card transactions that depend on the affected systems may stall until service returns.

The records behind those screens determine what each customer owns. Banks keep ledger records of every account and maintain backups of that data. Those records define how much money each customer holds. An outage on the customer-facing side interrupts the ability to view or move funds. It does not rewrite the underlying account history.

Ransomware can be the more disruptive of the two for a bank's operations. It can lock internal systems rather than just block the front door. Recovery can take longer as a result. Backups can let a bank rebuild its systems without relying on the attackers for a key. A DDoS attack usually subsides once the traffic stops or is filtered out.

The practical risk for customers during an outage is timing. A payment due on a specific date may not go through while systems are down. Knowing which bills are coming up, and having another way to pay them, reduces that exposure.

An outage can also become a lure for scammers. Messages claiming to come from the affected bank may ask customers to confirm account details or log in through a link. Contacting the bank through a phone number or website already known to be genuine avoids that trap.

A data breach at a bank exposes information, not money. The stolen records do not move a single dollar on their own. The danger to deposits comes later, when criminals use that data to commit other kinds of fraud.

A breach happens when an outsider gains access to records a bank holds about its customers. The exposed material can include contact details, account numbers and government identifiers. None of this changes the balance recorded in a customer's account. What changes is how much a criminal knows about that customer.

That knowledge makes phishing more convincing. A fraudulent email, text or phone call that cites a real account number or a correct home address looks far more legitimate than a generic message. The goal is usually to get the customer to reveal a password, a one-time code or a card number. Each of those can then open a path into the account itself.

SIM swapping is another follow-on risk. In a SIM swap, a criminal persuades a mobile carrier to move the victim's phone number to a SIM card the criminal controls. Personal details taken in a breach can help the criminal pass the carrier's identity checks. Once the number moves, text messages meant for the victim go to the criminal. That includes the security codes many banks send by text.

New account fraud uses exposed data in a different way. The criminal does not break into an existing account. The criminal opens a new one in the victim's name instead. A Social Security number combined with a name and birth date can be enough to apply for credit. The victim may not learn of it until a debt collector calls or a credit report shows an unfamiliar account.

A credit freeze is the response most specific to this risk. A freeze restricts access to a person's credit report. That makes it much harder for a criminal to get new credit approved in that person's name. A U.S. consumer places it separately with each of the three nationwide credit bureaus: Equifax, Experian and TransUnion. A freeze does not affect existing accounts or the credit score. It also does not stop fraud on a deposit account whose details a criminal already holds.

Timing is part of the risk. Stolen data can be sold, traded or reused. Fraud tied to a breach can therefore surface long after the incident itself. A breach notice signals a higher chance of suspicious contact, not a loss that has already happened.

Four habits do much of the work of protecting money in a bank account. They are real-time transaction alerts, unique passwords, phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication and reporting problems to the bank right away.

Transaction alerts are an early-warning system that many banks and credit unions offer. Customers can often set notifications for any withdrawal, any transfer above a chosen amount, a new payee, a password change or a login from an unfamiliar device. Alerts arrive by text, email or push notification from the bank's app. Their value is speed. A customer who sees an unexpected transfer within minutes can contact the bank while the problem is still small.

Alerts work alongside a regular look at the account itself. Checking recent activity in the app every few days can catch small test charges. Fraudsters sometimes use these to confirm that a stolen card or login works before trying a larger withdrawal.

Reused passwords are a common way into accounts. When one website is breached, criminals try the same email and password combination on other sites. This technique is called credential stuffing. A password used only for the bank account means a leak somewhere else cannot unlock it.

A password manager makes unique passwords practical. It generates long random passwords, stores them in an encrypted vault and fills them in automatically. Many password managers autofill only on the exact website where a password was saved. That behavior can help expose a lookalike phishing page.

Multi-factor authentication adds a second check beyond the password, but the methods differ in strength. Codes sent by text message can be intercepted if a criminal gets a mobile carrier to move the victim's phone number to a new SIM card. Authenticator apps generate codes on the device itself, so a hijacked phone number does not expose them.

Passkeys go further. A passkey is a cryptographic credential linked to one specific website and stored on the user's own devices. It cannot be typed into a fake site. That design makes it resistant to phishing. Where a bank offers passkeys or an authenticator app, those options are stronger than SMS codes.

Reporting a problem is safest through a trusted channel. The phone number on the back of a debit card or on the bank's official website is the reliable choice. Calling a number from an unexpected text or email risks reaching a scammer instead of the bank. Writing down dates, the names of staff spoken to and any case numbers creates a record if the dispute takes time.

If a bank does not resolve a problem, consumers can file a complaint with the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau. The agency forwards complaints to the company involved and asks it to respond.
