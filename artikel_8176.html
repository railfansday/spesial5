The Dump Truckers: Behind the Tramily Name

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

I awoke in our tent at the Muskrat Creek Shelter to the sound of birds chirping. Through the open flap of my vestibule I could see nothing but a wall of green rhododendron and gray fog. Yet another late winter misty morning in the Appalachians.

The climb up Standing Indian Mountain was kind to us, offering a gradual incline. Nearing the top we walked across a spooky, socked-in ridgeline. Beige dead grasses and deep crimson bare branches of a leafless shrub lined the dirt trail. Above us the fog tangled eerily in crooked branches of naked trees. The summit offered no views. We only knew we’d reached it because of the sign.

Later in the day I saw another sign: the first indication that spring was coming. The tiniest blue flowers, no bigger than my pinky nail. Called azure bluets (also known as Quaker ladies), these are indeed classified as “spring ephemerals.” They’re wildflowers that bloom early in the season, capitalizing on the additional sunlight before the trees grow their summer canopy.

As Show and I walked with Leigh, a North Carolina native, she pointed out another local plant to us. Growing alongside the trail was a low-growing, leafy plant with maroon heart-shaped leaves. “This is called galax,” she informed us. Typically this plant is green in color, but we were now seeing it with it’s cold weather and sun defenses up. A shade dweller, galax produces red and purple anthocyanin pigments in the winter to both protect it from excess sunlight due to the bare trees but also to help with absorbing additional light energy while the sun is weaker.

If anyone reading this was on trail behind us this year, it’s possible you noticed the name “Dump Truckers” signed in a log book here and there. Or noticed a comment thread inviting you to participate in a “Dump Truck Challenge” on FarOut’s Albert Mountain waypoint. Maybe you thought, what’s with this dump truck stuff? Well, here’s the backstory.

To explain this I have to take it back to my thru-hike of the Pacific Crest Trail in 2023. Anyone who’s hiked the PCT northbound may remember a challenge of the same name posted to the Washington FarOut map. Essentially, it’s an unofficial time trial tradition that thru-hikers complete to put their 2,400 miles worth of trail legs to the test. We call it this because the grueling climb requires immense effort from your glutes, also known as your ass, also known as your “dump truck.” Roughly 700 feet of elevation over 0.7 miles up a steep pitch between Snoqualmie and Stevens Passes. That’s where the challenge originates, as far as I’m concerned.

Spectacle Lake in Washington on the PCT, located just a few trail miles south of the DTC. Photo taken on my 2023 thru-hike.

For whatever unremembered reason, Show and I recalled this challenge as we approached Albert Mountain in North Carolina. This mountain has significance because its summit is the 100 mile marker of the Appalachian Trail northbound. An idea sparked: what if we brought the Dump Truck Challenge to the Appalachian Trail?

Why we decided to implement this at merely mile 100 versus over 2,000 is neither here nor there. The timing was coincidental, not impeccable. We wanted something special to commemorate having hiked our first 100 miles and this turned out to be the thing. And in our defense, the AT DTC is theoretically easier because it’s shorter: only 0.3 miles up, a little under 400 feet. How bad could it be?

Can you spot the Albert Mountain fire tower through the trees?

In comparison to the one on the PCT? Bad. At least for me.

Show started first, taking off at a rather quick pace from the bottom of the first steps by the last road crossing before the summit and fire tower, the trial’s official starting line, as established by us. I waited a bit at that first stair to leave some distance between us, not that it was necessary. I was fairly certain I wouldn’t catch him. But just in case.

I went next. Plugging into my earbuds with some upbeat music on, I clicked the button on my watch to start the timer. Foolishly, I gave into the adrenaline coursing through my body and pushed off at far too fast a clip. About halfway up I was already gassed, having paced it incorrectly. I’m a bit ashamed to admit I more or less gave up and walked the rest of the hill at my normal pace, gasping for air, sweat pouring down my face. Nearing the top I picked it up to a half-hearted hustle for the last 100 feet or so to the firetower: the finish line.

I tagged the metal scaffolding at 9 minutes 37 seconds. Show had smoked me with his time of 7 minutes 25 seconds. I didn’t let the results bother me. It was so early on in the trail that it was unreasonable expect a challenge like this would come easy. Besides, it was all in good fun, not about the score.

Leigh, Noah, and Cous Cous each arrived at the fire tower not long behind us, finishing their DTCs in 7 minutes 40 seconds, 7 minutes 47 seconds, and 9 minutes 22 seconds, respectively. Everyone was beat physically, but feeling rewarded mentally for having completed the challenge and reached 100 miles!

“I’m putting a comment on FarOut, what should I say?” Show asked me. We wanted to see if others behind us would do the Dump Truck Challenge. For the one on the PCT, it’s customary to comment “Dump Truck Challenge” followed by your trail name and time for everyone to see.

“Hmmm,” I thought aloud. “How about… how fast can you blast your ass?”

Looking back months later, I’m proud to report that quite a few hikers have completed the DTC since March! It’s kind of a thing now, and it’s cool to think we started it. Hopefully the tradition continues for seasons to come. So if you’re planning to do the AT in a future year, make sure you blast your ass up Albert and comment your time on FarOut. Let’s keep this going!

Reaching the 100 mile marker called for a group photo. Those of us who were there at the time gathered by the railing of Albert’s scenic overlook. I set up my tripod, coiling its bendy legs around the scaffolding of the fire tower, and trotted back over to snap the photo.

It wasn’t the only picture taken. I have no memory of who actually said it (perhaps Show, that would track), but one of us cheekily suggested we take another, this time mooning the camera. Afterall, we had busted our butts to tag this mile marker… After some coy protests from Cous Cous, some mild peer pressure, and an eventual giving-in, the five of us turned around and exposed our bare asses.

I’ve put a lot of thought into if and how I want to share photos of this nature online. It wouldn’t be such a big deal if this were a one time thing. But the “butts out” photos, as our tramily referred to them, kind of became A Thing. A tradition we carried on throughout the entire trail, that commemorated every 100 miles we hiked. It was a significant part of our tramily culture. And we got quite creative with them as time went on, selecting specific locales and picking our poses. In a way it feels wrong to exclude them entirely, like I’d be omitting a huge plotline in the story. It was one of the things that made our tramily ours.

The problem here is that I do not have explicit consent to post said explicit photos from everyone featured in them. In fact, I’m actually under strict orders from one person NOT to post them online.

So here’s what I’m going to do. Firstly, I have been and I’m going to continue to keep them off of social media (Instagram, YouTube) entirely. The only place they will be posted are here on The Trek. You’ll get glimpses of my thru-hikes elsewhere, but you have to be willing to read the blog if you want the full and complete story. That’s how I’ve always operated with my online trail content. So it will be an intimate part of the story only my readers will get to know. (Y’all are my favorites. Don’t tell the folks on the other platforms that.)

Secondly, I’m going to blur the butts (and in one case, that person entirely). You’ll still be able to tell it’s a “butts out” picture because you’ll see it enough, but it will be pixelated so not to reveal any fine detail. Thirdly, I’m only going to post the photos that include me. There were a few times when the tramily was on slightly different schedules and took separate butts out photos as we individually reached those mile markers. There needs to be the autovulnerability of my own mild nudity occurring with this. Lastly, I will never caption the photo with who is in it. Most of the time our faces aren’t included anyway. This will allow for a tad more anonymity.

Most importantly, I’ve also directly asked everyone who is featured in more than just one or two of these photos for their consent to post the censored versions on the blog. They were all appreciative of being asked first and have consented. So without further preface, below is our first ever butts out tramily photo!

I can’t remember exactly when, but it was sometime between this photo and the following couples days when we were in the town of Franklin, North Carolina that we began calling ourselves “The Dump Truckers.” It referred to both the Dump Truck Challenge and the butts out photo, which really were one and the same reference. I do remember gathering everyone’s phone numbers and adding them to a group chat with that name. We called ourselves this for quite awhile—over half of the trail. It was only after losing a long-term original member that we felt it was best to rebrand the band. That’s all the blatant foreshadowing you’ll get on that for now.

Show and I spent the next couple days and nights surrounded by the most other hikers we’d been around yet. The whole crew from Muskrat Creek Shelter and then some were moving at about the same pace we were. One afternoon, a few miles shy of camp, a whole group of us including myself, Show, Noah, Leigh, Cous Cous, Alex, Braxton, and Bill stopped at a rhododendron-canopied stream to “soak the dogs.”

We pulled off our shoes and socks, switched into camp shoes (or simply bare feet) and stuck our aching, barely broken-in feet into the frigid water. Braxton didn’t even bother to remove his trail runners, instead doing a “full shoe soak” as he called it. It was a fun memory of everyone gathered together, resting and chatting and laughing, all on the same but simultaneously separate journeys.

The night before our second town stop in Franklin, North Carolina the whole crew camped at Rock Gap Shelter, less than 4 miles south of Winding Stair Gap, our exit point for Franklin. All those mentioned in the previous paragraph were there, and then some. Rewind, a mid-50s man named Pantry who we’d briefly met once or twice without proper introduction, and a few others I never formally met all showed up for the night.

As I was collecting water in our CNOC from the piped spring on the side of the shelter, Pantry came over and introduced himself. “I heard you guys are from New Hampshire?” he confirmed, “I am too, actually. Well, unfortunately I live in fucking Mass right now, but I still like to say New Hampshire.” His heavy Boston accent and liberal use of the F-bomb would have pinpointed him definitively to either of those states without him directly name-dropping them.

He was the first other New Englander we’d met out there. Most of the others were from the south, midwest, or various other parts of the country. I also learned from this brief exchange that Pantry, like me, was a repeat offender. He had already thru-hiked the AT in 2019, the year before my first thru. I knew via reading pre-hike Trek blogs that there was going to be at least one other second-timer out there this season (shout out to Nor’easter) but here was another. It surprised me that there were even three of us like-minded hikers that were willing to do this whole thing twice.

Show and I also got to know Alex a bit better by hanging out with him at the Rock Gap shelter. As the dreads, face tat, and watermelon fanny had indicated, he was indeed an interesting character. Since that first interaction when he’d dejectedly told us he’d see us at Katahdin, he’d gotten a trail name: Rufio.

If you’re familiar with the ’90s movie Hook, a spin-off of the Peter Pan story starring Robin Williams and Julia Roberts, you’ll immediately get the reference. At the time, I didn’t get it, because I hadn’t seen the movie. But since returning home from the trail, I have watched the movie on high recommendation from Rufio and as research for the blog. If, like me, you’re not familiar, Rufio’s character is the de facto leader of the Lost Boys in Neverland. He and the gang of forever-kids frequently shout “Bangarang!” as a sort of battle cry to rally the troops or simply an ecstatic expression of wild joy. So that’s who I heard shouting at the state line the other day, I put two and two together in my mind.

The name and meaning behind it were certainly fitting. It was evident that Rufio (Alex, not the character) fully embraces Neverland’s “never grow up” mindset. And NOT in a Peter Pan syndrome way, implying he’s immature and rejects adulting. In a good way: he’s a person who lives big and bold, refusing to lose touch with his childlike playfulness and whimsy no matter how old he gets.

Throughout the time I spent with Rufio he mentioned more than once that he never thought he’d live to be as old as he was. Born a few months before me in the fall of 1990, he was also 35. He revealed on this night at the Rock Gap shelter that he’d had a tumultuous past including times of drug addiction and drug-related incarceration. Thirty-five might not seem like a ripe old age to the average person, but that’s pushing 60 in addict years. Heavy drug users such as many of the famous musicians in the so-called “27 Club” don’t even make it to their thirties. Knowing this, I completely understood why he connects so strongly with the forever young trope in Peter Pan and Hook.

Hearing some of Rufio’s stories, I considered revealing that I’m also an addict—but didn’t, not yet anyway. I’m not entirely sure why it is, but I still feel squirmy outing myself as sober to new people. Even four years into sobriety. I’d imagined that this uncomfortability would have diminished by now, but the reality is that it hasn’t. Maybe I’m simply out of practice. I don’t “work a program” like AA where I’m repeatedly finding myself in a room full of people proclaiming that “my name is Kelly and I’m an alcoholic.” Nor do I have many sober friends or acquaintances in real life (though I interact casually with a few online).

That may be part of it, but the squirminess has deeper roots. In Shame. While I’m extremely proud of all the progress I’ve made in my recovery journey, simultaneously, the shameful beliefs about my past self still linger. Honestly, that decade of drinking feels, in hindsight, like it was not only a lifetime ago, but another person’s life entirely. Like I got transported into another body and mind for awhile. Now I’m back, scratching my head going, what the fuck was THAT? Like waking up from a bizarre nightmare.

If you knew me in my drinking days (my apologies, first off) I was wild, loud and obnoxious, and unpresent. Now, I still identify as being wild, in a different sense. I’m naturally more quiet and reserved, down-to-earth. There’s a glaring disconnect between these two identities that’s hard for me to reconcile. I worry I now present so clean-cut and (mostly) mild-mannered that people, particularly other addicts, won’t believe me when I claim the label. Especially that they’ll hit me with the “you don’t look/seem like an alcoholic…” or “I never would have guessed…” which will trigger the shame spiral of: why the hell did I end up this way? Someone like me shouldn’t have ended up this way.

Personally, I got a lot of my former “spunkiness” from the booze. It doesn’t feel like an organic part of me that was exacerbated by the drink. Whereas, for whatever reason, I perceive that other addicts innately always had that spunk and carry it over into sobriety, shamelessly making others laugh with their war stories. I just feel awkward and pathetic telling mine, so I usually don’t. Here I go again with the imposter syndrome. Jeez, I can’t even positively embrace a “negative” identity. I’m a real addict, too! I swear! Ridiculous, haha.

I’ll close out this tangent by saying that I hope to someday loosen the tight grip of shame and take comfortable ownership of my spunky past in front of others more. I’m sure like a lot of things it will take time and practice, apparently more than I’d bargained for. I greatly envy those that espouse sobriety shame-free. Not the case for this alcoholic.

Why is it named this? Who named it? We may never know.

Why is toileting being encouraged in this one specific area? The fact that this was fairly close to a stream was the most alarming part.

Now that I’ve added these photos, I suppose I have SOME sober spunk. I do have my Silly Goose Certification, a prestigious honor I don’t take lightly.

In addition to finding out Rufio’s recently claimed trail name, another one unfolded in real time around the picnic table that night at dinner. Braxton had pulled out a quart-sized Ziploc of single use sauce and condiment packets, the kind you get a fast food restaurants. The bag was stuffed to the brim with all varieties, from McDonald’s mustard to Taco Bell Fire Sauce to soy sauce from Asian restaurants. Someone commented, “damn dude, that’s a lot of sauce packets!”

“Your trail name should be Sauce Packet,” I suggested.

“I mean,” Braxton added, “I have been saving up all of these for like two years…”

Everyone laughed. The time-effort that had gone into collecting the packets pre-hike solidified it. The name quickly got shortened to just “Sauce,” which I approved of. “Sauce” has a nice clean, bold ring to it. Being present for a trail name origin story is one of my favorite things on a thru-hike. I love watching a core memory play out; the instant it clicks that an initially random moment will be anecdotally significant for this person. A tale they’ll tell over and over again whenever asked about their trail name. It’s exciting to witness someone who doesn’t yet have one click with and accept their trail identity. I love it.

Embracing my toddler snacks identity in the baby food aisle of Vons in Mammoth Lakes, CA on the PCT.

I don’t know if it’s just me and my group that did this, but occasionally we’d give someone a private trail name. As I’ve mentioned in previous blogs, this was the case with the guys I called “First Cathole” and “Snorlax.” We never saw them again beyond the first week, so I have no idea if they ended up getting official trail names or even completed the trail. But my names for them are a way to refer to someone whose name I never knew, but also in memoriam of those first funny interactions with them.

Not long after Sauce accepted his new name, another woman we didn’t know was cutting cheese. In the literal sense. “Does anyone want any?” she offered to the masses, “I packed out way too much.”

“I do!” Cous Cous exclaimed, always accepting of any and all free food.

The woman cut him a slice with her pocket knife and reached out to hand it over. Unfortunately, the transfer got fumbled and the cheese slice dropped to the dirt. “Oh no,” she said hurriedly, “don’t eat that, I’ll cut you another.”

Before anyone knew what was happening, she started pounding the reject cheese into the ground under her trailrunner. Surprisingly it didn’t automatically disappear as a result of this brute force action, it only got flatter and dirtier. Great, I thought, now we’ve got cheese ten feet away from the shelter where we’re sleeping… I made a mental note to circle back and scrape it off the ground and put it in my trash bag before we all went to bed. I felt weird doing so in the moment, like I was presenting “holier-than-thou” or something by doing the right thing and packing it out.

Debriefing on this later once the woman wasn’t in the vicinity, I discovered I wasn’t the only one that disagreed with her cheese disposal method. We all agreed it was not only NOT LNT, but quite strange, and began referring to her from then on as “Cheese Stomper.”

“I would have still eaten it if she hadn’t stomped all over it!” Cous Cous sighed, shaking his head at the thought of five-second-rule-perfectly-good food gone to waste.

We didn’t see CS again for a very long time, but months later eventually ran into her somewhere in Pennsylvania. I honestly didn’t recognize her (still wouldn’t if I saw her on the street) but after seeing her again Leigh reminded me, “you know who that was?? Cheese Stomper!”

As the sky was fading to black, the Lost Boys (Rufio, Show, Noah, Cous Cous, and I) settled down into our sleeping bags in the shelter. The night was mild and still. All of the good vibes from the evening’s hang hung in the air. It relaxed me to be in nature, in good company. Rufio inhaled one last hit off his bowl and exhaled a cloud into the darkness, illuminated lime green in the beam of his headlamp. “Goodnight, Neverland!” he spoke serenely into the night.
