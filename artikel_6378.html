New Discovery Could Transform How We Treat Chronic Back Pain

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Restoring a protein that declines in aging spinal discs reduced DNA damage in human cells in the lab, revealing a potential new target for back pain treatments.

For people living with persistent back pain, everyday activities such as walking, bending, or even sitting comfortably can become difficult. New research from the University of Utah points toward a possible way to address one of the underlying causes of age-related back pain by protecting the cells that keep spinal discs healthy.

Spinal discs are cushion-like structures between vertebrae that allow the spine to move. In a study published in Aging Cell, researchers found that older and damaged discs contained lower levels of a protein called BLST, which is encoded by the ZNF865 gene. The study is the first to characterize the function of both the gene and its protein.

To understand whether declining BLST levels contribute to disc deterioration, the researchers used CRISPR, a gene-editing tool that can be used to regulate the activity of specific genes. Restoring BLST production in human disc cells reduced signs of cellular aging and DNA damage, while decreasing its activity in mouse and rat models led to disc degeneration and pain-related behaviors. The findings suggest that BLST helps maintain healthy spinal discs by protecting cells from damage and dysfunction as they age.

“This paper directly shows a novel understanding that DNA damage is a driver of back pain, and that we can regulate BLST therapeutically with CRISPR,” said Robby D. Bowles, the study’s lead researcher and an associate professor of biomedical engineering. “Back pain is the number three leading cause of disability as measured by disability-adjusted life years, only behind cardiac disease and drug use disorders.”

Although increasing BLST activity could eventually offer a way to prevent or treat age-related back pain, additional preclinical studies and, ultimately, clinical trials are needed to determine whether targeting the protein with CRISPR is safe and effective in people.

The research brought together Bowles and members of his laboratory in the John and Marcia Price College of Engineering, specialists in orthopedics and arthritis at the Spencer Fox Eccles School of Medicine, and researchers at Huntsman Cancer Institute at the University of Utah.

Because DNA damage is involved in numerous diseases, the discovery could have applications well beyond spinal health.

“It has been the highlight of my career to work on a protein that was unknown and be the first to identify its function,” Bowles said. “While this work focuses on its application to back pain, the mechanisms identified have the potential to be applied to treat cancer, neurodegeneration, and any disease in which DNA damage is involved.”

Further research is needed to establish whether the protective mechanisms observed in spinal disc cells also play a role in age-related cancers.

“The fact that a single gene can restore normal cell function and reverse aging-related effects is incredible,” said Jason Gertz, a professor in the Department of Oncological Sciences at the University of Utah and co-leader of the Genomics, Epigenetics, and Metabolism Program at Huntsman Cancer Institute. “BLST is widely expressed across tissues and could be a promising target for cancer treatment and prevention, since boosting BLST activity should improve the repair of DNA damage and the overall health of aging cells.”

Reference: “ZNF865 (BLST) a Novel Regulator of DNA Damage and Cell Senescence in Back Pain” by Christian Lewis, Hunter Levis, Jonah Holbrook, Jacob T. Polaski, Timothy D. Jacobsen, Josh Mizels, Molly Czachor, Sarah E. Gullbrand, Brian Diekman, James C. Iatridis, Jason Gertz, Brandon Lawrence and Robby D. Bowles, 9 October 2026, Aging Cell. DOI: 10.1111/acel.70754
