A generation feeling powerless: College students are overwhelmed by climate anxiety

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

New research reveals widespread climate anxiety among college students, with many feeling powerless and universities facing calls to provide more support.

Only four percent of college students in a recent survey reported feeling hopeful about the climate crisis frequently or “a lot.”

Many others reported a bleak mix of powerlessness, frustration, and anxiety.

Yet universities may not be doing enough to help students manage these difficult emotions.

That’s the uncomfortable takeaway from a pair of interdisciplinary studies out of the University of Utah.

Researchers surveyed hundreds of undergraduates about how climate change makes them feel and what, if anything, helps them cope.

The research team, led by environmental scientist Jennifer Follstad Shah and psychologist Monika Lohani, surveyed 547 students enrolled across three environmental studies courses and one communication course.

They asked a simple question: when you think about climate change, what do you feel?

Fifty-four percent of students reported feeling powerless frequently or “a lot.” Fifty-five percent said the same about frustration.

Anger, worry, fear, and anxiety clustered right behind, each hitting the “a lot” category for roughly one in five students. Meanwhile, 58 percent said they never felt happiness about the climate situation.

Only four percent reported feeling hopeful frequently or “a lot.” Even when students who experienced hope less often were included, the figure reached just 40 percent.

Here’s where the study gets interesting. Environmental studies majors did score higher on the researchers’ Climate Anxiety Index.

But once the statistical models accounted for other variables, a student’s major stopped being a significant predictor of climate anxiety altogether.

The final hierarchical regression model, which explained 52 percent of the variance in climate anxiety scores, pointed to different culprits.

These included political ideology, how often students encountered climate information, whether they ranked climate change as a top personal concern, and their levels of general anxiety.

“They’re not alone,” Shah said. “There’s a lot of students feeling that way, and likely a lot of adults feeling that way.”

The relationship between climate anxiety and generalized anxiety turned out to be striking.

Climate anxiety and general anxiety were closely related, although the researchers could not determine whether one contributed to the other.

Students with moderate-to-high climate anxiety tended to report low-to-moderate general anxiety.

These findings suggest that climate and general anxiety are closely related but remain distinct psychological experiences.

“It may be that climate anxiety is a contributor to general anxiety, as well as the converse,” Shah said.

Gender told a similar nuanced story. Women in the study reported significantly higher general anxiety than men, and initially appeared more climate-anxious too.

But once general anxiety was accounted for, gender dropped out as a predictor entirely.

The findings suggest that differences in general anxiety, rather than gender itself, may help explain the higher climate anxiety initially reported by women.

Not all information sources hit the same way. Classroom learning and online media – especially social media – were both significant predictors of higher climate anxiety.

However, information from friends, family, and co-workers barely registered.

Fewer than 30 percent of students reported getting climate information from family or colleagues with any regularity.

The findings suggest that climate change may come up less often in everyday conversations than in classrooms or online.

One interaction caught the researchers’ attention. For students outside environmental studies, more online climate exposure correlated significantly with higher anxiety.

For environmental studies majors, that link vanished.

Some of these students described engaging in “hopescrolling” – deliberately curating their feeds toward solutions-focused content rather than doom.

Lohani’s companion study dug into what students actually do with all this distress. Some found relief in nature, pets, self-care, or learning more about the science itself.

“The knowledge is empowering them to understand the intricacies. That makes them not feel hopeless,” Lohani said.

“All the progress in terms of technology that has come out to combat the carbon footprint actually made them feel more hopeful.”

But many students described shutting down entirely. One wrote: “Sometimes I just don’t feel like doing anything at all because it makes me so anxious.”

“Those who are overwhelmed, they might be catastrophizing or using avoidance or distraction, which ultimately is not good for their well-being, or efforts to address the climate change crisis,” Lohani noted.

“Those who are apathetic towards it, that is not bad for their well-being in the short run, but ultimately it is affecting them and their community.”

Both researchers argued that campuses need to stop treating climate anxiety as background noise.

Their recommendations are concrete: establish climate anxiety support groups within counseling centers, fund cross-departmental forums for open discussion, and train instructors to expect emotional responses to climate material.

The University of Utah’s own environmental studies program has already begun hosting Good Grief Circles, modeled on the Good Grief Network co-founded by Laura Schmidt, a 2014 Utah graduate.

These peer support sessions give students space to process ecological distress without judgment.

“Don’t be afraid to talk about it and let people know that you feel this way,” Shah said. “There’s power in those bonds.”

The study has its limits – the sample skewed female (66 percent), White (69 percent), and politically left-leaning (63 percent).

It was drawn from a single university in the arid Mountain West, where shrinking Great Salt Lake levels and megadrought make climate impacts impossible to ignore.

The researchers are calling for broader studies across more diverse campuses and majors.

Despite these limitations, the findings suggest that climate anxiety extends beyond students majoring in environmental subjects.

It is closely linked to general anxiety and affects students across academic disciplines, raising questions about whether universities provide enough emotional support.

The students surveyed said, overwhelmingly, that they want more opportunities to talk about it. The question is whether anyone’s listening.

The study is published in the journal Sustainability.
