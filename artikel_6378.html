Even remote wildlife can’t escape microplastics in their blood

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Scientists found microplastics in the blood of wild tapirs and giant anteaters in Brazil, including animals living far from human development.

As the afternoon sun began to fade over Brazil’s countryside, a team of researchers drove along dusty dirt roads searching for giant anteaters.

Two spotters rode in the back of the truck, scanning the landscape for signs of movement. When they spotted an anteater, the team approached on foot with two long-handled nets.

Capturing the animal usually took about a minute, but the information gathered would help answer a much bigger question: How far has plastic pollution spread into the natural world?

The researchers were investigating whether wild animals living closer to human activity had more microplastics in their blood.

Their findings revealed a clear connection between human disturbance and plastic exposure.

But one discovery was particularly concerning.

Even in Brazil’s remote Pantanal, the world’s largest tropical wetland, every animal sampled had microplastics in its blood, despite living more than 40 miles (65 kilometers) from the nearest development.

The results suggest that plastic pollution is reaching wildlife even in places that appear largely untouched by humans.

Scientists had widely assumed a connection between human activity and plastic in wildlife.

To test it, the researchers needed several records for the same animals, namely blood samples, GPS movements and detailed maps of human disturbance.

Study co-author Justin Calabrese of the Center for Advanced Systems Understanding (CASUS) described the difficulty of getting all those records together.

“Such integrated datasets simply did not exist,” Calabrese said.

Marcia de Almeida Monteiro Melo Ferraz and Michael Noonan, both at the University of British Columbia Okanagan, conceived the study.

Teams from two long-running conservation programs in Brazil did the fieldwork.

From February 2021 to November 2022, those teams collected blood from 26 lowland tapirs and 21 giant anteaters.

The animals lived at a soy farm on the Amazon’s deforestation frontier, farmland in the Cerrado savanna and a cattle ranch in the Pantanal.

For the anteaters, the work took place near two federal highways, where the Anteaters and Highways Project studies collisions between vehicles and wildlife.

Each blood sample went into a glass tube, which the field team sealed with aluminum foil to prevent contamination.

To record where the animals went, adult tapirs wore GPS collars that logged an hourly position. The anteaters wore GPS trackers as well.

Once in the lab, each sample went through a chemical treatment to break down the blood. The team collected the solids left behind on a filter.

Raman spectroscopy then identified the materials from the way they scattered light from a laser.

When the team had checked all 47 blood samples, 43 contained microplastics. The particles averaged about 0.0004 inches (10 micrometers) in length.

Pieces of that size need a microscope to be seen.

Counts for individual animals ran from zero to more than 1,200 particles in a milliliter of blood. The average for all the animals was about 74, and a milliliter is roughly a fifth of a teaspoon.

For comparison, the average fell within the range already reported in human blood by other studies using spectroscopy.

The tapirs had about 99 particles per milliliter on average and the anteaters about 43. The difference between the species was not statistically significant.

GPS records provided more than 440,000 locations, allowing researchers to map the home ranges of 40 animals.

To describe the land within those areas the researchers used the Human Footprint Index.

This global map has scores running from zero for undisturbed land up to 50 for the most heavily modified.

An animal did not need to live across uniformly disturbed land for a link to appear.

The team found more particles in blood when the most heavily modified spot in an animal’s range had a higher footprint score.

Average footprint scores across entire home ranges were not significantly associated with microplastic levels.

The distance comparisons also pointed to less plastic in animals living farther from development, which here meant urban areas, mines and power plants.

The same was true of animals living farther from waste management facilities.

Calabrese said the paper “is the first to demonstrate this linkage for terrestrial mammals,” meaning mammals that live on land.

The researchers also checked how much the highest values influenced the findings. They ran each test again without the animals making up the top 10% for that measure.

For the distance tests, that meant leaving out the animals living farthest away.

After that check the footprint link remained, while neither distance link was still statistically significant.

Animals with more farmland in their home ranges did not have higher microplastic levels in their blood.

The authors suggested that farmland may add less direct exposure to microplastics than other kinds of human disturbance.

The types of plastic offered another comparison between sites, with differences the authors described as consistent with local land use.

Animals at the Amazon farm carried more polyamide, the main material in fishing nets and ropes, than animals on Cerrado farmland.

The Cerrado animals had more PVC, a material used in irrigation pipes, than animals in the Pantanal.

The authors described the site comparisons as case studies, with uneven numbers of animals at each site.

For the animals at the cattle ranch in the Pantanal, the distance from the nearest waste facility was about 70 miles (112 kilometers). Each of them still carried microplastics in its blood.

The researchers also obtained a value of about 10 particles per milliliter of blood from their model for land with a footprint score of zero.

The authors suggested that the value may partly reflect limitations of the model.

Even so, they wrote that direct contact with land changed by people was unlikely to be the only route into wildlife.

The authors identified wind and water as likely routes carrying microplastics into remote habitats. Cities, industry and farms supply airborne microplastics that may settle far away.

Runoff, rivers and seasonal floods provide ways for particles to move far from their sources as well.

Microplastics in the water and sediment of the Pantanal were already known from previous studies.

The highest measurements in those studies came from places near rivers flowing out of urban areas.

This observational study found associations between human disturbance and microplastic levels in blood, but it cannot establish cause and effect.

The researchers had a single blood sample from each animal. Their method could not identify particles smaller than about 0.00004 inches (one micrometer).

Future studies could collect blood, soil and water samples from the same locations to help trace the particles’ sources.

The team did not collect those environmental samples and could not yet match the plastic in blood to its source.

The measurements also concern the amount of plastic, without examining its effects on the health of these wild animals. Laboratory experiments have shown that microplastics can harm animals.

For the years 2001 to 2020, the increase in Brazil’s average footprint score was 42%, whereas the worldwide increase was 26%.

Thus, as land development continues, the authors suggested that the plastic levels in land animals would continue to increase.

They described keeping wildlife farther from development and waste facilities as a possible way to lower the amount the animals carry.

The study is published in the journal Nature.
