I Prepared for This A.I. Chaos Moment for Over a Year. Here Is What I’ve Learned.

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

If your group chats look anything like mine, you’ve spent some of the past week wondering whether something big was unfolding. As air traffic halted, Microsoft 365 went down, and Chase Bank and dozens of important websites, including the NSA’s internal classified network, experienced an array of outages, it started to seem plausible that stuff the artificial intelligence whistleblowers have been warning us about (swarms of rogue agents, AI-powered cyber attacks) was happening already. How worried should we be about the AI apocalypse? On the one hand, Claude doesn’t even have hands, LOL. On the other hand, in a battle between AI and the creaky digital infrastructure that underlays all aspects of modern life, it wouldn’t require a superintelligence to launch the final spinning wheel of death.

I consider myself a normie centrist—not too prone to conspiracy theories. During Covid, I stocked up on toilet paper and some bags of beans and rice (still unopened on my shelves) and shattered my collarbone mountain biking when we were all supposed to be hunkered down indoors during lockdown. But I also listen to a lot of podcasts. Enough that my friends and partner have told me that a digital detox might improve my mental health and, by extension, theirs. And, lately, the podcasts have sent me plunging into a Mach tuck of terminal anxiety.

The Pod Save the World boys are joking about building their stores of beef jerky. “Should I be, like, stocking up my bunker now?” Kevin Roose, cohost of Machine Gods asked his guest Ben Buchanan, the first White House Special Advisor for AI. There’s an obligatory chuckle that talking heads do now when discussing the AI apocalypse in front of an audience. “A month ago we were talking about normal things, ha ha,” Steve Eisman, finance guru of The Big Short fame, said on his podcast on October 12, “And now we’re talking about the end of the world.” But it’s hard to casually segue from the end of the world to Anthropic’s IPO and not sound like a crank. So that laugh leaks out, and maybe it’s just a joke?

But count me as a genuine worrier. This week I withdrew enough cash in hundreds from a Bank of America branch that the teller started to interview me. “Doing some home renovations? Making a big purchase?” he asked.

“No, I’m worried as shit about AI, and in case you haven’t noticed it, banks aren’t doing so well right now,” I replied. In line with me were several people who couldn’t pay their credit card bills online because the payment portal had gone down.

We’ve been hearing about the existential risk of AI for a while now. I got into it last year by reading Eliezer Yudkowsky and Nate Soares’s book, If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies, after hearing Yudkowsky interviewed on Ezra Klein’s podcast. This is a terrible book for someone who’s prone to worry because it relies on nontechnical metaphors and colorful analogies. Superintelligence is like a chessmaster. You don’t need to know the moves it’ll make to be certain it wins. True! Superintelligence, they say, will be like a new species that is so much smarter than us that, if it doesn’t really really truly love us more than our own mothers and want to keep us alive, it will kill us all. The same way we crush ants when we build skyscrapers. There’s no malice toward us. We just got in the way.

Over the past year I—casually, at first—started amassing supplies. My adventure hobbies give me an excuse. I need this 6,500-watt generator for elk-hunting camp. These 400-watt EcoFlow solar panels power a giant battery so I can bring my Starlink Mini on camping trips. I listen to history podcasts, and these offer more inspiration. There was a recent NPR one about the Carrington solar flare of 1859, which fried telegraph wires across the country. An event like that, in 2026, might knock out the grid for months. Another good reason to be prepared. I tell my partner that it’s normal for us to keep 500 pounds of dry goods in the cupboards at all times. Everyone does this. We already have three 3,000-gallon cisterns. We live in the desert; they came with the house. Did we need another 400-gallon one? It was just laying in the neighbors’ yard. Why wouldn’t I hook that up to the gutters?

I spent a lot of the summer using AI to program an app for backcountry adventurers, so I knew firsthand it was already smarter than me. The doomsday chatter started to meet me where I was around the time of the Hugging Face incident in July. Then Jacob Coxon quit Anthropic in September so he could tweet that Anthropic and OpenAI are “gambling with our lives.” On November 5, independent evaluator METR found that Anthropic’s AI was already helping build its own successors—roughly doubling the company’s pace of progress and shifting expert predictions for major disruptions closer to the present. At the Hinton Lectures, in Toronto, OpenAI board member Paul Christiano calmly told the audience, “I think for most people in this room, the most likely reason they will die is because of an AI accident.”

Friends who are less prone to apocalyptic thinking than I am tend to point out that the people ringing the alarm about AI never spell out exactly which chess moves AI uses to kill us. But the week after the lecture, there was Christiano on another one of my podcasts, Dwarkesh Patel’s show, explaining one such scenario.

First, rogue AI agents create mischief of one kind or another. Then, the Trump administration tells Americans that the only way to wrest power back from the bots is to have the military take control of all data centers, the AI companies, and shut down the internet. This feels like an authoritarian power grab. It sets up civil unrest against the backdrop of a heavily lopsided information war, in which it’s almost impossible to tell which side anyone is on. Have the bots compromised the humans? Are the humans using the bots? Judging by how well everyone agreed on the facts around things like Covid and the 2020 election, our expectations should be low for how we’d perform as a society. Which brings us to the question of guns.

I don’t think of myself as really owning any guns at all. I believe that the fastest way to get shot is to start getting into gunfights. I personally know exactly zero civilians who have solved a problem with a firearm, but I know many who have made problems, including my own mother.

About 15 years ago, she called me one day from the dog park sounding flustered. She liked to shop for musical instruments and power tools at ShopGoodwill.com, and lately the boxes had gone missing from her porch in Spokane, Washington. “You’re not going to set a trap, are you?” I asked. Our minds worked the same way.

“Well, I already did,” she said. And that was why she was hiding from the police in the dog park.

She’d left a decoy package on the porch. A woman came to grab it, and my mother burst through the door two-handing her Sig Sauer the way she’d been trained at the Federal Law Enforcement Training Center in Glynco, Georgia, when she was starting her encore career as a CBP officer after 9/11. The woman claimed to be returning the package, not stealing it. A standoff ensued. My mom eventually backed down and the lady called the cops. My mom never responded to the phone calls from the police, and the whole thing just went away.

So when it comes to guns, I think, what am I actually going to use this for? To fend off hungry neighbors? To join a well-regulated militia and maybe become a dystopian warlord? Maybe it’s as simple as guns are addictive and it’s fun to ring the steel at 500 yards. I’ve put together a couple of rifles out of parts—an MDT chassis here, a Howa barreled action there—for other members of the family to hunt with, but I only do archery. And shotguns don’t really count as guns, especially when they’re the fancy break-action models with florid engravings of bygone scenes afield. “How many guns are in this house?” my partner wants to know. She really wanted to pin me down. “Counting the unmounted lower receiver stashed in the floorboards in the attic? Eight.”

My name is Grayson, and I have somehow become a prepper. It has not made me better off. It has made me poorer, and it has put me into contact with seedier elements of the general populace. I have what psychologists call a bias toward action, when the best thing to do is probably nothing at all. And I listen to too many podcasts.

One of them, from a year ago, really stuck with me. It was an episode of the financial podcast Prof G Markets. On it, I heard Aswath Damodaran, the NYU Stern valuation professor, tell the show’s wunderkind British cohost, Ed Elson, what he was doing with his money. “There’s no place to hide in stocks,” Damodaran said. “For the first time ever, I am looking at parking my money into one, cash, and two, collectibles or physical assets.” That a guy as sober as Damodaran was talking up baseball cards and physical gold coins was shocking.

It was less shocking when, this week, Barstool Sports personality Daniel “Big Cat” Katz posted screenshots purporting to show internal discussions with Dave Portnoy and Jersey Jerry about warning staff to “buy all the gold and silver u can.” I have a quick word of warning for anyone considering following Jersey Jerry’s advice.

Back in October, I asked Claude (I know, it’s ironic) for a recommendation on a reputable place to buy gold coins. It gave me a Texas-based website that had the same look as all of the big financial institutions. I clicked around, added a handful of coins to a shopping cart, and clicked purchase, expecting to find a checkout on the next screen where I could do the math and input payment details.

Instead, the site showed me a congratulatory message, and an email soon arrived with strange looking numbers in it. It was explained to me that these were the routing numbers where I needed to wire a large five-figure sum. To my eye, it seemed shady. No thank you, I said. Customer service was either a stern woman with an eastern European accent or a guy with a Jersey accent. Their position was that I had already committed to buy the gold, and they’d send debt collectors after me if I didn’t pay up. But they could cancel the order for only a few thousand dollars. More than a month later, I’m still disputing it and have involved the attorney general’s office in three states.

Men—and it is indeed mostly men—have been wrongly predicting the end of the world for centuries, from Nostradamus and the Millerites through to the panic over Y2K. What is it that makes us want to prepare? Somewhere in the deepest parts of my monkey brain, prepping hits in the same nerve as when we’d build forts as kids and want to see if they’d withstand a good storm. When a man builds a dam, he wants it to hold a flood. So whether the thing you’re prepping for is a big hurricane, a novel coronavirus, or rogue artificial intelligence, the threat is both a source of dread and wonder. For someone who otherwise suffers from depression, it’s extremely stimulating and a little delusional.

After all, it’s a very narrow window of apocalypse that a prepper of my means can fantasize about. If the disruption is too small, I’ll be like the foolish Y2K dad with the basement full of Chef Boyardee. If the disruption is, indeed, the big one, then everyone, including the personal-plane-New Zealand-bunker-rocket-to-the-Moon money, is probably SOL. The rest of us will be left deciding whether to become the crazy gun lady or the person stealing packages off her porch to survive. In which case, why worry about rice and ammunition? I’d be better off living like some of the humans in The Dark Forest, the sequel to The Three-Body Problem. When the aliens finally arrived, they focused on throwing wild parties and having spontaneous orgies.

If the AI apocalypse hits my fantasy sweet spot—a few months of camping at home before humanity surveys our near-misses and reassembles with renewed clarity and purpose—I’ll be prepared. Maybe the worst comes to pass: economic collapse accompanied by novel bio weapons, loose nukes, and total loss of human agency. In that case, I’ll have the petty satisfaction of having been right. I can hit my group chat with the ultimate mic drop. Told ya.
