A source at Aeroporti di Roma (ADR), which manages the city’s two airports, confirmed Italian press reports that the hoax threats for the Helsinki-Rome and Dublin-Rome flights came from an anonymous email written in Italian with the subject heading “segnalazione minaccia” (“threat warning”).

The email was signed on behalf of “Fighters of the Male State” claiming to be targeting a “feminist regime” and wanting to make its “mark on history”.

The email, which was sent to ADR and affiliated businesses, also threatened to “turn the skies red” with explosions.

“The competent authorities are conducting the necessary investigations to establish the origin and circumstances of the threats, in accordance with established security protocols.

ADR Security played a key supporting role in managing both alerts, providing dedicated personnel to escort all vehicles operating beneath the aircraft, assist with baggage unloading operations, and carry out checks on both baggage and passengers.”

While specific details regarding the three flights targeted in Madrid have not been disclosed, relevant security authorities have confirmed there is no active threat.

The airport’s press office confirmed to the Guardian only that:

“This morning, Adolfo Suárez Madrid-Barajas Airport received a bomb threat affecting three flights.

A security committee was convened under the coordination of the State Security Forces. Following an assessment and the relevant checks carried out by the security forces, the threats were ruled out.”

Aviation experts say fake threats occur daily but the multiplicity of threats adjudged to be more credible, even if malicious hoaxes, appears to point to coordinated action.

Former airline pilot Captain Martin Drake, a technical advisor to the Balpa union, said airlines have security teams who assess each threat on a traffic light scale: green to keep going or red to be grounded, with an amber option only for flights yet to take off, allowing tighter checks and searches of the plane before clearing it to fly.

“If you want to deliberately disrupt a flight, then you need to do so once it’s airborne,” said Drake. A pilot will have a QRH – a quick response handbook – whose instructions will likely include to descend quickly as well as divert to the nearest airport on receipt of a red alert. At cruising altitude, “aircraft pressurisation would do a lot of the work of any improvised explosive device.”

Diversions are expensive as well as highly disruptive, but airlines have little choice in the face of any remotely credible threat.

The ready availability of airline operations information on the internet means that any hoaxer now has a level of detail about the schedules, the type of plane and its technical specifications that would have eluded all but insiders decades ago.

Rhys Jones, an analyst at Osprey Flight Solutions, an aviation intelligence firm, said the confidence of airlines in European airport security should not mean any change to normal protocols after the hoaxes, but “one of the natural responses will be to see double-downed security, media coverage and public concern about travelling”.

He said the threats seemed linked but “a reasonably chaotic campaign with no particular region targeted”, and while it could weaken future security responses through the normalisation of threats, simply creating disruption could be regarded as a success by those hoaxing airlines.

EU transport commissioner Apostolos Tzitzikostas has said that it is “too early to draw conclusions on whether the reported incidents are linked,” as he stressed that “any threats against civilians are unacceptable.”

“Ensuring the safety and security of citizens remains a priority,” he said.

“We are closely monitoring the situation and are in contact with the affected Member States and the European Aviation Safety Agency.

I have asked the national authorities, that have the responsibility for assessing security threats, investigating incidents and implementing appropriate aviation security measures, to cooperate closely with airport operators, airlines and law enforcement authorities.”

All three planes that landed at Bulgarian airports following bomb threats have been cleared by the authorities with no threat confirmed, the transport ministry has said.

In a statement to the Guardian, the ministry said:

“Following a bomb threat, established security procedures were activated in relation to three Ryanair aircraft operating scheduled flights to Sofia from Madrid, Copenhagen and London.

The flight crews and the airline decided that the flights from Madrid and Copenhagen would continue to Sofia Airport, while the flight from London was diverted to Plovdiv Airport.

Upon landing, the necessary inspections were carried out in accordance with the applicable procedures and no issues were identified.

The airline is arranging transportation for passengers from Plovdiv to Sofia.

Airport operations at both Sofia Airport and Plovdiv Airport have returned to normal.”
