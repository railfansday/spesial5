Early Sepsis Interventions Didn't Reduce Risk of Death

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

— The PITSTOP trials found no advantage to giving antibiotics or liberal IV fluids in the ambulance

For patients with suspected sepsis, early use of antibiotics or more liberal intravenous fluids by paramedics didn't significantly lower risk of death, two randomized trials showed.

Among adults transported to an emergency department with suspected sepsis, the hospital mortality rate was 18% for those who received ceftriaxone in the ambulance versus 18.4% for those who received placebo (risk ratio [RR] 0.97, 95% CI 0.81-1.17, P=0.77), reported Damon Scales, MD, PhD, of Sunnybrook Health Sciences Centre in Toronto, and colleagues in JAMA.

In the second trial, also published in JAMA, those randomized to a liberal IV fluid resuscitation strategy had a hospital mortality rate of 18% compared with 18.3% for those randomized to usual IV fluid care (RR 0.98, 95% CI 0.80-1.20, P=0.82), Scales and colleagues said.

"It is possible that early administration of antibiotics in previous nonrandomized studies served as a surrogate for other aspects of care that contributed to improved outcomes," the authors noted. "It is also possible that there is no benefit to providing antibiotics 3 hours earlier than usual care."

As for the fluids results, Scales and team wrote that "the absence of a mortality effect may ultimately reflect the limited influence of a fluid management strategy alone on outcomes that are determined by multiple interacting processes, including infection severity, antibiotic treatment, source control, and timely initiation of vasopressors and organ support."

Sepsis is characterized by life-threatening organ dysfunction from extreme response to infection. Early administration of antibiotics and IV fluids to counteract hypoperfusion has been linked with reduced mortality, but treatment delays are common.

Guidelines from the Surviving Sepsis Campaign conditionally recommend starting antibiotics in the ambulance in some adults, but that recommendation rests on very low certainty of evidence and no randomized trial results. Optimal initial fluid volume also hasn't been established in randomized trials.

The PITSTOP-Antibiotics and PITSTOP-Fluids trials were conducted in four Canadian paramedic services between March 2020 and August 2025. Patients were adults with suspected sepsis, defined as suspected infection, fever, and hypotension.

In the antibiotics trial, 2,048 patients were randomized to either 1 g of intramuscular ceftriaxone or a placebo of intramuscular 0.9% saline. The fluids trial included 1,646 patients randomized to receive either a liberal fluids strategy of up to 2 L of 0.9% intravenous saline regardless of subsequent systolic blood pressure (SBP) or usual care with IV saline only when SBP was less than 90 mm Hg, which was stopped when SBP reached 100 mm Hg.

The primary endpoint for both trials was hospital mortality, which was defined as death at any time from before hospital discharge to day 90.

Median patient ages in the antibiotics and fluid trials were 75 and 76 years, respectively, and 56.7% and 57% were men. The most common suspected infections were pneumonia (21.4% to 23.6% across the trials' four groups) and urinary tract infection (21.9% to 23.9%).

Paramedics' sepsis suspicions were closely matched by those of emergency department clinicians, who also suspected sepsis in 96.2% to 97.8% of patients. In the antibiotics trial, 92.5% of ceftriaxone patients and 92.2% of placebo patients received an IV antibiotic in the hospital. That didn't happen until more than 3 hours after paramedics had reached a patient, however, with a mean time to hospital antibiotics of 201.1 minutes in the ceftriaxone group and 207.0 minutes in the placebo group.

In the fluids trial, patients in the liberal fluids group did receive more fluid than those in the usual-care group -- a median 1,500 mL compared with 500 mL, respectively. Practical reasons kept paramedics from fully delivering a guideline-recommended 30 mL/kg of IV fluids to all liberal fluids patients, Scales cautioned. "Even more aggressive fluid resuscitation in the liberal fluids group may have resulted in greater impact," he noted.

Fewer liberal fluids patients than usual care patients were still hypotensive before their care transfer (44.9% vs 55.9%). Acute kidney injury rates were lower with liberal fluids than usual care (31.4% vs 39.1%; RR 0.80, 95% CI 0.70-0.92). Adverse events were uncommon in both trials' groups.

Study limitations included the trials' sizes, which were likely underpowered to detect small but clinically important differences in mortality.
