Where the Hell Is Harry? And More of This Year’s Biggest Grammys Snubs and Surprises

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

Looking over the nominees list for the 2027 Grammy Awards as they were announced this morning, some major names might jump out as missing. And while some of those omissions have perfectly reasonable explanations — BTS chose not to participate in this year’s awards, in a move that was widely read as a response to the introduction of the new Best Asian Pop Music Performance category, and Drake’s Iceman literally included a bar about his decision not to submit that album  — others have us scratching our proverbial heads. For instance: Did they seriously blank Harry Styles? For real? Here’s a list of the most outrageous snubs in the 2027 Grammy nominations, plus the most pleasant surprises we’re seeing.

Kiss All the Time. Disco, Occasionally, the fourth studio album from Harry Styles, is the kind of record not everyone gets on first listen — it’s slightly disorienting compared to the star’s past releases, but it settles in an intriguing and rewarding way over time. Seems that the Recording Academy never quite came around to it. Styles earned zero nominations for the 2027 Grammy Awards. It’s hard to believe KISSCO stepped too far outside of the bounds in which he created Harry’s House, which took home Album of the Year in 2023. Both albums are the product of an artist searching to create a tangible reflection of how they listen to and experience music in their own life. Styles reconnected with long-time collaborators Kid Harpoon and Tyler Johnson for this one. While it didn’t yield hits as big as “As It Was” or “Watermelon Sugar,” its tracklist is full of ambitious swings, like the Jersey-club-influenced lead single “Aperture” and the career standout “Season 2 Weight Loss.” Fans might say that deep cuts “Are You Listening Yet?” and “Pop” would have made better single choices than “American Girls” and “Taste Back,” which are solid but feel safer in comparison. Still, with its sharp edges of dance-rock and existentialist pop, KISSCO stands out among this year’s slate of releases. It’s perplexing to see it shut out completely. —Larisha Paul

Kahan followed up his massive breakout hit Stick Season with The Great Divide, a sprawling folk-rock odyssey that grapples with love, fame, strained relationships, and — yes — his home state of Vermont. He released the 17-track album in April, and surprised fans by dropping a deluxe edition, The Great Divide: The Last of the Bugs, hours after. With songs like the heartland anthem “American Cars” and the tender gem “Orbiter,” the record solidified his stance as one of today’s most loveble and relatable stars. Kahan took all the necessary steps here: The record debuted at Number One on the Billboard 200, he performed on SNL, appeared on the cover of Rolling Stone, and has been delivering the songs to his devoted fanbase in stadiums all over the world. He reportedly submitted in several categories that included Album of the Year and Record of the Year (for the title track), yet he only pulled off a nod for Best Contemporary Folk Album. It’s disappointing, to say the least, and it only makes those “Orbiter” lyrics hit harder: “California’s so much more than some awards show.” —Angie Martoccio

Anyone who’s seen Olivia Rodrigo live knows she’s a rock star, and she counts rock legends from Weezer to Elvis Costello among her fans. But it’s still a pleasant surprise to see the Recording Academy recognize that aspect of Rodrigo’s artistry, nominating her alongside the Rolling Stones, Phoebe Bridgers, and Geese (featuring her onetime dinner companion Cameron Winter) in the Best Rock Performance category. It doesn’t hurt, of course, that the nominated track, “What’s Wrong With Me,” is a collaboration with the Cure’s Robert Smith. —Brian Hiatt

It’s been a record-breaking year for women in country music, from Ella Langley’s “Choosin’ Texas” reign to female artists populating the top five slots on Billboard’s Hot Country song chart for weeks in a row. Now they dominate the entire Best Contemporary Country Album category: Kelsea Ballerini’s Mount Pleasant, Carter Faith’s Cherry Valley, Megan Moroney’s Cloud 9, Kacey Musgraves’ Middle of Nowhere and Langley’s Dandelion are the nominees. It’s the first time in history that solo female artists have nabbed every slot in this category (formerly known as Best Country Album) — and it’s a rebuke to country radio, which still prioritizes male voices despite all of this monumental success. The most surprised this morning, probably? The program directors still trying to rationalize a playlist of all bros. —Marissa R. Moss

Gearing up for the nominations, there were rumblings that this year’s Best New Artist category might be a family affair, if siblings Audrey Hobert and Malcolm Todd both made it in. Todd submitted his R&B stunner Do That Again in several categories — including Album of the Year — but was completely shut out. And while his song “Chest Pain (I Love)” was sampled on Don Toliver’s “E85,” which earned two noms for Best Rap Song and Best Melodic Rap Performance, Todd himself is not considered a nominee. Meanwhile, his sister Hobert released her smash pop debut Who’s the Clown on Aug. 15, 2025 — days before this year’s eligibility window began — but she did plenty enough in the months that followed to qualify for a Best New Artist nomination. She earned it, with her sleeper hit “Sue Me” entering the Billboard Hot 100 over a year after its release, an opening slot for Charli XCX next year, and a feverishly devoted fanbase that was eager to see her take home the award. And as Hobert recently told us, she’s loved watching award shows her entire life, and she had a blast at the 2024 Grammys, when her pal Gracie Abrams brought her to the ceremony. “I cried the entire night,” she said. “They kept bringing me Champagne. I couldn’t see straight. I was telling people crazy things. It was the best night of my life.” We’re hoping the Grammys give both of these talented sibs a better night next time around. —A.M.

It was hard to go anywhere online this year without hearing the opening refrain from “OhYeah,” the title single from Steve Lacy’s excellent July album, which soundtracked some of the more epic moments in Spider-Man: Brand New Day. Beyond just the single, tracks like “Nothing” and the Erykah Badu-assisted “Pure Colour,” made for one of the year’s most innovative records, easily deserving of a Grammy nod in one of the pop categories — or, at the very least, somewhere in the Recording Academy’s ever-expanding field. (Not even liner notes?) For 2022’s Gemini Rights, he was awarded the Grammy for Best Progressive R&B Album. Surely, this year, his new work deserved some kind of recognition. —Jeff Ihaza

How many legends do you have to make great music with in a single year to get a Producer of the Year nomination? Andrew Watt produced songs and albums by Paul McCartney, the Rolling Stones, Madonna, Michael Stipe, and Brandi Carlile, but that somehow wasn’t enough for the Recording Academy. —B.H.

Four of the five nominees for Best Rock Album are fairly predictable choices: Your Favorite Toy by Foo Fighters, The Wow! Factor by Muse, Foreign Tongues by the Rolling Stones, and Frozen Charlotte by Jack White. But for the fifth slot, the Grammys made a rather surprising left turn by selecting oddball Canadian art-rock duo Angine de Poitrine’s Vol II., as opposed to safer picks like something by the Black Crowes or the Strokes. Angine de Poitrine were also nominated for Best New Artist, Best Rock Performance, and Best Alternative Music Performance. Let’s hope it’s enough for them to get Khn and Klek, as they’re known, some performance time on the show. (For the uninitiated, Angine de Poitrine play wildly high-energy shows while wearing papier-mâché masks and polka-dot costumes.) It would be a great opportunity for this group to gain a broader following, and confuse the living shit out of millions of people. —Andy Greene

The three albums that Drake released this year are nowhere to be seen in the Grammy nominations, which is how he wants it — but he is up for Best Latin Song. Yes, you read that right. The rap superstar has received a nod for his Spanish-language feature on Karol G’s “Ahí.” It’s a little ironic, considering Drake’s longstanding differences with the Academy; the last time he won a Grammy for one of his own songs, in 2019, he gave a speech about how little the awards mean to him, and he’s chosen not to submit his own work since then. But that hasn’t stopped Drake’s collaborations from receiving nods over the years, and this time he’ll be up against acts like Ca7riel & Paco Amoroso, Rosalía, and Jorge Drexler. —Maya Georgi

Hey, hey, they’re the Monkees — and their best album just got a Grammy nomination, their first since the Sixties. What finer way to celebrate the sixtieth anniversary of these legendary sunshine-pop pioneers? It’s in the category of Best Album Liner Notes, for the Super Deluxe Edition of their 1967 psychedelic smash Pisces, Aquarius, Capricorn & Jones Ltd. The nominated writer: the great Andrew Sandoval, longtime Monkees scholar and author of acclaimed books on them and the Kinks. Pisces was one of that year’s biggest hits, with classics like “Pleasant Valley Sunday,” “She Hangs Out,” and “What Am I Doing Hangin’ Round?” (The album stayed at Number One for five weeks, until it got dethroned by Magical Mystery Tour.) It’s reason to celebrate—especially since the last time a Monkees record got a Grammy nod, it was “I’m a Believer” in 1968. —Rob Sheffield

Gracie Abrams didn’t receive any nominations this year, despite reportedly submitting her third album Daughter From Hell in almost all of the Big Four categories, amongst others. The singer-songwriter had a big year, leveling up with her third studio album, which earned her her first Number One on the Billboard 200 chart. Abrams also has a history with the Academy. Back in 2024, she earned a Best New Artist nomination, and a year later she received a nod for “Us,” her collaboration with Taylor Swift. What makes things even stranger is the fact that superproducer Edgar Barrera did earn a Songwriter of the Year nod for helping Abrams write “Broke My Heart,” which appears on Daughter From Hell. If only the Academy had a good reason for this shutout. —M.G.

When the Best Album Cover category returned last year and rightfully awarded Tyler, the Creator (as well as nominating the delightful Perfume Genius), it seemed to spell good fortune for the coming years. But this next class of nominees are … a little bewildering. A poignant photograph of Mavis Staples might be the most intriguing of this batch, and Rapsody’s God Gotta Afro & Gold Hoops makes a strong impression, too, but you’d think Charli XCX’s hotly debated Music, Fashion, Film photo shoot with male muses John Cale, Marc Jacobs, and Martin Scorsese might have been worth discussion. Same with Slayyyter’s Wor$t Girl in America, whose neon-red dollar-sign motif dominated 2026, and let the listener know exactly what to expect from her scuzzy electroclash breakthrough. How about the negative close-up of Kelela for New Avatar, a plant preserved in amber for L’Rain’s Fata Morgana, or the hand-painted illustration of Underscores’ hometown mall for U. Where were any of these stunning covers? Instead, the nominees are full of head-scratchers that leave us wanting more. —Jaeden Pinder

For all of the talk of rap’s faltering relevance in the mainstream, it’s surprising to see Baby Keem’s long-awaited album Casino, easily one of the best rap projects of the year, get mostly shut out from the awards. “Good Flirts,” the record’s standout track featuring Kendrick Lamar and a pitch perfect Momo Boyd, is up for Best Rap song, but in a Best Rap Album field that includes Ye’s, uh, controversial project Bully, it’s a shame not to see one of rap’s brightest young stars get more recognition. —J.I.

Björk may be one of the most recognizable and innovative names in music, but in her three decades as a solo artist, she has yet to be recognized in the Grammy’s Big Four Categories — until now. The Icelandic singer received nods in both the Record of the Year and Song of the Year categories for her work on Rosalía’s symphonic pop banger “Berghain.” Björk not only offers her signature vocals on the track, she also helped write it. And believe it or not, Björk has yet to win a Grammy of any kind in all those years. Could this finally the year she takes one home? —M.G.

If you follow the Yellow Brick Road deep into the Grammy nominations, you’ll find some artists heading off against one another as unusual adversaries. Wicked: For Good is up against The Wizard of Oz at Sphere in the Best Compilation Soundtrack for Visual Media category. Michelle Obama will be campaigning against Lil Jon, Michael J. Fox, Paul McCartney, Tim Curry, and Dolly Parton in the Best Audio Book field. Ariana Grande’s “immersive mix” of Petal will be facing off against Frank Zappa’s One Size Fits All in the Immersive Audio Category. And finally something’s about to happen with Mitski when she finds herself lobbying to win Best Recording Package for Nothing’s About to Happen to Me over John Lennon’s Power to the People. May the most surprising artist win! —Kory Grow

FLO and Ravyn Lenae are R&B stars, pop stars, something in-between stars — essentially, they’re everything but 2027 Grammy Award nominees. The British girl group missed out on nominations for their second studio album, Therapy at the Club, which seemed an obvious nominee for Best Progressive R&B Album. Blue Island, the third studio album from Lenae, on the other hand, could have realistically lived anywhere between the genre categories given its expansive sonic palette. In that regard, Lenae and FLO’s shutouts also speak to the limitations of how the Grammy Awards categorize music and how artists who move fluidly between genres are often relegated to an odd gray area as a result. “Leak It,” the engrossing lead single from Therapy at the Club, blows away nearly everything nominated in the Best Pop Duo/Group Performance category, and Blue Island standouts “Potential” and “Handle” wouldn’t have been out of place in Best Alternative Music Performance. Turns out Lenae was right when she sang: “Know I might be more than you could handle.” —L.P.

This year kicked off with a string of hotly anticipated rap albums, including A$AP Rocky’s Don’t Be Dumb, the culmination of almost a decade’s worth of hype. The album was a clean, cohesive take on rap from a generational icon and included some of Rocky’s most consistently entertaining material. After debuting at Number One, Rocky went on to deliver one of the year’s biggest rap tours and even dropped more songs on a deluxe version of the album, all while campaigning pretty heavily for the album’s Grammy consideration. That he received no nominations is a bit surprising, and feels a tad rude. —J.I.

This year’s country nominees include familiar faces like Lainey Wilson, Kacey Musgraves, and Chris Stapleton, but more than a few left-field surprises from outside the genre. The biggest curveball might be Black Francis of the Pixies, who scores his first-ever Grammy nomination in the Best Country Song category. The unlikely nod comes via Stephen Wilson Jr., who interpolates the Pixies’ “Where Is My Mind?” in his nominated song “Hardwired.” Queen Latifah also lands her first country nomination, thanks to her appearance on an all-star remake of Dolly Parton’s Seventies classic “Light of a Clear Blue Morning.” That collab with Parton, Lainey Wilson, Miley Cyrus, and Reba McEntire is up for Best Country Duo/Group Performance, where it will face off with another first-time country nominee, Mumford & Sons, nominated for their song with Stapleton, “Here.” John Mayer’s in that race too for yet another collab featuring Lainey Wilson — the guitarist plays some licks on her listicle song “Phone, Keys, Wallet.” —Joseph Hudak
