I’ve Run 14 Marathons. Here Are My 5 Race Recovery Tips to Get You Running Again Soon.

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

How to ease soreness, refuel well, and rebuild motivation after crossing 26.2.

Ask any marathoner: There is nothing like the energy of race weekend to get you amped up to keep on running. Feeling the weight of that finisher’s medal hanging around your neck has a magical way of erasing all the aches and exhaustion you pushed through for the past few hours (not to mention the past several months of training).

But what you do in the hours and days immediately following 26.2 miles can determine how quickly you bounce back—not only physically but also how soon you’ll feel emotionally ready to get moving again. Marathons are draining, and that finish-line euphoria won’t keep sustaining you through continuous daily runs if you don’t let yourself recover fully.

After conquering 14 marathon finish lines in all seasons and a few back-to-back marathons too, I’ve learned a few key lessons about how to successfully get back to running postrace. Keep these in mind if you’ve just crossed a marathon off your list and want to lace up your running shoes again soon.

The first time I ran the New York City Marathon, I lived in a fifth-floor walk-up apartment on the Upper East Side. Going down those stairs the next day was excruciating, but I went outside anyway and went for a walk—and my legs felt immensely better a day later, compared with when I didn’t take those steps after other races.

Obviously, you should give yourself some downtime and relax after a marathon, but when I make a point to walk around more often right after a race and to do some other light activity (like easy pedaling on my Peloton) over the following few days, it always helps my legs feel way better sooner. Sitting on the couch all day will only increase your stiffness.

Regardless of whether you hit a PR, qualified for Boston, or barely made it over the finish line, completing a marathon is a big accomplishment. I’ve found that taking some time after 26.2 to celebrate and appreciate my body for what it can do—and my mind for what it can push through—is an important step before jumping right back into running. When I push ahead without taking joy and pride in what I achieved, I wind up feeling run down and unmotivated.

If you feel like having a decadent meal and celebratory beers as part of that acknowledgment, Jeff Gaudette, owner and head coach at RunnersConnect, says go for it! But to feel up to running again soon, have something nutritious first.

“The focus on recovery needs to be more dialed in if you're going to do another race soon,” he says. “That means nutrition and refueling a little more diligently. You can still enjoy pizza and beer, but make sure you get some protein and healthy carbs beforehand.”

Research backs this nutrient combo up: One 2018 study in Nutrients found that eating both carbs and protein immediately postrace helped marathoners’ physical fatigue, mental energy, and soreness improve within three days compared with those who ate carbs alone.

Your body just did a lot; give it some extra TLC to help your muscles recover faster. Gaudette recommends trying out all the “little recovery tricks”—ice baths, dips in a hot tub, a massage—and seeing what helps you get back to 100 percent faster.

My fave: slipping on compression socks after a marathon and sleeping in them, too. Wearing them feels like my calves are getting a hug, and I always think my legs are a little less sore the next day when I use them. (Some research also backs up the benefits of compression gear for easing postexercise soreness.)

If you already have another race approaching, recovery is even more important—as is the training you put in between races. My worst marathon performances were when I ran Boston to Big Sur (both races within two weeks); I had not trained well enough for the hills and probably didn’t do enough to help my body recover between the races, and I paid for it.

How you structure recovery and training between two races depends on how much time you have. “If they’re really close, like within two weeks, the entire focus is on recovery and getting your legs to feel as good as possible,” says Gaudette. (See: ice baths, massage, etc.) “If there’s a longer break between, say three to six weeks, then after you fully recover, you'll want to have a week or two where you do some mileage and/or solid threshold work. Otherwise, you risk basically not training for five to six weeks—including the taper before the first race and then the time between the races.”

That’s enough time for your fitness to fall off and you to feel less prepared for race number two.

If you don’t already have a race on the calendar, use that finish-line motivation you’re feeling now to look ahead for another race you want to take on, even if it’s months away.

I never feel as energized to take on another marathon as right after I finish one (and the days leading up to it). I’ve learned I’m most likely to sign up for another race if I use that momentum to register for one. And with your next race on the books—whether you’ll be aiming for a PR in another marathon or going for a chill 5K with friends—it’s that much easier to get back to running and clock miles consistently because the goal is right in front of you.
