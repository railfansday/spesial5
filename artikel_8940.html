Zipp’s Cheapest Carbon Wheelset Makes a Strong Case for Itself—But There’s One Catch

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

A major-brand carbon wheelset at a lower price, with a great warranty and one trade-off road riders should consider.

Gear-obsessed editors choose every product we review. We may earn commission if you buy from a link. How we test gear.

Zipp’s 404S replaces the 303S with a deeper 50mm rim for modern road riding. Zipp says the new profile saves about two watts over its predecessor. At $1,300 for the pair, the wheels bring a familiar name and a lifetime warranty to a price well below the brand’s flagship models.

I rode the 404S on my steel Ritchey Road Logic, logging roughly 800 miles over two months. They’re a totally fine, solid wheelset. They feel settled at speed without riding harshly, accelerate well once they’re rolling, and remain stable in gusty conditions. In other words, Zipp made a perfectly good wheelset that will likely come stock on many SRAM-equipped bikes.

On the road, the 404S fades into the background of your ride (in a good way). The wheels feel comfortable without feeling harsh. Get them up to speed, and they are reassuringly solid. In terms of crosswind performance, I’d rate them quite good. They aren’t quite on the level of something like a Roval Rapide CLX III (basically the gold standard for crosswind performance), but they still feel very stable even on a gusty day. Once I'm moving, they have the responsive feel I expect from a 50mm wheelset, whether I’m pedaling through a corner or getting back up to speed on a rolling road.

The weight shows up more on steep climbs or when accelerating out of a slow corner; these wheels don’t have the immediate, lively feel of lighter aero options. That doesn’t make them feel sluggish in normal riding; it’s just the clearest difference I noticed after riding lighter wheels.

Otherwise, I found little to separate the 404S from more expensive wheelsets with a similar rim depth. They do the basic job well: they feel stable in the wind, comfortable on imperfect roads, and once you’re up to speed, their weight disadvantage largely disappears.

The 404S uses a Tubeless Straight Side, or hookless, rim design. That means checking tire compatibility and pressure limits before you ride. Zipp recommends tires from 28 to 39mm, and says the wheels are optimized for 28–29mm tires for best aero performance, with a maximum pressure of 72.5 psi. Many riders might miss that if you run a 30 or 32mm road tire, the maximum pressure limit drops to 65 psi, according to ETRTO.

Those limits may not affect riders who already use wider tires at lower pressures. But I still see hookless as a miss for road wheels in 2026 for many reasons. Sure, a handful of high-profile issues in the pro peloton didn’t help, but neither did the steady drip of unease coming from inside the industry itself. While Zipp has stood behind the standard, I’d rather give the company credit for acknowledging that many riders (including me) would prefer a hooked option. instead of dealing with a relatively low max tire-pressure limit, consulting pressure charts, and reading tire-compatibility lists.

ENVE, arguably the biggest proponent of hookless road wheels, has already made this pivot. The brand's flagship racing models, the SES 4.5 and 6.7 Pro wheels, both feature a “mini hook.” This leaves only Zipp and Giant/Cadex sticking with hookless for now, and given my predictions earlier this year, I would guess both are at least considering their options.

At $1,300, the 404S sits between a few other 50mm carbon wheelsets. HUNT’s 50 Carbon Disc is listed at $1,249, with a 50mm rim, 22mm internal width, and a hooked tire interface. HUNT lists its weight at 1,464 grams, making it a close alternative for about $50 less, with a hooked rim.

Shimano’s Ultegra C50 is another 50mm option, listed at $1,570 for the pair. It has a hooked rim and a 21mm internal width, making it a little more expensive than the Zipp, with a narrower rim bed.

The harder comparison for Zipp is basically any wheel from Light Bicycle. Light Bicycle’s complete Airia wheel builds start at $1,144, and that base build is claimed to weigh 1,218 grams. The 55mm-deep test set weighed 1,101 grams without tape or valves, though that specific build cost about $1,407. So the tested Airias were slightly more expensive than the 404S, but they were 5mm deeper and dramatically lighter (also hooked).

That makes the 404S a reasonably priced option from a major brand, but the weight is hard to overlook when lighter and sometimes cheaper wheels are available at similar or lower prices. But the 404S does have one way it stands above its competition. It comes with a lifetime warranty that covers not just manufacturing defects but also damage from intended use. This will appeal to many riders who would rather have a dependable wheel than a slightly lighter one.
