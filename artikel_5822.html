Parents' Autoimmune Disease Linked to Kids' Type 1 Diabetes Risk

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

— Large Swedish study highlighted shared familial susceptibility

Parental autoimmune diseases were associated with an increased risk of type 1 diabetes in offspring, according to a Swedish registry cohort study.

In an analysis of nearly 1.5 million individuals, children born to mothers with autoimmune disease during pregnancy had a 57% higher risk of developing type 1 diabetes (adjusted HR 1.57, 95% CI 1.46-1.68), according to Xingbo Mo, PhD, of Karolinska Institutet in Stockholm, and colleagues. Paternal autoimmune disease before childbirth had a comparable association (adjusted HR 1.51, 95% CI 1.37-1.67).

When parents had type 1 diabetes, the magnitude of risk was higher. Maternal type 1 diabetes was linked with a more than fourfold risk increase (HR 4.51, 95% CI 3.72-5.48), whereas paternal type 1 diabetes was associated with an even higher hazard (HR 5.79, 95% CI 4.70-7.13), they reported in JAMA Pediatrics.

"A clear gradient in type 1 diabetes risk was observed according to parental type 1 diabetes status, with the highest risk among offspring with both parents affected," the authors wrote. "The largely comparable maternal and paternal associations suggest that shared familial susceptibility may play an important role in the intergenerational transmission of type 1 diabetes risk."

While maternal and paternal estimates showed no overall difference for composite autoimmune disease, researchers identified variations across specific disease categories. Paternal associations were stronger for endocrine conditions -- most notably autoimmune thyroiditis -- whereas maternal associations were stronger for ankylosing spondylitis and gastrointestinal autoimmune disorders.

"Stronger associations for endocrine autoimmune diseases, particularly autoimmune thyroiditis, are consistent with the established clustering of endocrine autoimmune disorders and their shared immune-genetic susceptibility," the researchers noted. Yet the "disease-specific heterogeneity suggests that selected conditions may also involve parent-specific or potential intrauterine influences," they added, which "support a multifactorial model of intergenerational autoimmune transmission."

In an accompanying editorial, Mikael Knip, MD, PhD, of Biomedicum Helsinki in Finland, emphasized that an "obvious" practical takeaway is that offspring of parents with autoimmune disease warrant monitoring for type 1 diabetes.

"Consensus guidelines have been published for screening and monitoring of individuals at risk for type 1 diabetes," Knip wrote, noting that while screening efforts "may be variable based on resources available," children with a familial history "represent a definite risk group for progression to clinical type 1 diabetes."

Benjamin Nwosu, MD, chief of endocrinology at Northwell Health Cohen Children's Medical Center in New York, who was not involved with the study, underscored the necessity of early detection.

"Currently, in our practice, we screen children with type 1 diabetes for autoimmune thyroid conditions and celiac disease, because it's been published that children with type 1 diabetes have higher risk for the development of celiac and autoimmune thyroid conditions," Nwosu said in a statement.

He pointed out that the authors "did not mention that 40 to 50% of the risk for type 1 diabetes is carried on what's called the major histocompatibility complex class 2 genes or loci."

"Once you know that, you know a parent who has type 1 diabetes has some possibility of passing this condition to the offspring, not in a Mendelian autosomal dominant fashion, because type 1 is caused by what's called a polygenic aggregation of genes. But the risk is higher than in unaffected parents," he said.

The researchers evaluated 1,455,645 children born in Sweden between 2001 and 2014, with follow-up extending through 2023 (median age was 15.3 years, and 51.4% were male). Overall, 6.1% of children were exposed to maternal autoimmune diseases before delivery.

After adjusting for unmeasured shared familial factors using cousin-comparison models, the maternal association remained statistically significant (adjusted HR 1.27, 95% CI 1.08-1.50), with higher risks specifically noted for maternal endocrine autoimmune disorders and maternal type 1 diabetes.

The study team noted several limitations, including the lack of register data regarding clinical disease severity, autoantibody levels, disease activity during pregnancy, or medication use. "Such factors may modify fetal immune programming and could not be evaluated in the present study," they acknowledged.

Nwosu also cautioned that the results might not fully translate globally, noting Sweden "is located in the northern latitudes, where type 1 diabetes is almost endemic."
