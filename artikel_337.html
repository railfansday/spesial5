SpaceX returned its Starship rocket on Saturday—but there's a catch

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

STARBASE, Texas—The black-and-silver ship fell out of the sky, emerging from the cloud deck near the very end of its flight. Incongruously, the rocket flew horizontally until the last moment, when three Raptor rocket engines ignited and it swooped into a vertical position.

Slowly, gently, haltingly, the Starship upper stage eased its way toward the waiting tower where two arms extended outward, awaiting expectantly. The margins are so incredibly narrow. Two nubs on either side of the rocket, each protruding only about 1 foot, were the only purchase the rocket would have on the arms.

Then, less than a minute after it came into view, the Starship upper stage was snagged by the launch tower, as snug as you like. And so it was all over. SpaceX, nearly a quarter of a century after its founding, had just taken a hugely significant step toward developing and optimizing a massive, rapidly reusable rocket.

This was a breathtaking feat; a brilliant technical achievement that required the best engineering minds to think through a million different difficult problems. And on Saturday evening, following a handful of practice runs into the Indian Ocean, they did it before the entire world.

Watching Starship launch from here a little less than a day earlier, and knowing it had orbited the planet multiple times, added to the sense of unreality in seeing this spaceship return to Earth under its own power and be captured by the tower.

It felt like stepping into the future. SpaceX has dazzled us before by landing rockets on barges and returning humans to space with Crew Dragon. But this flight took things to another level. Starships are huge. They carry a huge amount of energy coming back from orbit. And until a few years ago, it seemed beyond fantastical to think about capturing a rocket with what are essentially outsized chopsticks.

Humans have built reusable spacecraft that flew into orbit before. In the 1970s, flush with confidence after the triumphant Moon landings, NASA designed and built several Space Shuttles. They had solid rocket boosters that were fished from the ocean and reused. The large orange external tank was not recovered after each flight, but the orbiters went to space for two weeks, or even longer, before returning to Earth and landing on a runway.

The shuttles were engineering marvels. But during the return to Earth they acted as gliders, not igniting their engines. And their heat shields were delicate flowers, requiring painstaking refurbishment after each flight. At the outset of the shuttle program, the space agency contemplated sending dozens of humans into space at a time, and carrying cargo at a cost as low of $25 per pound.

Reality never matched these rosy projections. The crew space capped out at seven astronauts, and the cost-per-pound was closer to $25,000. Each orbiter cost an extraordinary amount of money to build; NASA only ever built five that went to space.

SpaceX has five Starships in an advanced state of development at Starbase today; and its factory houses the components for a dozen more. The company can currently manufacture a Starship upper stage in its South Texas Starfactory every three weeks. The factory was sized to produce a ship every three days. When SpaceX founder Elon Musk discussed these build targets in an interview five years ago, he sounded mad. But he no longer does.

The Space Shuttle was nearly a fully reusable space launch system, but it fell far short of low-cost rapid launch. Over 30 years the vehicle made 135 flights. Within a few years SpaceX aims to fly 135 times a year, then thousands of times.

Such was the significance of Saturday. SpaceX aspires to fundamentally change the relationship between humanity and the heavens. The first step is to get the entire rocket back. SpaceX has now returned both elements of a Starship rocket safely to its launch tower. It will take time to reach a high rate of success with such landings, but Saturday’s safe return offered a proof of concept.

The landings will come with increasing frequency; and so will the launches. Over time SpaceX will perfect the heat shield that is likely to be the most difficult aspect of rapid reuse. And eventually, as difficult as it may be to believe, what we experienced with amazement this weekend will become routine. One day, we will take this all for granted.
