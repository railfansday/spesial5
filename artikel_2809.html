Bright Side Debates: Jalen Green or Collin Gillespie?

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

It has not been an average Phoenix Suns season, despite the near .500 record through the first few games. With wins against the Houston Rockets and Denver Nuggets, the team showed they can compete with the best of the Western Conference. With blowout losses to the Portland Trail Blazers and the San Antonio Spurs, the squad showed how they often lack the chemistry needed to improve upon their surprising 45-win season a year ago.

But no matter whether the Suns win or lose, one question has consistently hovered: who should start in the backcourt next to Devin Booker? Jalen Green whose done so or Collin Gillespie, the team's first reserve.

Amid Green's recent struggles that have seen his minutes dip and the continued rumors around Dejounte Murray potentially being traded for him, the conversation has heated up over the past week, with the Suns' second unit that is led by Gillespie playing some of its best basketball and receiving praise from other teams.

So as the conversation continues to take over Suns media, we wanted to lay out the reasons for and against both to start to help paint a better picture of the conversation. Tom takes the side of Green being the starter while Holden goes with Gillespie.

When I evaluate this Suns team, I do so by attempting to project their playoff potential. Last year, 45 wins and escaping the Play-In tournament, courtesy of Jalen Green, was a wonderful surprise and a reason for some much-needed optimism after the Mike Budenholzer regime bottomed out. This season, getting swept by the Thunder, Rockets, Lakers, or even the Spurs would be a massive red flag flying over this current iteration of the Phoenix Suns. Our last two playoff opponents physically dominated us on the perimeter, running Booker off his spots, and turning us over and over and over again. When the pressure ratchets up, we need premier athletes in order to match up with the best of the West.Jalen Green, despite some inconsistencies, is a three-level scorer that can be, and has been, a deciding factor in winning games in the postseason. He did it in game two of the Rockets/Warriors series. He did it against Golden State again the very next year, when the Suns toppled the Warriors to advance through the play-in tournament. He was everything that night, posting an overwhelming offensive and defensive performance in a must-win postseason game and what would be the last victory the Suns could muster in the 2025-26 season. Even while getting swept by the Thunder, Green acquitted himself well. He put up 17, 21, 26, and 23 while nabbing 4 steals in the final game and totaling at least 5 rebounds every game of the series.

Jalen Green can break perimeter pressure and stack paint touches against even the best defenses, in a way that Collin Gillespie simply can't. The kind of downhill pressure Jalen can provide is invaluable to a team searching for ways to free up Devin Booker when teams make it their game plan to take him away. While Jalen's finishing leaves a little to be desired, teams have to respect his ability to get to the rim and yak-it-down right in their faces. The same can't be said for Collin, who went for 5 points against Portland in the play-in and followed up with 2 points in the game against Golden State. He didn't fare much better against the throttling perimeter pressure of the Thunder. Outside of coming alive for 20 points in the series finale, Gillespie posted 8, 7, and 7 points through the first three games, going 4/14 from three.

I view Jalen Green's early season struggles to find his rhythm in the starting lineup similar to how I view Khaman's struggles to find his footing in the league. What you give up now in wins and losses, you gain back at the end of the year by giving two physically gifted players the space and time to figure it out.

This season is about how the Suns finish the year, not about how they look 20 games in. Getting back to .500 gives the Suns the leash they need to allow Jalen Green to climb back to that ceiling that saw him take a young Rockets team to a two seed. Moving Jalen Green to the bench, after you ask him to sacrifice his game to play a facilitation role, would be peak selling-low on the second-best offensive player on the Suns' roster. Jalen Green has only played 46 games for the Suns. The best is yet to come, but Green will never hit those heights if he's relegated to playing a Malik Monk role. A role that Collin Gillespie is much better suited to play.

It's a little early to be analyzing the roster's ability to compete in the West only 20 games into the season, but if we are going to do that, a few things we should factor in.

Considering the talent upgrade at power forward with Miles Bridges, the continuity the roster has from a season ago, and the natural development that comes with a roster dominated by players in their 20s, it would be a disappointment if Phoenix got swept in an uncompetitive manner, as they did a season ago. If we're talking about playoff success built around a Devin-Booker-led unit, we need to talk about when he's thrived the most in the playoffs: when he's had a more traditional point guard in the starting lineup.

Since Chris Paul's departure following the 2022-2023 season, Booker hasn't played alongside many true point guards in the starting lineup outside of Tyus Jones. Phoenix has been swept in their two playoff appearances and a lot of that falls on how the Suns' offense has been predicated around Booker's burden to lead the offense while also needing to get himself involved. With Jalen Green in the lineup, Booker continues to start games out of position and not put his main offensive focus on what he's best at: scoring.

While Gillespie by no means is the talent or plays the same style as the Point God, he does allow Booker to slide back into shooting guard duties while playing alongside a guard that doesn't need to score or have the ball in their hands to produce. After a poor start to the season, Gillespie is shooting 39% from three this month compared to Green's 32%. He can add more consistent spacing than Green can.

Lastly, if we're looking at Jalen Green's playoff success as a whole. Yes, he has more experience and more highlights than Gillespie does, but consistently he's played well below his regular season outings. INCLUDING his strong 38-point performance in his second-ever playoff game, Green averaged just 13 points (nearly eight less than his regular season average that year), shooting 37% from the field and 30% from three. Outside of his big game, he didn't have another contest where he shot better than 38% from the field in his six other playoff games. And in his first playoffs with the Suns, he didn't do much better.

As much as the Suns need to develop the players they feel can help improve their playoff performance, they need to be thinking more about the players who help Devin Booker be the postseason killer he was earlier in his career. Gillespie's play style combined with Booker's familiarity with pass-first guards can give Booker the opportunity to play as he did in the postseason earlier in his career. Green would be better suited as the reserve who can carry the offensive scoring when Booker is on the bench.

We saw Collin Gillespie as a starter last season, and after 58 games started, the consensus, from the legacy media to the message boards, was that he wore down. His three-point percentage, in a record-setting year, fell from 42% to 39% from January to February, from 39% to 36% from February to March, and bottomed out at 25% in the five games the Suns played in April.What we've seen early from Collin off the bench is an ascending shooter making his case for the 6MOTY award and building a potentially special tandem with Luke Kennard. Off the bench, Collin is that guy. He's the number one or number two option every time he touches the floor. In the starting lineup, he becomes the fourth wheel, locked into holding defenders on the three-point line while Booker gets to his spots in the mid-range. Collin is at his best off the leash, when he's aggressively seeking his shot, when he's pulling up early in the shot clock for a deep three simply because he had a feeling he was going to make it.The Suns have a really good bench. We've seen the Thunder ride a deep bench to a championship and a Western Conference Finals appearance. The Suns might have constructed a championship-caliber bench, but if they move the leader of the bench to the starting lineup in order to improve three-point shooting and find a ball-handler to free up Devin Booker, they could risk losing that advantage. Jalen Green is not Collin Gillespie. He's never come off the bench, whereas that's always been the trajectory of Collin's career. Jalen Green can try to adapt to a bench role, but Gillespie was born in it. The Suns will lose on both sides if they were to sit Jalen in favor of Collin.Jalen Green will struggle to find his rhythm in reduced minutes off the bench. Collin Gillespie will struggle to find his shot as he spends the majority of his minutes opening up looks for Devin Booker. That connection with Luke Kennard? Gone. The Suns are already having to scrape by with Oso starting, when he would be much more effective working against backup centers as the hub of the second unit. The Collin-Kennard-Oso trio could end up being the difference between a play-in appearance and a 5th seed, but not if we bail on Jalen before he's played 50 games for the team. Jalen Green is not a bench player, since he's not a facilitator. There is no path forward for Jalen off the bench except as a trade piece, and when we keep having to trade players because they don't work on the roster, we will continue to burn through what little assets we have left and reduce our ability to make a real impact addition to the roster.Collin Gillespie is a bench player, and he's a damn good one. Potentially one of the best in the NBA, not just this year, but for the next two to three years at least. Starting Collin diminishes his value and negatively impacts winning long term.

Tom is right. Collin Gillespie would be the Suns' fourth or fifth option in the starting lineup, but that's what the Suns need. Unlike last season, Phoenix has a starting power forward in Miles Bridges who is looking to create their own shot and does so consistently. Adding a fourth player who tends to score at an efficient clip takes away from the team's efficiency.

Out of the 248 different four-man lineups the Suns have played this year, when Brooks, Green, Booker, and Bridges all shared the floor, Phoenix is -74 in 221 minutes this season, their lowest plus-minus out of any four-man lineup the team has deployed this season by 25 points, according to Hoops Junkie. And while the Suns are -32 when they deploy the four-man lineup of Bridges, Gillespie, Booker, and Brooks, they've played just 32 minutes together, and Gillespie's floor spacing provides the three more room to operate on offense.

As we've seen throughout Jordan Ott's coaching tenure, he's not afraid to play reserves late, and even if Green is starting games on the bench, it doesn't mean he's going to end them sitting next to the coaches. Green played a few games off the bench last year when he was returning from his hamstring injury, but it would most definitely be an adjustment for him to start games with the second unit. However, as Green's continued to struggle to start the season, his minutes have gone down, and he's beginning to contribute in more ways than just as a scorer.

Just because Gillespie is starting doesn't mean he can't maintain his relationship with the second unit, and Green can have more room to operate as a scorer without impeding other shot creators' productivity. The identity of the bench and the team's ability to consistently play hard has shown it's able to endure lineup changes like they did earlier in the season when Khaman Maluach got replaced by Oso Ighodaro as the starting center.

The Suns are already carrying the weight of the Bradley Beal dead money, so it's difficult to convince me they can compete in the playoffs if they are bringing 36 million dollars off the bench.

Trade Jalen before you bench him, because $36 million in a matching contract is at least going to bring back a starter. There is no other team in the NBA that is spending that type of money on a bench player, and the teams that have a bench player making close to that much aren't very good. Jordan Poole is making $34 million for the Pelicans. Jerami Grant is making $34 million for an upstart Grizzlies team, but who really expects them to make the playoffs? Who thinks that's a good contract?

Like it or not, the Phoenix Suns have $36 million invested in Jalen Green, and anything other than a temporary stint on the bench handicaps the Suns' ability to field a starting lineup that can compete with the best in the Western Conference come playoff time. Jalen Green is a volume, rhythm shooter. He needs to get more shots, earlier in the game. He needs to be on the floor for longer periods of time in order to reach flow state. Despite what seems to be the consensus on the internet, Jalen Green is not going to get better coming in off the bench. Even if he did, it would be impossible for him to justify his contract playing twenty minutes and scoring 8-12 points a game. It's a losing proposition from the onset, no matter how well Jalen plays.

Instead of sabotaging the kid, as they did with this facilitation role, double down on him. Give him the green light you've given every other player on the team, from Brooks to Bridges to Collin to Goodie to Kennard. Why is Jalen Green the only player asked to do less of what he's great at? Why is he not extended the same opportunity to lean into the aspects of his game that make him special as the rest of the team?

The Suns need to at least attempt to get their money's worth from this contract, whether Green is on the team after the trade deadline or not. Anything less would be a disservice to the team, the fanbase, and Jalen Green.

I think NBA teams know who Jalen Green is. He's a dynamic, undersized, inefficient scorer that can get hot and stays confident no matter how he's shooting the ball. He's already been traded before and has spoken on numerous occasions about life in trade rumors. The Suns also didn't sign Green to his contract; the Rockets did. his salary was an essential part in sending Kevin Durant to the Houston Rockets. Moving Green to the second unit isn't an example of Phoenix devaluing a player that they signed.

If Green becomes a reserve, it won't impact his trade value. In contrast, it will give him more room to operate and allow him more opportunities to do what he does best: score. Jalen Green is asked to do less of what he's best at because what he does best he does at an inefficient clip and doesn't provide the defensive impact that other inefficient scorers do, like Dillon Brooks. If Green comes off the bench despite his large salary, it gives other organizations around the league the opportunity to see the Suns' willingness to make decisions purely based on basketball and not give in to other variables beyond what's best for the team's on-court success.

Potentially, if Green shows that he can thrive as a reserve, it can show not only other teams, but most importantly the Suns, his ability to adapt to new opportunities, independent of what his salary is. Albeit rare, as Tom pointed out, players like Jrue Holiday and Alex Caruso have found opportunities to thrive in the second unit despite their large salaries, and if Green can do so, it can be a positive reflection on how much the Suns' culture has improved the last two seasons, considering all the turmoil that Bradley Beal coming off the bench resulted in for the Suns in the 2024-25 campaign.

The Suns have built a strong culture that has been praised by people close and far based on sacrifice. Someone's contract length or size cannot dictate playing time. It would go against what the team has been trying to build since the departures of Kevin Durant and Bradley Beal, when the team switched up their culture and play style.

You've heard the key points for both to start; now you be the judge.
