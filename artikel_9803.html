Your Crypto Exchange's Default Settings Could Change Your Tax Bill

Sell one bitcoin from a balance of two on an exchange, and the confirmation shows a price and a quantity. It does not show which Bitcoin you sold.

That detail decides the tax bill. Take an illustrative investor who bought 1 BTC at $20,000 and another at $90,000, both more than a year ago, then sold 1 BTC at $110,000. If the older coin counts as sold, the gain is $90,000. If the newer one does, it is $20,000. Same trade, same day, a $70,000 difference in reported gain.

For two years the IRS has let U.S. investors settle that question in their own records. That arrangement ends on December 31, 2026. From the first sale of 2027, the choice has to go through the platform holding the coins, which raises a plain question: what can a customer actually tell the platform?

To find out, I read the public tax pages of 15 U.S. crypto platforms between October 27 and 29, 2026. Six of them do not state which coins they sell first.

The rule sits in Treasury Regulation 1.1012-1(j). When a broker holds your coins, and you do not identify specific units by the date and time of the sale, the earliest-acquired units in that account are treated as sold first. That is first-in, first-out, or FIFO, and it runs account by account. Coins at one exchange are not pooled with coins at another.

The IRS granted temporary relief in Notice 2025-7 and extended it in Notice 2026-20. Through December 31, 2026, an investor can identify units on their own books, no later than the date and time of each sale, or record a standing order there before selling. For 2026 sales, the units identified that way control for federal income tax purposes, even if the broker's report shows something else.

After that date, the notice says, taxpayers "may not rely" on the relief. Unless new guidance arrives, the identification has to reach the broker, either as a standing order or as a lot choice on the trade.

A quieter line in the same regulation matters too. When a broker offers only one way to identify units, that method "is treated as a standing order or instruction." The notice switches this off during the relief period for investors using it. From 2027 it applies, so on a single-method platform, the platform's method effectively becomes the customer's own instruction.

I used each platform's own help center, tax pages and terms, read against the IRS rules, and no third-party summaries.

"Not disclosed" means I found no statement on the platform's public pages. It does not mean the platform has no policy. Coinbase, for instance, names FIFO for its institutional Prime accounts but not for retail customers, and Webull's FIFO language covers securities.

Those settings have their own fine print. Kraken's has to be changed from a desktop device and applies only going forward. Coinbase says the method must be chosen before the trade settles, and a new choice covers open and future trades only.

2026 is the first year brokers must report cost basis on Form 1099-DA, and only for "covered" assets: coins bought on that platform on or after January 1, 2026, and kept there. Anything moved in from a wallet or another exchange is noncovered. For people who self-custody, that can be most of what they own.

Robinhood's ordering is no quirk. Treasury Regulation 1.6045-1 tells a broker with no customer instruction and no adequate transfer records to report units it did not acquire for the customer first, and only then work from the earliest date. Your own default is plain FIFO. So the form can list different units from the ones you must treat as sold, and a worked example in Regulation 1.1012-1 shows exactly that.

The form can be right and your return can still need different numbers, with the adjustment shown on Form 8949. That matters because the IRS gets a copy of the form. As attorney and CPA David Klasing explained in a HackerNoon interview on John Doe summonses, the agency has used those orders to spot mismatches between crypto activity and reported taxes. Form 1099-DA puts that data on a standard form for every customer.

Find your method and date it. Look up your platform's default, save the page and note the day you checked. If nothing is published, ask support and keep the written answer.

Identify lots before each 2026 sale, not after. The relief requires the identification no later than the date and time of the sale, so a spreadsheet filled in during January will not rescue December trades. Use something that pins down basis and holding period, such as the purchase date, time and price.

Decide your 2027 setup before your first January sale. If your platform has a setting, pick the method and screenshot it. If it offers lot picking, plan to choose on every order. HIFO is not automatically the better pick: the highest-cost lot may be short-term, and using it first leaves your low-basis coins for a later year. Run both numbers on your own lots.

Export everything now. An earlier HackerNoon piece on crypto tax myths pointed out that most reputable exchanges let you download your transactions into a spreadsheet. Do that for every account, including the ones you rarely open, and keep withdrawals and deposits so both sides of each transfer can be matched.

Add missing basis before forms are generated. The windows are short. Strike asks customers to finish their details before it generates forms in mid-February. Robinhood's cutoff for 2025 sales was January 14, 2026, with a warning that updates after January 7 could delay forms. Assume the next window will be just as tight.

One date to remember: December 31, 2026. It is the last day a lot choice on your own books can count, and Strike warns that its tax year follows UTC, so there the year ends at 7:00 p.m. ET. The next marker follows quickly. Forms are due to customers by February 16, 2027, because February 15 is Washington's Birthday, and those will be the first to carry basis.

The IRS has extended this relief once. Planning on a second extension is a gamble.

Platforms are also still catching up. Several help pages show the old $600 threshold for Form 1099-MISC, although the figure for 2026 is $2,000. So treat any table, this one included, as a snapshot.
