Worried about the AI bubble popping? How investing pros say to adjust your strategy

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Recent headlines coming out of the technology sector may have some investors feeling queasy.

Shares of Nvidia, the firm whose chips have powered much of the artifical intelligence boom, are down 35% since their high on October 6. Part of the downdraft came after company co-founder Jensen Huang resigned as CEO amid revelations of his romantic ties to a woman believed by the U.S. intelligence community to be a Chinese spy.

All told, tech companies in the S&P 500 are down nearly 17% from their early October highs, which has many investors wondering: Is the AI bubble we've all been hearing about finally ready to pop?

"People have been worried for awhile that we have a tech-heavy, top-heavy market that's overly focused on a single theme," says Dan Lefkovitz, a strategist with Morningstar Indexes. "They've drawn parallels to the dotcom era and railroads and every other transformative technology build-out we've seen."

Those concerns aren't likely to dissipate anytime soon. The 10 largest companies in the S&P 500 currently command 37% of the index, and 37% of the benchmark's weighting is in tech stocks as of Nov. 17. That is before Anthropic's initial public offering, which could add another major AI player to the top of the market. While the firm won't immediately join the S&P 500, it's reportedly targeting a valuation that would make it the tenth-largest publicly traded U.S. firm on the market when it begins trading.

A market that's top-heavy and heavily tilted toward a single industry doesn't a bubble make. Historically, market bubbles have come when the price investors will pay for a particular investment becomes disconnected from its underlying value — and the jury is still out on whether that's the case for major AI stocks.

But if the idea that a major chunk of your portfolio revolves around a single technology is keeping you up at night, it may make sense to adjust your strategy, financial experts say. Here's how.

If you're worried about a potential bubble bursting, your first step is to evaluate your portfolio's exposures, says Anthony Saglimbene, chief market strategist at financial services firm Ameriprise.

"You want to make sure you're diversified, and that not all of your eggs are in tech or the AI theme," he says, adding that, after years of outperformance, tech firms make up a huge portion of many passively managed, market-capitalization-weighted index funds. "That works well when tech is going well. It doesn't work right now when tech is having a sell-off."

If you determine you want to dial back your exposure to AI and technology names, however, the smart move isn't to take your ball and go home, says Lefkovitz. Rather, he says, you'd be wise to maintain your exposure to stocks while broadening what your portfolio holds.

"Exiting the market is an extreme option," he says. "People have been talking about the market being overvalued or too tech-heavy, too AI-heavy for years now. And if you had exited, you would have missed out on some tremendous gains."

So consider tweaking your portfolio at the margins, he suggests. That may mean adding exposure to an equal-weight version of your favorite index, rather than one that holds bigger companies in bigger quantities. You could also tilt towards indexes that track international stocks or smaller U.S.-based companies — both of which are less exposed to tech and AI names. The same general idea applies to dividend strategies and ones that focus on undervalued stocks, which tend to skew toward older-economy firms.

No matter which route you consider, be sure to talk with a trusted financial advisor before making any wholesale moves in your portfolio. The goal, says Lefkovitz, isn't to upend your strategy or abandon AI or tech altogether, but to dial things down to a level that you're comfortable with.

"There are ways to participate [in the stock market] without being so reliant on the top end of the market and that AI theme," he says.

You could examine your portfolio and find that you're entirely comfortable with your exposure to AI. Even with tech in turmoil, many market watchers see a persistent demand for AI infrastructure, such as microchips and data centers, and have faith that so-called hyperscalers — megacap tech firms such as Alphabet and Microsoft — will reap huge future profits from the current AI buildout.

While the current environment presents the risk of a short-term drawdown, younger investors hoping to cash in on AI should focus on the long-term opportunity the technology presents, says Jamie Cox, managing partner for financial planning firm Harris Financial Group.

"If you're Gen Z and you're worried about a tech bubble, you're not thinking about this right," he says. Those with decades to invest should be comfortable with the short-term ups and downs of the stock market because they have plenty of time to let fallen markets recover and ascend higher, he says: "Time is on your side."

Historically, the U.S. stock market has trended upward, rewarding investors who were willing to buy and hold stocks. That's not always an easy proposition in the short term, notes Ryan Detrick, chief market strategist at Carson Group, a wealth management firm. Broad-market pullbacks of 5% or more happen more than three times per year, on average, with drawdowns of 10% or more happening about once a year, he notes.

"Every year you're going to have volatility, and it's uncomfortable. But volatility is the toll we pay to invest," he says. "If you want the joys of longer-term stock returns over decades, you're going to have some very uncomfortable moments along the way."
