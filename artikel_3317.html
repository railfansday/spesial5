Switched at Birth: How Two Men Spent 36 Years Living Each Other’s Lives

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

I t was subzero in Grafton, North Dakota, when Liz O’Toole’s water broke. She roused her husband, Terry Morrison, his brown mullet tousled on the pillow beside her, and grabbed her hospital bag, plus a towel to keep the truck seat dry on the 43-minute trip to Unity Medical Center, a low-slung building where generations of Morrison’s family had been born.

O’Toole was excited yet nervous as Morrison laid on the speed. This was the first baby for the petite brunette, so she was just hoping for an uneventful pregnancy. But after nurses wheeled her into the facility, things took a turn. She was told her pelvic bones were too small and that she’d need to have a C-section.

Morrison watched through the window, his stomach too weak to take in the blood. A few hours later,  when the baby was finally clean and swaddled, he held his son for the first time. The nurses helped O’Toole breastfeed and rolled black ink on her baby’s feet, stamping them at the bottom of the birth certificate. They named their one and only child Jeremy; Terry liked the name. Simple as that.

Later that evening, Evelyn Newton finally let her husband, Keith Bylin, drive her to Unity Medical Center after labor had well and truly set in. This was her second child, and she knew that if she got to the hospital too soon, they’d confine her to a bed for hours on end. She’s not one to sit idle, even with a baby on the way. Bylin watched the ice crystals in the sky float under the moon, his fair cheeks ruddy with the cold, as they motored the 30 miles to the delivery room. The puce-green tiles looked dated to him, the room stark and bare. Same as the other couple, after giving birth, the nurses helped Newton nurse and stamped the baby’s feet at the bottom of the birth certificate, which read “Kyle Jerome Bylin.”

The babies went home a few days later: Jeremy to a single-wide trailer, where he dozed in a borrowed bassinet next to his parents’ bed; Kyle to the Bylin family farm and a hand-cranked baby swing in his older brother Darren’s room. Both were sweet babies, good sleepers. Kyle loved to be tickled until he squirmed — then tickled again. Jeremy begged his mom for baths. Both were loved. Aside from being white men born on the same day in the same hospital, Kyle and Jeremy don’t have too much in common these days. Kyle is a research librarian, enamored with technology. Jeremy works as a welding inspector for a wind-energy company and loves cars. Since that night back in 1988, they’ve lived 36 years without crossing paths. Still, they’re tied together by an unimaginable trauma. Somehow, they got mixed up back in the nursery, each going home wearing a hospital bracelet meant for the other child — and lived the life the other was supposed to live.

Now, after DNA revealed the shocking secret, the families are looking for answers. And demanding justice.

KYLE BYLIN GREW UP IN THE FARMING town of Adams, North Dakota, population 121. Boasting barely more than a church, grocery, bank, and gas station, it’s the kind of place where family ties go back for centuries. The Bylin farm has been around for four generations; angus cows and Paso Fino horses graze in the football-size front yard, which abuts the glittering Bylin Dam.

Obsessed with technology and rock & roll, Kyle wasn’t too keen on exploring the thick woods behind his house or wrangling cattle. As a kid, he’d cry if his mother tried to coax him up on horseback. Instead, he’d steal himself away in his bedroom or the basement, writing his own Dashboard Confessional-inspired lyrics on message boards as his brother and his father worked on Darren’s prized 1989 Nissan 240SX in the garage. Out his window, Kyle could see the silver grain silos wavering in the summer heat, his family’s land stretching off toward the horizon. This was supposed to be his future: working with his hands the way Bylins had done for generations. Not an ideal path for a kid who regularly trekked to Grand Forks to see the likes of Ozzy Osbourne, Mudvayne, and Rob Zombie wreak havoc on the Alerus Center stage. Still, Kyle never suspected that his family wasn’t, well, his. In family photos, he doesn’t look markedly different: There’s a Christmas snapshot of him, his dad, and Darren where he’s sporting one of those metal-ball necklaces that were so popular in the Nineties and a Slipknot T-shirt, his dark Caesar cut and glasses making him look like a young Freddie Prinze Jr. His father and Darren are more clean-cut, rugged blond Norwegians with rosy cheeks, but they all have the same intense, sparkling eyes. And then there’s his mom, Evelyn, petite and fine-boned with caramel curls. They’re not exactly twins, but any differences Kyle may have noticed between him and his family could be explained by Evelyn’s background: She was adopted by an American soldier in Germany during the Cold War. Her father was a chief warrant officer, so she spent her childhood shuttling between bases, learning to walk on the boat over to Ellis Island.

Kyle’s parents got divorced when he was in grade school, so he doesn’t really have any memories of them together — just shuttling between the farm and his mom’s trailer. But his childhood memories are mostly happy ones: shooting down a waterslide that seemed as tall as a building at Disney World with his brother, watching Old Faithful erupt with his dad, showing his mom a new litter of kittens up in the hayloft, their eyes still closed and fur damp.

While Darren followed in his father’s bootprints and joined the family farm, Kyle enrolled in a state university for a spell — the first in his clan to get a higher education. His path to a degree was circuitous though; he dropped out of that first school and became a music-tech journalist in Los Angeles, before following a girlfriend to Iowa, where she’d gotten a job.

Seated in a circle at his girlfriend’s parents’ house back home in Columbus, Ohio, fate intervened in 2017. Under the soft glow of Christmas lights, Kyle rifled through the pile of secret-Santa gifts in search of the most promising box. His girlfriend’s mom had gone all out: lotto tickets, candles, wine, you name it. When he pulled back the festive paper, though, Kyle glimpsed the distinctive, leaf-shaped logo of Ancestry.com, a prominent DNA-testing company. “I took it home and sent off the test and didn’t really think anything of it for a long time,” he says. Until years later, when he’d receive a message that changed everything.

IF YOU FOLLOW NORTH DAKOTA’S RED RIVER about an hour south from Kyle’s childhood home, you’ll eventually run into the single-wide where Jeremy grew up with his mom after his parents split in grade school. Unlike Kyle, Jeremy spent most of his childhood outside: building forts with other kids in the trailer park, catching frogs and lizards in the woods, and working on his red 1990 Nissan 240 SX.

His mother was happy for her son to be outdoors exploring while she was busy working. Liz O’Toole helped locals recover after the Red River flooded back in 1997, making sure everyone had the proper materials to rebuild. Later on, she got her master’s and worked at a rehab center. It was a childhood out of an adventure book, but Jeremy never felt quite right in his family. His parents were dark-haired and solid, while Jermey looked like Dennis the Menace, all blond locks and scraped knees.

“I used to joke around with my mom that I was adopted because I didn’t look like anyone in my family, and I didn’t really act like anyone in my family either,” he says. “I was mostly kidding. I mean, why would I think that my mom was putting on the charade of adopting me? The best life they could give me was a single-wide trailer on a double lot.”

Jeremy loved his parents, but he always felt like something was missing — especially when he kicked back to watch Supernatural every week under posters of Eminem flipping the bird and Tupac’s cocky smile. “I’ve watched every season of that show,” he says of the 2005 drama that saw two siblings fighting off the forces of darkness. “They would go to hell and back for each other. That was just that bond that I was always yearning for.” Some kids wanted a younger brother to lord over, but Jeremy wanted an older sibling, a protector.

His room filled with tanks housing frogs and turtles, Jeremy dreamed of becoming an oceanographer after a trip to SeaWorld at age nine with his grandmother and aunts. He stood, rapt, as Shamu did tricks in his tank, and still remembers how he got to pet a dolphin. But those plans were dashed when he got his high school girlfriend pregnant.

Jeremy got his GED — his infant daughter clutched in his arms in a frilly pink dress at his graduation — and married his girlfriend at age 18. School was briefly an option, but, as he says, “There’s no room and board for you, your baby mama, and your newborn baby. So that was scrapped, and I just became a blue-collar worker, like my dad.”

And so he and his wife moved into their own trailer, and Jeremy worked in a body shop and followed in his mother’s footsteps, helping build a wall that was erected to keep the Red River at bay after the flood. He was proud of that. “It’s a piece of me protecting that city for the rest of its existence,” he says.

Over the years, Jeremy’s little family grew — he and his wife had two more children — and Jeremy started working in the wind-farm business. The couple divorced in 2011. Eight years later, he remarried, and by 2023, he was settled: living in Colorado with a plum job in the quality-control department of a wind-tower-production company. That is, until his mother called him on his way home from work. His grandfather had died a year before and another family member had recently died by suicide, so he expected the worst when he thumbed over to the answer button on his iPhone.

“What now? What happened?” he asked, groggy after a long workday.

“Are you sitting down?” asked his mother.

“I can’t sit down any more than I already am,” Jeremy quipped, but his mother didn’t laugh. Instead, she broke news that gave credence to those suspicions that he’d harbored for so long: that his family wasn’t his family.

IT DIDN’T UNFOLD LIKE IN THE MOVIES: one big reveal, a tearful reunion. Instead, the truth about what happened that day in 1988 took years to come to light. Kyle was living in Iowa back in 2018, working for GoDaddy, when he finally took that DNA test. He forgot to look at the test results until Thanksgiving, when, back in North Dakota, he was dishing up turkey at his aunt’s place. She’d just gotten into digging into her family tree, so they booted up their tests to compare notes. Oddly, they didn’t match up on the site.

Kyle assumed he’d messed up his sample, or something had happened in the lab. He had no reason to believe his mother had cheated on his father — or that he’d been secretly adopted. And so, once more, he forgot about the test — until a woman named Martha Hotchkiss messaged him in August 2023. Kyle had shown up on her dashboard as a familial connection. Hotchkiss, a sunny woman from Minnesota with close-cropped gray hair, had been looking for a long-lost sister — a baby her mother had had out of wedlock and given up for adoption before she was born. Maybe Kyle was connected to that lost sibling somehow. Soon, Hotchkiss says, “We started down the rabbit hole of, like, ‘Well, how are we related?’”

By this point, Kyle was living in a sparse dorm room in Ypsilanti, Michigan, studying for his master’s in library research. He was busy, but still intrigued by Hotchkiss’ messages. They played detective for a while, then lost touch. Kyle was close to graduating, and Hotchkiss had kids to take care of. When they linked up again in January 2024, though, the conversation turned serious: Kyle had his mother, Evelyn, take a DNA test so the family could track some health issues. Since she was adopted, they wanted to make sure they knew what traits ran in the family. Kyle’s brother, Darren, had taken one, too. Darren and Evelyn matched. Kyle did not. Lying in his dorm-room bed, surveying the results in his phone’s glow, Kyle’s stomach dropped.

Kyle sent Hotchkiss a message about how he didn’t match with his mother or brother. “Does anyone in your family have any connection to Grafton, North Dakota? That’s where I was born in 1988. I’m not even sure what I’m suggesting,” he added.

The pieces started falling into place then. Hotchkiss had a sister, Liz O’Toole, who used to live in Grafton. “What’s your birthdate, Kyle?” Hotchkiss typed, heart in her throat. When he told her Jan. 26, the same day as her nephew Jeremy, and, yes, he was born at Unity, Hotchkiss’ fears were confirmed. “That’s when the bottom fell out of everything,” Hotchkiss says. She texted her wife, a doctor who surely knew more about genetics than she did. Her wife texted back, simply, “Shit.” Because it could only mean one thing: The babies had been switched.

At first, Hotchkiss considered keeping the news to herself. The men were nearing 40 — what good would it do to blow up their lives? But she thought about the slim possibility of someone involved in this inadvertently marrying a lost blood relative, and messaged Kyle the next day: “This series of messages has been super thought-provoking, and leads me to one conclusion: that you are the biological child of my sister, and that you and my nephew got switched at the hospital. Mind blown. All the other explanations are even more far-fetched.” Kyle’s mind, he says, was similarly blown.

A few weeks later, Hotchkiss sidled up to her sister in the fellowship room of their church, St. Matthews, where Liz was sipping on a coffee with her husband, Larry, after the service. “I need to tell you something,” Hotchkiss whispered. “Do you want to talk about it here or later?” Liz chose the former.

“OK,” Hotchkiss replied. “You got to get on Ancestry.com right away.”

THE NEWS SPREAD HALTINGLY THROUGH the families. After Jeremy’s mother, Liz, called him on that ride home from work, Jeremy and his wife huddled around the laptop, looking up pictures of Kyle and his parents and Darren on Facebook — Darren’s photos struck Jeremy dumb. The fair man was like his twin, the older brother he’d dreamed of while watching Supernatural as a teen. They even had the same facial expression in photos: serious, but boasting a quiet kind of self-assurance. “It was the first time seeing my face in someone else,” Jeremy says.

The next day, Jeremy logged back on to Facebook, navigating to Kyle’s page. He sent a message to the other man, laying out what his mother had told him and asking if Kyle was born at the same hospital on the same day. Kyle confirmed those facts and gave Jeremy his parents’ contact info. The two exchanged messages, but Jeremy was a little disappointed that Kyle didn’t want to talk much further than that, but Kyle was dealing with finals, his master’s finally within reach. He couldn’t think too much about the switch until he graduated — otherwise, he’d fall apart.

After all, Kyle had already been reeling from the news for a few weeks now. Before Martha spoke to Liz, Kyle and his brother told their parents about the switch separately: Kyle called his father, Keith, and Darren took his mother, Evelyn, ice fishing on the Bylin Dam. “I’m not so good about talking about feelings, but I was sick,” Keith says. “I was angry.” Evelyn was more worried about Kyle than anything else. “My first thoughts were ‘Why didn’t Kyle tell me himself? And how is Kyle? How is he doing? How is he feeling about this?’”

At that point, the families were still essentially strangers — as Kyle was reminded of when he spoke with Liz over Zoom for the first time. They may have had the same strong brow, dark eyes, and even similar glasses, but this was still his first time talking to the woman. “I described it as ‘Oh, what a nice lady from Minnesota,’” Kyle says of first talking to his birth mom. “I had no real kind of concrete connection.”

IT ALL FINALLY FELT REAL WHEN the families met in person. Liz first spotted Kyle through her living-room picture window. He was tall, she thought — like her ex-husband. It was April 2025, and Kyle was in Minneapolis, where Liz lives now, for a research conference, so it seemed like a good time to meet in person. He’d gotten lost on the way and was a bit harried when he knocked on the door to her compact blue house, a tall apple tree shading the modest yard. When Liz answered, they settled on a handshake. A hug seemed too personal for someone she’d essentially just met, Liz thought.

Once Kyle entered the foyer, the two clicked. Kyle noted the University of Michigan blanket he’d given to Liz as a gift after they first spoke resting over the back of a rocking chair — from his alma mater — and Liz took him on a tour of her basement packed with books about computer science left behind by her father. Kyle felt a pang when he learned his biological grandfather had died long ago. He’d been the head of the main-frame computer department at the University of Minnesota, and Kyle couldn’t help but think what it would have been like to have a relative so plugged into his own interests, the conversations they would have had at holidays.

As he and Liz sorted through photos, though, Kyle was thrilled to see himself reflected back in the faces of his lost family members. A nose there, the curve of a jaw there. He felt seen in a way he’d never had before. And after a hearty Midwestern meal of spaghetti and milk, the two finally hugged.

Kyle met his biological dad, Terry, that Thanksgiving when he went home to North Dakota to visit Keith. Climbing into Terry’s black diesel pickup, Kyle noted that they shared the same dark eyes. Terry was full of stories — how he raised hell as a kid, tearing through the countryside on snowmobiles, chasing girls. When the two pulled up in front of Texas Roadhouse to have lunch, Kyle gave Terry an appraising look.

“You know, Terry,” he said, “you probably held me back at the hospital, at least once.” Terry, the outgoing chatterbox, went quiet then. Terry is stoic now, talking about the revelation that he’d missed out on all those years with Kyle. “There’s nothing I can do about it. I just got to start accepting it for what it is now,” he says. “As I said [to Kyle’s dad], ‘Well, Keith, we’ll come out of this with an extra boy, and we got each other as a friend.’”

AFTER LIZ BROKE THE NEWS TO JEREMY, he called his father, Terry, while the older man was on the way to Fargo for supplies. “It’s hard to put it in words. There was disbelief at first,” Terry says now. “I went home [from the hospital] thinking I had Jeremy Morrison.”

It took Jeremy a few months to call Evelyn, his birth mother. Pacing between the hallway and bathroom, he peppered her with questions: Where was she from? What did she do for work? What was her favorite food? Evelyn noted that Jeremy laughed — and spoke — just like her son, Darren. Keith, who’d started therapy after the reveal, was just as nervous when he and Jeremy got on the phone. Still, Jeremy put him at ease; Jeremy and Kyle hadn’t grown up that far apart, so Keith and his biological son knew people in common in the farming world. As it turned out, Keith had had beers with Jeremy’s uncle more than once over the years — he’d even bought equipment from the man. And Keith told Jeremy all about Darren; it turns out the two men had basically the same car as teens, and both filled their rooms with fish tanks full of amphibians.

Jeremy saw his biological mother for the first time through the stucco windows of a chain Mexican restaurant in Grand Forks, North Dakota, in 2024. Sitting next to his wife, a basket of chips already on the table, he watched as a woman with cropped gray hair and his eyes walked up to the door. Evelyn recognized the man right away; even dressed in camo shorts, an arm full of tattoos, he looked just like her son Darren. They hugged, and Jermey’s wife took pictures, then sat down to eat — and talk. Jeremy doesn’t want to share what they discussed — it’s too personal — but he does say that “her independence, her stubbornness, her quick wit — yeah, I definitely got that from her.” It’s true: Both are quick to a wry joke; both have a way with words befitting a natural storyteller.

The next day, Jeremy made the hour-plus drive to the Bylin farm. As he pulled up in front of the big white house, the fields rippling with barley, soybeans, and sunflowers — cows long gone — Jeremy took note of the lake. He imagined himself playing hockey on the frozen surface during the winter, fishing in the summer heat. Keith was just beginning to set up for a Fourth of July potluck, dragging out tables and chairs onto the lawn, when he saw Jeremy walking toward him in jeans and a tank top reading “Wake Up and Smell the Freedom” in patriotic colors.

“To actually see him walk up to me, it’s kind of like seeing somebody come from the dead,” Keith says. “He just looks like my older son. He’s got the same walk. They got the same mustache.” Darren was supposed to attend the celebration, but ducked out at the last minute, overwhelmed by the prospect of meeting a brother he’d never known. In some ways, Keith says, the whole ordeal has been hardest on Darren, caught up in a circus he’s only tangentially a part of.

Jeremy and Darren did eventually meet up, a year later at the same Mexican joint he and Evelyn ate at, but neither wanted to talk much about what they discussed. “This is a mess that has affected many people in a confusing, sad, depressing way. The butterfly effect is endless,” Darren said in a statement to Rolling Stone after declining an interview. “No wonder Kyle and I have always been on such different paths in life. I was never able to look through his eyes and understand much. I sometimes wonder how the farm would look positively with a brother involved.”

AS THE FAMILIES SLOWLY GET TO KNOW each other, Keith can’t stop wondering what happened in the hospital all those years ago. Were the babies switched on purpose? And if so, why? Was it a mistake? The boys were the only babies in the hospital that day, according to local news — that week, even — so how, and when, did the switch occur?

Near misses and switches are apparently not as uncommon as one might think, according to experts. “We assume it’s extremely rare because the only cases we hear about are those that are detected,” says psychologist Nancy Segal, co-author of Accidental Brothers: The Story of Twins Exchanged at Birth and the Power of Nature and Nurture. She cites a study that estimates that 20,000 accidental baby switches happen per year, but most are corrected before the babies go home. “Not all such cases are ever going to be discovered,” Segal adds. “There are some that probably are never detected, and then some that may be discovered but never reported. I suspect they are more common than we think.”

Statistics aside, both families wanted answers, so Evelyn hunted down a lawyer who would take their case against the hospital. They filed a lawsuit back in July, suing for negligence, intentional infliction of emotional distress, negligent infliction of emotional distress, deceit, and medical malpractice. They’re asking for damages for “past and future pain, inconvenience … mental anguish, emotional distress, and other non-pecuniary damages,” according to the suit.

The hospital answered the lawsuit, denying any negligence and claiming that “defendants are without knowledge or information sufficient to form a belief as to the truth of the allegations.”

When asked for comment, the hospital responded: “We are currently working to better understand a highly unusual situation involving two men who apparently were separated from their biological parents at some point during their lives…. We recognize the profound impact this discovery has had on them and their families. Unfortunately, because of the passage of nearly four decades, the medical and staffing records that might have provided additional clarity no longer exist, and no members of the delivery team from that time are still employed by the hospital. While we deeply sympathize with the men and their families, we have found no evidence to support claims that Unity Medical Center or its staff were responsible for what occurred.”

Another had her own mixup in June 1975. “I got out of bed and got in my chair, and [a nurse] handed me my baby,” says the second woman, who also asked to remain anonymous. “I was just trying to get the baby to latch, and this nurse came just hustling back into the room and said, ‘That’s not your baby.’ Would I have noticed it days later? No, I probably wouldn’t have. It was back in the days where I was having a very difficult delivery, and they gave me ether.”

Lawyers for the hospital did not respond to questions regarding these claims.

Despite the drama, the legal battle, the meshing of two very different families, Kyle and Jeremy are still finding bright moments. Evelyn and Keith are getting to know Jeremy’s kids, and Kyle is getting closer to Liz. He even visited Martha and Liz in Minnesota over the summer, relaxing by Lake Michigan and exploring the sand dunes surrounding it. And the parents are bonding, too. Last Christmas, Liz pulled Kyle aside during a visit to Minnesota and, under the festive lights, told him Keith had done a great job raising him. When he heard that, Keith — the self-described stoic — cried.

And then there’s Jeremy and Kyle — those very different kids who grew up into very different men living each other’s lives. They don’t talk much now (unless it’s about the lawsuit), don’t live near each other, and don’t have much in common — except for trauma. There’s no real word for what they are to each other; they’re still figuring that out. They’re not brothers, they’re not related, but they are intertwined in a way that’s hard to ignore. Their pasts are linked, and, now, their futures are, too.
