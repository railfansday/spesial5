'Disney's Hexed’ Review: An Animation Era Ends With a Refreshing Mother-Daughter Story

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Taylor Gates is an Indiana native who earned her BFA in Creative Writing from the University of Evansville. She fell in love with entertainment by watching shows about chaotic families like Full House, The Nanny, Gilmore Girls, and The Fosters. After college, she moved to Los Angeles to pursue a career as a writer, editor, and filmmaker. Today, she’s a sucker for dramedies — especially coming-of-age stories centering around complex female and LGBTQ+ characters. She has been with Collider since May 2022.

It’s been an interesting couple of years for Disney animation. From cultural phenomena like Encanto and massive box office hits like Zootopia 2 to less successful releases such as Wish and Strange World, the last half-decade has seen both major triumphs and discouraging disappointments. The company’s Chief Creative Officer, Jared Bush, recently announced that their latest film would likely be the final project to feature the signature CG animation style that has characterized their movies for the past 20 years. Thus, Hexed’s performance will determine whether Disney can close a major chapter on a high note.

Hexed centers around a teenage outcast named Billie (Hailee Steinfeld), who is always getting herself into trouble — much to the dismay of her overprotective mother Alice (Rashida Jones). Billie’s latest act of rebellion involves stealing her mom’s car and breaking into her school to set an owl free, which lands her in the principal’s office. As the situation escalates and chaos ensues, Billie finds herself hiding in a bathroom stall, where the bracelet her mother has always made her wear falls off. She quickly learns that this piece of jewelry is the only thing that has been suppressing her magic.

After an argument with her mom, who is still reluctant to tell her the truth about her powers and identity, Billie gets sucked through a portal and plopped into a magical realm of witches called Hexe, filled with enchanted objects, exciting spells, and adorable familiars. Though it initially seems like a utopian place where Billie can finally fit in and be herself, Alice shows up and warns her that she is in grave danger. Her own mother, the selfish and cruel Queen Celeste (Jodie Foster), will not take kindly to a granddaughter coming in and threatening her status as Hexe’s sole savior. Billie and Alice are forced to put aside their differences and work together, along with Alice’s familiar, a three-eyed cat named Beef (Walton Goggins), to save each other — and the entire realm.

There has been some valid criticism in recent years about the way that animated films have done away with specifically trained voice actors in favor of Hollywood's biggest names. Luckily, Steinfeld (who is already beloved in the Spider-Verse series) and Jones prove themselves more than worthy of taking on these roles. Not only do they turn in great performances individually, but there’s a sense of real chemistry between the two, as well as a similar cadence and brand of quirkiness that makes their dynamic especially authentic and impactful. Though Foster could occasionally afford to go slightly bigger and campier, she turns in a satisfyingly juicy villainous turn that at times feels reminiscent of Donna Murphy’s Mother Gothel.

Four worlds and four completely different ideas of what a good life looks like. This is where you belong, what it would ask of you, and what you would get back in return.

You belong somewhere old enough that the mountains have names and somebody can still tell you who gave them. Middle-earth is for people who think the right thing is worth doing even when it will not be finished in their lifetime.

You want the sea, a crew, and absolutely nobody telling you where you are allowed to go. The Grand Line is the world for people whose main ambition in life is finding out what is over there.

You would rather be feared and useful than liked and useless. The Continent is a world where the monsters are the straightforward part of the job and the people are where it all gets complicated.

You want the myths to be real and loose in the actual world, and you want to find out at about thirteen that you were never anywhere near as ordinary as everybody kept telling you.

It helps that they have a solid script to work with. While many parents in children’s films are either written as one-dimensional and relegated to the sidelines or killed off entirely to further the young protagonists’ arcs, Hexed fleshes Alice out. Throughout the course of the movie, Billie learns to see Alice not just as her nagging mother but rather as a complex, nuanced individual with her own flaws, fears, and dreams. By highlighting the dynamics between Billie, Alice, and Celeste, the film does an impressive job of tackling themes of generational trauma and breaking cycles of abuse in a way that’s surprisingly sophisticated while still being age-appropriate.

When the Hexed trailer was first released, some viewers voiced criticism regarding the animation style and framing, which seemed primed to be cropped and put on TikTok. I can honestly say I didn’t have these complaints watching the full film, especially when the plot moves from real-world suburbia to Hexe. The magical realm itself is vibrant and immersive — it’s easy to understand Billie’s wonder and enthusiasm for this new environment. Details like the characters communicating via crystal balls instead of cell phones are a fun and clever touch, and there is some nice character design as well, particularly when it comes to Billie and Alice’s nail polish and jewelry.

The main issue with the animation lies in the fact that the camera moves around each scene so quickly that it’s difficult to truly take in and appreciate all the little details. Sometimes the film crosses the line from "lively" straight into "overstimulating." The design and characters are strong enough to stand on their own, but we rarely get a moment to breathe and take everything in, and instead are jolted from shot to shot or inundated with yet another out-of-place needle drop. No shade to Dua Lipa — all of her songs in this film are bangers — but unfortunately, they take you out of the story. One wishes that the directors trusted the audience to enjoy the story without manufacturing a frenetic, theme-park-like energy.

While the experience of watching Hexed can feel like a visual rollercoaster, following the film's plot sometimes has the opposite problem; its climax, in particular, gets a bit drawn-out and convoluted. While emotionally effective, the film's logic and character motivations aren’t always completely clear, which can muddle the stakes. Still, it’s pretty easy to forgive these issues due to some genuinely smart, unexpected twists and an abundance of heart that permeates throughout.

Hexed shares similarities with everything from The Wizard of Oz to Beauty and the Beast to Agatha All Along, though it mostly avoids feeling too derivative of other projects. Rather, it acts as a gateway for a younger audience to be exposed to this specific brand of magic, carving out its own place in the canon of witchy media. The excellent characters and their complicated dynamics, as well as the immersive world, are enough to cast a powerful spell.
