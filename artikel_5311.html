America’s defense budget is being squeezed to its lowest share of the federal budget since World War II as China narrows the military gap

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

America is approaching a “dangerous moment” for defense spending, according to a new report from Brookings. The think tank warns that rising outlays on Medicaid, Medicare, Social Security, and interest on the $40 trillion national debt are squeezing the military’s share of federal spending.The analysis, published at the start of this week, finds that since 1962 defense spending has been squeezed from 49% to 13% of the federal budget.Defense spending accounted for 2.8% of U.S. GDP in 2026 and is expected to remain at approximately 3% through 2030. This would put defense spending at its lowest share of GDP since World War II, said Brookings—a nonprofit public policy think tank based in D.C.

Projections that spending will remain relatively constrained are particularly concerning, Brookings’ Jessica Riedl writes. The U.S. is engaged in a conflict in the Middle East with Iran and its proxy groups, and Russia and Ukraine are engaged in a land war that has continued since Russia’s 2022 invasion.

Three years ago, the RAND National Security Research Division—a Pentagon research advisor—warned that “in many ways, China is outpacing the United States and has largely negated the U.S. military advantage in the Western Pacific through two decades of focused military investment.”

Without “significant change” in policy, the “balance of power will continue to shift in China’s favor,” the congressional report found.

And yet, in the years since, the factors stacking up against increased defense spending are only getting weightier.

The Congressional Budget Office’s latest roundup of government spending shows that $917 billion was spent on defense for fiscal year 2026, a 5.8% increase of $48 billion from the prior year.

The total for the government’s largest mandatory spending programs (Social Security benefits, Medicare, and Medicaid) came in at nearly $3.5 trillion, a $217 billion uptick on the year before and a 6.7% increase. The government ran a total deficit of $1.9 trillion, the CBO reported, adding to the current national debt burden of $40.7 trillion, per the latest Treasury data.

That debt comes at a cost. Interest payments rise as the government borrows more, particularly when borrowing costs remain elevated. The CBO said that for fiscal year 2026, the Treasury paid $1.14 trillion in net interest on the public debt—an 11.1% or $114 billion increase on the previous fiscal year.

Interest payments on the debt are a growing concern for D.C. policymakers. The U.S. Congress Joint Economic Committee (JEC) published a report in October that found interest payments in the future could consume more than half of available federal tax receipts by 2036.

JEC chairman David Schweikert noted: “Under this higher interest rate scenario, we could spend $23 trillion on interest alone over the next decade. That is spending not on Social Security, not on Medicare, not on national defense, but simply paying for yesterday’s borrowing.”

The next Congress faces difficult choices: The Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget has a countdown to when the combined trust funds of the Social Security system will be forced to start cutting benefits. The countdown currently stands at seven years and six months.

“The newly elected Congress—which controls the purse strings—faces difficult fiscal choices that will persist into the next presidential administration,” the Brookings report says. “Now, there is little headroom in the budget to increase defense spending to meet the proliferating defense challenges … Something will have to give. The advocates of a robust military and American global leadership should prepare to make the most of limited budgetary resources.”
