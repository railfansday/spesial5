A single brain signal may explain schizophrenia's most puzzling symptoms

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

Scientists have identified a brain signal that may help explain why schizophrenia produces such a wide variety of symptoms, including difficulties with perception, movement, and speech. The discovery suggests that problems that appear unrelated could actually stem from a shared disruption in how the brain processes information.

Schizophrenia is a complex psychiatric disorder that can affect how people experience the world, interact with their surroundings, and communicate with others. These symptoms are typically studied as separate features of the condition, but new research suggests they may have more in common than previously recognized.

The study, published in Science Advances, was conducted by scientists from McGill University, the McConnell Brain Imaging Centre, the University of Montreal, the Research Centre of the University of Montreal Health Centre, and the Douglas Research Centre. Their findings point to a common brain mechanism that may help explain several distinct symptoms of schizophrenia.

A Weakened Brain Signal Links Multiple Schizophrenia Symptoms

To investigate this connection, researchers examined beta-band brain activity in 25 people diagnosed with schizophrenia and 25 healthy participants. Beta-band activity refers to rhythmic patterns of electrical activity in the brain that are involved in coordinating information processing, perception, and movement.

Participants completed tasks designed to test how accurately they could judge the timing of visual and auditory information. The researchers also analyzed their speech to look for signs of disorganized thinking and communication.

The results revealed a striking pattern. People with schizophrenia showed weaker and slower beta-band activity than those in the control group. More importantly, the participants with the most pronounced abnormalities in this brain signal also tended to have greater difficulty processing the timing of sights and sounds, more disorganized speech, and more severe clinical symptoms.

These three features of schizophrenia are usually investigated independently. Finding that they were all associated with changes in the same brain signal suggests that a shared underlying process may contribute to the disorder's diverse effects.

A New Window Into How the Brain Processes Reality

"This gives us early but compelling evidence that measuring the brain's operations, especially how it builds and revises its own model of the world, may help us appreciate that there is a common thread behind the confusing array of symptoms we see in disorders like schizophrenia," said Dr. Lena Palaniyappan, senior author of the study, Professor of Psychiatry at McGill and director of the Centre of Excellence in Youth Mental Health at the Douglas Hospital.

The brain constantly uses incoming sensory information to develop and update its understanding of the surrounding environment. This process helps people interpret what they see and hear, anticipate events, and coordinate their actions. The new findings suggest that disruptions in this process could help explain why schizophrenia affects several different aspects of behavior and perception.

The researchers used high-resolution magnetoencephalography, or MEG, to measure brain activity. This imaging technique detects the tiny magnetic fields generated by electrical activity in the brain, allowing scientists to track changes in neural signals with exceptional precision. The scans were performed at the McConnell Brain Imaging Centre at the Montreal Neurological Institute-Hospital (The Neuro).

"What struck us was that the same weakened brain signal kept showing up wherever we looked, whether patients were processing a sound, a sight or completing a movement," said Hsi (Tiana) Wei, lead author of the study and postdoctoral scholar in McGill's Department of Psychiatry and the Douglas Research Centre. "Seeing the same pattern across these different domains suggested there may be a common underlying process linking the symptoms."

Could the Discovery Help Detect Psychosis Earlier?

Beyond providing new insight into schizophrenia, the findings could eventually help researchers identify young people who are at greater risk of developing psychosis. Psychosis involves a disruption in how someone perceives or interprets reality and can include experiences such as hallucinations or delusions.

Early identification is particularly important because the first signs of schizophrenia often appear during adolescence but may go unrecognized for an extended period.

"The very first sign of schizophrenia begins in our adolescence, but this often gets missed for a long time. If this finding is confirmed in larger samples, we may have a potentially useful method to identify who, among youth seeking help for mental health, needs extra support to prevent psychosis," Palaniyappan said.

However, the researchers emphasized that the results are preliminary. The study included only 50 participants, and additional research involving larger, independent groups will be necessary to determine how reliably the brain signal is associated with speech disturbances and overall symptom severity.

"This is a first, modest-size study, and the associations with speech and clinical severity will need to be tested in larger independent samples before we can be confident in them," Wei said.

If future studies confirm the findings, measuring beta-band activity could potentially become a useful tool for identifying people who need earlier support. Wei also suggested that the brain signal may deserve investigation as a possible target for future schizophrenia treatments.
