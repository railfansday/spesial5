How to Be an Adult – 16 Money-Saving Life Skills, One Step Each

Being an adult comes down to a short list of skills nobody teaches in one place, and most of them save you money, from the bank account to the kitchen. Learn them one at a time, starting this week with whichever one cost you money last month, and put its one step in place.

On the 2026 TIAA Institute-GFLEC Personal Finance Index, U.S. adults answered an average of 47% of its 28 personal finance questions correctly, the lowest score in the survey’s 10 years, and Gen Z averaged 38%.

Each skill you’re missing shows up as a charge or a loss, such as an overdraft fee, a month of credit card interest, a security deposit kept for damage you didn’t cause, or a 401(k) match you never collected. The 16 skills below each come with the one step that prevents that cost.

Your paycheck is what’s left of your pay after taxes, health insurance premiums, and retirement contributions come out, so it will be smaller than your salary divided by the number of paydays. Check every stub’s hours, pay rate, and deductions against your offer letter and the benefits you picked, because payroll errors repeat every pay period until someone catches them.

Overdraft coverage lets a debit card purchase or ATM withdrawal go through when your balance can’t cover it, for a fee. Banks can’t charge that fee on one-time debit card purchases or ATM withdrawals unless you opted in, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau says, though banks can still charge one on checks and recurring electronic payments. Use a free checking account, or one that waives its monthly fee for something you’ll do anyway, like getting paid by direct deposit, and say no to overdraft coverage, or cancel it if you already said yes.

Set every fixed bill to autopay from checking, and ask each company to move your due date to a few days after payday, so the money is there when the payment goes out. A late payment reported to the credit bureaus can generally stay on your credit report for seven years, on top of any late fee.

Most cards come with a grace period, the days between your statement’s closing date and its due date, and paying the full statement balance within it means no interest on your purchases. Paying less brings interest on the unpaid part and on new purchases from the day you make them. Set the card’s autopay to the statement balance, not the minimum payment.

Lenders and landlords read your credit report, so a mistake on it can cost you a lower rate or an apartment. Pull your free weekly reports from Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion at AnnualCreditReport.com, and dispute any account you didn’t open or late payment you didn’t make with the bureau that lists it and the company that reported it.

File a federal return every year your employer withholds income tax, even if you earned too little to owe any, because filing is how you get that money back. With income under the IRS Free File limit, you can prepare and file it free through one of the program’s partner companies.

Each month, list your take-home pay, subtract rent and the other fixed bills, set aside your savings next, and split what’s left among food, transportation, and fun. At the end of the month, the gap between the plan and what you actually spent shows where to move money next time.

Open a high-yield savings account separate from checking, and schedule an automatic transfer for every payday, even if it’s $25. The transfers build a starter emergency fund for surprise bills, so a car repair doesn’t go on a credit card, and keep growing it toward several months of expenses.

On move-in day, take dated move-in photos and video of every room, inside the appliances and closets included, and email them to your landlord. The same walkthrough at move-out gives you proof of the condition you left the place in, against any deduction for damage you didn’t cause.

More than half of U.S. food spending, 56% in 2025, went to food away from home, such as restaurants and takeout, according to the USDA’s Economic Research Service. Learn five cheap dinners you can make without a recipe, in batches big enough that the leftovers cover lunch.

Plan the week’s dinners, write the list from those recipes plus the staples you’re out of, and buy only what’s on it. The unit price on the shelf tag, the cost per ounce or per item, shows which size is cheaper, and it isn’t always the bigger package.

Wash most loads in cold water, and air-dry anything you can’t risk shrinking. Heating water takes about 90% of the energy a clothes washer uses, according to Energy Star, and cold water cleans most loads well unless they have oily stains.

If a parent’s plan covers dependents, you can usually remain on it until age 26. On a parent’s job-based plan, that generally holds even if you move out or turn down coverage from your own job. When you choose your own plan, compare the monthly premium with the deductible, what you pay for covered care before the plan starts paying, and confirm your doctors take the plan and it covers your prescriptions. Some plans don’t cover care from doctors outside their network, the providers they contract with, except in an emergency, and others charge more for it.

Your landlord’s insurance covers the building, not the things you own. Buy renters insurance with enough coverage to replace everything you own, and on your car, carry more coverage for injuries and damage you cause others than your state requires. A renters policy typically also covers you if you’re sued for injuring someone or damaging their property. If you cause a crash that costs more than your car policy’s limits, you cover the rest yourself, the Insurance Information Institute warns.

Find your employer’s match formula in your benefits portal, and as soon as you’re eligible, contribute at least enough to get the full match. If your employer matches 50 cents per dollar on the first 6% of pay, then on a $45,000 salary, putting in 6%, or $2,700 a year, brings in $1,350 from your employer. Putting in 3% leaves $675 of that match unclaimed.

Before you accept an offer or ask for a raise, look up what your job pays where you live in the Bureau of Labor Statistics’ wage data, which breaks pay out by occupation, state, and metro area. Those figures, along with the pay ranges in job postings for the same role, give you a number to ask for that isn’t tied to what you earn now.

To find where to start, pull up last month’s bank and credit card statements and add up every fee and interest charge. Fix the biggest one first with the skill above that prevents it.
