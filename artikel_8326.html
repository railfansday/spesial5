The Paradox of Seeking First Ascents in Western Nepal

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Western Nepal has plenty of unclimbed 6,000m peaks, and Fredrik Strang of Sweden and Sindre Kolbjornsgard of Norway went there planning to climb one. In the end, they settled for a slightly lower but equally memorable experience. It showed again that adventure goes beyond first ascents.

What I remember most clearly about that narrow summit is not the grade, the exposure, or the possibility of being first. It is the silence. No helicopters. No voices from a fixed-rope queue. No machinery, no aircraft, no distant sound of a settlement. Only the two of us, a serrated horizon, and an unfamiliar feeling that the mountain had asked a question rather than supplied an answer.

Sindre Kolbjornsgard, left, and Fredrik Strang. Photo: Fredrik Strang

After 18 expeditions to 8,000’ers and witnessing the evolution (or devolution) of climbing on these peaks’ normal routes, Strang and his partner knew they wanted something different. Quoting Ed Viesturs, Strang said: “The point is to climb, not just to summit.” With that in mind, the climbers set four criteria for their objectives:

Several route options face the climbers’ base camp tent. Photo: Fredrik Strang

Strang noted that there are many unclimbed peaks left in Nepal, but they were looking for a compelling climb, not just a dull summit for the record. They finally found one that satisfied their criteria, but then, as with so many other teams in Nepal this fall, harsh weather put their original objective out of reach. Not to mention that traveling Western Nepal is anything but simple.

“Our intended destination was in the Nyalu Lek area north of Simikot,” Strang revealed. “We planned to cross Nyalu La, a pass around 5,000m, with a support party that included porters and pack animals.

“Getting from Surkhet to Simikot took two rough days by road, with landslides having damaged or obliterated sections of the route. At Simikot, we learned that Nyalu La was probably impassable. Even reaching the place where we planned to start trekking had been uncertain.”

When they did reach the area, they confirmed that their planned peak was not an option. Hiking further down the valley, they pitched their tent at Phendol Kermi Camp. Snow had covered the trails beyond, but they simply had no time or money to move to some other region in Nepal. Instead, they decided to explore whatever was close to their camp.

During their stay, they encountered Mick Fowler and Victor Saunders of the UK. Bad weather had likewise spoiled the British pair’s plan to attempt an unclimbed 6,000m peak farther east, known only as Peak 6010, according to Mount Everest Foundation.. This would have been their second attempt on that peak. Ironically, dry conditions spoiled their 2025 attempt. This time, too much snow forced them back. On October 6, Strang and Kolbjornsgard made their first acclimatization climb on a 5,177m peak at 30°20′49.82″N, 82°03′54.82″E. They found no records of any previous ascent.

“From base camp, the mountain looked straightforward, and lower down, it largely was,” Strang recalls. “Steep grass, mud, and broken rock led toward the upper slopes. But at about 4,500m, the route disappeared beneath knee-deep snow, then waist-deep snow, and every step became a small excavation.”

In the following days, they scouted and flew their drone down side valleys, looking for possibilities. Their second peak was 5,227m in altitude, located at 30°07′50.86″N, 81°41′56.58″E. Again, there were no records of previous ascents. Parts of it were far from fun.

“Below 4,500m, progress was tolerable, but above that, we were back to forcing our way through knee-deep, sometimes chest-deep snow,” Strang wrote. “A tremendous amount of work for very little enjoyment.”

Luckily, the climb improved once they reached the summit ridge, on relatively easy mixed rock and ice, which they assessed at PD+.

“Fowler and Saunders climbed the mountain several days later and found an easier way to descend and reascend the step on its western side,” Strang noted. “Their solution was a reminder that the first line a team takes is not necessarily the best one.”

Still, the peak that most drew the climbers’ attention was a remarkable 5,337m tooth of rock above Yangchya Lake at approximately 30°07′09.36″N, 81°41′21.15″E.

“We began calling it Daara Peak — a name inspired by the Nepali word for a projecting tooth or fang and by the mountain’s striking shape,” Strang said. He notes that the name is provisional while they await a response from the authorities about their suggestion.

Strang describes the peak as “a saber-toothed tiger’s canine protruding from a mass of rock. The terminal pinnacle was about eight meters high, overhanging and sharply defined against the sky.”

The climbers about to reach what they called Daara Peak. Drone photo: Brizendra Bhattarai

The problem was approaching the peak in deep, heavy snow from Yangchya Lake. They failed on their first attempt to tackle its northeast headwall. After waiting out another snowy spell, the climbers returned for a second attempt on October 17-18.

The climbers dealt with 250m of rather poor rock, a complex approach to the headwall across a bergschrund with soft snow, and the uncertainty of moving on unfamiliar terrain. They had no idea of the difficulties ahead or whether they could protect the passages ahead on their way to the summit. The route included vertical granite walls, with cracks adequate for cams, some mixed terrain with the crux at M6, a steep couloir, and then the summit ridge: a granite edge with some 30cm of snow on top.

Climbing terrain on Daara Peak. Photo: Fredrik Strang

Rather than technical difficulty, the section was delicate due to exposure and a lack of comfortable belay stations. Yet, the climbers reached the summit on October 18, including the top of the final pinnacle, thanks to a crack in the rock.

Strang admits that the climbing details suggest a rather straightforward ascent: a 5,337m summit via its northeast side in five pitches with difficulties up to M6. However, Strang judges the climb on a different scale. “The technical crux was behind us, but it was the part I remember most vividly,” he explained. “It was the silence.”

Strang and Kolbjornsgard’s route up Daara Peak. Photo: Fredrik Strang

On many of the 8,000m peaks I have climbed, the human presence has become part of the landscape: camps, radio calls, fixed ropes, queues, and the distant sound of aircraft. Up here, none of that reached us. Mountains extended in every direction. There was no audience, no ceremony, no one to confirm that we had accomplished anything at all.

Strang also reflects on the paradox: They enjoyed the wild terrain but, if every climber chases solitude by visiting these last remote valleys, the character of these places will inevitably change. For Strang, the question is not to ban access. However, he says:

Perhaps some summits should remain untouched — not because we lack the skill or courage to reach them, but because future generations deserve to inherit mountains that have not all been reduced to objectives.
