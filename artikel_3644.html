Inside PS, The Private Members-Only Terminals at LAX, Miami, and Paris Airports

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

There are 10 terminals at the Los Angeles International Airport. One through nine are for the general public: terminal one for Southwest, terminal two for Virgin Atlantic, the international hub in the numberless terminal B. Each one is packed with weary, edgy travelers lined up for security, coffee, or a meal. You arrive at your terminal via rideshare and depart via rideshare—so long as your ride is a premium black car. If it isn’t, you must take a 20-minute bus ride to a parking lot called “LAX-it,” where you wait amongst the hordes for your UberX to arrive as it slowly inches through crippling traffic.

The 10th terminal promises to deliver you from this misery. It’s around the back of the airport complex, off the 105 in El Segundo, next to several cargo holds and a Gate Gourmet facility where airplane meals are carted off to various jets on the tarmac. When you arrive, no one asks for a boarding pass. A security guard checks your name off a list, and a valet whisks away your bags with care before you’re ushered into your own private suite with amenities galore: squishy velvet couches and boucle chairs, Assouline coffee table books, free iPhone chargers, and chocolate-covered almonds and gummy bears. A fridge comes with sparkling waters and canned matcha drinks—a far cry from the plastic Dasani bottles found at Hudson News. A private bathroom boasts Le Labo soaps.

While you’re waiting for your flight, you can get a manicure, a table massage, or even a haircut. You can request a pair of Normatec Elite compression boots (retail price: $1,099) to help with your circulation before takeoff. Or if you are feeling more social, you can head to “the Salon,” where abstract art hangs on the wall and chandeliers dangle from the ceiling. Order the coconut chia pudding or bagel-and-lox platter if you’re there for breakfast, duck confit agnolotti or a Mediterranean chicken panini on house-made ciabatta for lunch. For dinner, there’s a New York strip, and caviar service is always available.

When it’s time to go, you’ll pass through a private security check with a dedicated TSA agent. Then you’ll climb in a BMW (suitcases already loaded) which drives you across the tarmac directly to your plane. Forget boarding with Zone 8 or gatechecking your carry-on.

The Salon at PS at LAX, designed by Elle Decor A-List interior designer Cliff Fong.

This is PS at LAX—a private, members only terminal at Los Angeles International. It’ll cost you to get in: One-time access to the Salon (and nothing else) without a membership is priced at $1,295. For frequent fliers, an annual baseline membership starts at $1,250 and goes up to $4,850. Private suites cost an additional $3,650 or $4,950 for four people, depending on the type of membership.

Unsurprisingly, it’s become a see-and-don’t-be-seen hub for LA celebrities wary of attracting the camera-wielding masses at commercial airports (and the paparazzi that stake out the terminal). Gigi Hadid, Gabrielle Union, Hailey Bieber, Jamie Foxx, and Kourtney Kardashian are just some of the famous faces that are known to frequent PS at LAX.

But this year, PS is pushing beyond the Hollywood stars eager to escape the clutches of Deuxmoi. In the past six months, the firm has opened private terminals in Miami (taking over the historic Pan-Am space) and Dallas. Paris is coming in early 2027. The seclusion, the gourmet food, and the luxurious interiors (curated by Elle Decor A-List designers Jean Liu for Dallas and Cliff Fong for Miami and LA) are all nice draws, but there's another reason PS CEO Amina Belouizdad Porter cites for why customers are willing to shell out thousands of dollars on top of a first-class ticket.

The Atlanta airport on March 23, 2026. Government shutdowns have caused disastrous scenes at American commercial terminals.

Because, well, it has been. The last fiscal year has seen three government shutdowns, the longest of which lasted for 76 days. TSA Deputy Administrator Ha Nguyen McNeill told Congress that the agency experienced the longest security wait times in its 24-year history during the shutdown that stretched from February 14 to April 30, 2026. Some airports, including Atlanta, had lines up to five hours long. The logistical meltdowns hit at a time when air travel has never been more popular: In 2025, nearly 907 million people flew commercially in the United States. Belouizdad Porter says that those disruptions led to a direct increase in PS customers.

The growing crowds at airport lounges also nudged some travelers over the edge. Credit card companies long ago realized that offering lounge access enticed new customers, with Chase, American Express, Citi, Capital One, and more getting in the game. But demand exceeded square footage, and many of those lounges suffered from overcrowding and subpar service, selling the dream of an aero oasis that never was.

“There have never been more airport lounges,” David Mack writes in the New York Times, “Yet there also have never seemed to be more lounges that are not worth the hassle…Sometimes the lines for the lounges are the longest in the airport.”

(Though the only thing worse than an overcrowded airport lounge might be getting stranded outside. Reddit is currently up in arms about the obscene food prices at the newly renovated Burbank Airport in California—price tag: $1.3 billion—where a cheese and bean burrito costs $25.)

Another contributing factor? The rising costs of private jets. While the terms “one percent” or “.1 percent” are often thrown around when it comes to PJ owners, the reality is only 0.003 percent of America’s population can afford even fractional ownership. Demand for private aircraft surged after the pandemic, raising prices, and now the average net worth of those who own a jet is $190 million. For those with fractional ownership, it’s $140 million. Yet there are still plenty of mere decamillionaires looking to dodge the misery of the airport. As Sartre put it: Hell is other people.

Previously, “You could either fly round-trip first class for $6,000 but deal with the airport, the friction, and the crowd,” Belouizdad Porter says. “Or you could fly private round-trip and it's $200,000.” PS operates somewhere in between. “I think there’s a real appetite for that middle ground: the convenience of private aviation without the commitment or expense of chartering an entire plane,” says Lucie Kittel, founder of luxury travel advisory firm Domino Travel.

And besides, even if you do reach the level of private jet ownership, you still can’t always avoid the flying masses. A midsize jet that fits six to nine people costs between $10 and $20 million dollars and can fly 2,000 to 3,000 nautical miles. If it’s on the shorter end of that range, it can just make a trip from New York to California—but not to Europe. There’s also the issue of safety: The country is currently facing a pilot shortage, and more experienced candidates often work with mainstream airlines who can provide higher salaries. Larger planes can also better navigate rough weather.

Carriers like United and Delta know all of this, which is why they’re beefing up their luxury experiences. Delta recently announced it was investing over $1 billion in retrofitting its premium cabins, and new Delta One Terminals at JFK and LAX allow first-class ticket holders to avoid overcrowded TSA lines and the standard lounge. “Most of my clients flying out of JFK are choosing Delta One,” Kittel adds, “and they love the experience.”

Thanks to the artificial intelligence boom—the latest phase of a 15-year bull market for stocks—there’s been an explosion of multi-multi-millionaires in America. While not all of them can fly private, they certainly can devote large sums of money to separating themselves from the hoi polloi. Both startups like PS and established players are now competing for that sub-ultra-wealthy dollar.
