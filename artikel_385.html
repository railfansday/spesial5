El Niño Is Driving a Record Marine Heat Wave Off the California Coast

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

For the past 20 years, Daniel Rudnick has been collecting data from below the ocean’s surface. He’s never seen anything like what’s been coming back for the past few months, as a super El Niño fuels record heat off the California coast. The marine heat wave is affecting wildlife and helping to juice the record-setting storms hitting the state at the start of the rainy season.

The oceanographer helps to pilot small underwater drones known as gliders that dive thousands of feet below the surface and crawl their way back up, collecting data on salinity, temperature, and other metrics.

A color-coded chart showing temperature anomalies at different depths in the ocean—red for hotter, blue for colder—had to be readjusted because the red shades weren’t intense enough to accurately reflect just how hot things are, even hundreds of meters below the surface. Steric height—changes to sea levels driven by heat rather than melting ice—has reached the highest levels Rudnick has ever seen.

“I looked back at our data to make sure that I'm not making a mistake and it's correct—and it is,” Rudnick, who works at the Scripps Institution of Oceanography at UC San Diego, says.

In Monterey Bay, a station recorded a temperature of 64.72 degrees Fahrenheit (18.18 degrees Celsius) at a depth of 200 feet below the water’s surface in late October, breaking previous records. Other data shows even hotter water in San Diego at the same depth: 77 degrees Fahrenheit, an anomaly of 21.3 degrees Fahrenheit above normal.

El Niño “is the biggest source of climate variability” off the California coast, Rudnick says. The climate phenomenon is characterized by hotter-than-normal water in the eastern tropical Pacific and affects weather and oceans around the globe. His gliders have captured temperature anomalies across other events, including intense heat during the 2015–2016 super El Niño. But this year’s El Niño is shaping up to be the strongest on record, and its impacts are equally supersized.

“It’s fair to say it’s off the charts,” he says.

This year’s El Niño is adding heat to an already-simmering ocean. Since January 2025, coastal California waters have been hit by a massive heat wave, with temperatures in some places reaching 8 degrees Fahrenheit above average in August. Add in human-caused climate change—oceans off Southern California have warmed 1.8 degrees Fahrenheit since the 1970s alone—and it makes for unprecedented conditions now.

“This is a bunch of things that have had to come together to make this even physically possible,” Daniel Swain, a climate scientist at the University of California Agriculture and Natural Resources, says. “And even then, it's kind of astonishing.”

Rudnick agrees. “The heat we get from El Niño stacking on top of the heat we already have, that’s the thing that makes this most unusual,” he says.

Part of what’s driving the higher ocean temperatures and sea levels off California are what’s known as Kelvin waves. These slow, large waves transport warm water from west to east across the Pacific. They in turn push down the ocean’s thermocline, a transition layer into the deeper ocean where temperatures begin to decrease rapidly.

“It isn't like there's some heater in the deep ocean suddenly,” Swain says. “It's just that we've turned off the air conditioner and we're allowing the surface waters to warm.”

Kelvin waves can also help to raise sea levels along the California coast by “keeping the water from moving physically offshore,” Swain says. While Kelvin waves are imperceptible to the average person, their impacts aren’t.

Californians have been living through several weeks of intense coastal flooding and big waves enhanced by these higher sea levels. San Francisco and the surrounding area are under a coastal flood advisory through Friday morning, and waves have reached over 30 feet (9 meters) in height and even set off seismographs. (Another storm is set to hit the Bay Area this weekend.) It’s also contributing to heavier rainfall, with locations from San Francisco to Burbank setting hourly rainfall records in recent days.

But the warmer water itself is also contributing to this temporary sea level rise, thanks to simple physics: Warmer water takes up more space, helping push up sea levels.

The warm water is driving weather even more unprecedented than the intense storms. On Tuesday, two rare twin waterspouts formed over San Francisco Bay, the result of cold storm air moving over unusually warm waters. These phenomena are generally seen off the coast of Florida or the Mediterranean—not in the usually frigid waters of the Pacific.

“The air temperatures are somewhat winter-like right now, but the water ain't,” Swain says. “It’s an indicator that things aren’t as they usually are in this part of the world.”
