Best Open Earbuds (2026): Shokz, Bose, Soundcore, and More

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Open earbuds are the big new thing in headphone land. Unlike in-ear variants, open earbuds are like tiny speakers that rest on the ear and let outside sound in naturally, so you can fully hear the world around you. Some use a hook style that wraps around the back of your ear, while others are clip-ons that look more like statement-piece earrings. Style aside, they are excellent for anyone who finds traditional earbuds uncomfortable or wants full environmental awareness for activities like running, biking, or even just doing chores around the house.

I’ve tested dozens of models since 2022, and while I still recommend noise-canceling earbuds, I also love open earbuds for specific use cases.

They’re clip-ons that wrap around the earlobe, with the speaker end resting at the ear canal opening and the other end behind the ear. Importantly, the top of your ear is free for glasses, sunglasses, or hats. Like nearly all open earbuds, they let in sound from your surroundings, so don’t expect them to be a great companion for long-haul flights or daily gym sessions.

The physical button controls on each earbud are much easier to use than touch-sensitive alternatives, especially when running or exercising outdoors. The charging case is surprisingly small, smaller than most other options on this list. And, importantly, the call quality is great. These buds use the same AI-powered chip as the Soundcore Liberty 5 Pro, and while they don’t sound quite as good, they are noticeably better than other open earbuds, especially in noisy environments.

The best part? They cost less than many similar competitors I tested.

The Bose Ultra Open Earbuds were a big deal when they launched in 2024—a brand-new design from Bose and one of the biggest swings from a major brand in clip-on open earbuds. Two years later, the brand launched the second-generation version, and they are a solid, if expensive, option. That’s mainly because of sound quality—these earbuds sound clearer and more defined than most competitors. Another differentiator is physical button controls on each bud, which I find easier to use than touch alternatives, especially when running or biking.

Like the Soundcore and Shokz earbuds, they are very comfortable, so much so that I sometimes forgot I am wearing them. That’s one reason I love this $50 option—the PearClip Pros aren’t super pricey and are a great way to find out if you even like open earbuds in the first place, especially the kind that clip onto the outside of your ear.

They are far less refined than Shokz or Soundcore. Things like the app and touch controls can be a little finicky. But that’s common at this super-low price point, and you can always upgrade to a more premium earbud if you fall in love with the clip-on form factor.

The Shokz OpenRun Pro 2 are more like headphones than earbuds—with a wraparound headband, long battery life, great sound quality (for open headphones), and no charging case. They use a combination of bone conduction (think vibrations) and air conduction (think tiny speakers pointed toward your ears) to deliver clearer sound than most smaller open earbuds, especially in noisy environments. That combination of technologies is why these are some of my favorite earbuds for outdoor fitness activities like running and biking.

I do wish these headphones were a little cheaper. I’m currently testing the recently released, more affordable Shokz OpenRun Air 2, and I’m hopeful they can be a budget-friendly alternative.

JLab Epic Open Sport for $100: JLab’s Epic Open Sport stands out for its controls: the JLab emblem is a touch sensor, and each earbud also has physical buttons on top, making it a breeze to control. EarFun Clip for $70: For around $70, these are a solid pick for anyone on a budget. Suunto Spark for $179: The Suunto Spark has a similar story to the Suunto Wing 2: it's built for athletes and syncs with Suunto fitness watches, but the similarly priced Shokz Openfit 2 outclasses it in the areas that matter (comfort, sound quality, battery).

What Are Open Earbuds and How Do They Sound?

At a glance, open earbuds seem a lot like typical wireless earbuds. The big difference is that, instead of going inside your ear canal, they rest somewhere on the outside of your ear, leaving it open so you can easily hear the world around you. I use them all the time for outdoor exercise like running or biking, where I want my music but also need to hear other runners, bikers, and cars to stay safe.

Because they leave your ears open and the sound comes from farther away (outside your ear rather than inside), audio performance isn’t as clear, intimate, or bassy as regular earbuds. They also typically lack noise-canceling features, so they are not a great fit for the office or air travel.

Open earbuds are perfect for outdoor activities or when you want to talk to other people without taking off your headphones. When I run or go for a bike ride, I only use open earbuds so I can hear my surroundings and stay safe. I also like using them around the house when I am cooking or doing laundry.

Semi-open earbuds, like Apple’s AirPods, sit inside the ear but don’t have eartips that create a sound seal. They can offer better audio quality than other open earbuds because the speakers and microphones are closer to your ear and mouth. Semi-open earbuds are comfortable, but they’re usually pretty loose—they can fall out easily and may be a poor option for fitness activities.

I test open earbuds like I test any pair of headphones: in the real world, in my daily life. I wear each pair for at least a few weeks to test comfort, battery life, ease of use, and onboard controls. I listen to podcasts, watch TV shows, jam out to my favorite playlists, and call friends.

With open earbuds in particular, I also wear them on jogs, bike rides, and errand runs to get a better feel for how stable they are on my ears. I don’t expect them to cancel any noise, so I don’t test them on airplanes or loud commutes—they aren’t really made for that. My main audio source is Spotify Lossless, but, when possible, I test other formats and codecs as well.
