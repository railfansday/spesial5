Hearts boss Vrancken is playing with fire by turning the guns on his own players

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

By CALUM CROWE, SCOTTISH DEPUTY CHIEF SPORTS WRITER

Published: 12:17 EST, 10 November 2026 | Updated: 12:26 EST, 10 November 2026

Not for the first time this season under Wouter Vrancken, Hearts suffered a complete and utter implosion as they were thrashed 5-2 at home to Falkirk last weekend.

This is a team who lost 6-0 to Sturm Graz over two legs in the Champions League qualifiers back at the start of the campaign.

A team who were then battered 6-1 away against Benfica in Lisbon in the Europa League qualifiers – and a team who were also thumped 7-1 against Celtic at Parkhead a few weeks ago.

Hearts also lost the derby to Hibs just over a week ago in the semi-finals of the Premier Sports Cup at Hampden.

Now comes an annihilation against Falkirk where Hearts were 4-0 down by half-time. What a shambolic way to lose a long unbeaten record at home.

Even accounting for what had gone before in some of those matches this season, what happened on Sunday at Tynecastle felt like a new low.

Hearts were a shambles. But what was even more alarming was the manager's inability to stop the bleeding and make any changes from the touchline.

All of which poses a very simple question. Have Hearts and Jamestown made a mistake with Vrancken? At the moment, he looks hopelessly one-dimensional.

But it was his post-match comments on Sunday afternoon which actually became even more alarming than the performance itself.

Wouter Vrancken was 'embarrassed' by Hearts' 5-2 home loss to Falkirk

Vrancken branded it 'embarrassing' and a 'disgrace'. He called his players 'childish' and basically insinuated that they lacked any spirit or character until the youngsters came off the bench.

In the midst of a horrendous injury crisis and with players dropping like flies, that is a very dangerous game for any manager to play. He threw them under a bus.

By publicly slaughtering his players like that during a time of adversity and when many of them are being asked to play out of position, he is playing with fire.

Those are the type of comments that could very easily lose a dressing room. His comments would have gone down like a lead balloon among the squad.

Hearts have had the best part of a dozen players missing through injury. Vrancken himself has branded it an injury crisis unlike any other he has experienced throughout his career.

But what is most curious in all of this is that Vrancken seems totally unwilling to engage in any self-reflection throughout all of this.

After the shellacking at Celtic Park a few weeks ago, he lamented the fact his team had made too many individual errors.

There was no acknowledgment that his system had set them up to fail. No acceptance that pressing Celtic man-for-man and leaving players one-v-one at the back was a huge part of the problem.

Since he arrived in the summer, Vrancken's preferred style of play has been front-foot attacking football. There have been times when it has clicked reasonably well.

When he had his key players fit, Hearts were a good watch. But when the guts of a full team were then ripped out due to injury, it has quickly disintegrated into chaos.

Continuing to demand the same high-pressing and attacking style of play without meaningful adjustment is not principled. It is naive, inflexible, and tactically illiterate.

When the structure collapses, as it did against Falkirk and in the 7-1 defeat at Celtic, the emphasis returns to individual mistakes rather than collective vulnerability created by the system itself.

It is a classic example of a manager publicly hanging his players out to dry rather than choosing to accept his own part in the wider failings.

Hearts are not in crisis yet. They still sit fourth in the table, just a point behind Falkirk. But this is a team who have clearly regressed from where they were last season.

Vrancken was hired on the basis that he could bring a more dynamic style of play and move the team forward from the platform left behind by Derek McInnes.

As written in these pages a few weeks ago after the defeat to Celtic, there is absolutely no prospect whatsoever of this Hearts team challenging for the title this time around.

The current combination of a horrendous injury list, a rigid tactical approach and increasingly pointed public criticism of the players is a dangerous mixture that threatens to undermine everything.

Scottish football's fixture congestion, the demands of European competition and the physical toll of the season have exposed the limits of Vrancken's one-dimensional plan.

Adaptation is not a betrayal of principles. It is a basic requirement of the job when circumstances change this dramatically.

The international break offers a brief window. A handful of bodies may return for Hearts' next fixture against Dundee at Dens Park.

The more important return would be a change in tone and a willingness to adapt the demands placed on those players left still standing.

Publicly branding his players as 'a disgrace' while refusing to acknowledge that the system itself is contributing to their vulnerability is a sure-fire way to get himself sacked.

Vrancken's Hearts squad has been ravaged by injuries this season

The manager is entitled to be angry. He is entitled to demand better. He is not entitled to ignore the context he is operating in or the effect his words will have on a group already stretched to breaking point.

Vrancken arrived with a clear identity and early promise. The test now is whether he can evolve that identity under pressure or whether he remains locked into one way of playing regardless of the resources available.

Hearts cannot afford the latter. Much more of this public slaughtering and the players will switch off. Vrancken will have lost them completely.

Players will run for a manager who shows understanding of their situation even while criticising their standards.

They will begin to switch off for one who appears to place the preservation of his methods above the protection of the group.

That is the risk Vrancken is running. It is a risk no manager in the middle of a crippling injury crisis can afford to take.
