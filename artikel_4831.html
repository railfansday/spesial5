The 10 Best Fitness Trackers for Women, Tested and Reviewed by Editors

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

Finding the right fitness tracker is less about buying the most advanced wearable out there and more about choosing the one that will actually work for how you’ll use it to help you meet your fitness goals. You may want a smartwatch that can handle calls and texts along with fitness data tracking, or maybe you’re looking for a distraction-free screenless band that quietly tracks your metrics in the background. If running is your thing, you may want a smartwatch with GPS accuracy and real-time training metrics that are easy to read during a run, and if you’re focused on improving sleep, you may prefer a smart ring that won’t feel bulky overnight.

For this guide, we considered a range of fitness trackers for different training styles and tracking needs, from simple bands for everyday activity tracking to running watches, smartwatches, screenless wearables, and smart rings, all designed to monitor sleep, recovery, fitness, and health trends over time.

Whoop earned the top spot for the best overall fitness tracker because it provides deeper understanding of your data while also giving context of what all that data means. I’ve been using a Whoop for the past several years, and of all the fitness tracker options I’ve tested, it remains my hands-down favorite. It delivers some of the most useful recovery, sleep, and strain insights in a distraction-free design that’s so comfortable I often completely forget that I’m wearing it. The battery life is impressive, and I only have to charge mine twice a month.

Whoop 5.0 is built on the foundation of an app that offers analysis of your metrics paired with context, so you actually learn what all the numbers mean for your health and training. The screen-free design means that instead of buzzing constantly or encouraging you to check another display, it quietly tracks your metrics in the background and turns that data into recovery, strain, sleep data to give you an overall picture of your fitness within an app.

The sensor platform uses multi-wavelength optical heart rate sensors, skin-temperature sensing, blood oxygen monitoring, and accelerometer and gyroscope motion sensors to collect data continuously, tracking your resting heart rate, heart rate variability, respiratory rate, blood oxygen, skin temperature, sleep stages, strain, and recovery.

The app then interprets those numbers, in a way that feels more actionable than raw data alone, to generate a daily readiness score that indicates how much physical exertion your body is up for on any given day (00033though it’s always important to listen to your body regardless of what a wearable says). If you wake up with a low recovery score, for example, Whoop may recommend reducing workout intensity. If your sleep debt is climbing, it will show how much rest you need to get back on track.

It also offers other behavior change suggestions that could improve how you feel based on the data it collects about you and your habits over time. In fact, the in-depth habit tracking feature offers some of the most valuable insights into how day-to-day behaviors affect us physiologically. As a trainer, this has been one of the features I've seen make the most long-term impact with clients, as it really helps them connect the dots and understand how everything is interrelated.

Whoop 5.0 does require a membership and there are three different tiers, the main difference being the level of insights and analysis. Whoop One covers the core Whoop experience, including sleep, strain, recovery, steps, activity monitoring, heart-rate tracking, and women’s hormonal insights, which helps us see how menstrual cycle phases may relate to sleep, recovery, and performance. Whoop Peak adds a broader health and wellness layer, including health monitor, stress monitor, healthspan, and more advanced coaching-style insights for users who want to connect daily habits with longer-term performance and recovery trends (this is the tier I personally go with). Whoop Life is the most advanced tier, adding heart-health features such as ECG, irregular heart rhythm notifications, and blood pressure insights where available. Ultimately, the difference between the tiers is less about the tracker experience itself and more about how much interpretation, health context, and advanced monitoring you want.

The tradeoff is cost. Since Whoop uses a membership model, it will end up being more expensive over time than a tracker that doesn’t require a subscription. It also is not the best choice if you want live pace, distance, or workout stats on your wrist during a run. For that, a Garmin, Apple Watch, Samsung Galaxy Watch, or COROS watch will be more practical.

The Google Fitbit Inspire earned its spot as the best budget pick because it gives you the core fitness-tracking features most people want in a slim and approachable design. It’s not trying to be a full smartwatch or a performance running watch. Instead, it focuses on the basics: steps, heart rate, workouts, sleep, active minutes, and general wellness trends.

That simplicity makes the Fitbit Inspire a good entry point for someone who wants to become more aware of their fitness data without getting buried in advanced metrics. It can track walks, gym workouts, casual runs, and sleep, while the Fitbit app makes the data easy to understand. You can quickly see how much movement you got throughout the day, how long you slept, and how your habits are trending over time. Additionally, you’ll get a stress management score, guided breathing sessions, and reminders to move.

It is also one of the easiest trackers to wear all day. The band is slim enough to feel less bulky than a smartwatch, and the display is small but still easy to use for checking basic stats. If your main goals are walking more, staying accountable, improving sleep habits, or getting started with activity tracking, the Inspire offers strong value.

The main limitation is that it’s not necessarily built for serious training. Depending on the model, the GPS may rely on your phone rather than being fully built into the device, and runners who want advanced pace analysis, training load, recovery guidance, or race tools will likely outgrow it. But for the price, it’s one of the most practical fitness trackers for everyday use.

The Apple Watch Series 12 blends health tracking, workout features, smartwatch convenience, and safety tools better than almost any other wearable. If you already use an iPhone, this is the most seamless option in our guide.

The Series 12 features more advanced sensor capabilities, including an upgraded optical heart sensor that measures heart rate every five seconds, and tracks HRV more frequently. WH associate fitness editor Addison Aloin wrote in her review that the Series 12 has “a great baseline of health and fitness features.”

The Series 12 tracks a wide range of workouts, including running, walking, cycling, strength training, HIIT, yoga, Pilates, swimming, hiking, and many more. During activity, the display is bright and you can easily view data like pace, distance, heart rate, time, and workout zones during runs and workouts. It also integrates smoothly with Apple Fitness+, the Health app, and third-party training apps.

One of the most meaningful updates the Series 12 got is a new readiness score, which uses recent activity, training load, your sleep score, and overnight metrics like heart rate, respiratory rate, skin temperature, and recovery HRV to estimate how prepared your body is for exertion. The score runs from 0 to 10 and gives you a recommendation for your day, such as Recover, Pace Yourself, Ready, or Go For It. “My only complaint is that it’s kind of hard to find the readiness score on the watch and in the Health app,” Aloin says.

Since it’s so much more than just a tracker you wear during workout, the Apple Watch also offers a lot of versatility. It can handle notifications, calls, music, maps, timers, contactless payments, fall detection, and emergency features, making it a strong option for anyone who wants one device that can move between workouts and everyday life.

It is also a good pick for people who like motivational nudges. Apple’s activity rings are simple, visual, and surprisingly effective if you respond well to daily goals. If you want coaching-style feedback without digging through complex training dashboards, the Series 12’s newer software can translate health and fitness data into easily digestible prompts.

The Series 12 offers up to 24 hours of everyday battery life, and Apple says 15 minutes of charging can add up to 12 hours of battery. That makes it more manageable for overnight sleep tracking, but it still requires more planning than fitness trackers that last several days or longer on a single charge. “I’ve found the battery life is awesome for everyday use, but usually does need a charge every other day or so,” Aloin says.

The Google Fitbit Air gives you the quiet, distraction-free experience of a screenless wearable at a more approachable price point than premium recovery bands. The Fitbit Air tracks core metrics like activity, heart rate, sleep, and wellness trends over time, then sends that information to your phone.

Since there’s no screen, you’re not checking your wrist throughout the day. That makes it a strong option for anyone who wants to be more mindful about movement and sleep but doesn’t want a smartwatch. It’s also a good fit for people who are curious about screenless tracking but are not ready to commit to a more expensive platform. It offers a simpler, lower-pressure way to see whether passive health tracking actually helps your routine.

The Fitbit app is another advantage. Fitbit’s platform is approachable, especially for beginners, and tends to present health data in a way that feels less intimidating than more athlete-focused apps. That makes the Air a strong choice for casual users who want guidance without performance overload.

The main limitation is that a screenless budget tracker will not replace a running watch or smartwatch. You will not get the same real-time workout stats, app support, phone features, or advanced training analysis. But if you want affordable, low-distraction tracking, Fitbit Air is a compelling pick.

The Coros Pace 4 is our best lightweight pick because it delivers serious training features in a watch that feels easy to wear. After testing the watch for two months, MH deputy features editor Carina Hsieh said that it feels so lightweight that you forget it’s there. It’s especially appealing for runners and fitness-minded users who want more data than a basic tracker can provide but don’t want a bulky smartwatch.

Coros has built a strong reputation among runners because its watches tend to offer excellent battery life, practical training tools, and reliable GPS. The Pace 4 continues that tradition. “I was pleasantly surprised by how responsive and quick the GPS signal was on runs,” Hsieh said. She also noted that the watch had an exceptionally long battery life during her testing, and that she only had to charge it twice in two months.

For runners, the Pace 4 can track pace, distance, heart rate, route data, workouts, recovery, and training trends. Features like race predictions, virtual pacing, breadcrumb navigation, and personalized training tools make it feel like a strong contender for anyone who is a serious runner. It is also useful for people who cross-train, since it can handle gym workouts, cycling, walking, and other activities.

A big reason to choose the Pace 4 over other smartwatches is that it feels purpose-built for fitness. You’re not paying for a huge app ecosystem or phone-like features. Instead, you’re getting a lightweight training watch that can help you understand your fitness, recovery, and progress.

The Samsung Galaxy Watch 9 is the best fitness tracker for Android users, especially anyone with a Samsung Galaxy phone. It offers a polished smartwatch experience with robust health and fitness tools, making it a bit like an Android equivalent to the Apple Watch. WH beauty editor Nicole Saunders, who has tested multiple wearables over the years, says she usually struggles to wear trackers consistently because the bands were uncomfortable overnight. But the Galaxy Watch 9 changed that. “The band is surprisingly comfortable to wear and, dare I say, actually looks good,” she said.

This smartwatch tracks everyday activity, steps, heart rate, workouts, stress, sleep, and recovery-style metrics. It also includes Samsung’s Vitals feature, which monitors overnight patterns including heart rate, respiratory rate, blood oxygen, skin temperature, and other sleep-related signals, then compares them against your personal baseline. Saunders says it took about a full week for her baseline to set up, and anything outside of her normal range appeared in orange in the app, making it easy to compare metrics and spot subtle changes over time.

It also tracks a wide range of exercise modes, and its running features are useful for people who want coaching-style tools. Samsung’s body composition measurement feature can also be helpful for observing trends over time, though the numbers should be treated as estimates rather than medical-grade measurements. Saunders also liked the sleep features, including sleep quality tracking and snore detection, which she found both funny and potentially useful for better understanding nighttime habits.

The watch pairs easily with Galaxy phones, and the Samsung Health app organizes fitness and wellness data in an approachable way. If you want a tracker that can monitor workouts in the morning, handle notifications during the day, and track sleep at night, the Galaxy Watch 9 is a strong choice. In her testing, Saunders found it to be stylish and effective, adding that it would make a great gift for someone starting a health journey or looking for more detailed insights.

The Oura Ring 5 offers robust health and sleep tracking in the most discreet package of all the fitness trackers in this guide. If you don’t like wearing a watch around the clock or simply prefer jewelry over a wristband, Oura is the easiest fitness tracker to live with. In her review, WH associate editor Olivia Luppino said it’s the only fitness tracker she’s ever used that she hasn’t wanted to take off.

The Oura Ring’s biggest strength is passive health tracking. It’s designed to unobtrusively monitor your body in the background, and then translate that information into readiness, sleep, activity, stress, temperature, and recovery insights in an easy to use app. It can be especially helpful for people who want to understand how sleep, illness, stress, menstrual cycle changes, and daily habits affect energy.

For sleep tracking, the Ring 5 is one of the more comfortable options since a ring is less bulky than a watch, and many people find it easier to wear overnight. The app also makes sleep data easy to understand, showing not just how long you slept but how restorative that sleep may have been.

The tradeoff is workout tracking. Oura can track activity and has improved its fitness features, but it’s still not the best choice for people who want real-time stats during runs, strength workouts, or cycling sessions. It can also interfere with grip during weightlifting and kettlebell training. If you want to glance at pace, heart rate, or intervals mid-workout, choose a watch. If you want discreet, all-day and all-night health tracking, choose a smart ring.

For runners, the Garmin Forerunner 570 sits in a sweet spot between advanced training watches and an everyday wearable. Garmin makes several excellent Forerunner models, but the 570 offers a mix of running metrics, GPS tools, recovery insights, smartwatch features, and comfortable wearability for runners who want more than the basics without jumping straight to flagship option.

The Forerunner 570 is designed to be useful for structured training with its capability to track runs, intervals, long runs, tempo sessions, races, recovery runs, and cross-training workouts. It also gives runners access to Garmin’s broader training ecosystem, including performance metrics, suggested workouts, recovery guidance, HRV status, sleep data, training readiness-style insights, and race-related tools. For someone training for a 5K, half marathon, marathon, or simply trying to become a more consistent runner, those features can be super useful.

It has a bright AMOLED display, touchscreen support as well as physical buttons, a feature that’s especially notable for runners since touchscreens can sometimes be frustrating with sweat, rain, or gloves. Battery life varies from 8 hours to up to 10 days depending on how many features you’re using.

The biggest drawback is price. Though it’s not the priciest Forerunner offering out there, the Forerunner 570 is not inexpensive, and newer runners who only want pace, distance, and heart rate may be perfectly happy with a more affordable Forerunner model. But runners who want a capable watch they won’t outgrow, the 570 is a great option.

The Garmin Cirqa is the best screenless tracker for lovers of the Garmin fitness ecosystem, since it integrates seamlessly with Garmin’s other tech. It delivers health and activity tracking without adding another display to your life, and is especially well suited for anyone who wants to use Garmin Connect but doesn't want to wear a full Garmin watch all day long.

That makes Cirqa especially useful for athletes who already own a Garmin running, cycling, or outdoor watch. You may want to wear your Forerunner for workouts and during runs, but prefer something smaller and screen-free for sleep, recovery, or everyday tracking. The Cirqa fills that gap.

It also has a cost advantage over some screenless competitors because it doesn’t require a subscription. That makes it appealing for Garmin loyalists who want ongoing tracking without committing to a monthly membership.

The Cirqa isn’t the best choice if you want live workout stats, on-wrist coaching, or a display for pace and distance, and a Garmin watch would be better for that. But as a low-distraction daily tracker that complements Garmin’s training platform, Cirqa is a welcome addition to the category.

The Google Fitbit Edge bridges the gap between a simple Fitbit band and a full smartwatch, making it a strong choice if you want a display but don’t want a bulkier smartwatch. It gives you a screen for checking stats, starting workouts, reviewing progress, and seeing basic information at a glance.

Compared with the Fitbit Air, the Edge is more interactive, compared with the Inspire, it’s a more advanced option, and compared with a Pixel Watch, it’s simpler to use and more fitness focused. It offers enough features to be useful throughout the day, tracking activity, workouts, sleep, heart-rate trends, and daily wellness data, while still keeping the overall experience lighter than a smartwatch. If you want a compact Google Fitbit tracker with a screen and stronger features than the entry-level models, the Edge is the best fit.

A good heart rate monitor is one of the most important features to look for in a fitness tracker, whether you’re training seriously or just getting started. Heart rate data can help you better understand exercise intensity, track effort during workouts, and monitor resting heart rate over time, which can be a useful marker of cardiovascular fitness. While wrist-based trackers typically aren’t as precise as chest straps, they can still offer a helpful read on exertion levels during moderate to intense activity, according to a 2019 study in Cardiovascular Diagnosis & Therapy.

Sleep tracking can be a useful feature because sleep plays a major role in recovery, energy, mood, and overall performance. By tracking patterns like sleep duration, consistency, and overnight restlessness, a fitness tracker can help you spot habits that may be affecting how you feel the next day. Many models also break down sleep stages, show trends over time, and offer recovery or readiness insights based on your nightly data. While the numbers aren’t always perfect, they can provide a helpful starting point for understanding your routine and making small changes, like adjusting bedtime, limiting late caffeine, or improving sleep consistency.

Weight may not seem that important at first, but it matters when you’re wearing a fitness tracker all day and night. A larger, heavier model can feel more noticeable over time, especially during sleep or long workouts. There’s no single ideal size, and the best option will depend on how you plan to use it.

If you run, hike, or spend a lot of time outdoors, it may be worth choosing a more durable model, even if it’s slightly bulkier. For everyday wear, walking, strength training, or mostly indoor workouts, a lighter, lower-profile tracker may be more comfortable. Ultimately, the right fit comes down to your activity level, wrist size, and what you’ll actually want to keep wearing.

Many of the best fitness trackers can run for days or even weeks on a single charge, such as the Whoop 5.0. But battery doesn't always equate to quality, given that an option like the Apple Watch Series 12 needs to be charged every day and it’s still one of our absolute favorites. Ultimately it comes down to how a trackers charging needs will fit into your routine.

As with all tech, you want a fitness tracker that’s easy to use. If your new tracker isn’t intuitive for you to set up or use daily, you’re far less likely to use it consistently. Ultimately, the only way to know for sure is to try it out yourself to see if it works for you. That's why it’s smart to buy a fitness tracker with a generous return policy.

We tested them during our workouts, on our runs, in Pilates class, and more. And of course, we tested them during sleep. Whether you’re training regularly or just want something for everyday activity tracking, the models we tested for the latest update range from basic fitness trackers to powerful smartwatches and everything in between.
