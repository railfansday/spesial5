Sharper at Age 60: Later-Born Generations Have Slower Memory Decline

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

— Newer generations also entered old age with better memory scores

Recent generations entered old age with better memory, and their memory declined more slowly than that of earlier generations, longitudinal data from over 219,000 people showed.

Being born 10 years later was associated with a 10% higher memory score at age 60 (d=0.32 per decade), reported Anders Fjell, PhD, of the University of Oslo in Norway, and colleagues.

At every age, later-born cohorts showed a higher probability of having stable memory. Each decade of later birth was associated with 64% higher odds of being in a stable memory period (OR 1.64, 95% CI 1.59-1.68, P<0.001), and 38% lower odds of meeting steep-decline criteria at the same age (OR 0.62, 95% CI 0.55-0.70, P<0.001).

"The combined advantage is large enough to plausibly account for the reported 13% per decade decline in dementia incidence," Fjell and colleagues wrote in Nature Medicine.

"It's known that later-born generations score better on cognitive tests. What this study shows is that the memory changes we observe during aging have also slowed," Fjell told MedPage Today.

"By age 80, about half of the generational advantage comes from each of these sources," he added. "The second half is new, and the total advantage is large enough to account for the reported fall in dementia incidence at a given age in Western countries."

In 2020, an analysis of seven population-based cohort studies in the U.S. and Europe from the Alzheimer Cohorts Consortium showed that the incidence rate of dementia declined by 13% per decade consistently in studies from 1998 to 2015.

In the U.S., the number of people living with dementia is projected to rise sharply as populations age, but dementia incidence among Medicare beneficiaries is falling.

To better understand recent trends, Fjell and colleagues evaluated over 783,000 memory assessments from more than 219,000 individuals in five longitudinal studies in the U.S. and Europe who were followed for up to 30 years. Cohorts came from the U.S. Health and Retirement Study; the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing; the Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe; the Irish Longitudinal Study on Ageing; and the Longitudinal Aging Study Amsterdam. Participants were ages 50 and older at baseline.

The researchers focused on verbal episodic memory as assessed by word-list recall, an indicator of Alzheimer's-related cognitive decline. Substudies assessed hippocampal volume change.

The slower decline of later-born generations was mirrored by slower hippocampal atrophy, the researchers observed. The fastest decliners had the most improvements over time.

The Lancet Commission estimated that nearly half of global dementia cases may be prevented or delayed through lifestyle and environmental changes, Fjell and co-authors pointed out. Because cognitive performance in adulthood correlates with childhood function, generational differences in memory decline may reflect early-life conditions that build cognitive capacity, they said.

Midlife factors like hypertension treatment and physical activity also may play a role, Fjell noted. "We cannot pinpoint the causes with certainty, since these are observational data," he said. "Likely drivers are the past century's improvements in education, childhood conditions, and cardiovascular health, which also means the findings support prevention as a life-course project rather than something that begins at 65."

There's no guarantee this positive development will continue, he pointed out. "The gains we see have been created by improvements in living conditions, education, and health throughout the 20th century, and they did not happen by themselves."

The study had several limitations, the researchers acknowledged. Faster decliners were less likely to complete all assessments over time, they said. Findings were from studies in high-income countries in Europe and North America, and may not apply to other parts of the world.
