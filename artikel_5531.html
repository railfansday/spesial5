The 15 most memorable presidential concession speeches

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

Free daily briefing on global business news.

From Adlai Stevenson in 1952 to Hillary Clinton in 2016, losing candidates found 15 ways to accept defeat on the record.

Every presidential election leaves one candidate facing a room full of people who hoped for a different night. The winner gets the celebration. The loser inherits a harder assignment. He or she must walk to a microphone, admit defeat in public, and tell supporters what to do with their frustration. Few speeches carry stakes so personal and so public at the same moment. The candidate has spent an entire campaign arguing that the other side would steer the country wrong. Now the same voice has to concede that voters chose that side. How a defeated nominee handles that turn tells the public whether the transfer of power will feel settled or contested.

No law requires any of it. A congratulatory call from the runner-up to the victor carries no legal force, yet the custom dates to 1896. The habit had survived 120 years by 2016 without any statute behind it. Nothing in the process compels a gracious word, a pledge of cooperation, or even an appearance onstage. Each also-ran decides what to say and how much of the hurt to show. The latitude turns every farewell into a self-portrait its author paints at the bleakest point of a political career. Some of those portraits outlast the final tally.

TheWrap writer Rosemary Rossi gathered 15 of these addresses in a list the outlet ran on November 7, 2020. The entries move in election-year order, opening with the 1952 race and closing with the 2016 contest, a stretch of 64 years. Each entry identifies who came up short, offers a sentence or two on how the race slipped away, and pairs that context with one line quoted from the remarks. Rossi assigns no ranks or scores and spells out no selection criteria beyond calling the moments memorable. The original page embeds video clips beside the text. The 15 entries below follow her sequence.

Adlai Stevenson answered his 1952 loss to Dwight D. Eisenhower with a joke he credited to Abraham Lincoln, a man he claimed as a "fellow townsman." Stevenson recounted that someone had asked how he felt. He met the question by reaching for a tale Lincoln used to tell.

Lincoln's anecdote centers on a little boy who stubs his toe in the dark. The child, Stevenson recalled, felt "too old to cry, but it hurt too much to laugh." The image suits a grown man absorbing a public defeat. The pain registers, yet open grief would clash with the moment.

Stevenson's decision to frame his confession as a response to someone's inquiry softened its edge. The admission arrived on request. The borrowed voice did similar work. A stubbed toe is a small, ordinary injury, and casting himself as the boy shrank an election verdict to a child's scale.

The quoted passage names no rival and lodges no complaint about the outcome. Stevenson let a secondhand punchline carry his emotion, and the device gave him distance from his own disappointment. Speaking through Lincoln, he acknowledged the sting without dwelling on it.

A candidate who owns up to the hurt of a setback, and does so with a quip, spares his audience the job of consoling him.

Richard Nixon lost the 1960 presidential race to John F. Kennedy by a narrow margin, and Rosemary Rossi traces the defeat to his shaky turn in the first televised political debate, sweaty upper lip and all. Her account places a physical detail, visible to anyone watching, at the center of the outcome. In Rossi's telling, the camera helped settle a contest for the White House the first time candidates faced off on screen.

Nixon kept grievance out of the concession line Rossi quotes. He offered his congratulations to Senator Kennedy on a fine race, addressing his rival by his Senate title and crediting Kennedy with the effort itself. The excerpt mentions neither the debate nor the slim gap between the two men. Its single sentence directs all of its praise at the winner and leaves the speaker's own setbacks unspoken.

The quote, set beside Rossi's context, shows a defeated candidate praising his opponent's campaign after a result that, in her view, his own stumble on screen helped decide.

Hubert Humphrey sent his congratulations to Richard Nixon after the 1968 election, eight years after Nixon had absorbed a presidential defeat of his own. The Democrat's gesture reversed the roles of 1960 and put Nixon on the receiving end of a courtesy he once had to extend to Kennedy.

Humphrey brought deep standing inside his own party to the moment. He backed President Johnson's policy on Vietnam. His domestic record held a steady liberal line, and he had earned the regard of Democratic leaders.

Humphrey's courtesy mattered more because of who offered it. A nominee with the trust of his party's leadership, yielding to a Republican, gave Democratic voters a model for accepting the outcome and moving on.

Nixon's path shows that a lost race need not end a presidential career. The man who offered congratulations in 1960 accepted them in 1968.

George McGovern conceded to Richard Nixon in 1972, the year Nixon won reelection with the Vietnam War at its height. McGovern used the moment to defend the system that had turned him away.

His central plea asked the crowd not to lose faith in the country's political process, and he backed the request with a claim of progress. The same process, he argued, "has yielded the much-valued improvement in these past two years." The quoted line leaves the improvement unnamed, yet its time frame matters. Two years of gains set against a single loss cast the outcome as one setback inside a longer climb. The framing handed his followers a fresh reason to stay engaged after the count went against them.

McGovern's appeal to patience carried extra weight in wartime. A candidate who lost as the fighting peaked could have told voters that democracy had failed them. He urged a demoralized movement to keep its faith in the ballot box.

Supporters at the Sioux Falls Coliseum, where McGovern bowed out on election night, roused themselves for final choruses of "We want George." The electoral map delivered a colder verdict. Nixon carried 49 states and claimed every electoral vote but 17 of the country's 538, according to Time. The distance between a loyal hall and a hostile map defined the challenge his plea had to meet.

McGovern carried his conciliation to the winner in writing. His telegram to Nixon voiced hope that the president would guide the country toward peace overseas and toward justice at home, and it pledged McGovern's full backing. The wish for peace abroad pointed at the conflict that loomed over 1972. A nominee whom voters shut out of nearly every state still treated the man who beat him as a partner in ending it.

Laryngitis silenced Gerald Ford $F after his 1976 loss to Jimmy Carter. His wife, Betty Ford, delivered his concession in his place while he stood behind her, staring over her shoulder. The defeated president's words reached the crowd through another person's mouth, and their author watched without a word.

Betty Ford's statement turned on a phone call placed before she spoke. She told the audience her husband had asked her to pass along word that he had called Carter, now the president-elect, a short time earlier and congratulated him on his victory. Her account gave each man his title in the same breath. Ford was still the president who had made the call, and Carter had become the president-elect who took it. One sentence marked the handoff between the two men, and her public reading confirmed a private gesture that came first.

Ford's own message to Carter went further than congratulations. His telegram pledged support to the incoming president and promised that his administration would help Carter begin his term without friction. The wire also urged the country to set aside the campaign's divisions and unite again. Ford's surrender of the race moved through three channels: a phone call, a written telegram, and his wife's spoken statement. Each one carried the same acceptance of the result.

Fatigue showed on Ford while listeners heard his written message. He did not smile, and the moment shook him in plain view. A president without a voice faced the audience and let it read the loss on his face. Ford's illness changed the messenger and left the message whole.

Jimmy Carter's 1980 defeat ended his White House tenure after a single term and handed the office to Ronald Reagan. Rossi credits him with a graceful handoff to his successor. His concession focused on the privilege of the job he was leaving. Carter counted himself among the few people ever blessed with the chance "to help shape the destiny of this nation."

PBS NewsHour reports that Carter reminded supporters of a pledge he had made four years earlier never to lie to them. He also admitted he could not claim the defeat left him unwounded.

The two halves of the speech pull in different directions. One treats the presidency as a rare gift, and the other refuses to disguise the sting of losing it. Carter offered both in the same appearance, and neither cancels the other.

Ronald Reagan defeated Walter Mondale in a 1984 landslide and secured a second term in the White House. Mondale had challenged a sitting president, and the scale of the loss left him no narrow count to contest and no ambiguity to explain away.

Reagan had beaten a Democratic nominee four years earlier, taking the presidency from Jimmy Carter in 1980. Mondale faced the man who had ended his party's hold on the White House. The 1984 result marked the second consecutive cycle in which Reagan sent a Democrat to the concession podium.

Mondale answered the rout by turning outward. He rejoiced in the country's democracy and in the freedom of its people, the same voters who had turned him down. Then he declared that he and his supporters accepted the verdict those voters had delivered. He spoke of the acceptance in the plural, as something the whole room did together. Mondale stood for the crowd in front of him as much as for himself and drew those supporters into the act of consent.

Mondale's choice of "verdict" cast the election as a judgment the public hands down and a candidate receives without appeal. Reagan's lopsided margin made the word fit. A landslide leaves a beaten campaign no opening to dispute the count, and Mondale did not reach for one. By embracing the term, he lent his own standing to the process that had rejected him and turned a personal setback into a public vote of confidence in the system that produced it.

Michael Dukakis lost the 1988 race to George H.W. Bush, Ronald Reagan's vice president, and he answered the defeat by assuring supporters that "he will be OUR president, and we'll work with him." TheWrap prints the possessive in capital letters. The emphasis puts shared ownership of the office ahead of party loyalty and asks the losing side to claim the new administration as its own.

Bush's résumé raised the stakes of the request. Voters had kept the White House in the hands of Reagan's own deputy, and Dukakis's backers had lost to the heir of the team they spent a campaign fighting. Dukakis still asked them to call that heir their own.

Collective language shaped the acceptance. Dukakis prefaced the promise by claiming to speak for "you and all the American people" in a single stroke. Speaking for the whole country turned a partisan loss into a decision every listener now shared. The framing folds his crowd into the national verdict and leaves them no separate camp to retreat into.

A pledge of cooperation rode along in that same sentence, and Dukakis tied it to a purpose. His next words carried a warning and a plea in one breath: "This nation faces major challenge ahead, and we must work together." The alert recasts teamwork with the victor as a duty owed to the public.

Self-blame completed the picture. Rossi reports that Dukakis faulted himself for giving too much of his time to his job as governor of Massachusetts. The admission traces the outcome to his own choices about his calendar, and it points no finger at the voters or at Bush. Locating the fault in his own hours gave his base no reason to doubt the count itself. A nominee who blames his own schedule and embraces the winner as his president leaves his followers no grudge to carry into the next term.

George H.W. Bush, the president TheWrap calls "Bush 41," lost the 1992 election to Bill Clinton after breaking his "Read my lips: no new taxes" pledge. Rossi sets Clinton's climb in the polls after Bush abandoned the vow. Clinton then took the presidency.

Bush devoted his concession to the mechanics of the handoff. He told the country that his entire administration would "work closely with his team to insure the smooth transition of power." The line names no policy fight and airs no campaign grievance. It commits the whole staff of a sitting president to helping the challenger who beat him settle into the job.

His closing line widened the frame from staff to citizens: "There is important work to be done and America must always come first." The words ask supporters to place the country ahead of their disappointment. The quoted remarks leave Clinton unnamed, which keeps attention on the work and off the man who won.

By TheWrap's account, a broken promise cost Bush the office, and he left it by offering a fresh one to the rival who unseated him.

Bob Dole lost the 1996 election to Bill Clinton and built his concession around a single distinction. He told the crowd that the president was his opponent, not his enemy.

The phrase splits political rivalry from personal hostility. An opponent competes for the same prize under shared rules, and the contest ends once voters decide. An enemy remains a threat after the decision. By placing Clinton in the first category, Dole framed his defeat as the close of a contest and gave his voters no grounds to treat the outcome as a wound to nurse.

The distinction mattered more because Clinton already occupied the office. Dole was conceding to a sitting president who had just secured a second term, and his words affirmed that president's standing to keep governing. Supporters could go on disputing Clinton's choices without questioning his right to make them.

Dole's sentence ends the fight and leaves the disagreement intact, which is the whole job a concession has to do.

Al Gore won the popular vote in 2000, yet George W. Bush captured the Electoral College and with it the White House.

Gore aimed his concession at the man who beat him. He addressed Bush as "president elect," and the title itself granted the result its legitimacy before Gore said anything else. He then urged an end to the partisan resentment the contest had left behind. He closed the passage by asking that God "bless his stewardship of this country."

Stewardship casts the presidency as something held in trust for the public. By choosing that word, Gore handed the trust to his opponent in the same breath that he gave up his own claim to it.

Gore's gesture carried added weight because more voters across the country had chosen him. He accepted an outcome the electoral tally decided and wished his rival well in office. His farewell shows that a graceful exit can survive a split verdict, one in which the defeated candidate outpolled the victor.

George W. Bush won a second term in 2004 by painting John Kerry as a candidate who flip-flopped on the Iraq War, as Rossi tells it.

Kerry built his concession around a phone call with the president and walked supporters through what the two men discussed. Division and unity framed the conversation, in his telling. "We talked about the danger of division in our country and the need — the desperate need — for unity, for finding common ground, coming together. Today, I hope that we can begin the healing." Kerry repeated "need" and attached "desperate" the second time, turning a routine courtesy into an urgent plea.

The quote stacks three phrasings of one wish. Unity, common ground, and coming together arrive in a single breath, each restating the last. The stacking suggests Kerry saw a deep rift in the country.

Kerry's "we" put winner and loser on the same side of one issue, the risk a fractured country posed. He presented the call as a joint conversation. Supporters heard their nominee describe the man who had beaten him as a partner in that worry.

The Iraq War, the issue Rossi says sank Kerry, goes unmentioned in the quoted lines. Kerry aimed his remarks at "division in our country" and set "Today" as the starting point for repair. The nominee Bush had portrayed as inconsistent closed the race by returning to one idea several ways in a few sentences.

John McCain ended his 2008 bid by praising Obama's victory. He named the toll of "a contest as long and as painful as this has been" and then turned to the winner, declaring that "his success alone commands my respect for his ability and perseverance" within the same sentence.

McCain treated the result itself as reason enough for regard and then credited the way Obama achieved it. He tied the outcome to "inspiring the hopes of so many millions of Americans who had once wrongly believed that they had little at stake or little influence in the election of an American president." The word "wrongly" carries a judgment. McCain told his audience that those Americans had misread their own power, and he gave Obama credit for changing their minds.

McCain gave the quoted passage over to his rival's achievement and left his own candidacy out of it. His pairing of "long" and "painful" with "perseverance" links the hardship he named to the quality he admired. The lines present Obama's triumph as earned through endurance and through his reach among voters who had once felt shut out of choosing a president.

Mitt Romney carried his party's confidence into the 2012 election, yet Obama secured another four years in the White House. Rossi describes the GOP as all but certain that Romney would win the presidency.

The nominee shared that faith. He went into election night so sure of victory that he had not drafted a concession speech, according to PBS NewsHour. The remarks he delivered let some disappointment show, and they left no doubt about his respect for the electoral process.

Romney used his concession to call on the country's leaders to reach across the aisle. The appeal asked officeholders in both parties to work with each other after a campaign that had pitted them against one another.

A Republican nominee who had expected to take power closed his bid by asking leaders in both parties, his own included, to cooperate with a president his side had just failed to unseat.

Hillary Clinton entered the 2016 race as the polls' sure bet to become the 45th president, in Rossi's telling, yet Donald Trump pushed past 270 electoral votes by carrying the major battleground states. Many people, according to Rossi, had considered a Trump victory impossible. The former first lady watched a reality star claim an office the surveys had marked as her own.

Clinton gave a formal concession speech the next morning. A night passed between the returns and her public words. She voiced her disappointment and acknowledged the pain of the loss before she made any demand of her audience. The request came last, when she asked her backers to accept the result. The sequence let supporters hear their own hurt named before they heard what she wanted from them.

Her central line about Trump framed tolerance as a debt: "We owe him an open mind and a chance to lead." The verb "owe" turns patience into an obligation the defeated side carries. The pronoun "we" places Clinton inside the group she addressed. She shoulders the same duty she assigns. The line seeks two separate things. An open mind concerns attitude, a willingness to hear Trump out. A chance to lead concerns time, a stretch in which he can govern before anyone passes judgment. Neither half of the quoted line endorses Trump's agenda or praises the campaign that beat her.

Clinton aimed the appeal at people who had gone into the vote as favorites, the camp with the farthest distance to cover before making peace with the outcome. The forecasts had prepared her voters for a celebration, and the defeat arrived as something many had ruled out. From the candidate expected to win, a plea for an open mind required her listeners to surrender a prediction and a hope within a single day.
