8 ways to stay warm and cut your heating bill this winter, according to AAA

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Sealing drafts, using sunlight, lowering the thermostat, and maintaining your heater can trim winter energy use, even for renters.

Cold weather has a way of turning home comfort into a money question. Hot drinks come out, holiday decorations go up, and the thermostat starts getting a lot more attention. Then the utility statement lands. The U.S. Department of Energy reports that heating can swallow nearly a third of a household's utility bill. A cost of that size returns every winter. Small decisions about how a home holds its temperature, and how the people inside stay cozy, can shift a meaningful slice of that spending.

Prices in 2021 sharpened the squeeze. The New York Times reported that natural gas went for double its 2020 level that year, with heating oil and propane climbing as well. The U.S. Energy Information Administration projected at the time that a somewhat colder-than-normal winter could push bills up 94 percent for propane users, 50 percent for natural gas customers, and 15 percent for those with electric heat. The agency was describing a possible winter, yet its numbers showed how far a cold snap could stretch a budget tied to one fuel. The most effective structural defenses carry a steep price of their own. Insulation and windows with two or three panes keep warmth inside better than any other strategy, but retrofitting an existing building with them can prove expensive. Many renters cannot make major changes to the places they lease.

Via, the magazine of AAA Mountain West Group, published a tip sheet by Mandy Ferreira on Nov. 22, 2021, laying out eight tasks and habits for owners and tenants alike. The piece compiles advice. It reports no survey, test, or original analysis of its own. Its figures come from outside authorities it names, among them the U.S. Department of Energy, the U.S. Energy Information Administration, The New York Times, and Pacific Gas and Electric. A few estimates appear without any named source. Readers should treat its dollar figures and forecasts as a 2021 snapshot. The list below covers every tip in the magazine's lineup, in sequence, and each one stays within reach of anyone who can't take on a renovation.

Light or moving air at the spot where an exterior door meets its jamb signals a need for new weatherstripping, and a door without any stripping deserves its first set. An evening spent next to a leaky window teaches how much cold can push through cracks and seams that seem too narrow to matter. Such openings act as stealthy thieves of heat, and strips of weatherstripping and beads of caulk shut them down.

Air infiltration, the movement of outside air in and out through gaps in a building's shell, drives up heating and cooling bills and leaves occupants less comfortable. Ill-fitting doors and windows, the line where a house sits on its foundation, and the holes around pipes and wiring that pierce the walls all give that air an easy path. A leak at any joint, including where brick or wood siding meets the foundation or walls, admits moisture that can warp wood and contribute to mold.

Daylight around a door or window frame offers one clue to a draft, and a homeowner can shake windows and doors to see whether they shift or run a hand along caulk lines to feel a current. Incense smoke or a damp hand can reveal the flow, and a basic building pressurization test makes leaks easier to find. A lamp outside a closed door exposes gaps in jamb stripping by shining through wherever the door fails to meet its stop.

Weatherstripping earns a place beyond entry doors, sealing the hatch to an attic or crawlspace. Run caulk along baseboards and the edges of window frames, and inspect the stripping on any window that opens, swapping it out once it wears thin. Caulk belongs at the joint between a window frame and the outer wall, but it should stay out of the channel between sash and jamb, where it would lock the window shut. Weatherstripping handles that space. The material comes in strips built to stop drafts around the moving parts of doors and windows while those parts sit closed. Vinyl or metal V-strip demands cutting tools and some skill to install, which may put it beyond some do-it-yourselfers.

Outlets and light switches set in uninsulated walls can let cold air through, and foam gaskets seal those openings. Most of these checks rely on sight or touch, letting a household pinpoint its leaks before buying any materials.

About three-quarters of the sunlight striking a double-paned window passes through the glass and becomes heat inside the room. The pane works as a small heater that never sends a bill. Any household with sun-facing double-paned windows owns a source of warmth it does not have to buy.

Opening curtains and blinds on windows that catch direct sun during the day captures that energy at no cost. A closed blind halts the light short of the room, and the warmth the light would have carried never arrives. Leaving coverings shut through a bright afternoon means paying the furnace to replace heat the sun offered for nothing. The habit requires no tools, no purchase, and no landlord's approval. Tenants who cannot alter the glass itself can put it to work.

Orientation determines how much a window can contribute. Green Building Advisor reports that in data for Providence, R.I., south-facing windows delivered more daylight and solar heat than windows facing any other direction between September and April. The same figures showed a single south-facing window outgaining three windows facing north, east, and west combined from November through February. Rooms on the south side of a home deserve the first attention when the blinds go up each morning. A home whose main windows face north, east, or west will collect a smaller share of this free warmth. Its occupants will get more mileage from the other tips in this lineup.

Climate sets a second boundary. Anchorage, Alaska, stood alone in the outlet's January comparison of cities as the place where heat escaping through south-facing glass exceeded the solar gain. Every other city on the list came out ahead, with sunshine through south windows outweighing what the panes lost. Outside that one northern outlier, the data give households in those cities a reason to raise south-facing blinds on January days.

Tight-fitting cellular shades, also called honeycomb shades, cut the heat a window loses by at least 40 percent, according to the U.S. Department of Energy. The agency equates that reduction at the glass with about 10 percent less energy spent on heating. The two numbers describe different scales. The larger one covers a single window, while the smaller one spans a home's entire heating load. The gap between them suggests windows account for only part of the warmth a house loses.

Pleated, creased fabric gives these shades their structure. The folds form pockets shaped something like hexagons, and the whole shade moves up and down to open and close. Each shade carries two layers of pleating, according to tucson.com. One pocket of air sits inside the shade itself, and a second forms in the space between the shade and the window glass. Trapped air in both places pays off most in colder climates.

Heavy drapes and insulated curtains make strong alternatives to cellular shades for keeping a room warm after dark. Shutters offer another approach. Their frame mounts to the wall around a window, and the gap left between the glass and the room serves as a layer of insulation. The air space protects less than the insulation inside walls. Every option here places a barrier between the room and the cold panes, yet the federal number covers snug cellular shades alone, leaving drapes, curtains, and shutters without a measured payoff.

Adding a layer before touching the thermostat puts heat where a person feels it. The advice flips the usual winter reflex and asks people to take care of their bodies before they turn to the air around them. The wardrobe portion starts at the floor with cozy socks and slippers, then climbs to sweaters and a blanket. Every item on that list is ordinary clothing or bedding, and none of it draws power.

A toasty drink or a hot meal heats a person from the inside out. Daily habits, such as a morning mug or an evening dinner, become part of the household's defense against the cold. The kettle and stove take over a small share of the furnace's work.

Electric blankets and reusable heat packs bring comfort in short order and use minimal energy. CHOICE home heating expert Chris Barnes said heated throws are energy efficient and use less electricity than a portable heater or air conditioner. A plug-in throw sits between a sweater and the furnace. It supplies active warmth to one person and leaves the rest of the house out of the arrangement. A reusable pack works through many rounds, sparing the buyer a repeat purchase.

Renters can follow every step here. None calls for a tool, a contractor, a landlord's sign-off, or a single change to the building itself. The underlying logic comes down to scale. A blanket covers one body, while a central system heats every room it serves, occupied or empty. The habits hold up no matter which fuel feeds the furnace, whether propane, natural gas, or electricity. The article's subtitle pitches its tips as a way to shrink both a carbon footprint and a utility bill. Clothing, food, and heat packs serve both goals, letting a household dial back the heat without feeling the chill.

Dropping the thermostat 7 to 10 degrees at night or during work hours can shave up to 10% off heating costs. Night and the workday cover the stretches when a household sleeps under the covers or leaves the house empty. Few people notice a cooler room from under a comforter or from a desk across town.

Waking hours at home call for 68 degrees or lower. The two targets add up to a daily rhythm. A modest temperature holds while people move around the house, and a deeper dip follows once they go to bed or head out the door.

Pacific Gas and Electric credits a programmable thermostat, which keeps temperatures in line with a household's schedule, with savings of up to $180 a year. These devices switch heating or air conditioning on at preset times, according to Energy.gov. A schedule takes memory out of the equation. The overnight and workday drops depend on someone remembering to make them twice a day, and a programmed routine handles both without a reminder.

Smart thermostats push control past the front door, letting owners change the temperature from wherever they happen to be. A forgotten adjustment on the way out no longer commits a household to warming empty rooms all day.

Heat pump owners may need to skip the daily dips. Energy.gov reports that programmable thermostats used to be a poor fit for heat pumps, though a few manufacturers now build models for those systems that make a setback cost effective. A heat pump guide on Energy.gov tells owners of air-source units to hold the thermostat steady for the best efficiency. Switching one off in an empty home can trigger wasteful operation once the system restarts. The guide reserves setbacks for absences longer than a day.

A heat pump owner who wants nightly savings should confirm that the thermostat's maker designed it for that equipment, and otherwise save deeper setbacks for trips that last more than a day.

A washing machine can devote as much as 90% of the energy in a typical cycle to heating water, which hands the temperature dial outsized control over what each load costs. Everything else the machine does during a wash splits the remainder. Anyone hoping to shave the laundry room's share of a winter utility bill can start with a single knob.

Switching from hot to warm halves the energy a load requires. Turning the dial to cold cuts consumption by more than 50%. Each step down targets the same expense, the heating of the water, so the savings come out of the largest slice of the cycle's demand. Every cold load repeats the reduction, and the total grows with each wash through the heating season.

Air drying pushes the gains past the washer. An indoor rack lets clothes dry without the machine whenever space allows, and an outdoor line works on days free of rain and freezing temperatures. In the coldest stretch of winter, that limit steers drying toward the indoor rack. Skipping the dryer conserves power and spares fabric, which helps garments last longer.

A longer cycle at lower heat draws less energy than a hot, fast run, even though the clothes take more time to finish. Households that keep the dryer in their routine can trade time for a smaller energy draw.

The Department of Energy recommends that anyone shopping for a new dryer choose a model with a moisture sensor. The sensor switches the machine off once the clothes reach dryness, which keeps the drum from tumbling a finished load. The agency adds that the feature trims energy use and limits the wear that over-drying inflicts on clothing.

Across washer and dryer, the same pattern holds. Less heat shrinks spending at every stage of the laundry routine, from the wash to the final tumble.

LED bulbs draw more than 75 percent less energy than incandescent bulbs, and each one shines 25 times longer before it burns out. Every swap leaves a fixture pulling less than a quarter of the electricity it once used. The savings grow with use, and a lamp that burns for hours each evening returns the most. The change demands no new habits once the bulb sits in the socket. A household flips the same switches as before and collects the reward.

Longevity reshapes the math of keeping a room lit. One LED can serve as long as 25 incandescents installed one after another. A home that switches buys fewer replacements and spends less time swapping dead bulbs. Its power bill and its shopping list shrink together. The bulbs leave the heating system untouched, yet they pare down the electric portion of the same utility statement. The benefit also outlasts the cold months. A thermostat setback pays off while the furnace runs, whereas an efficient bulb keeps saving through spring and summer.

Holiday strings carry the advantage into decorating season. LED string lights run cooler than incandescent strands and can keep glowing for decades. As many as 25 strands can draw from a single socket, enough to power a sprawling display from one outlet. A set bought once can return each winter for years.

Timers and smart plugs attack waste from the other direction. They switch festive lighting on once darkness falls and shut it off at bedtime. Nobody has to remember to unplug the decorations, and no power flows to lights shining over a sleeping house. The devices impose on holiday lights the same schedule-driven discipline a programmable thermostat brings to heating.

Efficient bulbs lower the price of every lit hour, and a timer erases the hours nobody sees. Together they squeeze the lighting bill from both ends.

A furnace air filter needs cleaning or replacing once a month through the winter, or on the schedule its manufacturer recommends. The chore opens a short upkeep routine that helps a heating system waste less energy. Each step targets a different spot where a home's heat can stall or slip away.

Baseboard heaters, radiators, and warm-air registers work best when they stay clean and free of obstructions. A sofa pushed flush against a baseboard unit or a curtain draped over a register stands between the room and the output the system produces. Sliding furniture a few inches and tying back drapes cost nothing.

An open fireplace damper on a night without a fire leaves a direct opening between the living room and the outdoors. Closing the damper whenever the fireplace sits idle shuts off that route. A chimney the household no longer uses needs its flue sealed for good.

Any ductwork within reach needs solid insulation and airtight seals. The ducts carry warm air from the furnace to each room, and gaps or bare runs along the way spend that heat before it arrives.

A heat pump can cut the electricity a home uses for heating by up to 50%, and the same equipment handles cooling in summer. In cold months, a heat pump moves warmth indoors from the outside air, and in warm months it sends indoor warmth outside. Homes without ductwork can use an air-source model in its ductless form, known as a mini-split.

A ductless mini-split pairs an outdoor unit with one or more head units mounted on an interior wall or ceiling. The outdoor unit pulls heat from the air despite low temperatures, and refrigerant carries that heat straight to the indoor heads. Four of the five steps here tune equipment a home has in place, and the heat pump alone calls for buying a new system.
