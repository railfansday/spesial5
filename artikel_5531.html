5 financial products and where FDIC deposit insurance stops

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Free daily briefing on global business news.

FDIC insurance follows the product and the issuer, not the label. See how it treats 5 familiar products, from checking accounts to Treasurys.

Few financial promises have held up as well as federal deposit insurance. Since the guarantee began in 1934, no depositor had lost a cent of insured money as of 2022, according to the FDIC. For money inside the guarantee, the protection is about as certain as anything in finance. With a record like that, the question that matters is not whether the guarantee works but what it actually covers. Coverage depends on the type of product and on who issued it, not on the bank name over the door or the label on a monthly statement. As a result, the money people assume is protected and the money that really is protected do not always match.

Insured and uninsured products can look alike to a customer, so lawmakers and regulators have put the job of telling them apart on the label. In 2012, banks were already required by law to tell customers whether the FDIC insured a product and to print that on account statements and advertising, according to Carol Kaplan, a spokeswoman for the American Bankers Association, in remarks reported by CNBC. Regulators have since revised those rules. Provisions of Part 328, the FDIC regulation on how banks present deposit insurance, took effect on April 1, 2024, according to the Federal Reserve, FDIC and OCC. Regulators' continued attention to these rules suggests the confusion is real. A disclosure can tell a customer that a product lacks insurance, but it cannot tell them what that means for their money. That second question is the one a customer actually needs answered.

Investopedia set out to sort which financial products FDIC deposit insurance protects and which it leaves out. Its picks include checking accounts and cashier's checks, which the guarantee covers, as well as mutual funds and Treasury bills, which it does not. That sorting is a starting point rather than an answer. A product on the covered side can still fall outside the guarantee when a company other than an insured bank holds or issues the money. The uncovered side includes products as different as a stock fund and a government bond, and a product's place on that side says nothing by itself about how safe the money is. For each product, the useful question is what actually stands behind the money and where that protection stops.

A checking account is the plainest case of protected money. Investopedia counts it as a deposit at an insured bank, which means it is covered automatically up to $250,000 in each ownership category. The limit applies to each ownership category rather than to each person, so one customer's total insured amount can rise above $250,000 when the money is held in different categories. For most households, the dollar cap is not where a checking account's protection ends. The weak point is something a monthly statement never shows.

Automatic coverage depends on a condition that the words "checking account" don't reveal: the customer's money has to be on deposit at an insured bank. A customer who signs up through another company is covered only after that company has placed the money with an insured institution, according to the Federal Reserve, FDIC and OCC. The guarantee protects the customer against the failure of that bank. It does nothing for the customer if the nonbank company in between collapses. Checking accounts are counted among covered products, yet the people who run the insurance say customers may not see this gap. "End users may not be aware that access to their funds may depend on the third party and that deposit insurance does not protect against losses resulting from the failure of the third party," the Federal Reserve Board, FDIC and OCC said in a joint statement. The useful test is whether the company holding the money is an insured bank, and if it isn't, which bank is.

Even with an insured bank behind the account, the records decide whether the guarantee reaches each customer. When a company pools many customers' money at one bank, regulators require that each owner's identity and interest can be determined from the records, according to the Federal Reserve, FDIC and OCC. If the company keeps poor records, a customer's claim can be left in doubt. An insured balance is also not always one the customer can reach right away, because the guarantee covers the amount but not a quick way to get it back.

The name describes the product, but the safety of the money depends on who holds it and whether the records prove it belongs to the customer.

A savings account receives the full protection the guarantee was built to give, because it is a deposit, and that protection covers the interest a saver earns as well as the money the saver puts in. Savings are protected dollar for dollar, interest included, up to $250,000, according to Investopedia. Coverage begins automatically when a saver opens the account at an insured bank, so being protected takes no form and no choice from the saver. Because interest is protected under the same limit, it also counts toward that limit. A saver whose balance sits near $250,000 can move past the insured amount through earned interest alone, without depositing another dollar. The guarantee does not fail in that case. The insured amount stays fixed while the balance grows, and the saver has to keep track of the total.

A saver can insure more than $250,000 at a single bank by owning money in more than one way. The limit covers each depositor, at each insured bank, in each ownership category, and every category carries its own limit. The categories are ordinary ones that many savers already use, such as individual, joint, pay-on-death and certain retirement accounts. A saver who opens a second savings account in the same name at the same bank gains no extra protection, while a saver who titles money under a different category can. Someone who spreads assets across several qualifying accounts could in theory reach insured totals of $500,000, $750,000 or $1 million. Those totals depend on arranging the accounts correctly and are not a guaranteed amount.

The savings guarantee is generous but literal: it protects money according to how each account is owned, not according to how safe the saver believes it is.

A cashier's check or a bank money order is a single payment that the issuing bank has promised to honor. When a bank issues these official items, they count as covered deposits, though they are not ordinary accounts, according to Investopedia. The slip in a buyer's hand is protected as a deposit at that bank. That protection comes from the bank's promise to pay and not from the paper itself, so the issuer is the detail that decides everything.

The words "money order" on a slip do not tell the buyer who issued it, and only a bank-issued money order is insured. The FDIC says it never insures companies that are not banks and offers nothing when such a company goes bankrupt. Deposit insurance exists to protect customers when the issuer collapses. Only a buyer whose money order came from a bank has that protection if the issuer fails. For any sum the buyer can't afford to lose, buying the money order from a bank rather than from a non-bank seller is the safer choice.

A bank-issued item carries the same ceiling as other covered deposits. The guarantee reaches at least $250,000, counted separately for each depositor at each bank in each ownership category. That ceiling matters most when people buy these items for large payments, because any amount above it falls outside the guarantee. Buyers can work out their coverage with the FDIC Electronic Deposit Insurance Estimator.

An official item is protected only when a bank issued it, and only up to the limit the guarantee sets.

A mutual fund is outside deposit insurance because of what it is. FDIC coverage depends on whether a product is a deposit product, Investopedia reports, and investments such as mutual funds fall outside that coverage. The FDIC lists mutual funds among the products it does not cover. No bank name on the door, branch location or design of the account statement changes that, because the exclusion rests on the type of product. Knowing that the FDIC will not protect the money settles one question. An investor still needs to know whether anything else protects it.

A fund held in a brokerage account does have some protection, but it comes from a separate program with a narrower purpose. The likely protector of a brokerage account is the Securities Investor Protection Corporation, whose coverage reached up to $500,000 in 2023, with a lower limit for cash, according to CNBC. That is a high limit for an ordinary investor's account if the brokerage firm fails. That coverage depends on how and where the fund is held, so an investor does not receive it automatically the way a bank depositor receives deposit insurance. SIPC also pays nothing when the securities in an account lose value. It guards an investor against the collapse of the firm, not against a drop in market prices.

Deposit insurance guarantees the dollars a saver puts in. SIPC protects an investor only if the firm holding the account fails, and it promises nothing about what the fund is worth. A mutual fund can be worth less tomorrow than it is today, and that possibility is what makes it an investment. No program insures against that risk, and investors accept it in exchange for the chance of a higher return.

Before buying a mutual fund, an investor should decide whether they can afford to see it lose value, because no program will make up the loss.

A U.S. Treasury bill, bond or note is not covered by FDIC deposit insurance because it is not a bank deposit at all. Investopedia lists Treasury bills, bonds and notes among the products the FDIC does not cover, and these securities carried no insurance in 2012, according to CNBC. The exclusion comes from the form of the product, not from any weakness in it. Treasurys therefore show most clearly that "uninsured" and "risky" are separate questions. No insurer backs a Treasury because the buyer is lending to the government itself, not to a bank that could fail.

Financial planners treat Treasurys as the standard for safety even though the FDIC does not cover them. "They're as good as it gets from a safety standpoint," Jude Boudreaux, a CFP and senior financial planner at The Planning Center in New Orleans, told CNBC in 2023. Top-tier safety and a lack of FDIC coverage both apply to Treasurys because of who makes the promise to repay. Deposit insurance backs a bank's promise to its customers. With a Treasury, the government makes that promise directly, with no bank in between. A saver who compares a Treasury with an insured account is weighing two routes to government-backed protection, not a safe product against an unsafe one.

The "not covered" label cannot tell a buyer which uninsured investment is sound. Bond investments are also excluded from the FDIC guarantee, so the same label applies to the government's own debt and to debt from other borrowers. For any product the FDIC does not insure, the safety of the money depends on who issued it.

For a Treasury, the useful question is not whether the FDIC covers it but whose promise the buyer holds, and here that promise comes from the government itself.
