Recently I attended the 35th anniversary celebrations of Smile as a Gift—Úsmev ako dar—a charitable organization in Bratislava, Slovakia, that assists children in foster care and children’s homes, as well as families threatened by poverty, violence, imprisonment, or illness.

Slovakia also has a Commissioner for Children, Jozef Miklosko, who is committed to defending children’s rights under the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child. Jozef and I spent many hours talking about Slovak “social orphans," children who have been separated from their parents because of a complicated mixture of neglect, poverty, illness, imprisonment, abuse, family breakdown, and, more recently, displacement by the war in Ukraine. They are now under the care of the social welfare system. Each child has a different history and reacts differently to harsh conditions. But they are all affected. What is happening in Slovakia is happening everywhere. Hundreds of millions of children in every country are vulnerable to adversity. Growing up under such stressful conditions undermines health, development, and learning.

It was only 28 years ago that researchers introduced the concept of adverse childhood experiences, ACEs, as a way of measuring the cumulative effects of childhood adversity. Since then, many studies have shown that ACEs appear to extend across the lifespan, increasing the likelihood of unhealthy behaviors, mental illness, and a range of chronic physical diseases.

Professor Karen Hughes of Bangor University in the UK and her colleagues conducted a particularly thorough review. The 37 studies involved more than a quarter of a million people. They found that more childhood hardship increases the risk of widespread challenges later in life. The links were relatively weak for obesity, physical inactivity, and diabetes; stronger for smoking, heavy drinking, cancer, heart disease, and respiratory illness; and stronger still for mental health problems and risky sexual behavior. But the most disturbing associations were with drug misuse, violence toward others, and violence directed against oneself.

Most Abused Children Abuse Others in Adulthood

Surprisingly, the commonly held belief that children who suffer physical abuse are at increased risk of becoming abusive parents themselves, and that children who have been sexually abused become adult sexual offenders is refuted by many academic studies. Most abused children do not grow up to abuse others.

Scientific research focused for a long time on the risks associated with adversity. The prevailing deficit model described children from high-stress backgrounds as being at risk for impairments in learning and behavior. The intervention goal was to prevent, reduce, or repair the damage.

Recent studies show that damage and adaptation can occur simultaneously. How do some individuals manage to flourish despite painful experiences that leave others struggling? The following brief biographies may throw some light on the subject that academic studies fail to do.

We are all familiar with Charles Dickens, but most of us don’t know that his father was imprisoned for debt, and at 12, Dickens was sent to work in a shoe-blacking factory. The humiliation and insecurity of that experience haunted him and informed much of his writing. He made the suffering of impoverished children one of the great moral themes of his writing.

Abraham Lincoln’s childhood was marked by poverty, exhausting physical labour, and limited schooling. When Lincoln was 9, his mother died. His father remarried a widow with three children. Lucky for young Abe, his stepmother was not the folkloric evil stepmother but a significant positive influence. She encouraged his love of books, treated him affectionately; they shared a close relationship for the rest of her life.

Although their childhoods differed considerably, Charles Dickens, Abraham Lincoln, and many others I don’t have space to mention spent their childhoods in environments that offered limited educational opportunities. However, they shared an awareness of the limitations of their present lives and found people who encouraged them and helped them to progress. Their childhood experiences shaped what they noticed, cared about, and fought for.

In some instances, having been powerless may make a person particularly aware of the powerlessness of others. Unfair treatment may motivate some individuals to commit to fair play and justice.

While we cannot deny that adversity, especially early adversity, has a corrosive effect, it is equally true that some people, like the ones mentioned here and thousands of others, have overcome and grown from it.

Childhood Adversity Requires More Than Individual Resilience

ACE research shows how experience becomes embedded in our biology and psyche—how childhood becomes embodied.

Recovery from adversity is not about forgetting or papering over the hurts and wounds. Such recovery often takes place in a residential setting, a safe place where truly dedicated and caring people provide many opportunities for reparenting, developing trust, good communication, education, counselling, and a variety of workshops such as those on forgiveness that can help patients grow into the healthy adults they were meant to be. Individual resilience is not enough. Children need people and institutions that give them opportunities to recover.

Adversity is not a witch’s curse, as in Sleeping Beauty; it is not destiny. It is also not necessarily ennobling. It is not one or the other. The human mind is complex. Someone may be highly successful professionally and a failure at personal relationships. Another may struggle with anxiety or depression yet be remarkably generous and compassionate.

You may ask: Do I carry any scars from my childhood? And if so, how have they affected me?

In many cases, adversity predates birth. I shall address that in my next post.
