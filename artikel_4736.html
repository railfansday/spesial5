Why We Might Give Tattooed Strangers a Little More Room

Picture two people standing in line ahead of you. They look identical, except that one has visible tattoos. Would you find one more attractive? Would you feel equally comfortable standing close to both?

We know that physical appearance influences how we judge other people. But I wondered whether something as simple as a tattoo could also affect how comfortable we feel interacting with someone. To investigate this, I conducted a study, recently published in Evolutionary Psychological Science, involving 198 adults in the UK. Participants viewed two computer-generated figures, one male and one female, each shown with and without tattoos. The figures appeared at different distances, ranging from one metre to two-and-a-half metres. Participants rated how comfortable they would feel interacting with each figure at each distance, as well as how attractive they found them.

People generally felt slightly more comfortable with the non-tattooed figures than with their tattooed counterparts. The average comfort ratings were approximately 5.12 versus 4.95 on a seven-point scale. The difference was small but statistically significant, and it appeared for both male and female figures. I had expected tattoos to matter more when someone appeared closer. Surprisingly, they didn't. Although participants felt more comfortable as the distance increased, the effect of tattoos did not significantly change with distance. This suggests that tattoos may influence our general impression of someone rather than specifically making us uncomfortable when that person gets too close.

Here, the results depended on who was looking. Male participants rated both male and female figures as more attractive when they did not have tattoos. Female participants, however, showed no significant difference in attractiveness ratings between tattooed and non-tattooed figures. This is interesting because tattoos have sometimes been associated with masculinity, dominance, and risk-taking, characteristics that could potentially make someone more attractive. Yet women in this study did not find tattooed men more attractive than men without tattoos. In other words, the same physical feature can influence people's judgments differently, depending on the observer.

What I find particularly interesting is that participants knew nothing about the figures they were judging. They had no information about their personalities, backgrounds, or behaviour. Yet changing one visible feature was enough to influence their ratings. It is a small example of something much broader: how readily we form impressions of others based on appearance, often without knowing why.

This question has interested me for years and is also central to my recent book, Psychology of Attractiveness and Aesthetics: How We Perceive Beauty in People and Artwork, in which I explore how our visual experiences shape what we find attractive and aesthetically pleasing, from human faces and bodies to works of art.

The tattoo study adds another piece to that picture. Appearance does not simply influence what we find beautiful. It can also affect how comfortable we feel around other people. And perhaps the most interesting part is that our reactions to someone's appearance may tell us as much about ourselves as they do about the person we are judging.

Pazhoohi, F. The Effect of Tattoos on Comfort Distance and Attractiveness Perceptions. Evolutionary Psychological Science. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40806-026-00506-1
