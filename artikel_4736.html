How can you reconnect with yourself if you have rejection sensitive dysphoria? Many of us with this lose touch with ourselves, as rejection sensitive dysphoria is an intense emotional sensitivity to being judged, criticized, or failing at essential roles or relationships. To avoid the intense pain of rejection, many people who experience this dysphoria develop habits like perfectionism, people-pleasing, and masking. These behavioral patterns make rejection sensitive dysphoria more than just how we interact with rejection; they can activate these patterns and significantly affect our lives (Mutti-Driscoll, 2026).

To help reverse the effects, reconnecting with yourself is important.

To do that, it helps to use the "wise mind" concept from Marsha Linehan's dialectical behavior therapy. According to Linehan, we all have a wise mind: the part of ourselves not driven solely by logic or emotion, but a hybrid of the two. This is our most mature self, and even if we cannot access this mind, it is still there.

Richard Schwartz, who created internal family systems theory, also examined this: beyond our reactive patterns of avoidance and control, there is self. This is our true self or our most authentic self—grounded, embodied, authentic, and never lost. Even if we feel mired in attempts to avoid or control, it is still there, and we can still find it. That is the part of the journey where we can reconnect with who we really are (Dore, 2021).

Reconnecting with yourself is a journey. I wish I could say it happens instantaneously, but it is a journey. And these are things we will have to do over time to establish and maintain that connection.

We can have compassion and advocate for that child. The reason we might advocate for ourselves as children is that it can be easier to have compassion for our younger selves. Also, experts think there was something pure about us, especially before age 10, before we started absorbing expectations. If we can access what we liked to do and enjoyed, we can sometimes reconnect with something more authentic than how we became as we advanced through the teen and adult years.

Oftentimes, that is a strength for neurodivergent people—getting back to creativity if we have strayed from it. We can reflect on past creative moments, not just when we are making art; we can be creative in problem-solving, helping others, or organizing people. We can intentionally tap into current interests we may have felt embarrassed about and have not pursued, or we can reignite old ones.

Think about what is important to us, and try to reconnect with the actions that bring us closer to it and those that take us further away. Developing this is like a foundation for our true self. When we know what is important to us, it's easier to access that self. We can find ways to remember it and clarify what that means, making it less abstract: What would it mean to live my values each day?

Values are important; it is not just feel-good fluff. For many of us with ADHD and autism, connecting to what is meaningful is huge for stimulating our brains and motivating us to persist and get things done (Solden and Frank, 2019).

Even if we do not feel super loving toward ourselves, we can still take actions that care for ourselves (Dore, 2021). We can be the parent who takes care of us: What would we do for ourselves even if it was hard to initiate or too boring? What would we do to show ourselves love? We can also make those tasks less painful and more engaging through rewards and accountability.

You can choose your own adventure when you reconnect.

A reminder: This is a journey. If you try one of these strategies and it doesn't work instantaneously, it is not a failure, and you are not a failure. It is a journey, and putting it in an all-or-nothing box is not the goal:

The journey of personal growth is up and down. It is not about arriving, which I know we'd prefer. It is about holding these opposites at the same time: We can make progress, knowing there is more to do. That is okay. We can still celebrate our growth and be proud of ourselves while knowing we still want to improve—that is part of being human.

Any small action we can take to reconnect with ourselves is an important investment in healing from rejection sensitive dysphoria. I hope you will join me in this important journey! You are worth it.
