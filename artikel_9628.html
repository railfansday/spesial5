Same Product, Different Price: What an AI Shopping Agent Learned From 24,394 Items

Finding prices is easy. Proving two listings are the same product is the hard part.

Ask any AI shopping agent to find the best price and it will happily return one. The harder question is whether that price is for the same thing. A vacuum listed at two stores can differ by model year, bundle or pack size, and a price comparison that misses that is worse than none at all.

Deal.ai, an AI shopping agent built by Famous Labs, has been measuring both problems: how much prices really differ for identical products, and how hard it is to know two listings are identical in the first place.

From October 29 to November 4, 2026, Deal.ai compared 24,394 new products sold at two or more of 16 major U.S. retailers, including Amazon, Walmart, Target and Best Buy. Each store's price had to be read within an hour of the others.

The results were consistent. About two in three products had prices at least 5% apart across the big retailers. Among the 4,684 products sold at four or more retailers, a shopper who checked one store picked at random paid, on average, 15.6% above the lowest listed price. Checking two stores cut that to 7.4%, three stores to 3.7%, and four to 1.1%.

The biggest names were not always cheapest. At the same moment, another big retailer listed the item for less 37% of the time for Walmart products, 34% for Amazon, 41% for Best Buy and 51% for Target.

Those numbers only mean something if every price is for the same item. Deal.ai's answer is to separate matching from pricing.

In a live recheck on November 5, Deal.ai drew 600 products at random and kept the ones with a brand and a model number or barcode. A separate AI model then read each store's own listing title, with prices hidden, and kept only listings that were the identical item: same model, size, capacity, pack count and generation. Colour could differ; bundles, refurbished and open-box listings could not.

Of 4,990 store listings, 1,886 passed. That is a strict filter by design. It throws away near-matches that a looser system would happily compare, which keeps a "lower price" from really being a smaller pack or last year's model. Hiding prices from the matching model matters too: it stops the model from being swayed toward a match by a price that looks plausible.

"Finding prices is the easy part. The hard part is making sure every store is selling the exact same product, the same model and the same size," said Alex Mehr, Ph.D., co-founder and CEO of Famous Labs, the company behind Deal.ai. "That's what our agents are built to check, so shoppers can trust the price comparison they see."

Deal.ai puts that work behind a simple interface. Shoppers ask for what they want in plain English, and the agent researches the options, compares live prices across thousands of stores and sends them to the store with the best deal. Its agents also run every hour to surface unusually good deals, each with a deal score out of 10. A free deal checker takes a link to any deal and finds the same item at every big store.

For developers, Deal.ai also offers an MCP server, so other AI assistants can call it directly instead of scraping stores themselves.

Price data is abundant. Trustworthy comparisons are not. The gap between one store and the best price is large enough to matter to shoppers, but only if the agent is honest about which listings truly match. Strict matching, with prices hidden from the matcher, is a simple rule that makes every number after it more believable.

Deal.ai is free at deal.ai. The full study is published on Deal.ai's research page.
