The crackle of a fire on a cold winter night can add charm to any home. However, fireplaces aren’t particularly efficient heat sources, especially in chilly temperatures. And electric fireplaces, space heaters, and other supplementary heat sources can leave you in the dark (and out in the cold) during a power outage.

Instead, consider adding a wood stove—an appliance that provides a country aesthetic and serves as a primary or secondary heat source. Modern wood stoves can be safe, affordable, and efficient if you clean and maintain them regularly.

More than 2 million of the roughly 12.5 million wood stoves in the U.S. are older, uncertified models that put out far more particulate pollution than newer ones, according to the EPA, and some states prohibit or restrict these wood-burning devices. All of the wood stoves recommended here are EPA-certified, and they still crank out enough BTUs to make your home or cabin cozy.

Before you buy, think carefully about where you’ll install your stove. You’ll need proper clearance from combustible materials in walls and floors, and the stove’s weight has to work for your house.

Wood stoves come in all shapes, sizes, and designs. Choose one that suits your space in terms of style and efficiency. Here’s how to decide what you need in a wood stove and what you don't.

There are two types of modern wood stoves: catalytic and non-catalytic. Both reduce air pollution by reburning exhaust to limit emissions, but catalytic stoves use a different process that is slightly more efficient at this second burn. Non-catalytic stoves are popular because they’re less expensive and slightly more efficient at producing heat.

Because the cost of a wood stove with a catalytic converter is currently prohibitive for the average homeowner, the wood stove models we’ve recommended below are all non-catalytic.

The size of your wood stove matters not just for the space it can heat, but also for safety. Before you install a wood stove, check your state requirements for clearance. Federal guidelines stipulate that the stove should be installed at least 3 feet from any combustibles in the wall, ceiling, or floors.

If you have children, also consider how much clearance you might need around the stove for a child safety gate and where you plan to stack and store firewood.

British Thermal Units (BTUs) might sound fancy, but it's just a measurement of how much heat is being generated. This differs from the heating area of a wood stove because it refers to the energy the stove produces in an hour. A rule of thumb: You need 20 BTUs for every foot of space you want to heat. Use this online BTU calculator to dial in exactly how many thermal units your home needs.

Decide whether you’re using your wood stove as a main heating source or for supplemental heat in the winter. In general, a small wood stove can heat about 500 square feet, while a medium wood stove warms up to 1,000 square feet. A large model will heat a room of 1,500 square feet or more.

More isn’t always better. To keep costs in check and your wood stove efficient, consider if you really need to heat every space in your home or just the living areas your family frequents.

Modern wood stoves are lighter than ever, but many large models can still tip the scales at several hundred pounds. Depending on the stove's weight, you may not be able to install it in a mobile home or in certain structurally vulnerable spots in your house. Instead, look for a wood stove approved for mobile homes by the Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD).

If your goal is an outdoor wood stove portable enough for camping or backpacking, choose a lightweight model with components that collapse into a compact package.

The size of the logs and the kind of wood you put into your wood stove is about more than convenience or cost. It can also affect burn time and efficiency, which is especially important if you’re using your wood stove as a main heating source.

Generally, most wood stoves fit logs about 16 inches long and 3 inches in diameter. As you can imagine, wood stoves designed for smaller logs may leave you rolling up your sleeves and grabbing the axe more often than you might like.

Since 2020, nearly all new wood stoves sold in the United States must be certified by the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) to meet its 2020 emissions standards, which limit fine particle pollution to 2.0 grams per hour (2.5 g/h when tested with cordwood). All of the wood stoves we’ve recommended below meet this standard, and you can look up any model in the EPA’s database of certified wood-burning heaters.

A wood stove’s effectiveness in converting wood into heat is expressed as a percentage of combustion efficiency. Efficiency matters because it directly translates into how much wood you’ll burn to keep your home warm. Most modern EPA-certified stoves fall between 70 and 80 percent efficiency, and every few points add up over a full heating season in fewer logs split, stacked, and hauled inside.

From insulating the chimney to adhering to proper stovepipe clearance, to wall shields and floor pads, there’s quite a bit to do when you install a wood stove, and much of it is mandated by building codes and state or federal laws. To protect your safety, it's critical that you don't miss a step, which is why I recommend leaving the installation of the wood stove to a professional.

Clean your wood stove (and chimney) thoroughly and often to avoid creosote buildup, a highly flammable byproduct of burning wood.

While cleaning your chimney, tidying up your wood stove is pretty straightforward. When the stove is cool, lay down a cloth or newspaper, then empty the ashtray and any ashes from the firebox into an ash bucket. Then wait at least 24 to 48 hours before you dump the ash bucket so any slightly warm embers don’t catch fire.

You can wipe down the interior with a soft, wet cloth and use a chimney brush to scrub out the stovepipe and loosen any residue. If the exterior has rust, you can use a wire brush or sandpaper to remove it.

Just be aware that if you plan to leave the fire unattended and it goes out overnight, you’ll have to restart it, which is harder than simply feeding a live fire.
