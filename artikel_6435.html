Anti-aging pills and peptides aren’t the legal gray area they seem, but proving they work is harder

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

Most anti-aging products already count as drugs under U.S. law, a new analysis finds. However, proving that they work at all is not simple.

Pills, powders and injections sold as longevity products to slow aging are sometimes said to fall into a legal gray area, since U.S. regulators don’t treat aging as a disease. A new legal analysis finds that view mistaken.

Under U.S. law, a product doesn’t have to treat a disease to count as a drug. Anything meant to change how the body works qualifies, and that covers most longevity products.

The harder problem is scientific, the authors wrote: showing that a product slows aging takes a long time, and researchers don’t yet agree on what to measure in the meantime.

Camila Strassle and Holly Fernandez Lynch of the Department of Medical Ethics and Health Policy at the University of Pennsylvania’s Perelman School of Medicine wrote the analysis. Patricia J. Zettler of Ohio State University’s Moritz College of Law was a co-author.

It’s a legal argument, not a test of any product, and it uses the United States as its example.

The best-known longevity products include metformin, rapamycin, NAD+ precursors, resveratrol, fisetin, quercetin and peptides, which are short chains of amino acids.

Some already have approval from the Food and Drug Administration for other jobs. Metformin is approved to control blood sugar, and rapamycin to suppress the immune system. Many others that are popular for longevity have never been approved for anything.

Camila Strassle told Earth.com that anyone weighing a longevity product should know that “none of these products have been approved by the FDA for anti-aging, meaning that they haven’t been proved safe or effective for that purpose.”

The U.S. National Institute on Aging set up the Translational Geroscience Network to standardize research in the field. More than 80 trials are affiliated with it.

Many companies, though, sell products for anti-aging effects without running studies to show they’re safe or that they work for that purpose.

The federal law that governs medicines defines a drug in two ways. One half covers products meant to diagnose, treat or prevent disease. The other covers products meant to affect the structure or any function of the body.

That second half means a product can be a drug without being a treatment. So whether the FDA calls aging a disease or not doesn’t decide whether the agency oversees longevity products, or whether it can approve them.

The FDA has done this before. It approved hair growth products, and Botox for smoothing facial lines, as drugs even though neither targets a disease.

It has also signaled it’s willing to approve two drugs meant to extend dogs’ lives, with no disease claim attached.

The law defines a drug the same way for animals and people. So the authors read that as a sign the FDA will treat many human longevity products as drugs too.

Some longevity products are sold as dietary supplements, which don’t need FDA approval before they go on sale. However, many of them don’t qualify.

A supplement has to be swallowed, so anything injected, rubbed on the skin, worn as a patch or sprayed in the nose can’t be one. A product meant to prevent or treat a disease of aging isn’t a supplement either, whatever its label says.

Selling a product “for research purposes only” doesn’t get around the law. Unapproved drugs can go to people enrolled in a clinical trial the FDA has authorized. Companies can’t sell them to the public in the name of research.

Prescribing an already approved drug for longevity, a practice called off-label use, has the strongest legal standing short of approval for aging itself. But many longevity products have no approval to start from, and drug makers generally can’t advertise off-label uses.

Some states want to stretch their “right to try” laws to cover unapproved longevity products. Those laws have been limited to patients with life-threatening conditions who want experimental drugs outside a trial.

Montana expanded its law to cover everyone, including healthy people, in 2025, and New Hampshire has proposed similar bills. However, states generally can’t exempt anyone from federal law, the authors state, so those efforts have limited legal effect.

Compounding, in which a pharmacy makes a custom version of a drug for one patient, is another route. It’s allowed only for a distinct patient need, such as an allergy to an inactive ingredient, and only with certain substances.

In July 2026, an FDA advisory panel narrowly voted to recommend adding several peptides, including some used for longevity, to the list that pharmacies may compound.

Many of the panelists had financial interests in peptide businesses. They voted that way even though the peptides’ safety and effectiveness hadn’t been shown and FDA staff had raised serious concerns.

Even with the rules in place, the FDA has what’s called enforcement discretion. This is the freedom to decide not to act against companies that ignore these rules.

Under leadership friendly to the longevity industry, the agency might give these products low priority. It seems ready to do that for certain peptides.

“Legitimate industry should welcome such enforcement, and the public should demand it,” the authors wrote.

Strassle said enforcement matters for more than the evidence behind a product. She explained that it ensures products contain what their labels state, and in the doses claimed, which helps protect buyers.

Enforcement also protects companies that pay for real studies from rivals selling unproven ones.

Strassle said little has changed since the analysis was finished. “The enforcement landscape hasn’t shifted significantly since we finished the piece.”

Strassle said the authors see a long pattern behind the market. “There is a long history of people making scientifically unsupported promises to capitalize on people’s very reasonable desires to live long and healthy lives.”

The authors hope that the government will step in as it has for many other kinds of medical treatments.

Skipping that oversight has cost lives before. In 2012, a fungal meningitis outbreak traced to compounded steroid injections killed more than 50 people and sickened hundreds.

The most direct path is approval for a structure or function claim, which means showing that a product causes the change in the body that it promises. Ordinary clinical studies can test that, though it would still be unproven whether the change adds healthy years.

Proving that a product prevents disease or extends healthy life is slower, because those outcomes take a long time to show up.

Drug trials often measure a stand-in instead, a marker that predicts the outcome that matters and changes sooner. Researchers call these surrogate endpoints.

There’s no agreement yet on which ones would work for aging. Candidates were discussed at a public meeting in May 2026 held by the Reagan-Udall Foundation for the FDA and the Advanced Research Projects Agency for Health.

Which measurements can reliably stand in for a longer, healthier life is still an open question. The authors want the FDA, regulators in other countries and research funders to work with longevity researchers on what evidence is both possible to gather and strong enough to trust.

The full study was published in the journal Science.
