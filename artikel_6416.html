Monterrey’s mountain folds tell a story millions of years in the making

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

An astronaut photograph reveals Monterrey’s sweeping mountain ridges, where ancient rock folds shape the landscape around a growing Mexican city.

Monterrey has spread across the plains of northeastern Mexico, but its mountains leave little room for straight lines.

Long ridges bend beside the city, tracing folds in the rock that formed tens of millions of years before streets and factories occupied the landscape.

Today’s Image of the Day captures this meeting of urban growth and ancient geology.

Photographed from the International Space Station on August 26, 2026, the scene shows Monterrey alongside the sweeping ridges of the Sierra Madre Oriental.

From the ground, these mountains form a familiar skyline.

From orbit, their repeating curves reveal a much larger pattern: a landscape compressed, lifted, and gradually carved into the terrain that surrounds the city today.

The ridges along Monterrey’s southern edge began as layers of sediment that became limestone during the late Mesozoic era.

Their present shape developed much later, as forces within Earth’s crust squeezed and folded the rock.

Mountain building in the region occurred roughly 80 million to 50 million years ago.

Rather than remaining flat, the layers buckled into a series of folds, creating the foundation of the parallel ridges visible today.

Erosion then changed the relief. Over millions of years, weaker layers wore away more readily than resistant rock, helping turn the folded formations into distinct ridges and valleys.

That sequence explains why the mountains appear to follow one another across the landscape. Their curves reflect related layers shaped by the same broad episode of deformation.

The photograph offers a view of both processes at once. Folding established the pattern in the rock; erosion made that pattern more apparent at the surface.

Monterrey is the capital of Nuevo León and a major center of Mexican industry.

Steelmaking and ironworks form part of an economy that also produces glass, plastics, textiles, and processed foods.

Its metropolitan area had about 5.3 million residents in the 2020 census. That figure describes the wider urban region, rather than just the municipality of Monterrey.

The city occupies a semiarid floodplain beside the mountains. As development spreads, the contrast between the built environment and the surrounding terrain becomes especially striking.

A city map can show roads and municipal boundaries. This view from space adds another set of boundaries: steep slopes, narrow valleys, and rocky ridges that influence where development can extend.

The mountains are more than a backdrop to Monterrey. Their position helps define the shape of the urban area, bringing ancient geological structures directly into the geography of everyday life.

The Río Santa Catarina connects the mountain landscape with the city below. After cutting through the Sierra Madre Oriental, it crosses the urban area and carries runoff from its watershed.

For much of the time, the river holds little water or appears dry. In this semiarid setting, however, a quiet channel still has an essential function.

Summer rainfall can send substantial volumes of water through it. The channel provides a route for that runoff to move across the metropolitan area.

It also supports living communities within the city. Vegetation along the river provides habitat for plants and animals and creates connections through an otherwise heavily developed landscape.

Part of the river channel in the metropolitan area received state protection as an urban park in 2008. Its value extends across seasons, serving wildlife even when little flowing water is visible.

Separate mountain masses also rise beside Monterrey’s urban neighborhoods. Among them is Sierra Las Mitras, a protected ridge that reaches approximately 4,900 feet above the city.

Its slopes hold a changing sequence of habitats. Cacti and thorny shrubs occupy lower, rocky ground, while cooler, wetter conditions support oak and pine forests higher up.

That change shows how elevation can create different environments within a relatively small area. The journey uphill passes through habitats that contrast sharply with the dry terrain below.

Cerro de la Silla, known for its saddle-shaped profile, adds another distinctive landmark to Monterrey’s mountain setting.

As Monterrey expands, its ancient ridges continue to shape the city’s boundaries and guide its growth.
