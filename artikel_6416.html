Scientists found a hidden ‘aging clock’ in human blood that can predict future diseases and early death

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Scientists have figured out a new way to measure how fast your body is actually aging on the inside, rather than just counting the years you have been alive.

Two people born the same year can have bodies that age at different speeds. A routine blood draw may now be enough to tell which is which.

In a study of 18,476 people aged 1 to 96, some had blood chemistry that read older than their birth certificate. Those people went on to develop fatty liver disease, high blood pressure and diabetes more often.

More of them died over the following years, too.

The older readings lined up with everyday habits. Smokers’ blood read about 1.2 years older than nonsmokers’, and people who exercised read about half a year younger.

Linlin Cao, Kun Qian, Jun Pu and Wei Xu of Renji Hospital at Shanghai Jiao Tong University led the study, with participants from six clinical centers in China. It’s observational, so it shows patterns, not causes.

Blood plasma carries thousands of small molecules left over from the body’s chemistry. The field that measures them is called metabolomics.

The team measured 232 of these molecules, in each person, with a laser-based instrument that needs a droplet of plasma and about 30 seconds per sample.

From those, 56 molecules changed most reliably with age. Some rose steadily, such as creatinine, a waste product of muscle.

Others fell early in life and stayed low, such as a building block of collagen that was highest in childhood and dropped fast before age 20.

A set of 12 machine learning models, stacked together, used the 56 molecules to guess each person’s age. The guesses were close.

In 5,252 healthy people used for training, the guess landed within about two years on average. In 5,456 healthy people held back as a test, it landed within about 4.5 years.

The difference between that guess and a person’s real age is the number that matters. The team called it metabolomic age acceleration, and a positive value means the blood reads older than the calendar.

Everyone gave blood in 2019. The healthy participants answered a questionnaire on diet, sleep, smoking, exercise and family history, and 3,745 of them returned for a health check in 2023. Deaths were tracked through January 2025.

The team compared the quarter of healthy people with the most accelerated blood chemistry against the quarter with the slowest.

The accelerated quarter had more new cases of fatty liver disease, high blood pressure and diabetes, and the same held for coronary heart disease and stroke.

The accelerated quarter also died at a higher rate. There were 291 deaths among the 6,169 people with death records, and the accelerated quarter accounted for a larger share of them.

For predicting death, the blood clock edged out two standard yardsticks, a frailty index and the American Heart Association’s Life’s Essential 8 score. It also added information when combined with them.

People of the same age can follow different aging paths, and this is one way to see it.

Several habits lined up with the clock. Current smoking was associated with blood chemistry about 1.2 years older, secondhand smoke with about 0.6 years, shift work with about 0.9 years, and hair dye with about a year.

Sugar, fried food, processed meat and organ meat were also associated with an older reading. Exercise was associated with a reading about half a year younger, and mixed grains with about a third of a year younger.

In 1,500 people who gave blood again in 2023, the clock moved with their habits. Those who became more active or ate less fried food saw their blood chemistry read younger by two to three years.

People newly exposed to secondhand smoke, eating more preserved food, or crossing into overweight saw it read older. The authors wrote that these habit links are observational and shouldn’t be read as causes.

Other studies have tied biological aging to teenage body weight, early menopause and even the time between pregnancies. Air pollution also leaves a chemical trail in the blood.

In 7,768 people who already had a chronic disease, the age guess worked less well, so the team built a different measure.

Using the same 56 molecules, they modeled each person’s risk of death and then asked how far it sat above or below what their age would predict.

They called that age deviation. Eight molecules carried the death signal, and the strongest was 3-methylindole, made by gut microbes from the amino acid tryptophan. The collagen building block, 4-hydroxyproline, pointed the other way, toward lower risk.

Higher age deviation went with worse kidney numbers, more organ systems affected and more conditions at once. Chronic kidney disease had the largest link.

People with high blood pressure who got it under control by 2023 saw their age deviation improve more than those who didn’t.

Nearly everyone in the study was Chinese, so the clock needs testing in other populations before it travels. Most people gave blood only once, and the authors wrote that more frequent sampling and longer follow-up are needed.

The identities of some of the 56 molecules are confident matches rather than certainties.

The open question is whether changing a habit to move the clock changes what happens to a person afterward. The authors wrote that intervention studies would be needed to find this out.

The age-prediction tool, called MetS-Age, is public for researchers to run on their own data.

The full study was published in the journal Science Translational Medicine.
