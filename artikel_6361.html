Some dishwashers might have to be hardwired to a dedicated circuit rather than using a plug-and-play connection. Hardwired dishwashers are more secure and permanent, while units with power cords are easier to install and offer more convenient disconnection in case of repairs. Both styles require access to a 120-volt, grounded outlet. Check the specs of the dishwasher you’re considering to ensure it aligns with your desired connection type.

Most households will be fine with a standard built-in dishwasher that slides under the counter and hooks into existing plumbing. However, portable and countertop models have come a long way and are now legitimate options for renters and small kitchens. Options like the GE Portable Freestanding Dishwasher, roll to the sink when needed and store away when you’re done.

Meanwhile, countertop dishwashers live on a counter or sturdy cart, and are ideal for smaller spaces like studios, where a full-size unit may not fit. Just know that compact models often compromise on features like noise reduction and capacity due to their smaller size.

For most households, dishwashers generally come in two widths: the standard 24-inch and the compact 18-inch. A 24-inch model is usually the best choice for families, having enough room for bulky cookware and larger loads. In contrast, an 18-inch model is a space-saving solution for small kitchens or narrow cabinet cutouts. I used an 18-inch machine in a previous apartment and found it filled up fast, forcing us to run cycles more frequently.

Manufacturers like to quote capacity in “place settings,” but what really matters day to day is interior flexibility and vertical space. Look for a tall tub design, which maximizes interior height so you can fit oversized items like baking sheets and pots on the bottom rack. A model with a third rack and an adjustable second rack, like the Whirlpool Eco Series Top Control Dishwasher, will give you enough flexibility to wash an entire day’s worth of cookware in a single load.

One of the biggest differentiators between modern dishwashers and older models is noise, which is measured in decibels (dB). Anything around 50 decibels or below is generally considered very quiet and shouldn’t overpower a normal conversation. Standard units that produce sound at around 51 decibels are more noticeable, especially during spray and drain cycles.

If you’re particularly sensitive to noise or often run the dishwasher at night outside of your energy company’s peak hours, look for models that promise quieter performance. Many of the newest dishwashers run quietly enough that you’ll barely notice them in the background. There have been multiple times when I’ve opened my Samsung dishwasher at home mid-cycle because I didn’t hear it running.

One of the most frustrating parts of using a dishwasher is opening the door to find wet dishes. Fortunately, modern dishwashers utilize several methods to ensure they come out bone-dry.

Some rely on traditional heated dry cycles, which raise interior temperature to evaporate moisture. Others take a more energy-efficient approach called condensation drying, where residual heat from the hot rinse water evaporates moisture against the cooler stainless steel walls. Newer models, like the Midea 24-Inch Top Control Built-in Dishwasher, have an auto-open feature, which automatically pops open the door at the end of the cycle to vent steam and ensure that even plastic containers dry completely.

Modern dishwashers pack more features than ever. When shopping around, you’ll see things like third racks for utensils, height-adjustable upper racks, soil sensors, foldable tines, and WiFi and app control. Many of these features give you the flexibility to load your dishes more efficiently, which means you can wash more items in a single load and are less likely to need to rewash plates or glasses.

Also consider where a dishwasher’s controls are (hidden on top versus front-facing), whether the tub is stainless steel, and other things that could potentially enhance your experience. Just remember that every added feature usually nudges the price up. Whatever you buy, make a habit of cleaning your dishwasher a couple times per year to keep it running like new.

Most households will be fine with a standard 24-inch dishwasher, which offers enough room for everyday dishes and larger cookware. But if you’re working with a smaller kitchen or apartment, an 18-inch model saves valuable real estate. “Your choice depends on available space, how many dishes you typically wash, and the size of the pans you cook with,” says Glenn Lewis, president of Mr. Appliance, a Neighborly company.

Yes, and it’s worth checking before you buy. “They need a dedicated 120-volt electrical connection, a water supply line, and a drain connection, all of which must meet local building codes for safe operation,” Lewis says. If you’re replacing an existing unit, the connections are likely already in place, but if you’re adding a dishwasher for the first time, you may need to hire a professional to set up the plumbing and electrical.

It’s possible, but proceed with caution. “You can if you have basic plumbing and electrical knowledge, but improper installation can lead to leaks or electrical issues, so many homeowners opt for professional installation,” Lewis says. If you’re swapping out an old unit and the connections are already there, a DIY install is more manageable. But for a first-time installation, professional help is usually the safer bet.

An Energy Star certification means the dishwasher meets strict efficiency standards that might save you money over time. “Energy Star-certified models use less water and electricity, not only to run the dishwasher but to heat the water used by the dishwasher,” Lewis says. “Lower energy means lower utility bills and reduced environmental impact over time.” If you’re choosing between two similar models, going with the Energy Star-certified option is usually the smarter long-term investment.
