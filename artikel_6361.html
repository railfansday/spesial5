Telling wild frogs apart often means clipping toes, but one treefrog can be recognized by its eyes

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

An Amazonian treefrog’s golden eyes carry a distinct pattern of black lines, letting scientists recognize frogs without clipping their toes.

Field biologists who need to follow individual frogs often mark each one by clipping off a toe. In one Amazonian treefrog, the eyes carry a mark of their own.

The egg-eating spiny-backed frog, Osteocephalus oophagus, has golden irises crossed by thin black lines that spread out from the pupil, and the pattern differs from frog to frog, a bit like a fingerprint.

So a frog that has been photographed once can be picked out later from a picture of its eye, without cutting it and, most of the time, without touching it.

Mélissa Peignier, a researcher in the University of Bern’s Division of Behavioural Ecology in Switzerland, ran the project with eight colleagues, including first author Lauriane Bégué.

Over three rainy seasons in French Guiana, the team photographed the eyes of 103 frogs and recognized most of them again later in the same season.

Peignier first noticed the treefrogs while working on a different animal, a poison frog common in South American rainforests.

“At the time, I was doing my Ph.D., studying Allobates femoralis in the field in French Guiana,” Peignier said.

“My first reaction was simply something along the lines of, ‘Wow, what beautiful eyes!’ That initial curiosity gradually turned into a genuine interest in the species, and I realized how little we actually know about them.”

Wildlife researchers have long marked animals with tags, brands or clipped toes so they can tell them apart later.

For amphibians, toe-clipping has often been the standard method, even though it can affect a frog’s survival, body functions and behavior.

Osteocephalus oophagus spends its days in the forest canopy and comes down at night to about 6.5 feet (2 meters) above the ground.

“Other studies have used toe-clipping, which involves removing some of the digits. Personally, I was uncomfortable with this approach, especially given that these are arboreal frogs that rely on their limbs to climb trees,” Peignier said.

Many frogs can be identified by their skin markings, the way zebras can by their stripes, but this species’ back markings are faint, patchy and missing on some frogs.

Iris recognition was first built for people and has since been tested on geckos, cattle, goats and tigers. Frog irises had been used to tell species apart, but never to recognize individual frogs.

The fieldwork ran from March to May in 2021, 2022 and 2023. The team worked on a river island of about 12 acres (5 hectares) in French Guiana’s Nouragues Nature Reserve, near the Nouragues Ecological Research Station.

Male treefrogs come down from the canopy every night to call for mates. Females come down every four to seven days.

The team searched from 6:40 p.m. to 2 a.m. They checked 39 sets of small flowerpot pools set about 4 feet (130 centimeters) off the ground, along with tree hollows and other natural pools.

The first time a treefrog turned up, a researcher caught it by hand, photographed it and let it go where it was found. Close-ups of its eyes went into a photo library on a rugged tablet.

At every later meeting, the researchers photographed both eyes without touching the frog. If they found a match in the library, they left the frog alone. One eye was usually enough, and the second served as a check.

“Honestly, the frogs themselves are incredibly beautiful and surprisingly chill, which makes working with them a real pleasure,” Peignier said.

Across the three seasons, the team logged 392 captures of 103 different frogs. In 2021, 16 of 24 frogs were found and recognized again, later that season. This amounts to 66.7 percent of initial captures.

In 2022 the share was 47 of 59 (79.7 percent), and in 2023 it was 13 of 23 (56.5 percent) initial captures.

Every season, more than half of the frogs turned up again and were matched by their eyes alone. So the iris patterns stayed stable long enough for the team to follow frogs through a breeding season.

The researchers checked their identifications with Wild-ID, free software developed at Dartmouth College to match animals’ coat and skin patterns. They ran it once on left-eye photos and once on right-eye photos, so each eye was checked on its own.

Mating pairs of treefrogs posed no problem. The team could photograph a male clinging to a female’s back and didn’t have to pull the two apart.

When a pair did have to be handled at a first meeting, the frogs usually resumed their mating embrace within an hour.

Three frogs first caught in 2021 were found again in 2022. None of the 23 frogs seen in 2023 had been recorded before.

The three returning frogs showed little to no visible change in their irises. The team’s best explanation is that frogs moved away or were replaced by new ones, not that their eyes changed.

But the researchers used no second marking method, so they couldn’t tell whether missing frogs were still on the island and simply went unrecognized.

Results from other animals are mixed. Human iris patterns stay the same for life.

In Atlantic salmon, iris patterns held steady for only several months, while in one gecko species they stayed stable over the long term.

Several other frogs have complex irises, including Osteocephalus taurinus, Hyloscirtus tigrinus and Sachatamia ilex. But a pattern can be too complex to match quickly in the field, as may be the case for Trachycephalus hadroceps.

In contrast, the plain irises of the red-eyed treefrog, Agalychnis callidryas, may not differ enough from frog to frog.

The authors recommend testing the method species by species before it’s used more widely. Peignier hopes other researchers will adopt it.

“In a world where biodiversity is declining at an alarming rate, it is essential that we minimize our impact on the animals we study,” she said.

“If we can reliably identify individuals using naturally occurring features, we may be able to reduce the need for more invasive marking techniques while still collecting the data needed to answer important scientific questions.”

For O. oophagus, the open question is whether one photo can identify a frog for life. Answering it will take frogs studied in captivity, or eye photos paired with a second identification method, such as genetic testing or a permanent mark.

The full study was published in the journal Herpetozoa.
