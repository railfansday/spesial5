Satellites measure a changing Earth with help from a laser in the Arctic

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

A laser station in Svalbard tracks satellites as they pass over the Arctic, helping scientists measure changes in ice and sea level.

Today’s Image of the Day looks down on the west coast of Spitsbergen, where glaciers reach the sea and tan streaks cut through turquoise water.

Near the shore of one of those fjords, a research station does much of its work in the opposite direction. It aims a laser into the sky to find out exactly where satellites are as they pass overhead.

Landsat 9 captured this view of Norway’s Svalbard archipelago on July 30, 2026.

The laser system at the Ny-Ålesund Geodetic Observatory officially debuted that October.

It’s the northernmost satellite laser ranging facility in the world, a distinction that matters because of how often some satellites pass within reach.

Imagine tracing a line around a globe that passes close to both poles. Turn the globe a little and draw another line along the same path.

The two lines cross the equator far apart, but they come close together in the far north. As Earth rotates beneath a satellite in a near-polar orbit, its path over the ground follows much the same pattern.

That gives Ny-Ålesund more chances to track the same satellite. NASA’s station in Greenbelt, Maryland, might catch a polar-orbiting satellite two or three times a day.

At Ny-Ålesund, a satellite on that kind of route can come within view on almost every orbit.

The Arctic station also fills a geographic gap in a network of more than 40 laser ranging stations around the world.

The station sends a short pulse of light toward a satellite equipped with special reflectors.

Some of that light returns to a detector on the ground. Since scientists know how fast light travels, they can convert the round-trip time to a distance.

Half the total distance traveled is the range from the station to the satellite. A series of those measurements helps researchers work out the satellite’s path.

The new system can send thousands of pulses a second, compared with tens per second for older equipment.

Its detector is sensitive enough to register a single photon, the smallest unit of light. That matters because a satellite may send back only a tiny fraction of the light that leaves the ground.

A faster laser gives the station more chances to collect those faint returns during a pass.

One satellite that can benefit from the Svalbard station is ICESat-2. It carries a laser of its own and points it down to measure the height of ice sheets, sea ice and other surfaces.

In that case, the satellite times light returning from Earth. At Ny-Ålesund, the station times light returning from the satellite.

The two measurements answer different questions that matter to the same mission.

To see why, imagine measuring the height of a glacier from an aircraft. Knowing the distance down to the ice gets you partway there.

You also need to know the aircraft’s altitude at the moment of the measurement. The same principle applies in orbit.

A precise record of where ICESat-2 was when it made a reading helps researchers determine the height of the surface below and compare it with measurements from later passes.

Those comparisons depend on a common set of coordinates called the terrestrial reference frame.

It gives scientists a consistent way to describe where objects are on and around Earth, even as the ground itself shifts.

Laser ranging helps maintain that system, so researchers using different instruments in different parts of the world can compare measurements of things like rising seas and moving tectonic plates.

There’s also a local complication for the team in Svalbard. The observatory sits next to a bird sanctuary where access is restricted during the summer breeding season.

The station can operate remotely when staff can’t get to the site, a capability NASA can carry over to future installations.

The Landsat image gives us one summer view of the fjord. Understanding how the landscape changes takes years of observations that can be lined up and compared.

From a patch of coast in that image, the Ny-Ålesund station helps make those comparisons possible, one pulse of light at a time.
