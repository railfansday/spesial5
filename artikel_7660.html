Cordless drills make it easier to bore holes and drive screws without a cord restricting movement. Among the most useful battery-powered tools, they serve everyone from occasional DIYers to busy renovation pros. To find the best cordless drills for different projects, we tested 14 highly rated models, from heavy-duty hammer drills to compact drill/drivers.

Choosing a quality drill means finding the right balance of power, control, and comfort for the work ahead. We evaluated cordless electric drills for quality, torque, ease of use, and power, testing each function to identify where every model excelled. Our top recommendation is the Bosch GSB18V-65B12 18-volt ½-inch hammer drill driver, which is well-balanced, highly maneuverable, and offers great cost value.

Whether you need extra muscle for demanding work or a smaller tool for crafting and household repairs, a good cordless drill should suit your projects and budget. Our tested picks cover a range of needs.

The following cordless drills represent many of the drill brands known for reliability and value, and we tested each one. When choosing from different types of drills, start by matching their features to the needs of your projects. The most powerful cordless drill isn’t necessarily the best fit for occasional repairs, especially if its weight makes it tiring to use. As with all the best cordless tools, the ideal tool for the job tends to balance versatility, comfort, and value. Understanding how to use a drill also helps users get the most out of whatever drill you choose.

Battery voltage helps indicate a cordless drill’s capabilities, but it doesn’t measure power on its own. Motor design, gearing, and battery performance also matter. When choosing a battery-powered drill, consider the work you plan to tackle rather than voltage alone.

The distinction between 18V and 20V MAX can be confusing. These labels describe the same voltage class: 20V is the maximum initial battery voltage measured without a workload, while 18V is the nominal voltage. The difference isn’t a startup surge, nor does a 20V MAX label guarantee more power than an 18V model. Similarly, many drills marketed as 12V use batteries rated at 10.8V nominal.

Use these categories as a starting point:

For masonry projects, knowing how to drill into concrete will help you select the appropriate tool, bit, and technique.

Battery capacity, measured in amp-hours (Ah), also matters. At the same voltage, a higher Ah rating generally means longer runtime, though actual results depend on the drill and workload. Smaller packs keep the tool lighter, while larger packs support longer work sessions but usually add weight and cost. A higher Ah rating alone doesn’t guarantee more drilling power. For occasional projects, a compact battery may be sufficient; for extended use, consider a larger pack or a spare.

Torque is the twisting force a drill delivers, while speed, measured in revolutions per minute (RPM), describes how quickly the bit turns. A lower gear generally provides more torque for driving fasteners and drilling with larger bits. Higher speeds suit many smaller-bit drilling tasks, though the appropriate speed depends on the material and bit.

Most drills have an adjustable clutch that limits the torque transmitted to the chuck. When resistance reaches the selected setting, the clutch disengages to help prevent overdriving screws or stripping their heads. It responds to resistance rather than a preset screw depth, so choosing the right setting helps maintain control. Knowing how to change a drill bit also helps ensure the bit is properly seated and secured.

Look for at least 10 clutch settings to accommodate different fasteners and materials. A drilling machine with two gear settings offers more versatility, while a variable-speed trigger lets you fine-tune speed within the selected range.

A drill’s size and weight affect comfort, especially during extended use. Lightweight models can weigh about a pound, while heavy-duty cordless hammer drills may exceed 8 pounds. Extra power often adds weight, so choose a tool that balances capability with comfortable handling. Check whether the listed weight includes the battery, which can make a noticeable difference.

A compact drill is easier to maneuver in confined spaces, and a smaller body doesn’t necessarily mean less power. Consider both weight and overall dimensions when comparing models.

Most cordless drills have keyless chucks for quick bit changes. Chuck capacity determines the largest bit-shank diameter the tool accepts, rather than the size of hole it can drill. Many general-purpose models have ⅜- or ½-inch chucks, while some compact drill/drivers use ¼-inch hex holders that accept only compatible hex-shank bits.

The best drill bits for a project must suit both the material and the drill’s chuck. Check compatibility before buying a bit set.

Corded drills sometimes come with a brushed motor. They are cheap and reliable, but the brushes eventually wear down and require replacement. Another challenge with brushed motors is that they create friction, which drains the battery faster in cordless models.

The best cordless power drills often use a brushless motor. These virtually maintenance-free motors use the battery much more efficiently. However, brushless motors cost more. As a result, many budget cordless drills still use brushed motors.

For a typical DIYer, the difference is probably negligible. Those who want to maximize performance and runtime, particularly professional users, often choose a brushless cordless drill.

Like most modern battery-operated drills, the models in this guide use rechargeable lithium-ion batteries. Runtime depends on battery capacity and workload, while charging time varies with the battery and charger. For extensive drilling, a kit with a spare battery lets you keep working while the depleted pack charges.

Note that manufacturer runtime claims often factor backup batteries into their runtime estimates. For instance, the specs on a drill that comes with two batteries, each lasting 1 hour, may list a 2-hour runtime.

Additionally, many cordless drills include a smart charger that replenishes the battery faster, usually within 15 to 60 minutes. A charge indicator allows you to monitor battery capacity.

The following are answers to some of the most common questions.

The primary use of a cordless drill is for drilling holes and driving screws. However, you can also get numerous attachments, which are sometimes included in cordless drill sets. Stirrers, for example, can mix paint and other liquids. You can also attach a sanding drum, a wire brush for rust removal, or a polishing bonnet. Our guide covers more uses for your cordless drill/driver.

Corded models were once more powerful tools. But today, equally powerful cordless models rival them. However, cordless models lose some of their power over the course of the charge, while corded models perform at a consistent level for as long as they’re plugged in.

An electric screwdriver is usually much smaller, has less power, and cannot drill holes. The cordless drill can drill holes and also drive screws.

New and improved models enter the market all the time, so it’s difficult to name one brand as the best overall. Bosch, DeWalt, Makita, and Milwaukee all offer tools for different needs and budgets. Comparing individual models—including the best DeWalt drills—is more useful than choosing by brand alone.

It very much depends on the amp hours of the battery and how hard the drill is working. A battery can last as little as 15 minutes, though many will run between 30 minutes and 1 hour. Buying a spare battery for your rechargeable drill lets you swap it out and keep working while the other charges.

Brushless electric motors are more efficient and powerful than brushed motors. They often last longer, too. But higher performance also comes at a higher price. For occasional light-duty drilling, brushed cordless drills are more cost-effective.

The best voltage depends on the user’s needs. For instance, 12-volt drills are inexpensive and perfectly capable of light-duty drilling and driving, while 18-volt drills deliver the higher torque and faster RPM needed for medium- and heavy-duty projects.
