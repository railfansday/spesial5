Ferroptosis, a form of cell death induced by overwhelming membrane lipid peroxidation, is a vulnerability of many cancers, including cancers resistant to current molecular and immune-based therapies1,2,3,4,5,6,7. Triggering of ferroptosis therefore provides an opportunity to achieve elimination of difficult-to-treat cancers1,2,3,4,8,9. Studies in mouse cancer models have demonstrated that anticancer immunity, mediated by dendritic cells (DCs) and CD8+ T cells, is essential for effective tumour eradication by ferroptosis-inducing strategies10,11,12,13. However, ferroptotic cells limit this response8,9,10,14, resulting in immune escape and tumour growth through mechanisms that remain unclear. Here we demonstrate that ferroptotic cancer cells activate a molecular program resulting in aberrant production of prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), which limits the stimulation of anticancer CD8+ T cell responses by type 1 conventional DCs (cDC1s), thereby promoting immune escape. In ferroptotic cancer cells, lipid peroxidation-mediated membrane permeabilization enables Ca2+ influx, which activates p38 MAPK signalling and thereby drives PGE2 production. This signalling axis is uncoupled from cancer cell death and can be targeted to selectively interfere with immune-inhibitory PGE2 production while preserving ferroptotic cancer cell elimination. In the context of cancer cell ferroptosis, ablation of PGE2 production or disrupting PGE2 signalling restores cDC1-mediated anticancer CD8+ T cell responses and leads to effective tumour elimination. Our findings identify a key mechanism that limits antitumour immunity and prevents tumour elimination upon ferroptosis induction in cancer cells, which could be exploited for cancer therapy.

Uncontrolled lipid peroxidation is integral to ferroptosis5,15. Cancer cells are commonly protected from unrestrained lipid peroxidation and spontaneous ferroptotic cell death by molecular defence systems such as the system xc––glutathione (GSH)–glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) pathway5,16, in which GPX4 is the key enzyme mediating lipid peroxide detoxification. Strategies to promote ferroptosis by blockade of ferroptosis defence systems, such as GPX4 inhibitors, have been considered promising approaches to achieve tumour elimination in patients with difficult-to-treat cancers8,9. Studies using preclinical mouse models have uncovered that effective tumour elimination by ferroptosis-inducing strategies requires anticancer immunity by DCs10 and tumour-infiltrating CD8+ T cells11,12,13. Yet recent findings have suggested that DC-driven anticancer CD8+ T cell immunity is suppressed in ferroptotic tumours8,9,10,17. Emerging evidence has implicated that the unrestrained generation of membrane lipid peroxides in ferroptotic cells can promote the production and release of molecules that may impact immune cell functions18. However, what causes the suppression of DC-mediated anticancer CD8+ T cell immunity in the context of tumour ferroptosis remains largely unclear.

Potentially relevant immunoregulatory factors produced by ferroptotic cancer cells are oxylipins, bioactive lipids generated through peroxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acid-containing membrane lipids, a hallmark of ferroptosis15. To assess ferroptosis-induced oxylipin production and identify key factors, we profiled the oxylipin secretome of mouse BRAF(V600E) and NRAS(G12D) melanoma, 4T1 breast cancer and MC38 colorectal cancer cells following RSL3-induced ferroptosis. Across all these cancer cell lines, RSL3-treated (ferroptotic) cells displayed increased production of oxylipins compared with untreated (non-ferroptotic) controls (Fig. 1a). Of note, in all groups, the eicosanoid PGE2 was by far the most abundant oxylipin upregulated by ferroptosis induction (Fig. 1a). Similar to ferroptosis induction by RSL3 (which inhibits selenoproteins beyond GPX4), we observed strong PGE2 production after genetic ablation of the ferroptosis-protective enzyme GPX4, ferroptosis induction using the GPX4 inhibitor ML210 or erastin-mediated inactivation of system xc–-mediated cystine import (Extended Data Fig. 1a–f). Thus, ferroptosis may trigger the production of PGE2 in cancer cells, which is a notable finding given the important role of PGE2 for suppression of anticancer immunity19,20,21.

By contrast, stimulation of ultraviolet B (UVB)-induced apoptosis, doxorubicin-induced immunogenic cell death or necrocide 1 (NC1)-induced necrotic cell death failed to promote PGE2 production (Fig. 1b and Extended Data Fig. 1g). However, PGE2 production was induced in cells undergoing necroptosis (Extended Data Fig. 1h). These findings imply that PGE2 production is selectively induced by as yet unknown molecular events in ferroptotic cells and that these events, at least to some extent, may be shared with necroptotic cells.

Using liproxstatin-1 (Lip-1), we further validated that ferroptotic cancer cells depend on lipid peroxides to produce PGE2 (Extended Data Fig. 2a,b). This was not the case for key effectors of other cell death pathways, such as caspases or receptor-interacting serine–threonine-protein kinase 1 (RIPK1; Extended Data Fig. 2a,b). We extended our analyses to a broader panel of mouse and human cancer cells, including established cell lines, cells from spontaneous mouse cancer models and cells from patients with pancreatic cancer. In all these cells, RSL3-induced ferroptosis triggered markedly increased PGE2 production (Extended Data Fig. 2c–e). Together, these data suggest that ferroptosis promotes aberrant production of PGE2 in mouse and human cancer cells, prompting us to investigate the underlying molecular mechanisms and consequences for anticancer immunity.

Effective anticancer immunity often depends on cDC1s22,23, which fulfil essential roles in the priming of anticancer CD8+ T cell responses24,25 and effector differentiation and expansion of CD8+ tumour-infiltrating T lymphocytes (TILs)26,27. These cDC1 functions are impacted by immunoregulatory factors derived from dying cancer cells22,23,28; however, how mediators released by ferroptotic cancer cells impact cDC1s and their functions is not known.

To address this issue, we generated cDC1s from bone marrow cells, exposed them to conditioned medium from RSL3-induced, ferroptotic BRAF(V600E) melanoma cells and assessed their functional properties after activation with polyinosinic–polycytidylic acid (poly(I:C); Fig. 1c). The conditioned medium from ferroptotic BRAF(V600E) cells strongly compromised the capacity of cDC1s to produce CXCL9 (Fig. 1d,e and Extended Data Fig. 3a), a chemokine required for cDC1-mediated orchestration of CD8+ TIL responses26,29. Similar results were observed for cDC1 production of the T cell stimulatory cytokine interleukin-12 (IL-12)30 (Extended Data Fig. 3b,c). By contrast, no impairment was observed for cDC1s exposed to conditioned medium from cancer cells undergoing apoptosis or doxorubicin-induced immunogenic cell death (Fig. 1d,e and Extended Data Fig. 3b,c). Impaired CXCL9 production was similarly observed for splenic cDC1s treated with conditioned medium from ferroptotic BRAF(V600E) cells (Extended Data Fig. 3d). These results suggest that factors released by ferroptotic cancer cells disable cDC1s from producing molecules required to promote anticancer CD8+ T cell immunity.

cDC1 functions are controlled by transcriptional programming31,32. We therefore subjected cDC1s activated by poly(I:C) following exposure to ferroptotic or apoptotic BRAF(V600E) cell conditioned medium to transcriptional profiling by RNA sequencing. We found that ferroptotic, but not apoptotic, cancer cell-derived factors induced prominent transcriptional changes in cDC1s (Fig. 1f), including impaired expression of genes encoding stimulatory cytokines (including Ifnb and Ifnl2), T cell-recruiting chemokines (Cxcl9 and Ccl4) and integrins important for T cell activation (Itgal; Fig. 1f). We also confirmed the inhibitory effect on cDC1 cytokine and chemokine production at the protein level (Extended Data Fig. 3e). Of note, we did not detect a defect in upregulation of MHC or costimulatory molecules (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Table 1), implying that ferroptotic cancer cell-derived factors do not prevent cDC1 activation. Of note, many of the genes impaired in cDC1s following exposure to ferroptotic cancer cell-derived factors were shared with a core gene set of PGE2-mediated cDC1 dysfunction (Extended Data Fig. 3f,g and Supplementary Table 1), although genes beyond this set were also impaired. Consistently, gene set enrichment analyses revealed a prominent impairment of PGE2-regulated functional cDC1 genes (Extended Data Fig. 3h), whereas genes associated with migration or antigen processing (that is, molecular programs not regulated by PGE2 signalling in cDC1s19), remained unaffected (Extended Data Fig. 3h). Together, these data suggest that ferroptotic cancer cells promote cDC1 dysfunction and that this is mediated by PGE2.

Similar to cancer cells, ferroptosis induction caused aberrant PGE2 production in Pfa1 cells (Extended Data Fig. 4a), an established mouse embryonic fibroblast model often used to study the molecular underpinnings of ferroptosis33. This was linked to markedly increased transcript and protein expression of the key PGE2-producing enzyme COX2 (encoded by Ptgs2; Fig. 2a and Extended Data Fig. 4b–d). Lip-1 treatment rescued this effect (Fig. 2a), whereas stimulation of lipid peroxidation by cumene hydroperoxide promoted both Ptgs2 expression and increased PGE2 production in Pfa1 cells (Extended Data Fig. 4e,f). Together, these results point towards a requirement of membrane lipid peroxide generation for PGE2 production through transcriptional regulation of COX2. Using COX inhibitors, we also confirmed that COX activity was required for PGE2 production by ferroptotic Pfa1 cells (Fig. 2b), but dispensable for execution of ferroptotic cell death (Fig. 2c).

A key event caused by lipid peroxides in ferroptotic cells is the influx of extracellular Ca2+ (ref. 5), which constitutes an early event in pro-ferroptotic cells before the onset of cell death. Consistently, microscopy imaging revealed high intracellular Ca2+ levels in Pfa1 cells early after RSL3-mediated ferroptosis induction (Fig. 2d,e), which was similarly observed for multiple mouse cancer cells (Extended Data Fig. 4g,h). Time-resolved live cell imaging of ferroptotic Pfa1 cells confirmed that Ca2+ influx followed peroxide accumulation, but occurred before the cell death-associated loss of plasma membrane integrity (Fig. 2f). Of note, preventing Ca2+ influx through polyethylene glycol (PEG)-mediated blockade of membrane permeabilization-enabled osmotic molecule exchange5,34,35 (Fig. 2g and Extended Data Fig. 4i,j) abrogated the upregulation of Ptgs2 mRNA (Fig. 2h) and COX2 protein expression (Fig. 2i) in ferroptotic Pfa1 cells, resulting in a loss of PGE2 production (Fig. 2j). We also validated that lipid peroxide generation was only minimally affected by PEG treatment (Extended Data Fig. 4k,l) and that ferroptotic cells become permeable to other small extracellular molecules5, including sodium ions (Na+; Extended Data Fig. 4m–p).

Gain-of-function experiments using ionomycin, which selectively transport extracellular Ca2+ into cells, revealed that Ca2+ influx alone was sufficient to promote COX2 upregulation and PGE2 production across Pfa1, BRAF(V600E) melanoma and MC38 colorectal cancer cells (Extended Data Fig. 4q,r). Thus, although small molecules beyond Ca2+ enter ferroptotic cells, they probably do not contribute substantially to the activation of COX-mediated PGE2 production. Consistently, blockade of Ca2+ influx using the Ca2+ chelator EGTA confirmed the relevance of Ca2+ signalling for PGE2 production in ferroptotic cells (Fig. 2k and Extended Data Fig. 4s,t).

Ca2+ influx can regulate gene expression via p38 MAPK signalling, which requires p38 MAPK activatory phosphorylation (p-p38). Consistently, ferroptotic Pfa1 cells displayed a prominent increase in p-p38 levels (Fig. 2l,m), which was abolished by PEG treatment (Fig. 2l,m). Moreover, treatment with the selective p38 MAPK inhibitor SB203580 abrogated the upregulation of COX2 expression and PGE2 production in ferroptotic Pfa1 cells (Fig. 2n,o), demonstrating the relevance of p38 MAPK signalling for PGE2 production. Of note, the later execution of cell death remained unaffected by p38 MAPK inhibition (Fig. 2p) or blockade of Ca2+ influx (Extended Data Fig. 5a). These results suggest that Ca2+-induced p38 MAPK signalling is not required for ferroptotic cell death. We also confirmed that SB203580 did not induce cell death in non-ferroptotic cells (Extended Data Fig. 5a), and that the death of SB203580-treated ferroptotic Pfa1 cells was rescued by Lip-1 (Fig. 2q).

Oxylipin profiling revealed a selective requirement of p38 MAPK signalling for production of PGE2 and other COX-derived eicosanoids in ferroptotic Pfa1 cells (Fig. 2r), whereas other oxylipins remained largely unaffected. Similar to Pfa1 cells, PEG-mediated inhibition of Ca2+ influx or inhibition of p38 MAPK abolished PGE2 production in ferroptotic cancer cells (Extended Data Fig. 5b–e), demonstrating the relevance of Ca2+-mediated p38 MAPK pathway activation for PGE2 production also in ferroptotic cancer cells. We also confirmed that, in cancer cells, this mechanistic axis is already active in the early phase after ferroptosis induction (Extended Data Fig. 5f–r) and that p38 MAPK signalling is dispensable for the propagation of ferroptotic cell death (Extended Data Fig. 5s–v). Experiments with cells deficient in p38α (the p38 MAPK isoform commonly expressed in cancer cells) further validated that p38 MAPK signalling is required for PGE2 production by ferroptotic cancer cells (Extended Data Fig. 5w,x), but not their cell death execution (Extended Data Fig. 5y). We conclude that Ca2+-mediated activation of p38 MAPK signalling triggers PGE2 production by ferroptotic cells and that this mechanism is not required for ferroptosis-dependent cancer cell death (Fig. 2s). This molecular uncoupling enables to selectively interfere with p38 MAPK pathway activity to impair PGE2 production by ferroptotic cancer cells while preserving ferroptosis-mediated cancer cell elimination.

To test whether ferroptotic cancer cells depend on PGE2 to promote cDC1 dysfunction, we induced ferroptosis in COX-deficient Ptgs1/Ptgs2−/− BRAF(V600E) melanoma cells, which cannot produce PGE2 (Extended Data Fig. 6a–c). We then exposed cDC1s to conditioned medium from ferroptotic Ptgs1/Ptgs2−/− or control BRAF(V600E) cells, activated them by poly(I:C) and compared their gene expression profile by RNA sequencing (Fig. 3a). We also included cDC1s exposed to conditioned medium from apoptotic BRAF(V600E) cells, to test possible effects of dying cancer cell-derived factors unrelated to ferroptosis. We observed that PGE2 deficiency in ferroptotic BRAF(V600E) cells prevented cDC1 reprogramming (Fig. 3b) and, consequently, rescued the expression of functional cDC1 genes (for example, Irf8, Ifnl2 and Cxcl9; Fig. 3c). This regain of gene expression was similarly observed in a more detailed analysis of the core functional cDC1 genes selectively suppressed by factors from ferroptotic, but not apoptotic, BRAF(V600E) cells (Fig. 3d).

The inhibitory effect of PGE2 on immune cells36, including cDC1s19, is mediated by signalling through the PGE2 receptors EP2 (encoded by Ptger2) and EP4 (encoded by Ptger4). We therefore extended our RNA sequencing analyses to EP2/EP4-deficient cDC1s from ItgaxcrePtger2−/−Ptger4fl/fl mice19,26. Whereas conditioned medium from PGE2-producing ferroptotic control BRAF(V600E) cells compromised the upregulation of activation induced genes in (EP2/EP4-proficient) wildtype (WT) cDC1s, this defect was fully rescued in EP2/EP4-deficient cDC1s (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Table 2). This result demonstrates the relevance of PGE2–EP2/EP4 signalling for ferroptotic cancer cell-mediated impairment of cDC1 functions. In separate experiments, we further validated that CXCL9 and IL-12 production is rescued in both EP2/EP4-deficient cDC1s treated with conditioned medium from ferroptotic BRAF(V600E) cells and cDC1s treated with conditioned medium from (PGE2-deficient) ferroptotic Ptgs1/Ptgs2−/− (BRAF(V600E)) cells (Fig. 3f,g and Extended Data Fig. 6d,e). Using CXCL9 as a functional marker, we also confirmed that PGE2 was similarly required for ferroptotic cancer cell-mediated inhibition of cDC1s activated by stimuli reported to promote cDC1 activation in tumours (Extended Data Fig. 6f,g). In a T cell stimulation assay, we further probed the ability of cDC1s to stimulate CD8+ T cell expansion. This function was similarly rescued in cDC1s exposed to conditioned medium from PGE2-deficient ferroptotic BRAF(V600E) cells (Fig. 3h,i). Using celecoxib (CXB)-mediated blockade of PGE2 production, we also confirmed that other ferroptotic cancer cells depend on PGE2 to inhibit cDC1 function, as determined by CXCL9 staining (Extended Data Fig. 6h,i). Together, these findings suggest that PGE2–EP2/EP4 signalling mediates the impairment of cDC1 functions caused by ferroptotic cancer cells. Moreover, EP2/EP4 deficiency rescues cDC1s from ferroptotic cancer cell-mediated inhibition.

Our results raised the question whether cDC1s can mount effective CD8+ T cell responses against ferroptotic cancer cells when protected from inhibitory PGE2 signalling. To address this issue, we adoptively transferred CD8+ OT-I T cells, which express a transgenic T cell receptor (TCR) specific for the model antigen ovalbumin (OVA), into WT mice subsequently transplanted with ferroptotic, OVA-expressing control or Ptgs1/Ptgs2−/− BRAF(V600E) cells (Extended Data Fig. 7a). To determine the relevance of EP2/EP4-mediated PGE2 signalling in cDC1, we further included Clec9acrePtger2−/−Ptger4fl/fl mice, which lack EP2 and EP4 in CLEC9A-expressing cDC1. Analyses of OT-I T cell expansion in lymphoid tissues revealed that ferroptotic cancer cell-derived PGE2 impairs the productive generation of anticancer CD8+ T cell responses through PGE2-mediated inhibition of cDC1 functions (Extended Data Fig. 7b–e). Moreover, OT-I T cell expansion was lost in cDC1-deficient Batf3−/− mice challenged with ferroptotic BRAF(V600E)–OVA cells (Extended Data Fig. 7f), confirming the functional relevance of cDC1s for generation of anticancer CD8+ T cell responses.

To determine whether ferroptotic tumours depend on PGE2 production to restrict cDC1-mediated anticancer CD8+ TIL responses and escape immune control, we analysed the growth of (ferroptotic) Gpx4−/− or (non-ferroptotic) WT MC38 cells transplanted into WT and Clec9acrePtger2−/−Ptger4fl/fl mice (Fig. 4a). By microscopy-based analysis of the ferroptosis marker 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) and COX2 protein, we confirmed cancer cell ferroptosis leading to enhanced COX pathway activity in Gpx4−/−, but not WT, MC38 tumours (Extended Data Fig. 8). In WT mice, tumours formed by Gpx4−/− MC38 cells exhibited progressive tumour growth, which was only minimally slower than WT MC38 cells (Fig. 4b and Extended Data Fig. 9a). These data are consistent with inefficient control of ferroptotic cancers in immunocompetent mice. By contrast, Clec9acrePtger2−/−Ptger4fl/fl mice exhibited improved tumour immune control and fully rejected Gpx4−/− MC38 tumours (Fig. 4b and Extended Data Fig. 9a). By contrast, WT MC38 tumours displayed reduced growth but failed to be eliminated in Clec9acrePtger2−/−Ptger4fl/fl mice (Fig. 4b and Extended Data Fig. 9a). Of note, this pattern was similarly observed for Cd4crePtger2−/−Ptger4fl/fl mice in which T cells lack EP2/EP4 expression (Extended Data Fig. 9b), implying that ferroptotic tumours, in parallel to inhibiting cDC1, depend on PGE2-mediated inhibition of T cells to escape anticancer immunity.

We also genetically ablated p38α in Gpx4−/− MC38 cells, which disabled ferroptosis-induced PGE2 production (Extended Data Fig. 9c) and resulted in enhanced tumour growth control in WT mice (Extended Data Fig. 9d), consistent with the idea that p38 MAPK-induced PGE2 production by ferroptotic tumours promotes immune escape.

Of note, the efficient elimination of Gpx4−/− MC38 tumours in Clec9acrePtger2−/−Ptger4fl/fl mice was linked to markedly increased intratumoural numbers of CXCL9-producing cDC1s (Fig. 4c), indicating their enhanced anticancer activity. By contrast, the numbers of CXCL9-producing cDC1s remained low in Gpx4−/− MC38 tumours in WT mice, and in WT MC38 tumours in both WT and Clec9acrePtger2−/−Ptger4fl/fl mice (Fig. 4c). Confocal immunofluorescence microscopy further revealed a prominent increase of interactive clusters between cDC1s and CD8+ TILs in Gpx4−/− MC38 tumours in Clec9acrePtger2−/−Ptger4fl/fl mice (Extended Data Fig. 9e,f), pointing towards more productive cDC1-mediated instruction of anticancer CD8+ TIL responses19,26. Consistently, CD8+ TILs displayed substantially enhanced expansion in Gpx4−/− MC38 tumours in Clec9acrePtger2−/−Ptger4fl/fl mice compared with all other groups (Fig. 4d,e).

cDC1s fulfil an essential role in anticancer immunity by driving the expansion and effector differentiation of TCF1+CD8+ TILs (often referred to as ‘stem-like’ T cells) into TIM3+(TCF1−)CD8+ TILs with cytotoxic effector functions26. In many tumours, this process is critical for protective anticancer immunity37,38,39,40. The CD8+ TILs selectively accumulating in Gpx4−/− MC38 tumours in Clec9acrePtger2−/−Ptger4fl/fl mice predominantly encompassed TIM3+(TCF1−)CD8+ TILs (Fig. 4f and Extended Data Fig. 10a) with high PD-1 and CXCR6 expression (Extended Data Fig. 10a), indicating more productive effector differentiation20,40. Consistently, TIM3+ TILs in Gpx4−/− MC38 tumours in Clec9acrePtger2−/−Ptger4fl/fl mice showed markedly increased frequencies of cells expressing the cytotoxic effector molecule granzyme B (GZMB) compared with all other groups (Fig. 4g,h). We also confirmed that the numbers of stem-like TCF1+ TILs were comparable across all groups (Extended Data Fig. 10b), consistent with their unaffected tumour infiltration.

Of note, the few TIM3+CD8+ TILs detectable in Gpx4−/− MC38 tumours in WT mice, similar to Gpx4−/− MC38 tumours in Clec9acrePtger2−/−Ptger4fl/fl mice, displayed high expression of the proliferation marker Ki-67 (Fig. 4i and Extended Data Fig. 10c). This result indicates that CD8+ TILs, at least to some degree, mount proliferative responses also in ferroptotic tumours in the presence of intact PGE2 signalling on cDC1s. However, profiling of CD8+ TILs at later stages demonstrated that, in Gpx4−/− tumours in WT mice, these responses are not sustained over time, both in terms of Ki-67 expression, GZMB expression and CD8+ TIL expansion (Extended Data Fig. 10d–f). By contrast, CD8+ TIL responses in Clec9acrePtger2−/−Ptger4fl/fl mice in Gpx4−/− MC38 tumours further increased over time (Extended Data Fig. 10d–f). Thus, only Clec9acrePtger2−/−Ptger4fl/fl mice, but not WT mice, can mount protective CD8+ T cell immunity against ferroptotic tumours. By antibody-mediated CD8+ T cell depletion, we further confirmed the relevance of CD8+ T cells for elimination of ferroptotic Gpx4−/− MC38 tumours in Clec9acrePtger2−/−Ptger4fl/fl mice (Extended Data Fig. 10g–i).

Finally, to specifically examine whether pharmacological inhibition of COX-driven PGE2 production can achieve better immune control of ferroptotic tumours, we analysed the growth of Gpx4−/− MC38 tumours transplanted into WT mice with or without CXB treatment. In line with PGE2-mediated impairment of anticancer immunity against ferroptotic tumours, Gpx4−/− MC38 tumours in CXB-treated mice displayed markedly increased control of tumour growth (Fig. 4j). Of note, this effect was associated with prominently enhanced expansion of CD8+ TILs (Fig. 4k), with a high frequency of GZMB-expressing cells (Extended Data Fig. 10j). Similarly, tumours formed by Gpx4−/− BRAF(V600E) melanoma cells exhibited tumour regression in CXB-treated, but not in untreated, WT mice (Extended Data Fig. 10k). Consistent with these data, BRAF(V600E) melanoma tumour-bearing WT mice treated with a combination of CXB and the ferroptosis inducer imidazole ketone erastin (IKE) displayed enhanced tumour growth control compared with untreated mice or mice treated with CXB or IKE alone (Fig. 4l and Extended Data Fig. 10l). Together, these data suggest that PGE2 produced by ferroptotic cancer cells limits anticancer immunity by controlling cDC1-driven effector differentiation and expansion of CD8+ TILs, which enables ferroptotic tumours to escape immune control. Interfering with the PGE2–EP2/EP4 axis can elicit cDC1-mediated expansion and effector differentiation of CD8+ TILs in ferroptotic tumours and result in protective anticancer immunity.

Our results demonstrate that ferroptosis in cancer cells triggers aberrant synthesis of the bioactive lipid PGE2, enabling ferroptotic cancer cells to exert deleterious effects on cDC1-dependent, CD8+ TIL-mediated anticancer immunity within tumour tissue. This inhibitory mechanism is critical for immune escape of ferroptotic tumours.

Beyond highlighting that tumour ferroptosis causes suppression of anticancer immunity, as has been recently suggested8,9,10,14,17, our results reveal that this barrier to ferroptosis-inducing strategies mechanistically depends on PGE2 and its signalling through the PGE2 receptors EP2 and EP4, preventing cDC1-mediated expansion and effector differentiation of anticancer CD8+ TILs. As a result, interference with PGE2–EP2/EP4 signalling enhances the anticancer activity of cDC1s in ferroptotic tumours and induces protective, CD8+ TIL-mediated anticancer immunity. These findings therefore identify a molecular mechanism fundamentally linked to the metabolic underpinnings of ferroptosis that may be decisive for ferroptosis-mediated impairment of cancer immunity. Of note, it seems likely that PGE2 from ferroptotic tumour cells not only targets cDC1s but in parallel also interferes directly with anticancer TILs, as indicated by our data. PGE2 may further limit the anticancer activity of other innate immune cells such as type 2 conventional DCs (cDC2s), monocyte–macrophages or natural killer cells41.

We have revealed that PGE2 production by ferroptotic cancer cells involves p38 MAPK signalling activated through lipid peroxidation-induced, membrane permeabilization-dependent Ca2+ influx, which activates a molecular program that causes transcriptional upregulation of the COX–PGE2 pathway. Beyond providing a mechanistic explanation for high COX2 levels in ferroptotic cells, as has been recently observed18,42, our results uncover that this trait acquired by ferroptotic cells is uncoupled from the execution of cell death. As a consequence, interference with p38 MAPK signalling can disable the production of PGE2 while maintaining effective cancer cell elimination by ferroptosis inducers.

Multiple studies have highlighted the vulnerability of therapy-resistant and difficult-to-treat cancers to ferroptosis induction and the dependency of ferroptotic cancer elimination on anticancer immunity. Therefore, our discoveries that ferroptotic tumour cells antagonize anticancer immunity through the PGE2–EP2/EP4 axis and the uncoupling of this mechanism from ferroptosis-mediated tumour cell elimination have important mechanistic and clinical implications. Our results indicate that targeting PGE2–EP2/EP4 signalling to overcome ferroptosis-induced immunosuppression may provide an attractive means to unleash anticancer CD8+ T cell immunity and achieve cancer elimination by ferroptosis-inducing strategies. On this note, abolishing PGE2 production by inhibition of p38 MAPK signalling bears the potential to interfere with the immunosuppressive capacity of ferroptotic cancer cells without curbing ferroptosis-mediated cancer cell elimination. Our findings therefore identify p38 MAPK signalling-induced COX–PGE2 pathway activity in ferroptotic cancer cells as a potential molecular target to prevent immune evasion and improve cancer therapy by ferroptosis-inducing strategies. Of note, disabling the p38 MAPK–COX–PGE2 signalling may further benefit necroptosis-inducing strategies, as indicated by our findings with necroptotic cells.

Ten microlitres were injected onto a Sciex QTRAP 6500+ mass spectrometer associated with an Exion UHPLC operated in negative-ion mode using scheduled multiple reaction monitoring. Quantification was carried out using calibration curves and internal standards, with quality control pools and blank samples analysed throughout the run to ensure accuracy and reproducibility. For BRAF(V600E), NRAS(G12D), 4T1 and MC38 cancer cell lines, fold change of each analyte production during ferroptosis relative to DMSO control group was plotted against its final concentration in ferroptotic conditioned medium. For Pfa1 cells, analytes from each oxylipin subclass (eicosanoid, hydroxylated/oxidized lipids and protectins/resolvins) were ordered from high to low abundance in ferroptotic-conditioned medium and plotted as a z-score between different treatment groups.

RNA was isolated using Monarch Total RNA Miniprep kit (New England Biolabs) and complementary DNA (cDNA) was generated using a SensiFAST cDNA synthesis kit (Bioline). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed on a LightCycler 480 (LightCycler 480 software v1.5.1, Roche) using the TAKYON No ROX SYBR MasterMix dTTP Blue kit (Eurogentec) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Gene expression was normalized to the reference gene Hprt by using the ΔCt method. All primers were purchased from Eurofins.

In vitro-generated WT or ItgaxcrePtger2−/−Ptger4fl/fl CD103+ cDC1s were incubated for 16 h with conditioned medium from control or Ptgs1/Ptgs2−/− ferroptotic BRAF(V600E) melanoma cells followed by stimulation with poly(I:C) (10 μg ml−1) for an additional 4 h. RNA was isolated using an Arcturus PicoPure RNA isolation kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Library preparation was carried out using the Novogene manufactured internal kit. mRNA was purified from total RNA using poly-T oligo-attached magnetic beads. After fragmentation, the first strand cDNA was synthesized using random hexamer primers, followed by the second strand cDNA synthesis using dUTP for directional (strand-specific) library construction. Sequencing was performed on a NovaSeq X Plus with 25B flowcell 2 × 150 bp paired-end sequencing (300 cycles kit). The raw fastq files related to the sequence reads were mapped to the mouse genome, which was downloaded from the UCSC browser (GRCm38/mm10), using the STAR algorithm46. The reads were counted using the Rsubread R package47. These read counts were normalized, and differentially expressed genes were analysed using the iDEP (v2.01) web server48, utilizing the DESeq2 R package49 for differentially expressed gene identification (with fold change ≥ 1.5 and adjusted P < 0.05). Principal component analysis was performed using the prcomp R function and plotted using the ggplot2 R package. Volcano plots were rendered using the Enhanced Volcano R package (https://github.com/kevinblighe/EnhancedVolcano). The ‘Core functional cDC1’ gene set was defined as activation-induced genes significantly downregulated in activated cDC1s following incubation with conditioned medium from ferroptotic control BRAF(V600E) cells. Hierarchical clustering of core functional cDC1 genes was performed using the hclust function in R (Euclidean distance, complete linkage), and heatmaps were generated using the heatmap.2 function of the gplots R package. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed on core functional cDC1 genes and arranged according to log2(fold changes). Enrichment of gene sets was then conducted using GSEA (v4.2.3)50. Gene sets related to cytokine signalling and cell migration were extracted from the Molecular Signature Database (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/index.jsp). Gene signatures for antigen uptake and cross-presentation were from a previous study51. The gene set for PGE2 impaired functional cDC1 genes was from a previous study19. PGE2-impaired core functional cDC1 genes for correlation analysis were defined as activation-induced genes significantly downregulated in activated cDC1s following incubation with PGE2. The PreRanked tool from GSEA (v4.2.3) was used to assess the normalized enrichment score, and a false discovery rate (q < 0.25) was utilized to evaluate the statistical significance of the normalized enrichment score.
