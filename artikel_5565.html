GLP-1 Drugs May Have Benefits for Some Schizophrenia Patients

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

— Study showed link to fewer psychiatric hospitalizations in adults with schizophrenia and diabetes

Despite decades of development of antipsychotic treatments, preventing relapse and acute psychiatric crises remains a major challenge in schizophrenia care.

Because metabolic disturbances are increasingly recognized as potential contributors to psychiatric deterioration, therapies that target these pathways have drawn growing interest.

Prior research has shown that GLP-1 drugs can reduce body weight and improve cardiometabolic markers in patients taking antipsychotics. However, few studies have looked at the potential effect these popular agents have on psychiatric outcomes.

Real-world data recently published in Lancet Psychiatry suggested that initiating a GLP-1 drug was associated with decreased use of psychiatric acute-care services in adults living with both schizophrenia and type 2 diabetes.

"We were not entirely surprised by the direction of the findings, because there is a plausible biological connection between metabolic health and psychiatric outcomes," study author Najmeh Davoodian, MD, PhD, of Deakin University in Geelong, Australia, told MedPage Today. "People living with schizophrenia have a substantial cardiometabolic burden, and treatments that address this burden might also support psychiatric stability."

In weighted regression analyses, GLP-1 receptor agonist initiation was associated with a 24% lower risk of psychiatric hospitalization compared with non-initiation (HR 0.76, 95% CI 0.71-0.82), as well as lower risks compared with initiation of a DPP-4 inhibitor (HR 0.82, 95% CI 0.75-0.90) or an SGLT2 inhibitor (HR 0.83, 95% CI 0.78-0.88).

Similarly, initiation of GLP-1 agonists was linked to lower risks of psychiatric emergency department (ED) presentation compared with non-initiation (HR 0.71, 95% CI 0.65-0.77), and initiation of DPP-4 inhibitors (HR 0.87, 95% CI 0.80-0.95) and SGLT2 inhibitors (HR 0.80, 95% CI 0.73-0.88).

"What was particularly encouraging was that the association was observed for both psychiatric hospitalization and ED presentation, and against other glucose-lowering treatments as well as non-initiation," Davoodian said. "This consistency strengthens the rationale for further investigation, although it does not establish causality."

The protective associations were most pronounced among adults under age 55, men, and patients taking antipsychotics. In agent-specific analyses, semaglutide (Ozempic), liraglutide (Victoza), and tirzepatide (Mounjaro) were each consistently tied to decreased use of psychiatric acute-care services, while dulaglutide (Trulicity) was not.

The estimated 1-year number needed to treat with a GLP-1 drug versus non-initiation to prevent one acute psychiatric event was 52 in the overall cohort; this dropped to 32 among antipsychotic users.

Why GLP-1 therapies might stabilize psychiatric trajectories remains an open question. In exploratory analyses, Davoodian's group observed the strongest reductions in acute care use among patients who lost at least 5% of their body weight (HR 0.60, 95% CI 0.44-0.79 versus non-initiation), though lower risks also persisted among those whose weight remained stable (HR 0.69, 95% CI 0.61-0.78).

"Improvements in weight, glucose regulation, and insulin sensitivity might reduce metabolic stress and potentially support psychiatric stability," Davoodian said, noting that mitigating antipsychotic-induced weight gain could also help patients adhere to psychiatric regimens. Still, "there may also be pathways beyond weight loss. Experimental research suggests potential effects on brain signaling and cellular energy metabolism."

Thomas Blackwell, MD, of the University of Texas Medical Branch in Galveston, who was not involved in the study, pointed to central nervous system effects and the gut-brain axis as plausible contributors.

"Beyond improving metabolic health, GLP-1s are believed to reduce neuroinflammation and may improve mitochondrial function in brain support cells like microglia and astrocytes, which help maintain neurons," Blackwell told MedPage Today. "GLP-1s also act on receptors in the brain. The same activity that curbs appetite seems to reduce cravings for alcohol and nicotine ... If these drugs can change how the brain interprets those signals, it's reasonable to think they could affect other aspects of mental health."

Both Davoodian and Blackwell cautioned against viewing the findings as a reason to prescribe GLP-1 drugs solely for psychiatric indications. Because the study relied on retrospective records, unmeasured confounding cannot be ruled out, and the results apply specifically to patients with comorbid type 2 diabetes.

"Hospitalizations and ED visits are clearly defined events, but they don't tell us whether symptoms, mood, or overall functioning improved," Blackwell noted. "If patients improved by 20% on average, that would be very meaningful, but this might never show up in hospitalization rates."

Even so, the results carry weight for clinicians managing overlapping metabolic and psychiatric illnesses.

"Based on this study alone, I wouldn't start prescribing GLP-1s to patients with schizophrenia for psychiatric benefit," Blackwell said. "But for a patient with schizophrenia who also has type 2 diabetes, a GLP-1 might be a reasonable first choice. GLP-1s also reduce the risk of cardiovascular death, which matters a great deal in this population."

Davoodian agreed, emphasizing that metabolic health deserves close attention as an integral part of schizophrenia care. "For someone with schizophrenia and type 2 diabetes who already has a clinical indication for a GLP-1 receptor agonist, lower psychiatric acute-care use might represent a favorable secondary benefit," she said, calling for randomized trials with psychiatric endpoints to determine whether these associations reflect a true treatment benefit.
