The deal closed. When do the savings start?

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

For homebuilders, the timing of acquisition savings often comes down to the systems and Supplier connections already in place

Purchasing synergies can materially improve homebuilder acquisition savings and strengthen the economics of the deal. They can also be overstated when the deal model doesn’t fully reflect the cost and time required to achieve them.

Combining the purchasing volume of two builders should create leverage for better pricing and stronger margins. But before that leverage is real, someone has to determine what each company is buying, how their supplier agreements differ and what it will take to operate under a common set of processes.

That’s where the financial projections meet the realities of running the business.

I’ve worked on acquisition diligence and integration across different industries, and the question I believe deserves more attention isn’t how much a deal can save. It’s when those savings actually show up in the P&L. For homebuilders, technology has a significant role in the solution. Buying power only matters if you can use it Consider a builder acquiring a regional operation with established supplier relationships and a healthy pipeline of homes under construction. The opportunity is clear. The combined company is buying more materials across more communities, which should create leverage to negotiate better terms. Now look at what has to happen to capture those savings. The acquired company may use a different ERP, organize its purchasing data differently or have supplier agreements that don’t align with the parent company’s contracts. Before purchasing leadership can negotiate from a position of combined volume, they need an accurate, combined view of what that volume actually is. Meanwhile, both businesses still have homes to build. Construction schedules can’t be put on hold while systems are reconciled. It’s entirely possible to have a sound purchasing strategy and spend the first several months after closing working through the operational issues that prevent successful execution. As builders know better than anyone, these delays carry real cost. If the acquisition model assumes purchasing savings will start contributing to margin within two quarters and integration takes a year, you won’t get the return you underwrote. The savings may still arrive, but every quarter of delay is value that doesn’t come back. This matters even more when interest rates are high. There’s usually a second cost hiding in the same delay: running two sets of systems, two sets of processes and often two teams doing the same work until integration is complete. Those dual running costs rarely get the same scrutiny as the synergy number. Homebuilder M&A integration is a financial decision Technology deserves a more prominent place in acquisition diligence and homebuilder M&A integration, particularly when operating efficiencies account for a meaningful share of the expected return. Knowing which applications a company runs is a starting point. The more important question is how those systems will work inside the larger organization, and what it will cost to get them there. Can the acquiring builder get reliable purchasing data across both businesses on day one? Can existing systems exchange the information needed to keep construction moving? What will it cost to maintain separate environments while integration is underway, and for how long? These sound like operating questions. They’re also valuation questions. The same applies to proprietary technology. Many builders have invested heavily in systems developed specifically for their businesses, and there can be good reasons for doing so. The challenge arises when those systems must accommodate an organization they weren’t originally designed to support. Custom integrations require people and capital to build. They also require ongoing security and maintenance as the business changes, and that cost doesn’t end when the integration goes live. I want to understand those costs before committing to an acquisition, not discover them in the first integration budget review. The advantage of an established network One factor that is often overlooked in technology discussions is the value of connectivity that already exists. A homebuilding operation depends on relationships with suppliers, manufacturers and trades that extend well beyond its internal systems. Those connections become particularly important when a company expands into new markets or brings another Builder into the organization. At Hyphen Solutions, we’ve spent more than 25 years building a network that connects hundreds of Builders and more than 18,000 suppliers, trades and manufacturers across North America. That matters because a builder operating within an established network isn’t starting from zero. In many acquisitions, the acquired builder’s suppliers are already on the same network as the parent company. When that’s the case, many of the connections the combined company needs exist before the deal closes, and purchasing teams aren’t onboarding suppliers a second time while homes are under construction. Our technology connects construction scheduling, purchasing and supplier communication, and integrates with different ERP and back-office environments. That gives purchasing leadership the combined view of orders, pricing and supplier activity across both businesses that consolidated buying depends on, without replacing every existing system first. We’ve seen this play out over the past year as a number of builders combined operations, and the ones that moved fastest had something in common. Their companies already shared many of the same suppliers and trades on the Hyphen network. Bringing everyone onto a single instance went quicker, and the trades doing the work in the field had a much simpler transition. Nobody was asking a framer to learn a new system in the middle of a build. There will still be work to do following an acquisition. Supplier contracts have to be evaluated, processes aligned and technology decisions made. No platform eliminates those responsibilities. But when connections and integrations are already in place, there’s less to build, and the combined company gets to consolidated purchasing sooner. From a financial standpoint, that shows up in two places: lower cost to achieve, and a shorter path to realized savings. I would encourage builders to apply the same discipline to understanding what it will take to capture the expected value, and how much time and risk stand between the deal and the savings. If the investment thesis depends on consolidated purchasing, define what has to happen before the company can actually operate that way. Put a cost on the technology requirements. Build a realistic integration timeline into the model and align the timing of purchasing synergies with it. And ask these questions before an acquisition is on the table. The same infrastructure that supports integration also determines how efficiently a builder expands into new markets or adds divisions organically. For a CFO, the synergy number is only part of the picture. The timing and the cost to achieve it matter just as much to the investment decision. Before signing off on an expected savings number, I want to understand what has to change in the business for that number to become real.
