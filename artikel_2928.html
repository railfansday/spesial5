The AI Gateway Is Becoming The Enterprise Control Point

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

Joan Vendrell, NeuralTrust CEO and cofounder, has 15+ years of technology leadership experience advancing enterprise-grade AI security.

​In 2025, attackers slipped malicious instructions into the generative AI tools of more than 90 organizations, turning trusted assistants into engines for stealing credentials and even cryptocurrency, according to CrowdStrike’s 2026 Global Threat Report. What those incidents had in common wasn’t an exotic exploit but a missing layer of control between the application and the model.

That layer now has a name. As the number of models, co-pilots and agents multiplies, teams stop wiring every application directly to every provider and put an AI gateway in between. Gartner analysts describe it as an “intermediary between applications and AI services” that becomes a central point for “security, governance and observability.” The direction is right, but the way most organizations build that layer first is where the trouble starts.​

In most early rollouts, the gateway is treated as a routing and cost-control layer that load-balances across providers and tracks spend. Security is assumed to be happening elsewhere, inside the application, at the model provider or in a review nobody actually scheduled, and that assumption is exactly where the exposure begins.

The gateway has become the busiest and most privileged component in the AI stack, and adoption is no longer niche. Gartner researchers predict that 40% of enterprise applications will integrate task-specific AI agents by the end of 2026, up from less than 5% in 2025, and almost all of that traffic funnels through the gateway.

Every prompt a user or agent sends, and every response a model returns, passes through it. It holds the API keys, the routing logic and, increasingly, the connections to email, file storage and CRM systems through the model context protocol (MCP). A component that sees all the traffic and holds all the credentials becomes either the strongest control point in the architecture or its single largest weakness.

A gateway that moves requests faster without inspecting them industrializes the risk. The threats that matter most in AI are semantic, not merely network-level. In a prompt injection attack, an adversary hides an instruction inside content the model will later read, like a webpage, a PDF or an email, and the model can’t tell that planted instruction apart from a legitimate one and follows it.

Neither a firewall, rate limiter nor gateway tuned only for latency and cost will catch that. CrowdStrike found that AI-enabled adversary operations rose 89% year over year, and that 82% of detections in 2025 involved no malware at all, a sign that attackers increasingly rely on manipulating language and trusted access rather than deploying traditional code. When an agent behind the gateway can call tools, read documents and reach production systems, a single injected instruction can convert legitimate access into data exfiltration or an unauthorized transaction. If the gateway forwards that traffic without inspecting prompts, tool calls and responses, the most central component in the stack is also the least aware.

The instinct is to add security later, as a separate product downstream, but that misses the point of interception, and the cost of that gap is measurable. IBM’s Cost of a Data Breach Report 2025 found that a high level of shadow AI added roughly $670,000 to the average breach, that 63% of breached organizations had no AI governance policy in place or were still developing one, and that 97% of organizations hit by an AI-related incident lacked appropriate AI access controls. These describe basic controls that were never wired into the path AI traffic travels.​​

Security that lives beside the gateway rather than inside it also splinters accountability. Access decisions happen in one system, content inspection in another and logging in a third, and none of them holds the full context of who called what through which model, which is precisely the seam attackers work.

The deployments that hold up under scrutiny share a few traits. The gateway inspects in-line and in both directions, screening prompts and tool calls for injection and policy violations before they reach a model, and screening responses for sensitive-data leakage before they return to a user or an agent. It enforces identity and least privilege for nonhuman callers, so agents and MCP connections are scoped as tightly as any employee because an overpermissioned agent is the modern version of a shared admin password. It treats consumption as a security signal and not only a billing one. And it produces a complete, queryable audit trail of every request, model, tool and data path in one place.

That last trait is where the gateway earns its keep beyond security because regulators are converging on the same expectation. The EU AI Act, the NIST AI Risk Management Framework and ISO 42001 all assume you can demonstrate control and produce evidence of how your AI systems behave. A gateway that inspects and logs every interaction is the natural place to generate that evidence continuously, instead of reconstructing it under audit pressure.

Enterprises will keep centralizing AI traffic through gateways because the economics and operational simplicity are too strong to resist. The real decision is narrower, and it comes down to whether that central point is where policy is enforced, threats are caught and behavior is recorded, or merely a fast pipe that makes ungoverned traffic flow more efficiently.​
