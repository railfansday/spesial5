Why Auto Shops Are Missing From The Claims Automation Conversation

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Nayan Nandan, Co-Founder & CEO of Altis AI, building AI-native insurance claims for auto repair shops.

Earlier this year, my co-founder and I spent a couple of months on the ground in auto body shops across the Bay Area. We visited more than 50 shops and spent hundreds of hours sitting next to estimators and owners. We wanted to understand why shops that do good work still lose money on insurance claims.

Almost every time, the repair gets done right, but the estimate sent to the insurer doesn’t capture all of it: a pre-repair scan, a calibration, a module initialization after the battery is reconnected. The technician does the work, and the shop never gets paid for it.

Before my current role, I was an investor at a venture capital firm and a consultant at a major management consulting firm. I’ve read a lot of insurance market maps. Very few look at claims from the side that actually fixes the asset. From what I’ve seen, that gap is getting bigger as insurers automate more of the claims process with AI.

Insurers have had a strong couple of years. BCG, citing S&P Global Market Intelligence and NAIC data, puts the personal auto combined ratio at about 92% in 2025, down from 112% in 2022. Rate increases did a lot of the work, according to Triple-I research.

Meanwhile, the average repair check barely moved. CCC Intelligent Solutions reports the average repair cost was $4,818 in 2025, up just 1.7%, the smallest increase since 2017. The cars also got much harder to fix. ADAS calibrations showed up on 28.3% of repairable estimates in 2025, up from 21.8% the year before.

The number that says the most comes from CCC’s Q4 2025 data on direct repair claims: More than half of calibrations were added on a supplement, not the first estimate. More often than not, the original estimate misses a procedure the car needs, and it gets caught later, if at all.

There’s a safety side to this, too. In Seebachan v. John Eagle Collision Center, a Dallas County jury returned a roughly $42 million verdict in 2017. The shop had bonded a replacement roof onto a Honda Fit that Honda’s procedures said should be welded. The roof failed in a later crash, and the jury put most of the responsibility on the shop.

The OEM procedure defines what a safe repair includes. The insurer’s estimate defines what gets paid. The shop carries the liability for whatever falls in between.

That same procedure is also the shop’s best argument. A line item backed by the manufacturer’s documentation is hard for an insurer to deny. Finding the right procedure for a specific make, model, year and trim, and then tying it to the damage on the car, takes longer than most estimators have on every file.

Insurers saw this coming and invested early. The Bureau of Labor Statistics projects employment of claims adjusters, appraisers and examiners to decline 5% between 2024 and 2034, partly because software can now assess damage from photos.

Faster claims are good for policyholders, but almost all of this is being built for the payer. On one side of a claim, software checks every estimate against the carrier’s rules. On the other, an estimator with a year or two of experience is often working from memory and a stack of PDFs.

The latest AI models can read across thousands of pages of OEM procedures, and they can now be affordable enough to run on every claim, not just the big ones.

I am the CEO of a company that builds AI tools for collision shops, but my point goes beyond a specific solution. Whatever tools you use, three things are worth doing:

1. Audit a sample of closed files. Pull 20 recent repairs and check which OEM-required operations never made it onto the estimate. That’s a rough measure of what you’re leaving on the table.

2. Get procedures onto the first estimate. Every calibration caught upfront means one fewer supplement and fewer days of cycle time. Carriers also track cycle time on direct repair program scorecards.

3. Keep a human in the loop. An estimate the shop can’t defend line by line just creates a longer fight. Use AI to find what’s missing and attach the source, then let someone who understands claims negotiate it.

With these steps, the biggest challenge I’ve seen is change management. Claims managers and estimators with two or three decades of experience are understandably skeptical of changing how they work, and that’s usually where adoption stalls. ​

Auto is a good place to start because OEM documentation gives clear, defensible authority for what a proper repair should include. But the same dynamic runs through all of property and casualty. Roofers, restoration companies and contractors all work on insured physical assets. They all have to get paid by a counterparty that is better resourced and increasingly automated.

I don’t think the answer is an arms race between shop AI and insurer AI. The better outcome is both sides working from the same source of truth: the manufacturer’s procedure, tied to each line on the estimate. ​
