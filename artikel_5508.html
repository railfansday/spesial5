Flu, COVID-19, RSV, norovirus and strep throat: symptoms, tests and treatments

How to tell winter illnesses apart, from flu and COVID to RSV, norovirus and strep throat, with the symptoms, tests and treatments that distinguish each one

A fever, a sore throat and a pounding headache on a winter morning could signal any of several infections. The first day rarely reveals which one. Five common winter illnesses — flu, COVID-19, RSV, norovirus and strep throat — often get lumped together as "a bug going around." They differ in what causes them, which part of the body they target, how fast they start and what treatment helps.

Three of the five are respiratory viruses. Influenza, SARS-CoV-2 and respiratory syncytial virus all infect the airways, and their early symptoms overlap. Norovirus is a different kind of virus altogether. It attacks the gut, causing vomiting and diarrhea. It has nothing to do with influenza, despite the common nickname "stomach flu." Strep throat stands apart for another reason. It is caused by Group A Streptococcus bacteria, which makes it the only bacterial infection in this group.

Knowing how to tell winter illnesses apart matters because some treatments depend on timing. The flu antiviral oseltamivir, sold as Tamiflu, works best when started within 48 hours of the first symptoms. Paxlovid, the COVID-19 antiviral made of nirmatrelvir with ritonavir, must be started within five days of symptom onset. Antibiotics such as penicillin or amoxicillin treat strep throat. They also lower the risk of complications including rheumatic fever. They do nothing for any of the four viral illnesses.

The people most at risk and the main dangers also differ. RSV usually feels like a common cold in healthy older children and adults. It is most dangerous for infants and older adults. Norovirus spreads through contaminated surfaces, food and close contact, and its main danger is dehydration. Flu can lead to complications such as pneumonia.

Symptoms can point in a direction, but they rarely settle the question alone. A rapid molecular test, a home antigen test, a rapid strep test or a throat culture is what identifies the cause. The slides below cover five common winter infections, not every seasonal illness. Each one sets out the typical signs, the timing, the tests that identify it and the treatment or prevention options available.

Flu tends to arrive abruptly, with fever, chills and body aches setting in over a few hours rather than several days. That sudden start matters because oseltamivir, the prescription antiviral sold as Tamiflu, works best when it is started within 48 hours of the first symptoms.

Influenza viruses cause the illness, and they infect the nose, throat and lungs. The typical picture includes a fever, shaking chills, aching muscles, a headache and a dry cough. Many people also have a heavy, draining fatigue that can keep them in bed.

The speed of onset is one of the clearest ways to tell flu from a common cold. A cold usually builds slowly, often starting with a scratchy throat or a runny nose. High fever and severe body aches are less common with a cold. Flu can leave someone who felt fine in the morning bedridden by evening.

Symptoms often appear one to four days after a person is exposed to the virus. That window can help someone work out where an infection came from, such as a sick coworker or a crowded holiday gathering. People can also spread flu before their own symptoms begin.

Oseltamivir does not wipe out the virus overnight. Taken early, it can shorten the illness and ease symptoms. It requires a prescription from a clinician. Reaching a doctor or a telehealth service soon after symptoms begin gives the best chance of starting the drug in time.

Symptoms alone cannot reliably separate flu from COVID-19, which can also bring fever, cough and aches. A rapid molecular test or a lab test confirms which virus is responsible. The result can shape treatment, because the antivirals used for the two illnesses are different drugs.

Pneumonia is one serious complication of flu. Trouble breathing, chest pain or a fever that returns after the illness seemed to improve are reasons to get prompt medical care.

An annual flu vaccine is available. It is updated each year because the influenza strains in circulation change from season to season. The vaccine lowers the risk of getting infected and of developing serious illness.

A home antigen test is the quickest way to check whether winter symptoms come from COVID-19. A negative result in the first day or so of illness can be wrong. Testing again a day or two later is the usual advice.

COVID-19 is caused by the coronavirus SARS-CoV-2. Its symptoms closely resemble those of other winter respiratory infections. People commonly report fever, cough, sore throat, congestion, fatigue and body aches. That same mix can show up with flu or a common cold.

Early in the pandemic, a sudden loss of taste or smell stood out as a hallmark of COVID-19. That symptom has been reported less often with later variants of the virus. Its absence no longer rules COVID-19 out. A test is the practical way to know.

Home antigen tests use a nasal swab and look for proteins made by the virus. Early in an infection, the amount of virus in the nose can still be too low to detect. A single negative result in the first day or two of symptoms is therefore not the final word. A second test after another day or two gives a more dependable answer.

A clinic or lab can run a molecular test, such as PCR, instead. It detects the virus's genetic material and is more sensitive than a home antigen test. That option helps when home results and ongoing symptoms do not line up.

Timing matters for treatment as well as diagnosis. Paxlovid is a prescription antiviral that combines nirmatrelvir with ritonavir. It must be started in the first days after symptoms begin. It is generally prescribed to people at higher risk of severe illness. Ritonavir interacts with a number of common medications. A prescriber or pharmacist reviews what else a patient takes before it is dispensed. A prompt positive test keeps that treatment option open.

Updated COVID-19 vaccines are offered as the virus changes. Eligibility rules have shifted over time. A doctor, a pharmacist or a national health authority can give the current guidance on who should get one.

In most healthy older children and adults, RSV feels like an ordinary cold. Respiratory syncytial virus poses its greatest threat at the two ends of life — in infants and in older adults.

A typical case in a school-age child or adult brings a runny nose, a cough, a sore throat and sometimes a mild fever. Most people in this group recover at home.

Infants can face a more serious illness. In babies, RSV can cause bronchiolitis, an inflammation of the small airways in the lungs called bronchioles. Swelling and mucus narrow passages that are already tiny in a newborn.

The signs of bronchiolitis include wheezing, fast breathing and visibly labored breathing. A baby working hard to breathe may flare the nostrils. The skin may also pull in between the ribs with each breath. Trouble feeding is another warning sign. A baby who cannot breathe comfortably often struggles to nurse or take a bottle. Bluish lips or skin call for emergency care.

Older adults are vulnerable in a different way. In this group, RSV can move into the lungs and cause pneumonia. It can also worsen existing conditions such as asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and congestive heart failure.

No specific antiviral treats most RSV cases. Care is supportive. That means fluids to prevent dehydration and medicine to control fever. For infants, saline drops and gentle suction can clear a blocked nose before a feeding. Babies and adults with severe illness may need hospital care, including oxygen.

Prevention options widened in 2023, when several new products were approved in the U.S. Two vaccines for adults, Arexvy and Abrysvo, became available. A doctor or pharmacist can confirm who currently qualifies for either shot.

Infants now have two routes of protection. Abrysvo can be given during pregnancy. The antibodies the mother makes then cross the placenta and protect the baby after birth. The other option is nirsevimab, sold as Beyfortus. It is a lab-made antibody injected directly into the infant. Unlike a vaccine, it supplies ready-made protection instead of training the baby's immune system to produce its own.

Norovirus is a gut infection that mainly causes vomiting and diarrhea, often with stomach cramps and sometimes a low fever. It is not a respiratory virus and has no connection to influenza. That makes "stomach flu" a misleading name for it.

Where the symptoms show up is a useful first clue for anyone sorting out a winter illness. Vomiting, diarrhea and cramping point to the digestive tract. A cough, congestion and a sore throat point to the airways, where flu, COVID-19 and RSV cause their trouble.

Timing offers another clue. Norovirus symptoms usually begin 12 to 48 hours after exposure. That gap means the source may not be the most recent meal or contact. It could trace back to food, a gathering or a sick person from a day or two earlier.

The illness is usually short. Most cases run one to three days. The main danger during that stretch is dehydration. Repeated vomiting and diarrhea drain the body of fluid. Replacing those fluids is the priority while the virus runs its course.

Norovirus also complicates a common question: is it norovirus or food poisoning? The two can overlap. The virus can spread through contaminated food. In that case, a norovirus infection is itself a foodborne illness. It also moves through contaminated surfaces and close contact with someone who is sick.

Those routes make households especially vulnerable. Once one member falls ill, the people sharing a kitchen, a bathroom and close quarters with them can easily catch it.

Hand hygiene needs a different approach against norovirus. Washing with soap and water works better than alcohol-based hand sanitizer against this virus. A squirt of gel is not a substitute for the sink.

Surfaces need a stronger product as well. Bleach-based cleaners work against norovirus on countertops, bathroom fixtures and other areas that may have been contaminated.

No vaccine is approved for norovirus. Flu, COVID-19 and RSV all have vaccines. Protection against this stomach bug depends instead on handwashing, bleach-based cleaning and avoiding contaminated food.

Strep throat is a bacterial infection caused by Group A Streptococcus, and it is the one illness among these five that antibiotics can treat. A rapid strep test or a throat culture confirms it.

That bacterial cause sets strep apart from flu, COVID-19, RSV and norovirus. All four of those are viral infections. Antibiotics have no effect on viruses. A positive strep test is the signal that penicillin or amoxicillin will help.

The illness tends to show itself first in the throat. A sore throat comes on suddenly, and swallowing becomes painful. Fever often arrives with it. The lymph nodes at the front of the neck may swell. The trouble usually stays centered on the throat rather than settling in the chest or nose.

A look inside the mouth can show more. The tonsils may be red and swollen, sometimes with white patches on their surface. Some people also develop tiny red spots on the roof of the mouth.

What is missing matters as much as what is present. A cough and a runny nose usually point away from strep. Those signs fit better with a cold or another respiratory virus. A sore throat paired with congestion is a different picture from a sore throat with fever and swollen glands but no cough.

Even so, a checklist of signs cannot settle the question. Symptoms alone are not a reliable way to diagnose strep throat. Some viral infections also produce red tonsils and fever. To confirm the cause, a clinician swabs the back of the throat. A rapid strep test can return a result during the same visit. A throat culture is grown in a lab and takes longer.

Penicillin and amoxicillin clear the bacterial infection. Treating strep also lowers the risk of complications. One of them is rheumatic fever, a condition that can develop after a strep infection goes untreated.
