Millennial moms are facing pay cuts at exactly the moment they need to earn more

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Mothers rarely win back the pay they lose after a first child. Millennial women are having babies now, often without leave or day care to keep working

At 34, Alicia Adamczyk, senior editor of personal finance newsletter The Purse, said she'd reached the age when college-educated women see their raises peter out. She married later than women in past generations did, doesn't own a home yet, and is weighing whether to have children. Corinne Low, an economist at the Wharton School who studies gender and discrimination, explained to her that "women's earnings do tend to level off in their mid-30s."

"That's the pattern, that's the average," Low added, "but that's not destiny."

Women don't stall in their mid-30s because of their age. Men the same age keep pulling ahead of them. In 2010, women who were 25 to 34, including the oldest millennials, made about $93 for every $100 men their age earned per hour, according to Pew Research Center. Nine years later, their salaries fell nearly three times as far behind. Over those same years, many of them started caring for children or other relatives and cut back at work, while men their age didn't.

Women fall furthest behind after a first child. Millennial women are having their first children now, many of them while starting their careers over. They're cutting back at work in the same years they're trying to rebuild. The pay women give up in their 30s doesn't come back. Gen X $TWTR women hit their 30s a decade before millennials, and they still make about $80 for every $100 men their age earn.

American women in heterosexual relationships have their first child at about 29, on average, and within a year, they earn about a fifth less than they did before having their baby, according to a working paper by Census Bureau and RAND Corporation researchers, who tracked couples' earnings records from 2005 to 2021. By 34, Adamczyk's age, they barely win back any of the pay they lost. But their husbands keep getting the same raises they got before they became fathers, and as the years go by, more of the family's money comes from the husband.

Mothers cut their hours or leave their jobs when a first child arrives, even when they bring home more money than their husbands, according to the Census and RAND researchers.. Their husbands keep working the same hours and keep getting raises, and many of those families end up depending more on the fathers' pay than on the mothers'. Same-sex couples don't divide the work that way. Both parents keep working, and by the time their child starts school, the lower earner is often making more than before.

The researchers concluded that couples decide which parent cuts back by what they think mothers and fathers are supposed to do, not by who brings home the bigger paycheck. Harvard economist Claudia Goldin told Princeton University Press in 2021 that the mother usually becomes the parent the family can always reach. "Working mothers are 'on-call at home,'" Goldin said, "whereas working fathers are 'on-call at work.'"

Goldin says mothers wouldn't have to be the parent "on-call at home" if flexible hours and child care cost families less. "The route out of these inequities is to lower the cost of workplace flexibility and childcare," she said. Half of working parents don't even get paid time off from their jobs for a new child, a sick relative, or their own health, according to a Pew survey in March. When a baby arrives, one parent takes the time unpaid or leaves, and that parent is usually the mother.

When a child gets sick or day care closes early, mothers usually leave work to handle it. Goldin says they lose far more than missed hours. The best-paying jobs pay the most to workers available at any hour. "Doubling the time far more than doubles the earnings," she said. "That is what we call 'greedy work.'" Each time mothers leave work early, they fall further out of line for those raises. Fathers stay available and collect them. And when they become the biggest earners, their families have even more reason to keep the them at work the next time a child gets sick.

Some mothers eventually weigh what day care costs against what they now earn and decide to stay home. Low, the Wharton economist, told Adamczyk that the choice "is not about the spot price right now versus your wages right now," because by leaving the workforce, mothers give up the "career growth that might increase your work-life balance later."

Millennial mothers are choosing now between unpaid time off and leaving their jobs. Without paid leave, many more will quit, saving a few years of day care bills while giving up raises for the rest of their careers.
