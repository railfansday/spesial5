Extreme weather in India and China shared a hidden atmospheric connection

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

A new analysis reveals how atmospheric circulation connected extreme heat in India with a rapid shift from flooding to humid heat in southern China.

India baked under a stubborn heat dome while southern China lurched from flooding into dangerous, humid heat.

A new analysis links the May 2026 extremes to the same sprawling high-pressure system.

Meng Gao from Hong Kong Baptist University led the analysis, with collaborators from the Chinese Academy of Meteorological Sciences and Nanjing University of Information Science and Technology.

For much of early May, temperatures across the Indian region studied had been below normal.

Starting on May 17, that changed sharply, with temperatures staying over 1°C above the recent May average.

The researchers combined weather observations, atmospheric reanalysis, and climate simulations to trace the change.

Their maps showed daily average temperatures exceeding 40°C in some locations.

May 20 stood out. Across the region studied, it was the hottest May 20 in the record stretching back to 1979.

Humidity added to the strain. The wet-bulb temperature, which reflects the combined effects of heat and moisture, ranked third-highest for that date.

For people working outside, particularly in northern India, the combination made an already difficult season more dangerous.

The developing El Niño offered part of the explanation. By May, the central and eastern equatorial Pacific had entered El Niño conditions.

Atmospheric circulation shifted far beyond its shores.

The researchers linked this developing El Niño to a stronger western Pacific subtropical high. This broad area of high pressure also stretched farther west than usual.

Meanwhile, a persistent wave pattern in the upper atmosphere strengthened another high-pressure system over the Iranian Plateau. That system moved eastward.

Over India, the two highs joined, creating a stable “heat dome.” Air sank beneath it, warming as it descended and helping keep skies clear.

With more sunshine reaching the ground, temperatures continued rising. Warm waters in the northern Indian Ocean also helped strengthen the Pacific high, further supporting the persistent heat.

In southern China, the same Pacific high helped produce a different sequence of hazards.

Guangdong had endured repeated downpours since early May.

Particularly heavy rain between May 19 and 23 brought widespread flooding, followed almost immediately by humid heat from May 24–29.

The abrupt switch followed the high-pressure system’s movement northwestward. Rain eased, sunshine increased, and descending air warmed the region.

The atmosphere, however, remained laden with moisture after the downpours. The result was a “rainfall-to-deadly humid heat” sequence, with little time between the two emergencies.

The paper reports dozens of heatstroke ambulance calls in Shenzhen and thousands of livestock deaths in Maoming.

Such dangerous combinations of heat and humidity arrived one to two months earlier than usual.

Air temperatures alone did not explain what happened in Guangdong. Readings were near the top of the historical range, but comparable heat had occurred before.

The difference was the moisture accompanying them. Relative humidity during the dangerous hours averaged about 60%, compared with roughly 50% during similarly hot hours in earlier years.

Humid air makes it harder for sweat to evaporate, limiting the body’s ability to shed heat. That means familiar thermometer readings can conceal much greater physical strain.

For several hours, conditions exceeded a modeled threshold for safe, sustained activity among healthy adults aged 65 or older. That applied even to sitting quietly or standing with minimal movement.

That threshold describes a particular group under specified conditions. Heat tolerance varies with age, activity, and exposure to sunlight.

The timing was exceptional too: these conditions appeared earlier than in any year of the available 2011–2025 comparison record.

The team also asked how human influence had changed the likelihood of India’s heat.

Using 11 climate models, the researchers compared simulations including human influences with a preindustrial climate.

Their central estimate suggested human influences had made a similarly intense Indian heatwave roughly 27 times more likely.

The estimated range was wide: about 16 to 52 times, at a 90% confidence level. The authors caution that differences between models and limitations in correcting their biases affect the result.

The authors describe the finding as evidence of a likely human contribution, with the precise scale still uncertain. The estimate applies to the Indian event, not to the entire sequence across both countries.

A common atmospheric influence helps explain why distant places suffered heat at nearly the same time. Whether those heatwaves also reinforced each other remains unresolved.

For southern China, the order of events matters. Flooding can damage electricity supplies and other infrastructure, leaving residents without reliable cooling when the heat follows.

Warnings therefore need to account for what happened in the preceding days.

A heat forecast issued during flood recovery may reach people whose homes, workplaces, and access to electricity are already disrupted.

The researchers call for stronger infrastructure and better preparation in vulnerable regions.

In places facing rapid weather reversals, flood response and heat preparation may need to happen at the same time.

The study is published in the journal Advances in Atmospheric Sciences.
