‘Hershey’ Review: An Unbelievably Flavorless Biopic About America’s Most Famous Chocolatier

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

A very successful “values-based entertainment” company that was begat — in the parlance of its biblical heritage — from a film-filtering service that illegally edited “objectionable content” out of major Hollywood movies, Angel Studios has since become the world’s foremost purveyor of megachurch-approved multiplex content. The bigger they got (on the strength of projects like “The Chosen” and the QAnon cause célèbre “Sound of Freedom,” the latter of which pioneered a questionable ticketing model that continues today), the more that Angel removed from the films they released. Not just sex and swear words, but also anything that even vaguely resembled a soul. With “Hershey,” a decidedly vanilla biopic about the most famous chocolatier who’s ever lived, the studio has taken its socio-religious project to its logical conclusion: They’ve made a movie that’s been stripped of any flavor whatsoever. It’s the cinematic equivalent of carob powder. It’s what Ned Flanders will force his kids to watch next Halloween.

Somehow unimaginatively bland and Splenda-sweet all at once, this godforsaken affront to good storytelling may not have benefited from any of the material that its distributor has deemed amoral, but “Hershey” is so mind-numbingly absent the most basic friction of the human experience that I wanted to fall to my knees and thank the lord that a few errant Kisses made it past The Angel Promise on a technicality. It’s too bad Milton S. Hershey never used his platform to baselessly sow doubt in a foreign election, because at least one of Angel Studios’ co-founders would clearly have been cool with including that (ecocidal fascism is fine, just don’t say “fuck” while you’re razing the Amazon, as that would offend Jesus).

As you may have gathered by now, I am probably not the target audience for this unrepentant godslop.

Not because I’m agnostic (“Hershey” is as secular as Angel Studios’ output gets, its overt religiosity limited to a brief scene in which the movie’s namesake pleads for help from an unspecified higher power), but rather because I stubbornly, blasphemously insist that a womb-to-tomb biopic should bear at least some faint resemblance to the stuff of real life. I couldn’t possibly care less about the facts (though I struggle to believe that Hershey and his wife literally danced with joy after learning that a twist of fate had spared them from being aboard the Titanic), but there isn’t a single moment in this film that feels like it was written by a real person, let alone lived by one.

Let’s back up a beat. To be clear, Angel Studios didn’t technically make this movie — that distinction belongs to the spiritually aligned production company Dandelion Media, who partnered with Hershey Entities for a fully authorized feature-length commercial meant to “bring [the chocolate brand’s] story into culture in a more immersive way.” The success of that marketing spend remains to be seen, but it can’t hurt to highlight Mr. Hershey’s ostensible purity at a time when the company that bears his name has been under fire for diluting its products with compound coatings in order to save money. And so we have a punishingly straightforward film that paints the Pennsylvania icon as a square-jawed, sweet-toothed wife guy who loved candy, orphans, and sitting way too close to the edge of the massive water tower that overlooks the town he remade in his own image.

Directed by Mark Waters, but absent any trace of the poppy confidence he once brought to the likes of “Mean Girls” and “Freaky Friday,” “Hershey” opens with its first and only stylistic flourish as a match cut transforms an immaculate chocolate Kiss into a small lump of coal — Milton Hershey’s life would turn out to be sweet, but it sure didn’t start that way. The film that follows sadly feels less like “Lawrence of Arabia” than it does an AI-generated adaptation of Hershey’s Wikipedia page, its algorithmic sense of human interaction rendered all the more uncanny by its ecclesiastical approach to conflict (and also by its blown out cinematography, which suggests a very literal interpretation of Angel Studios’ promise to produce “movies and shows that amplify light.”)

Adhering to the prosaic structure and candied morality that the Erwin brothers have made endemic to the world of faith-based biopics and their immediate kin, the film sees Hershey’s life as a blessed march towards his eternal reward — one that’s only slowed down by the litany of speed bumps that pop up along his path to heaven.

Young Milton’s very serious Mennonite mom is a major bummer, but she lets him out of the house to work for a local printer. The JRPG-like town where he grows up in the 1860s seems inhospitable to dreams, but its muddy roads make it appear as though the streets are paved with chocolate. His dad (Alan Ruck, a long way from “Succession”) abandons him pretty fast, but not before bending down to the kid like Zooey Deschanel in “Almost Famous” and telling him: “You’re a Hershey, and that means when you follow your dream, you don’t let anyone get in your way.” Apparently his dad’s dream was to not live with his family anymore, but so be it. A good example is a good example.

Every stumbling block is immediately followed by a burst of divine providence, as Sharon Paul and Timothy Michael Hayes’ script adheres to the grindset mindset of the prosperity gospel while Rolfe Kent’s saccharine score blends everything together into a tasteless slurry of unrefined sugar. The film insists that a little sweetness goes a long way, as an orgasmic taste of rock candy is all that Milton needs to make it through the day, but it doesn’t heed its own advice. Milton’s passion leads him to a job working for a saintly confectioner named Joseph Royer (a beneficent Richard Kind, as close to a live-action Bing Bong as he’s ever been), who just smiles it off when the kid forgets to turn off the peanut roaster and nearly burns down the whole shop.

Later, after sprouting into Finn Wittrock and starting his own caramel company, Milton is miraculously spared from ruin several times over by virtue of his non-denominational faith in himself and the universe. One low moment is redeemed by a massive order from overseas. Another resolves with a traveling salesman busting into the Lancaster Caramel Company with an offer to invest in chocolate — the future of snacking! “This chocolate,” Milton sniffs, “it could be good if it wasn’t so bad.” I know the feeling. The next time disaster strikes, he inadvertently stumbles upon the Hershey Kiss in the very next scene. The plotting is schematic enough to make “Bohemian Rhapsody” seem like “Last Days” by comparison.

Somewhere in there Milton enjoys a meet-cute with a woman named Kitty Sweeney (Alexandria Daddario), a grinning beauty who credits caramels with curing her shyness. Her job as Milton’s wife is to be his biggest cheerleader, and then — as the years melt away — to collapse in undiagnosed pain whenever he leaves the room. At one point it is implied that she and Milton have sexual intercourse, but only so the movie can leverage the couple’s inability to conceive a child into a robust philanthropic regime that allowed them to house and educate any number of white male orphans.

Of course, expecting this movie to acknowledge the Milton Hershey School’s history of racial and social discrimination would be as delusional as thinking its third act might address the labor disputes that unsettled Hershey’s chocolate factory in the late 1930s. Such things are simply not sweet enough to befit a story about how some vague belief and a little stick-it-to-em are all a man needs to prosper in this great land — even if a warts-and-all version of that same story would still be a model of American success.

Considerably less textured than the chocolate bars that have become synonymous with its title character, Waters’ film is so determined to launder one man’s dreams into reality that it comes to assume its own kind of dream logic in return — one divorced from the basic relationship between cause-and-effect or an event and its meaning.

In what might be the single most deranged movie scene of the year, Milton and his ailing wife Kitty literally dance for joy on the bow of a ship after learning that a twist of fate has spared them from being aboard the Titanic, even though upwards of 1,400 other people went to a watery grave that night. I’m not sure that qualifies as bringing the brand’s “story into the culture in a more immersive way,” but it’s definitely what I’m going to be thinking about the next time I bite into a milky and delicious Hershey Bar. After watching this perversely chalky film about the company’s founder, it’s a wonder that I can still remember what their signature product even tastes like.

Angel Studios will release “Hershey” in theaters on Wednesday, November 25.
