10 Tiny Gear Upgrades That Make a Huge Difference

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

When evaluating gear to bring on trips, I do tend to be fairly stingy with weight. For something to make it into my backpack, it needs to add a level of enjoyment to my hike that surpasses the penalty I’ll feel on my back from the item’s weight.

Over the years, I’ve discovered a handful of small, relatively inexpensive upgrades to my gear that have made a massive difference in the ease of a day on trail. While not strictly necessary, I would suffer the weight penalty for each of the below items on almost any trail.

For years, I complained that the worst chore at camp was blowing up my sleeping pad. Most of my backpacking was done at or above 10,000 feet, and by the time my pad was inflated, my vision would be blurring and I would feel lightheaded and terrible.

Plus, blowing a bunch of moist air into my sleeping pad would quickly lead to mold growth. Within a month, I could see little spots growing inside the pad when I held it up to the light.

The last thing I want to do when I get to camp is blow up a sleeping pad. Photo via Katie Jackson

A tiny, rechargeable pump like the Flextail or — my personal favorite — the Alpenblow Lux weighs just a few ounces and will inflate my pad for me in under a minute. It saves me from losing my breath each night and extends the life of the pad. Mostly, though, it makes each night at camp significantly less annoying.

For years, I hiked with two Smartwater bottles in my side pocket — one for clean water and one for dirty water. Eventually, I got pretty disgusted with constantly reusing a type of plastic not intended for reuse. The thin walls of a single-use bottle get permanently crinkled, trap grime in the deep thread grooves, and shed inordinate amounts of microplastic into drinking water.

The Cnoc ThruBottle is what I currently reach for when I want an exact Smartwater replacement but with different materials. Photo via Katie Jackson

However, I unfortunately love the functionality of the Smartwater bottle, so any replacement I considered needed to behave similarly without adding a ton of weight. Brands like Mazama Designs, Igneous Gear, and CNOC have changed the game with the M!GO bottle, NOBO bottle, and ThruBottle, respectively. All three are durable, squeezable, and are compatible with the most popular backcountry filters, which has let me keep my current “one dirty, one clean” water bottle system on the trail.

Hear me out, because this one’s weird. Adopting the Trailbrush has actually lowered the weight in my pack.

I really struggle with brushing my teeth while hiking. I find that the mental exertion of getting from Point A to Point B leaves nothing in the tank. By the time I get into camp, set up my shelter, and cook dinner, the absolute last thing I want to do is have a bad time brushing my teeth in the woods without running water. My dental hygiene on trail was … not great.

I hate not being able to rinse my toothbrush with abundant running water so much. Photo via Katie Jackson

The Trailbrush is a little toothbrush head that attaches to a long-handled spork, which I found really reduced the friction in my nighttime routine.

Is it a super enjoyable toothbrush replacement? No. But having it right there when I finish dinner has increased my toothbrushing by about 1,000%. I frequently find myself in the comments of posts defending this little accessory.

If you think it sounds silly, then it’s probably not for you, but if you end each trail worried about how infrequently you actually brushed your teeth, I cannot recommend this little Trailbrush enough.

I’ve spent so long searching for the perfect rain jacket, and I’ve finally been forced to accept that what I want doesn’t exist in jacket form. No jacket out there will be genuinely waterproof, allow airflow, keep me warm without causing me to overheat, and stay effective for thousands of miles. You know what does do all those things? An umbrella.

I first used an umbrella on the CDT for sun protection down in New Mexico, but quickly realized how useful it was during rain and hail storms. Instead of sending it home at the end of the desert as planned, I carried it all the way to Canada. I’ll be carrying an umbrella on any hike calling for rain gear from here on; it’s too wildly practical and easy to pass up.

I think an umbrella has dramatically changed the way I view rain on trails. Photo via Katie Jackson

For the CDT, I used a Gossamer Gear umbrella and had no complaints. However, I have my eye on Hyperlite Mountain Gear’s umbrella, as I think the vents around the top would help stabilize it during heavy rain.

The day I let go of the hold Crocs had on me was the day I started finding more value in my camp shoes.

If Crocs work for you, that’s great. They make sense on paper: waterproof, fairly lightweight, and sturdy enough for a river crossing. But I got pretty fed up with how bulky they were in my pack compared to the use I got out of them.

I’ve made the switch to Xero Shoes Z-Trail EV Sandals and have been loving them. They weigh much less than a pair of Crocs, can still be worn with socks on, and take up about a fourth of the space in my pack.

Gone are the days of shoving my Crocs into my bear can to make them fit on my pack. Photo via Katie Jackson

The bottom still has great grip for those who sometimes hike in camp shoes. They’re so minimalist that I don’t feel bad carrying them even if I only use them around my tent.

Finding your own personal hygiene balance on a thru-hike comes down to personal preferences. I don’t carry deodorant or a lot of other things I use at home to stay fresh and clean; I’ve accepted that thru-hiking is nasty and I will probably just look and smell nasty while doing it.

I like strapping it to the front of my pack, since I’m more likely to grab it if I don’t have to take my pack off to reach it. Photo via Katie Jackson

However, the Kula Cloth is one thing I always bring, if only to keep myself from smelling like pee all the time. A Kula Cloth is a reusable, antimicrobial pee cloth that is an eco-friendly replacement for traditional toilet paper in the backcountry (for pee only! Not for #2!).

Before I started using one, peeing on trail meant either packing out toilet paper or a quasi-effective drip dry. The Kula Cloth snaps to the outside of my pack, where it dries in the sun throughout the day. It carries no odor, thanks to the silver thread woven into it. For such a tiny, lightweight addition to my pack, the boost in cleanliness is astronomical.

For the low, low price of mere pennies, you can have the most waterproof bag on the market.

I’ve spent hundreds of miles hiking with a pack cover. The trouble with this setup is that in prolonged, heavy rain, water still seeps into the pack through the unprotected back panel. Sick of I’ve arriving at camp with wet gear despite faithfully using a pack cover, I finally made the switch to a trash compactor bag.

I like not having to worry about buying the right size pack cover for my pack; the same trash compactor bag has taken me through almost the entire CDT and is still going strong. Photo via Katie Jackson

Instead of covering the outside of your pack, you line the inside of your backpack with a heavy-duty trash compactor bag before packing your gear. Everything critical, especially your sleeping quilt and down jacket, goes right inside that plastic liner. When a downpour hits, you don’t need to stop and add anything to your pack to keep your gear dry.

It weighs a fraction of an ounce, costs practically nothing, and guarantees your gear stays dry even if your entire pack gets soaked.

Sometimes, when hiking, I get very fixated on the task at hand and struggle to prioritize food and water breaks. If grabbing a snack means I have to stop, take my pack off, and dig around inside, I often just ignore the hunger and keep hiking until I’m way too crabby and ravenous to continue.

Through some trial and error, I’ve found that the most practical way around this is to keep all my daily snacks and drink powders in a fanny pack so I can access what I want without stopping.

That bad boy can fit a lot of snacks. Photo by Will Standish

Not only does having everything on-hand help me keep up with important tasks (eating, drinking, reapplying sunscreen, etc.), but it also shifts some of my gear weight to the front, which helps me with balance and back pain.

I use the 2.5L Trailmix Switchpack from Chicken Tramper Ultralight Gear, but any large chest pouch, side bag, or similarly-sized fanny pack will function just as well.

A pillow doesn’t make it into my thru-hiking gear list, mostly because I think a lot of the super light and super small options are also super uncomfortable. When pack weight and space are at a premium, a puffy jacket shoved into its own pocket works just fine for me as a pillow.

However, I can’t lie and pretend that a bulky, slightly heavier pillow doesn’t dramatically improve my sleep quality when I’m willing to just carry it along with me. I prefer pillows with foam inside because they work more like a pillow at home and less like an air-filled dry bag. The NEMO Fillo is a nice hybrid foam-and-inflatable option.

If you’re bothered by the texture of your pillow against your cheek, you can still throw a nice fleece on top to act as a makeshift pillowcase. Photo via Katie Jackson

This obviously won’t work for every trip, but I’ve found a lot of value recently in intentionally infusing fun into my outdoor adventures. Sometimes I’ll bring a craft, like my current knitting project. Other times, I reach for an inflatable pool floatie or, if I’m feeling extra strong that day, my inflatable paddle board.

I’ve even packed an extra swimsuit for a trail that took me right up by the ocean. Photo via Katie Jackson

Anything that gives me a good excuse to stop hiking for a bit and really sit in my surroundings has enhanced my hikes. Other good ideas for this category: a physical book, a watercolor set, a Cornhole set, a board game, or some fairy lights for your tent.

These things force me to slow down and have fun on trips when there’s no need to grind from dusk to dawn to hit a certain mileage goal.
