Jim Huffman Is Turning a Sweat Problem Into a Business Built for Growth

After taking Neat Apparel to the Shark Tank stage, entrepreneur Jim Huffman is betting on performance-driven apparel, smarter marketing, and the power of consistency. But his biggest motivation goes far beyond building a nine-figure brand.

Sweat is practically a badge of honor in fitness. It represents the final rep, the last sprint, the extra mile, and the work that happens long before anyone sees the results. For athletes and gym-goers, it’s part of the process.

As legendary soccer star Mia Hamm once put it, “The vision of a champion is bent over, drenched in sweat, at the point of exhaustion, when nobody else is looking.”

But while sweat might be a welcome sign of hard work in the gym, it’s not always something people want showing through their clothes.

For entrepreneur Jim Huffman, that everyday frustration presented an opportunity to rethink an overlooked wardrobe staple.

That opportunity became Neat Apparel, a performance-driven clothing company built around technology designed to prevent sweat from showing through clothing. Now, following an appearance on ABC’s Shark Tank, Huffman is looking to take the brand to an entirely new level.

The idea wasn’t necessarily born from a lifelong ambition to build an apparel company. Instead, it came down to recognizing a familiar consumer problem, understanding the potential of a differentiated product, and seeing an opportunity to apply his experience in business growth to something of his own.

Huffman is also the founder of GrowthHit, a growth marketing agency specializing in helping businesses scale. After spending years developing strategies and building growth systems for other companies, he began looking for an opportunity to put those same principles to work on a business of his own.

Neat presented an interesting proposition: a product with a clear point of differentiation, a problem consumers could immediately recognize, and technology that could demonstrate its value without requiring a lengthy explanation.

And while the business opportunity is compelling, his perspective on sweat extends beyond the product itself. In a world where fitness culture celebrates visible effort, Neat is built around a different side of the experience: helping people feel comfortable and confident whether they’re training, working, traveling, or navigating everyday life.

For Huffman, the challenge now is turning a product built around a relatable problem into a brand that can reach a much bigger audience.

And perhaps that’s where the connection between entrepreneurship and fitness becomes most apparent: Both demand consistency, a willingness to embrace challenges, and the ability to keep showing up when the results aren’t immediate.

After all, if sweat is proof that you’ve put in the work, why should it determine how confident you feel walking out of the gym?

Neat’s story began with technology originally developed through performance apparel research, with applications being explored for sports and military uniforms.

Huffman’s business partner, Tyler, had connections to the performance apparel industry, where the team was working with emerging fabric technologies. Through that process, they encountered intellectual property that eventually became the foundation for Neat.

The challenge was finding the right consumer application.

As Huffman explained, the technology had initially been considered for soccer, but the team recognized that athletes weren’t necessarily concerned about visible sweat during competition. The opportunity was to bring that technology to consumers who wanted to stay comfortable and presentable throughout the day.

Huffman became familiar with the product through his work with the apparel team. Having grown up in Oklahoma and spent time in Texas, he understood the frustration of trying to look presentable in hot weather.

“I used to have go in the bathroom and pat my shirt off to look somewhat presentable,” he recalled.

After receiving a sample, his reaction was immediate.

Rather than immediately jumping into the business, Huffman took a more calculated approach. He spoke with approximately 20 existing customers to understand whether the product had genuine demand.

The response was encouraging. Customers weren’t simply complimenting the shirts. They were asking the company not to go out of business, requesting additional colors, and wanting more products.

For an entrepreneur focused on growth, that feedback was important.

It suggested that Neat wasn’t just a clever product looking for an audience. It had an audience that was already looking for more.

One of Neat’s biggest advantages is that its technology can be demonstrated visually.

In an increasingly crowded apparel market, simply telling consumers that a shirt is different isn’t necessarily enough. Huffman understood that the company needed to show people why it was different.

The brand’s creative approach includes demonstrations that make the product’s purpose immediately recognizable, including videos featuring water balloons and fabric tests.

“We wanted to hit on not just the benefit but the use case,” Huffman explained.

That thinking also inspired one of the company’s more memorable marketing concepts: a wall of sweat-soaked shirts, displayed almost like a sports memorabilia exhibit.

Rather than relying on traditional product photography, the campaign allowed consumers to see the problem Neat was attempting to solve.

Neat isn’t necessarily asking consumers to abandon their favorite athletic brands. It’s asking them to consider another factor when choosing what to wear: how their clothing performs when temperatures rise.

Appearing on Shark Tank brought Neat to a much larger audience, but Huffman understands that television exposure is only one part of building a sustainable business.

The company’s next challenge is turning that attention into long-term growth.

Huffman has already seen how marketing decisions can dramatically affect a company’s ability to scale. Through GrowthHit, he’s worked with businesses across different stages of development, and those experiences have shaped how he approaches Neat.

One of the biggest lessons he’s learned is that businesses need to understand their market before investing heavily in growth.

“Honestly, it’s market selection,” he said. “I think people are like, ‘Oh, this thing’s cool. Let me see if I can sell it.’ And they’re not intentional of, is this a market that in five to 10 years is going up and to the right?”

He believes that even an excellent product can struggle when it’s introduced to a market that isn’t willing to pay for it.

His second consideration is the business’s financial model.

When Huffman and his team became involved with Neat, the shirts were selling for approximately $38. But the existing pricing structure made it difficult to support the advertising costs required to grow the company.

The team shifted its approach toward bundles and discounts designed to increase average order value, while also adjusting pricing.

The goal wasn’t simply to sell more individual shirts. It was to establish Neat as a wardrobe staple that consumers would purchase repeatedly.

“We’re trying to villainize the cotton T-shirt,” Huffman joked.

He clarified that the company isn’t against cotton. Rather, it’s trying to educate consumers about the difference between an everyday cotton shirt and a performance-driven option designed to stay with them throughout the day.

Huffman’s experience also reflects a broader shift in how fitness and lifestyle brands connect with consumers.

Traditional advertising still has its place, but polished production doesn’t automatically translate into stronger performance.

Neat discovered this firsthand after investing approximately $30,000 in a high-production video that failed to perform as well as expected.

Meanwhile, simpler creator-produced videos featuring the product generated stronger results.

“Our best performing ads are not the fancy overproduced ads,” Huffman said. “We spent $30,000 on a high production video, did not perform as well as I liked, and then we’ll throw a T-shirt at some 20-something, and they make an ad that really scales for us.”

That experience pushed the company toward user-generated content, influencers, and creators who could demonstrate the product in a more natural environment.

The approach makes sense for Neat because the product is inherently visual. Consumers want to see how it works, how it fits, and how it performs in everyday situations.

Huffman also recognizes that different social platforms require different strategies. TikTok audiences may respond to quick demonstrations and impulse-driven purchases, while Meta platforms can support a different consumer journey.

The lesson is that successful marketing isn’t necessarily about producing the most expensive content. It’s about understanding the audience and communicating the product’s value in a way that resonates.

While Neat’s growth and its Shark Tank appearance have brought Huffman plenty of professional excitement, his definition of success has changed considerably over the past year.

During the interview, Huffman shared the deeply personal experience that led him and his team to establish the Wrenegade Fund, an initiative supporting pediatric cancer research.

In May, his youngest daughter, Wren, began complaining of what initially appeared to be growing pains. Knowing that she was a tough child who rarely complained, the family decided to take her to the hospital.

The following day, they received devastating news: Wren had a rare form of bone cancer.

The diagnosis came at an especially demanding time for the family. Huffman had recently become involved with Neat, the business had taken on significant financial commitments, and the company was preparing for a major order.

Suddenly, the priorities that had once dominated his attention looked very different.

“You’re just like, how am I going to do all of this, right?” he recalled. “And so that’s where you really figure out real quick what you need to focus on and not what really matters.”

Wren underwent 23 rounds of chemotherapy and a life-changing amputation surgery known as rotationplasty.

Today, Huffman describes her as stronger than ever.

Her experience also inspired the family to turn an incredibly difficult chapter into an opportunity to help other children facing similar diagnoses. Through GrowthHit, the team began donating 1% of its profits to pediatric cancer research. They also launched the Wrenegade Fund to make supporting research a larger part of their mission.

In September, the family organized a fundraiser called “Pie Hard,” raising $60,000. For every $1,000 raised, Wren earned the opportunity to throw a pie in someone’s face.

The event brought a little humor into a mission rooted in something much bigger.

Huffman also shared how the family’s openness about Wren’s experience connected them with other families navigating similar circumstances.

One particularly meaningful connection involved a young girl in Montana who had been following Wren’s story. She was facing a difficult decision about surgery and learned about rotationplasty through Wren’s experience.

The families eventually met, and the girl was able to pursue the same procedure.

For Huffman, those moments have become a reminder of how much impact one family’s willingness to share its story can have on another.

“If there’s  an exit with this, it’s like pouring that money into pediatric cancer research,” he said of his long-term business ambitions. It’s a mission that gives the company’s growth a different kind of purpose.

For an entrepreneur balancing a marketing agency, an apparel company, and family life, consistency is essential. But Huffman’s approach to fitness is less about chasing an impressive physique and more about maintaining the mental clarity required to lead.

He currently completes three to four 30-minute HIIT workouts each week, usually at 6:15 in the morning.

“If I do it any time afternoon, I’m too tired and lazy and I won’t do it,” he admitted.

Getting his workout in early helps establish momentum before the demands of the day take over.

More importantly, he notices a difference in his mood and leadership when he maintains the routine.

“I am in such a better mood and a better leader when I get that going,” he said. “If I skip my workout, I’m probably a grump.”

It’s a familiar challenge for anyone trying to balance work, training, and personal responsibilities. Huffman’s solution isn’t necessarily complicated. He plans his workouts around his calendar and accepts that some weeks won’t go perfectly.

He also credits his wife, who works at Amazon and maintains her own fitness routine, with helping establish healthy habits at home.

“Partner selection is so crucial to this,” he explained.

For Huffman, having a partner who prioritizes fitness makes consistency easier. He also recognizes that maintaining a healthy relationship is essential to keeping his focus on the things that matter.

A demanding professional life can quickly become overwhelming when personal relationships suffer. Having someone who understands the ups and downs of entrepreneurship, supports the risks, and provides perspective has been invaluable.

His approach to fitness mirrors his approach to business: small, consistent actions can compound into something much bigger.

“We all know it’s like you need to be the 10-year overnight success where these small things compound,” he said. “That’s what works with fitness. That’s what works with diet. That’s what works with business.”

Huffman’s ambitions for Neat are substantial.

He wants to build the company into a recognizable household brand, beginning with men’s apparel before expanding into additional markets and product categories.

That could eventually include women’s apparel, wholesale opportunities, and international expansion. The company is also exploring additional styles, including polos, henleys, long sleeves, and potentially underwear.

For now, however, Huffman is resisting the temptation to expand too quickly.

“Shiny object syndrome is something I suffer from, and that can hurt a lot of businesses,” he said.

His immediate priority is to meet demand for the existing men’s collection, continue improving the product, and build a strong foundation before moving into new categories.

When asked what advice he would give someone entering the apparel industry, he emphasized two things: understand your customer and establish a genuine point of differentiation.

“Don’t be afraid to go smaller rather than bigger. Niche down because I think that’s where the future is going,” he said.

He also believes businesses need something more defensible than a recognizable brand name alone. For Neat, its underlying patent and product technology provide a point of differentiation that helps separate it from competitors.

Ultimately, Huffman isn’t measuring his success solely by revenue milestones, television appearances, or the possibility of building a nine-figure company.

When asked about his greatest accomplishment, his answer had little to do with business.

“It’s just like having a family that is along for the ride and loves being together with all of this,” he said.

He explained that he’s seen how professional success can sometimes come at the expense of family relationships, something he has no interest in sacrificing.

“It’s not a sexy answer, but it’s just like the biggest success is the little family we built.”

For an entrepreneur who has spent much of his career helping businesses grow, that may be the most important lesson of all: Success isn’t just about how far you can take a company. It’s about what you’re building along the way, who you’re building it with, and what you ultimately want that success to accomplish.
