Welcome back to World Brief, where we’re looking at China’s diplomatic maneuvering, the possibility of an Iranian attack on U.S. soil, and Ukraine targeting Russia’s bridge to Crimea.

U.S. President Donald Trump touched down in the Philippines on Tuesday for a few hours of shmoozing with some of the most powerful leaders in the Indo-Pacific, including his fellow members of the Quadrilateral Security Dialogue and his counterparts in the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN). With more summitry still to come, experts expect Trump to use this week to try and project power and confidence amid growing political challenges back home.

China, however, may have already beaten Trump to the punch.

Right out of the gate, Chinese President Xi Jinping has signaled that his administration is willing to act as a counterweight to the White House’s increasingly provocative trade policies. On Tuesday, Beijing agreed to cut duties on a swath of Canadian exports, amounting to roughly $280 million in tariff relief. In exchange, Ottawa promised to extend remission measures for certain Chinese steel and aluminum products by another two years.

“Stronger communication and cooperation between China and Canada will benefit not only our two countries but also Asia-Pacific and the world,” Xi told Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney during talks in Shenzhen, China. This was their third meeting since Carney took power in 2025, and it marked Ottawa’s continued efforts to diversify its trade away from Washington.

China also pledged on Tuesday to provide Peru with $22.5 million in aid to address what is expected to be the devastating fallout of this year’s El Niño. Lima applauded the measure, with President Keiko Fujimori stressing that “China is very important to us because it is our ​principal trading partner.”

Peru is one of the many places in Latin America where Beijing and Washington have competed for influence. Earlier this month, the U.S. State Department disclosed that it has committed $4.5 million to help Peru modernize its main port at Callao, in a move seen as a direct counter to a Chinese-controlled megaport less than 50 miles away that opened in 2024.

Perhaps most notably, though, is China’s potential openness to converse with Japan. A war of words over Taiwan, coupled with Beijing’s rare-earth export controls, has plagued Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi’s tenure. However, during Tuesday’s ASEAN gathering in Manila, Takaichi shook hands with Chinese Agriculture Minister Zhang Zhu. Although few words were exchanged, the fact that such an interaction occurred has raised hopes that Takaichi’s upcoming trip to Shenzhen may be more cordial than previously anticipated.

Yet Beijing’s biggest days may still be to come. On Wednesday, Xi will host Trump and Russian President Vladimir Putin for trilateral talks on nuclear disarmament and the Iran war. With Trump at his weakest point to date (entering his lame-duck term after sweeping midterm losses and a Nvidia spy scandal), experts anticipate that Xi will aim to capitalize on Trump’s rocky footing.

“Trump is liable to make large and unwise concessions to his imagined peers in order to secure political coups that he hopes can restore prestige and momentum to his badly weakened presidency,” FP columnist Howard W. French argued.

Dirty bomb threat? Iran is actively planning for potential strikes on U.S. soil, seven U.S. officials told CNN on Tuesday. Whether Tehran will or even can execute such an attack is unclear, and White House spokesperson Lauren Bis has insisted that “there are no credible threats to the homeland.” Still, even the possibility of such an assault signals growing alarm that a peaceful end to the Iran war may be out of reach.

CNN reported that, according to U.S. intelligence, Tehran is considering detonating a so-called dirty bomb or chemical weapon inside Washington to target senior Trump administration officials. Sources told CNN that this is not an indicator of an imminent attack but rather is one of several options that Iran may be exploring. Iranian officials, however, denied the report, with chief negotiator Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf calling it “false flags” and “war mongering.”

Experts, meanwhile, insist that Tehran may not have the capabilities or the desire to carry out such a plot. “An Iranian dirty bomb in Washington would be very hard to pull off,” retired U.S. Navy Adm. James Stavridis told CNN. “They’d have to move uranium halfway around the world and have a team in place to set it off. I don’t think they’ll do it. The U.S. response would be overwhelming.”

Trump expressed a similar notion on Tuesday. When asked about the CNN article, the U.S. president told reporters that Iran would “pay a price the likes of which no country has ever paid before” were it to attack the United States homeland.

Crosshairs on Crimean bridge. Ukrainian forces launched a second strike on Tuesday at a strategic bridge linking Russia to the Crimean Peninsula, which Moscow illegally annexed in 2014. “Russia built a myth around it as a symbol of Crimea being ‘Russian forever,’” Ukraine’s military command said in a statement. “Now, part of the structure lies in the water.” The attack marked the second consecutive assault on the critical crossing in just two days.

The Kerch Strait Bridge—often highlighted as one of Russian President Vladimir Putin’s signature infrastructure projects—is the only rail crossing between Russia and the Crimean territory. Moscow has repeatedly used the 12-mile road to transport military and civilian supplies to its troops as well as to exert control over Ukraine’s southern coast; the Kerch Strait acts as a chokepoint for naval access from the Black Sea to the Sea of Azov. In recent years, the route has also become a symbol of Russia’s yearslong occupation.

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky characterized this week’s attacks on the bridge as the culmination of weeks of preparatory strikes. Meanwhile, Russia’s Defense Ministry confirmed on Tuesday that the assault on Kerch Strait Bridge occurred in tandem with a massive Ukrainian drone operation; Moscow claimed to have intercepted 1,441 drones across 22 regions on Tuesday in one of Kyiv’s biggest such attacks yet.

What is Belgrade’s future? Hundreds of people gathered outside Serbia’s National Assembly on Tuesday to cheer on the country’s new parliament, the first since the ouster of President Aleksandar Vucic. Led by the grassroots Student List movement, the inaugural session kicked off what is expected to be the long process of forming a government.

Nearly two years of student-led protests against one of Europe’s most staunchly populist regimes culminated in the opposition’s sweeping victory in parliamentary elections last month. Student List lawmakers dominated in the polls, winning 140 seats in the 250-member National Assembly compared to the Vucic-led Serbian Progressive Party’s 103 seats. Vucic initially blamed foreign interference for his loss, without citing evidence, despite international observers noting that his party held an unfair advantage.

Now begins the difficult task of governing a country that has been led by Vucic for the past 14 years. On Tuesday, the Student List was expected to nominate university professor Aleksandar Cupic to be the country’s new parliamentary speaker. After that, the grassroots movement will still have to choose a prime minister and hold a snap presidential election; the deadline for the latter expires at the end of this year.

The end of Pope Leo XIV’s 12-day Latin America tour wrapped up this week with a painful surprise. While traveling in the popemobile before his final Mass in Peru on Monday, Leo was struck in the eye by a flying rosary. It is unclear who threw the prayer beads, as worshippers often toss gifts at the pope, who is known for his (usually) quick reflexes.

However, Leo has already shaken off the incident. “The nice thing about Peruvians, including the person who with a lot of faith simply wanted to offer me a rosary, are things you carry in your heart—or maybe in your eye—but that mark the life of someone,” Leo said on Tuesday.
