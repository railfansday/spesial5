Trump's birthright citizenship restrictions struck down again by DC federal judge he called 'hater'

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Fox News legal editor Kerri Urbahn analyzes President Trump's new executive orders targeting birth tourism and birthright citizenship, plus the legal battle with the ACLU and a Texas hospital settlement. Urbahn discusses the constitutional battles ahead.

A DC federal judge, previously dubbed by President Donald Trump as an "anti- Trump Hater," blocked Trump's second attempt to restrict birthright citizenship in a Wednesday ruling.

U.S. District Judge Christopher Cooper, who was recently in Trump's crosshairs after ruling against efforts to shut down the Kennedy Center for renovations and add Trump's name to the cultural institution, issued a 67-page ruling striking down an August executive order that barred U.S. -born children whose parents are suspected terrorists or gang members, or children born through surrogacy to foreign parents, from citizenship eligibility.

Cooper's ruling comes months after the Supreme Court struck down Trump's initial executive order, signed on his first day back in office. The order attempted to deny birthright citizenship to U.S.-born children whose mothers were in the country illegally or temporarily and whose fathers were neither U.S. citizens nor green card holders.

"As the Supreme Court has made clear, however, the President cannot carve out new exceptions to birthright citizenship for children born within the United States and subject to its laws," Cooper ruled.

Trump's second executive order was already partially blocked on Sept. 2 by U.S. District Judge Deborah Boardman in Maryland, whose injunction protected U.S.-born children of mothers who were in the country illegally or temporarily and whose fathers were neither U.S. citizens nor lawful permanent residents.

Cooper's ruling expanded protections beyond that class to include additional children born to certain foreign government and international organization employees, parents deemed "alien enemies" by the administration and foreign parents who used commercial surrogacy services in the U.S.

"The merits in this case are clear-cut. The Supreme Court has twice confirmed that all children born in this country are U.S. citizens, save only for the small handful who are not subject to U.S. law," Cooper wrote.

In addition to the Supreme Court's June ruling in Trump v. Barbara, Cooper referred to the high court's ruling in United States v. Wong Kim Ark, which determined that a man born to Chinese immigrants was a protected U.S. citizen under the 14th Amendment.

Cooper noted that Wong Kim Ark established narrow exceptions to birthright citizenship, including for children of foreign diplomats, children born aboard foreign public ships and children of enemy forces occupying American territory.

"While the Fourteenth Amendment may theoretically permit new exceptions beyond those listed in Wong Kim Ark, Congress has not authorized any such exceptions," Cooper wrote.

Cooper argued that membership in a terrorist organization alone does not satisfy the historical exception to birthright citizenship for children of enemy forces occupying U.S. territory. He criticized the Trump administration's contention that foreign invaders could occupy American territory without actually holding it as an "outside-the-box understanding" of what constitutes an occupation.

"To recap, a parent’s purported status as an alien enemy, standing alone, cannot strip a U.S.-born child of his right to birthright citizenship," Cooper wrote. "The alien-enemy exception, properly understood, applies only to children born to hostile enemy forces in temporarily occupied U.S. territory."

Cooper also rejected Trump's attempt to deny birthright citizenship to children born through commercial surrogacy arrangements involving foreign parents, finding that the circumstances of their birth do not exempt them from U.S. jurisdiction.

"Indeed, foreign parents choose surrogacy arrangements in the United States precisely to have the child be governed by U.S. law and have the surrogacy process ‘supervised by a court.," Cooper wrote.

He noted that, prior to Trump's second executive order, the State Department recognized the citizenship of children born on American soil regardless of whether they were born through surrogacy.

The Trump administration is expected to appeal the decision and request that the executive order be temporarily enforced while the litigation works its way through the courts, a process that could take months.

"This is just another ruling from an activist judge appointed by Obama attempting to thwart President Trump’s commonsense agenda," White House spokeswoman Lauren Bis said in a statement to Fox News Digital. "The birthright citizenship executive order is consistent with the holdings and analysis set forth by the Supreme Court."

The Justice Department could seek temporary relief from the U.S. Court of Appeals for the DC Circuit or seek intervention from the Supreme Court. Fox News Digital reached out to the Justice Department for comment.

In addition to ruling against Trump's Kennedy Center renovation efforts, Cooper ruled that Trump's Department of Government Efficiency must turn over records requested in a Freedom of Information Act lawsuit.

Trump has called Cooper one of the "Radical Left Democrats."

Cooper is also married to Amy Jeffress, who was Biden’s personal lawyer in 2025 and previously did some work for the Jan. 6 committee.
