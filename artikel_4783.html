Quieting Food Noise in Children

Does your child seem to think about food all the time? You may find that they often ask when the next snack is, worry about whether they will get enough of a favorite food, sneak or hide food, or seem really focused on foods they often don’t get. Sometimes this is called food noise. Food noise is when a child finds themselves thinking about food a lot, even when they aren’t physically hungry. Many of us adults experience this as well, so we can relate to how our children feel and understand that it can be hard to reduce these thoughts.

As parents, our first reaction may be to set more limits. We may find ourselves hiding certain foods, locking cabinets, controlling portions, telling our child when they have had enough, or encouraging them to choose a “healthier” option. These responses come from a good place because we want our children to develop healthy eating habits in the long term. But sometimes, this control causes the food noise to get even louder.

A few strategies to help quiet this noise involve creating an environment around structure, trust, and food neutrality.

Kids do well when they know what to expect, and eating is no different. A regular meal and snack routine reassures children that another opportunity to eat is coming.

Babies are born knowing how much they need to eat. They stop when they are full by pushing the bottle away and have telltale signs when they are hungry. Somewhere along the way, many kids stop listening to those hunger and fullness signals and begin relying on their parents and caregivers to decide how much they need.

This might look like encouraging them to take two more bites or finish everything on their plate and restricting how much they eat of more desirable foods when we feel like they are eating too much. This double-sided pressure often leads to children losing some of their ability to self-regulate their eating, which in turn leads to undereating or overeating at times.

Kids who become out of touch with these cues often have more food noise. When food feels scarce, they want it more. We encourage parents to let their children decide whether and how much they want to eat from what is provided at meals and snacks. When it becomes age-appropriate, we encourage children to make their own plates at meals. Parents can still serve hot foods if needed and ask the child how much they would like on their plate. This is not saying that they can eat as much as they want, whenever they want it. Not at all! It is saying that at the scheduled eating times, we want parents to step back and let kids take the lead in deciding how much they need to eat because only they know how hungry they are when they come to an eating time. Just like you and just like me.

At first, when children have this control, you may find that they will overeat. This is totally normal. They are learning, and it can take time for them to begin regulating on their own again. The important thing is to trust the process and keep practicing.

We often put foods into categories: good or bad, healthy or unhealthy. Kids pick up on these labels. When certain foods are placed on pedestals and others are treated as foods to avoid, it can have the opposite effect we intend. Kids become less interested in the food we call “healthy” and more interested in the foods we call "bad."

The take-home here is to avoid labeling food for yourself and when talking to your child. Kids are always listening to how we talk about food. Modeling simple, neutral food language helps them learn to see food without judgment or guilt.

Quieting food noise isn’t about teaching children to think less about food. It’s about creating an environment where they don’t have to think about it so much.
