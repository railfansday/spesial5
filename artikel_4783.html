Measurability Bias, HR Tech, and Work: The Unholy Trinity

In its very design, industrial/organizational (I/O) psychology (a.k.a. "psychology of work") reveals something nuanced and supremely relevant about what drives behavior in organizations.

Industrial psychology treats the individual as the unit of measure, while organizational psychology measures the group. The former tends to be measured quantitatively, while the latter tends to be measured qualitatively.

So, what falls on the “I” side (selection, training, performance management, core human resources [HR]) is very different than what falls on the “O” side (leadership, motivation, organizational development, culture).

From a technological investment point of view, “I” has a lot going for it. For starters, only quantitative data flows cleanly into a system of record, so there’s a built-in motivation for organizational use. But that’s not even the half of it.

Measurability bias describes the tendency of people to prioritize easily quantifiable metrics over qualitative factors that are difficult, if not impossible, to measure.

It’s not hard to understand why. Easily quantifiable metrics require a lot less thinking and ostensibly provide a lot more certainty. (Of course, “ostensibly” is necessary because they’re so often applied incorrectly.)

Human beings hate uncertainty at a cellular level, and we often embrace whatever we can to reassure ourselves that we are making the right decision. This tendency is heightened in professional settings because the stakes are so high. We go out of our way to leverage quantitative data for guidance, cover, and reassurance.

That’s why “I” gets the prize, and it’s not hard to see why. According to analysis by 451 Research, less than 2 percent of sales of the $94 billion HR technology market goes to the qualitatively measured “O” constructs.

The same is true for investment. HRO Today tracked investment in 2025 and found that nearly all of the $3.75 billion went into the “I” side.

And where the technology and investment go, so goes the human attention.

As a result, constructs like leadership, motivation, and organizational culture receive comparably tiny resources and attention. Even something squarely on the “O” side like employee engagement, which had a brief run as the HR metric du jour, only did so because it offered a quantitative measure of a qualitative construct.

If the bias for easily quantifiable metrics creates a disincentive to pursue qualitative constructs through technology and investment, can we at least assume that the upside is that we’re making great decisions, grounded in data and analytics? Nope.

What does the peer-reviewed research say about the efficacy of HR technology and investment on the “I” side of I/O psychology? There’s not a lot of it. What we do know isn’t promising.

Hiring? Research from Bauer and SHRM (2010) indicates that half of senior hires fail within 18 months, and that 50 percent of hourly workers leave within the first 120 days.

Training? A study by Saks and Belcourt (2006) found that two out of three trainees were not using the training one year later.

One could argue that HR technology has adopted these same practices and merely reproduced them at greater scale, as Capelli did in his HBR article (2019).

Performance management? Scullen, Mount, and Goff (2000) found that between 53 and 62 percent of variance in feedback is reflective of the feedback provider, not the feedback recipient. In other words, the feedback tells us more about the person providing it than the person receiving it. Kluger and DeNisi (1996) found that feedback actually harms performance more than a third of the time.

That research may be dated, but it reveals something fundamental about human behavior that performance management still fails to take into account.

There’s an ironic twist, to boot. What gets in the way of the efficacy of the heavily weighted technological investment in industrial psychology driven by measurement bias? Measurement bias.

What gets measured in selection? Volume of applications and time to fill. What doesn’t get measured? Outcomes. For training, it’s completion and satisfaction. And most performance management efforts have little to do with performance.

We don’t measure the outcomes of hiring decisions because it’s too hard to do and takes way too long. The same is true for training outcomes. By the time we know, we think (incorrectly) it’s too late to do anything about it. And performance management? The logical overriding motivation for managers is not to hurt anyone’s motivation moving forward, and that's not what performance management is designed to do.

Concerns about the effect of artificial intelligence (AI) on people and work are as well-founded as they are widespread. But they also overlook how AI can help us to be more human.

The ability to analyze qualitative data sets at a breakneck speed means that practitioners finally have access to the types of evidence that provide demonstrable proof of impact and return on investment (ROI).

Proper use of AI can tell us more about leadership, motivation, and culture at an organization than we have ever known. And it can do so in ways that support great decision-making, both strategically and tactically.

Our company is designing HR tools that can provide the benefits of psychological safety (surfacing truth) where it does not exist. We achieve this by summarizing anonymous qualitative data in real time, carefully framing the summary to avoid finger-pointing and surfacing the most productive steps forward.

We accelerate team decision-making by summarizing everyone’s opinion before the conversation begins, using pre-surfaced conflicting points of view as the starting point for discussions. We use AI to nudge people into creating better and more aligned goals across the organization. And now we can surface the way work really gets done through a combination of AI-document reviews and AI-assisted human-led interviews.

These are but a few examples of how AI can revolutionize HR and HR tech: by creating better sources of qualitative data. It may sound abstract, but the positive effect on work will be profound.

Yes, the impact that AI can have on the future of work is very scary. But interestingly, it also just might make the future of work way more human.
