Hackers Leak Visa, Mastercard and JPMorgan Customer Data

Three of the biggest names in payments, Visa, Mastercard and JPMorgan Chase, disclosed that some of their customers’ data was exposed after hackers breached third-party vendors, the latest jolt in a week of tech failures that has left Americans uneasy about the systems underpinning daily life.

The data was leaked Thursday by a ransomware group, according to a report from The Wall Street Journal. Crucially, all three firms said their own networks were not breached and that no payment systems were compromised.

A Visa spokesperson said no payment data such as card numbers was affected and that the incident involved a third-party service provider in the Asia-Pacific region.

Mastercard said a “limited amount of information from a subset of cardholders enrolled in a specific benefit program” was exposed, with no impact on its payment network.

JPMorgan said attackers targeted outside service providers and likely accessed marketing data, potentially including customer names and email addresses, with no disruption to customer services.

A ransomware group calling itself n0n claimed responsibility, according to cybersecurity firm SOCRadar. None of the three companies named the breached vendor.

Earlier in the week, JPMorgan customers hit problems accessing accounts and sending payments, which the bank attributed to a software update, and the issue spread into broader complaints about other banks.

Paired with the FAA temporarily grounding flights and an earlier Microsoft outage that locked people out of email, the week stoked unusual public anxiety about the fragility of critical tech. JPMorgan was explicit that the data leak had “no connection” to the earlier service disruptions.

For crypto users, the breach lands squarely within a growing danger that customer data, not just funds, is the soft underbelly of finance.

AI has supercharged both the scale and sophistication of cyberattacks, and 2026 has brought a steady drumbeat of data exposures. The record $6.3 billion Binance hack, tied to North Korea, laid bare how aggressively state and criminal actors are targeting financial infrastructure, while a wave of breaches has hit crypto firms through third-party vendors, echoing the vendor weakness at the center of this latest incident.

The payments giants have all leaned deeper into crypto and stablecoins this year, from Visa and Mastercard’s settlement programs to JPMorgan’s growing digital-asset footprint, making their security posture increasingly relevant to the onchain economy they’re racing to join.

For now, the firms say the damage is contained, but the episode adds to a mounting sense that no institution, however large, is immune.
