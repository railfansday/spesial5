These Billionaire Investors Made Big Bets Against Tech Stocks. Should You Worry?

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Paul Singer's Elliott and Paul Tudor Jones' firm added Nasdaq put options last quarter. Their filings show those bets are less one-sided than they look.

By Jonathan Ponciano – Nov 24, 2026 at 7:48PM EST

10 stocks we like better than Invesco QQQ Trust ›

Tech stocks slid again on Tuesday, and some of the market's most famous investors have been positioning for that kind of drop. Quarterly filings show that two billionaire-run firms added big bets against the Nasdaq-100 during the third quarter.

The bets use put options, which give the holder the right to sell at a set price and generally rise in value when the underlying investment falls. The filings, called 13Fs, were made with the SEC on Nov. 16 and show holdings as of Sept. 30.

Image source: OpenAI (AI-generated illustration).

Paul Singer's Elliott Investment Management held puts on 5.46 million shares of the Invesco QQQ Trust (QQQ -1.99%), which tracks the Nasdaq-100, according to its latest 13F. The filing values them at $4.04 billion, with only its Phillips 66 and Triple Flag Precious Metals stakes representing a bigger value.

Importantly, that figure is the value of the shares the puts cover, rather than what Elliott paid for them or what it could make. The filing doesn't show the strike prices or expiration dates, so the size of the actual bet is unknown.

Three months earlier, Elliott held puts on 3.48 million QQQ shares, valued at $2.56 billion, according to its prior filing. It also raised its puts on the VanEck Semiconductor ETF (SMH -7.26%) to 800,000 shares from 250,000, covering $487 million of shares.

But Elliott isn't only betting against tech. It also reported puts covering $1.27 billion of an industrials fund, $903 million of a consumer staples fund, and $865 million of a gold miners fund. It cut its puts on a technology-software fund nearly in half, to $873 million. As a whole, the spread looks more like protection across the whole market than a single bet against tech.

Paul Tudor Jones' Tudor Investment held QQQ puts covering 8.55 million shares valued at $6.32 billion, according to its 13F. That's up from 4.74 million shares, or $3.49 billion, in its prior filing.

However, Tudor also raised its QQQ calls, which gain when the fund rises, to 7.75 million shares, or $5.74 billion. So its puts outweigh its calls, but not by much. Its biggest bearish line wasn't tech at all: puts on the iShares Russell 2000 ETF, which tracks smaller companies, covering $8.75 billion of shares.

Separately, Stanley Druckenmiller isn't betting against tech, but he isn't betting on it either. He told the TomorrowX conference in Austin this month that he's "taking a break" from stocks and is close to neutral on equities while he waits for a clearer outlook.

Not much. These filings have real limits. Besides omitting strike prices and costs, a 13F doesn't show short sales or positions in securities outside U.S.-listed securities. A put can be an outright bet on a decline, or it can be insurance for a firm that owns a lot of stocks. Both Elliott and Tudor hold billions in other positions that those puts may be protecting.

The data is also somewhat stale. The filings show positions as of Sept. 30, and these firms trade often. Any of them could have changed by now.

What the filings do show is that experienced investors are paying for protection, especially against tech stocks and the broader Nasdaq. For long-term investors, the takeaway isn't to sell. Instead, check how much of the portfolio rides on a handful of names (particularly AI names), and make sure it's an amount they can live with if selling continues.
