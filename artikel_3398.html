Forget Photorealism: How ‘Wildwood’ Made Its Handmade Digital Artistry Visible

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

WETA FX has been called upon to do visual effects for everything, from orc armies to superheroes, alien planetscapes to the innards of a whale. On each project, they have to match the movie’s consistency. The goal is to make sure that the VFX blends perfectly into the overall visual style, not so unlike a certain Springfield dad creeping into a hedge.

Meeting that challenge often means the teams at WETA match and then build on live-action footage to get as close to a photorealistic look as possible. But adding digital effects to the wonderful world of stop-motion animation, as with “Wildwood,” WETA FX got to have more fun with creating a world that looks as textured and handmade as the puppets designed and animated by Laika Studios. “Everybody was super stoked about this film because it’s so different from what we usually do. People got to be artists,” VFX supervisor Sabine Laimer told IndieWire. “We’re really good at making effects invisible, but here we could actually showcase the artistry. All of our people here loved it.”

This applies to the way that WETA helped intensify battle sequences, the way they expanded sets so that they feel as fast as the impassible woods where most of “Wildwood” takes place, and the way they were able to match cinematographer Caleb Deschanel’s lighting to bring a unity to the digital and puppetry work within individual scenes. When designing the ivy that Alexandra (Carey Mulligan) seeks to control and that Prue (Peyton Elizabeth Lee) seeks to pacify, WETA’s digital leaves weren’t meant to mimic real leaves in nature. They were built to look like the paper leaves a stop motion animator would use.

“[The leaves in ‘Wildwood’] have a different light transmission. They have a different level of detail, depending on how close the camera is to them,” Laimer said. “[We developed] this sort of stage realism, if you want to call it that, rather than realism, and that makes a huge difference.”

One element where WETA needed to heighten the realism most was the ivy, which is eventually summoned into a sentient spire that threatens to overwhelm the entire world. “It’s alive, but it’s not a person. It has character, but it can’t feel like it’s a boss. It has to have intent, but it can’t be so direct that it feels like there’s a brain that’s going after the characters,” VFX supervisor Dan Macarin laid out the challenge of Laika’s brief to IndieWire.

“It had to creep, it had to attack, it had to consume, it had to overgrow,” Laimer added. “There was a lot of material there for us to work with, which was amazing, but we had to translate all of this intricate detail that they’ve built on their practical sets into a digital plinth environment and build it in a way that was malleable for us, and we had different stages of everything.”

Laimer and her team tested ways to mimic the Laika animators’ practical work and drew inspiration from the natural world, like time-lapses of pea shoots with an uncanny, jerky motion as they grow. They built different vignettes that could be placed inside specific shots so the ivy would read as dangerous, but not distract from the drama between the characters. For the moments where the ivy threatens to overwhelm the entire plinth, the WETA team actually used fluid simulation to get the feel of the ivy being like a tsunami, this swelling of constant, growing motion that is headed in a definite direction, not just moving at random.

“We looked at practical references like schools of fish, where they move in unison and create shapes and change directions on purpose. It was a similar thing, or like flocks of starlings in the air. You have to sculpt the [ivy] cyclone per shot so that it’s not distracting in the background, it’s not too busy, but at the same time still feels like it’s alive,” Laimer said.

Hitting that visual effects sweet spot was important in all the visual effects collaborations between WETA and Laika. The path to do that always ran through story, and what visuals would make the storytelling the most immediately legible to the audience. “WETA is really, really fantastic at doing third act battles,” Macarin said. “We’ve done a tremendous number of them over the years and decades, and so it’s not the scope that we have to worry about. We can handle all of that. It’s always, ‘How do we tell this story?’”

In the case of “Wildwood,” telling the story with visual effects meant simplifying a lot, so that even when we placed it behind the explosion of cannon fire (where it would never be safe for a practical camera to go), we still cared about the characters. “We were able to create aesthetic choices that still allow the audience to engage in the battle, but not necessarily put you suddenly into this photorealistic explosion or feel. This is a very colorful, emotionally driven sequence that just happens to have a battle around it,” Macarin said.

The most fantastical-seeming choices in “Wildwood” are all about character. “They shot everything with two suns,” Laimer said. “They had a smaller, sharper key light and then a softer, wider one, which created this really beautiful falloff on the faces that we had to create digitally. That was really cool.”

Getting to build expressive, beautiful worlds in animation — whether digital, stop-motion, or a combination of the two — usually is pretty cool, to be fair. And clearly, in the case of “Wildwood,” it brings out some of WETA’s best work.
