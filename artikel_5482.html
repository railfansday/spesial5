S&P 500 has risen in the 12 months after every midterm since 1950, LPL says

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Free daily briefing on global business news.

How the stock market after midterm elections behaved across nine S&P 500 midterm years from 1974 to 2022, and what stocks did in the 12 months that followed

Midterm election years have a reputation on Wall Street as the softest stretch of the four-year presidential cycle. The year after the vote has a different record. The S&P 500 has been higher 12 months after every midterm election since 1950, according to LPL Research.

That contrast between a shaky run-up and a firmer aftermath is the pattern this slideshow examines. Tracking the stock market after midterm elections means looking at more than politics, though. The vote rarely arrives alone. It lands alongside interest rate decisions, recessions, wars, oil shocks and financial crises.

This slideshow walks through nine midterm years, from 1974 to 2022. It also includes one slide on the broader presidential cycle pattern. The nine years are a selection, not a complete record. The 2006 and 2014 midterms are not covered, and the S&P 500 finished higher in both of those years.

Each year follows the same thread. It starts with the date of the election and the backdrop for markets that year. It then looks at whether the year's market low came before or after the vote. The last part covers what stocks did in the year that followed.

The answers vary. In 1974, the index fell about 30% during Watergate and a deep bear market. In 1982, a new bull market began on Aug. 12, months before the Nov. 2 vote. In 1998, the index rose about 27% despite a sharp late-summer selloff. In four of these years, the low came in October, weeks before voters went to the polls. In 2018, the low arrived on Christmas Eve, well after the vote.

All figures here are S&P 500 price returns. They exclude dividends. Total returns for investors who reinvested those payments would have been higher. Percentages and index levels are rounded.

The record offers context for anyone asking whether stocks rise after midterms. It is still a record of the past, built on a small number of elections. It is not a forecast, and it cannot say how markets will respond to the next midterm.

The midterm year has historically been the weakest of the four-year presidential cycle for U.S. stocks. The 12 months after the vote have a steadier record. LPL Research has found that the S&P 500 has been higher 12 months after every midterm election since 1950.

Yale Hirsch, who founded the Stock Trader's Almanac, is usually credited with popularizing the presidential cycle theory. It splits each four-year term into the post-election year, the midterm year, the pre-election year and the election year. Market historians track how stocks have tended to behave in each phase. In that framework, the midterm year is the soft spot. The pre-election year that follows has traditionally been the strongest.

The nine midterm years in this slideshow show how uneven that soft spot can be. Six of them ended with the S&P 500 below where it started. The losses ranged from about 1.5% in 1994 to about 30% in 1974. The three gains came in 1982, 1998 and 2010. The 1998 advance came despite a Russian debt default and the collapse of the hedge fund Long-Term Capital Management.

Timing is the other recurring thread. In 1974, 1990, 2002 and 2022, the S&P 500 hit its low for the year in October, before voters went to the polls. In 1982, a new bull market began on Aug. 12, less than three months ahead of the Nov. 2 vote. The 2018 midterm breaks that pattern. Its closing low came on Dec. 24, weeks after the election.

The forces behind each year differed. Federal Reserve rate hikes weighed on stocks in 1994, 2018 and 2022. Other years turned on Iraq's invasion of Kuwait, the Watergate scandal, euro-zone debt fears or the end of the dot-com boom. The vote was one event among many in each of them.

The LPL Research finding is a record of the past, not a forecast. Midterms come only once every four years. That means the record since 1950 rests on 19 elections. A pattern drawn from so few cases can fail the next time. Anyone asking whether stocks rise after midterms is looking at history, not a guarantee.

The S&P 500 fell about 30% in 1974, a midterm year dominated by the Watergate scandal and the 1973–74 bear market. The index reached its low on Oct. 3, 1974, weeks before voters went to the polls on Nov. 5.

On a price basis, the index began the year at about 97.55 and ended it at about 68.56. That decline came on top of losses in 1973, when the bear market first took hold.

The political backdrop set 1974 apart. Richard Nixon resigned the presidency in August 1974 as the Watergate investigation closed in. He remains the only U.S. president to have resigned. Vice President Gerald Ford $F took office. In September, Ford granted Nixon a pardon for any federal crimes he may have committed while in office.

The economy was under strain at the same time. The Arab oil embargo, which began in October 1973 and lasted into March 1974, had pushed up energy prices. Inflation reached double digits during the year. The U.S. economy was also in a recession that had begun in late 1973.

Political upheaval, rising prices and a shrinking economy all weighed on stocks at once. The market slid through the summer and into the autumn. The index rebounded from its October low before Election Day.

The vote delivered a clear shift in Washington. Democrats expanded their majorities in both the House and the Senate. The large class of new Democratic House members elected that year became known as the "Watergate babies."

The following year brought a different picture for investors. Stocks rose strongly in 1975. The recession ended in March of that year. The Federal Reserve had lowered interest rates as the downturn deepened.

The 1974 midterm year is an example of a market low that came before the vote rather than after it. It also shows how deep a midterm-year decline could run when a political crisis and an economic one arrived together.

The S&P 500 rose about 15% in 1982, and its turning point came well before the Nov. 2 midterm vote. A major bull market began on Aug. 12, 1982, less than three months before Americans went to the polls.

The index started the year near 123 and finished near 141. That works out to a gain of roughly 18 points. The figure is a price change only and leaves out dividends.

The year's low came in the summer, not in the fall. By Election Day, the rally was already more than two months old.

The backdrop was a deep recession. The Federal Reserve, led by Paul Volcker since 1979, had held interest rates very high to break the inflation of the late 1970s and early 1980s. Tight credit slowed borrowing and spending across the economy.

The National Bureau of Economic Research dates that recession from July 1981 to November 1982. Its official end fell in the same month as the midterm vote. It came soon after a shorter recession in the first half of 1980.

By the second half of 1982, the pressure was lifting. The Fed cut rates, and the recession was easing. Stocks began to climb as the outlook for borrowing costs improved.

The vote was the first midterm of Ronald Reagan's presidency. Reagan had taken office in January 1981, about six months before the recession began. Democrats gained seats in the House, while Republicans kept control of the Senate.

The order of events defines 1982. The market did not wait for the election, or for its results, before turning higher. It moved first, on shifts in monetary policy and the economy that were already underway in August.

The rally that began that summer marked the start of a bull market that ran through much of the 1980s. The index stood higher a year after the 1982 election. Gains in 1983 extended the climb that started in August 1982.

In 1982, the recovery was underway before any ballots were cast. The election arrived in the middle of a rally, not at its start.

The S&P 500 fell about 7% in 1990 as Iraq's invasion of Kuwait drove up oil prices and the U.S. economy slid into recession. Stocks hit their low for the year in October, before voters went to the polls on Nov. 6, 1990.

The index began 1990 at about 353 and ended the year near 330. Stocks climbed into the summer and reached a high in mid-July. The turn came on Aug. 2, 1990, when Iraqi forces under Saddam Hussein invaded neighboring Kuwait.

The invasion took Kuwait's oil off world markets. It also put Iraq's army on the border of Saudi Arabia, near the kingdom's oil fields. The United Nations imposed a trade embargo on Iraq within days. That embargo cut off Iraqi oil exports as well. Crude prices rose sharply through the late summer and into the autumn.

Higher fuel costs hit an economy that was already weakening. The National Bureau of Economic Research later dated the recession from July 1990 to March 1991. The savings and loan crisis was also still weighing on the U.S. financial system.

Washington responded to the invasion with Operation Desert Shield. U.S. troops began deploying to Saudi Arabia in August. A coalition of countries built up forces in the Gulf over the following months. With the outcome of the standoff uncertain, stocks slid into October and bottomed that month.

The 1990 vote was the first midterm for President George H.W. Bush. Democrats kept control of both the House and the Senate. By the time ballots were cast, the year's market low was already in place.

The larger gains arrived in early 1991. The coalition began the air campaign of Operation Desert Storm in mid-January. Oil prices dropped as the fighting started, and stocks rallied on the news. The ground war ended in late February 1991.

A year after the 1990 vote, the S&P 500 stood higher than it had on Election Day. The year followed a sequence seen in several other midterm years. Stocks fell during the year, set their low before the election and then recovered in the months that followed.

The S&P 500 ended 1994 about 1.5% lower in price terms, slipping from about 466 to about 459. Federal Reserve tightening dominated the year. Voters went to the polls on Nov. 8, 1994, and gave Republicans control of both chambers of Congress.

Interest rates set the tone. The Fed, led by Alan Greenspan, began raising its federal funds target in February 1994, starting from 3%. It raised the target six times over the course of the year, reaching 5.5% by December. That added up to 2.5 percentage points of tightening within a single year.

The February move also changed how the Fed communicated. For the first time, it announced a policy decision with a public statement after the meeting. Before that, traders had to infer changes from the Fed's activity in the money markets.

Bonds took the heaviest losses. Prices fell sharply as yields climbed. The selloff hurt investors who had borrowed to bet that rates would stay low. Orange County, California, was among the hardest hit. Its treasurer, Robert Citron, had used borrowed money to magnify the county investment pool's bets on low rates. The county filed for bankruptcy in December 1994 after the pool lost heavily.

The election landed in the middle of this tightening. Republicans won a House majority for the first time in 40 years. Newt Gingrich became speaker in January 1995. President Bill Clinton, a Democrat, faced a Republican Congress for the rest of his first term.

The Fed did not pause for the vote. On Nov. 15, one week after Election Day, it raised the target by three-quarters of a percentage point to 5.5%. That was the largest single increase of the cycle. The Fed made one more increase in February 1995, lifting the target to 6%, and then cut rates that July.

The index then rose well in 1995 as the economy slowed without tipping into recession.

The S&P 500 gained about 27% in 1998, a midterm year interrupted by a steep selloff in late summer. Stocks bottomed in early October, about a month before the Nov. 3 election, after the Federal Reserve began cutting interest rates.

The year started calmly. The index opened 1998 near 970 and pushed higher through the spring and early summer. Investors were still absorbing the Asian financial crisis of 1997. That crisis had hit currencies and stock markets in Thailand, South Korea and Indonesia.

The trouble arrived in August. On Aug. 17, 1998, Russia devalued the ruble and defaulted on its domestic government debt. The move rattled bond markets worldwide. Investors fled riskier assets and crowded into U.S. Treasurys.

That flight to safety upended Long-Term Capital Management. The Greenwich, Connecticut, hedge fund was run by John Meriwether, a former Salomon Brothers executive. Its partners included Nobel-winning economists Myron Scholes and Robert Merton. LTCM had made heavily leveraged bets that price gaps between related bonds would narrow. After the Russian default, those gaps widened instead. The fund's capital collapsed within weeks.

In September 1998, the Federal Reserve Bank of New York brought together a group of Wall Street firms to recapitalize the fund. The rescue used private money rather than public funds. Its aim was to prevent a disorderly unwinding of LTCM's positions across global markets.

The Fed then moved on rates. It cut its federal funds target three times that autumn, on Sept. 29, Oct. 15 and Nov. 17. Each cut was a quarter of a percentage point. The October cut was an unscheduled move. It came between the central bank's regular policy meetings.

The index climbed from its early October low through the weeks before and after the midterm vote. The gains held through political turmoil at the end of the year. The House impeached President Bill Clinton on Dec. 19, 1998. The S&P 500 still finished the year at about 1,229.

The recovery carried into the next year. Stocks rose again in 1999, with technology shares leading the market toward the dot-com peak in early 2000.

The S&P 500 fell about 23% in 2002, its third straight annual decline after the dot-com peak. The bear market's closing low of 776.76 came on Oct. 9, 2002. That was less than a month before the Nov. 5 midterm vote.

The index began the year near 1,148 and finished near 880. That price-return loss followed losing years in 2000 and 2001. Investors entered the midterm year still absorbing the collapse in technology and internet stock valuations that had peaked in early 2000.

Corporate scandals added to the pressure. Enron filed for bankruptcy in December 2001. Its accounting fraud put reported earnings across corporate America under scrutiny. WorldCom filed for bankruptcy in July 2002. It had disclosed billions of dollars in improperly booked expenses weeks earlier.

Lawmakers acted during the selloff. President George W. Bush signed the Sarbanes-Oxley Act on July 30, 2002. The law tightened rules on corporate financial reporting and auditor independence. It also made senior executives personally responsible for certifying their companies' accounts.

At the October low, the index stood about 32% below where it had started the year. Stocks then recovered part of that ground before the year ended. The year-end close sat about 13% above the Oct. 9 level, though still well below the January starting point.

Republicans won control of the Senate in the Nov. 5 vote. The market low had already passed by then. The election arrived during an early rebound rather than at the bottom of the decline.

The recovery was uneven. Stocks slid again into March 2003, in the weeks before the U.S.-led invasion of Iraq began. That retest held above the October 2002 closing low. The earlier mark stood as the bottom of the bear market.

A year after the 2002 vote, the S&P 500 was trading higher than it had been on Election Day. The timing of the 2002 bottom matched 1974 and 1990. In both of those midterm years, the market low also came before voters went to the polls.

The S&P 500 gained about 13% in 2010. Its losses that year came in the spring and summer on euro-zone debt fears. Stocks had recovered much of that ground before the Nov. 2 midterm vote.

The index started the year near 1,115 and finished it near 1,258. That price gain came in the second year of the recovery from the 2008 financial crisis. Unemployment was still high, and the economic rebound was uneven.

The main threat came from Europe. Greece's debt problems raised doubts in the spring about other heavily indebted euro-zone governments. In May 2010, Greece received a rescue package from euro-zone countries and the International Monetary Fund. Investors questioned whether that aid would contain the problem. U.S. stocks slid through the late spring and into the summer.

Trading itself added to the strain. On May 6, 2010, the episode later called the flash crash sent major U.S. stock indexes plunging and then rebounding within minutes. The event prompted regulators to review how electronic trading behaved under stress.

The turn came from the Federal Reserve. In late August, Fed chair Ben Bernanke used the central bank's annual Jackson Hole conference to signal that the Fed was ready to do more to support the economy. Stocks climbed through September and October as investors priced in more bond buying. The year's low had come in the summer, months before voters went to the polls.

The election itself shifted power in Washington. Republicans won control of the House of Representatives, while Democrats kept their Senate majority.

On Nov. 3, the day after the vote, the Fed announced its second round of bond purchases, known as QE2. The plan called for buying $600 billion of longer-term Treasury securities by the end of the second quarter of 2011. With a major policy decision arriving a day after the political result, the market's post-election moves cannot be credited to the vote alone.

The following year was choppier. The S&P 500 ended 2011 almost exactly where it began. A renewed euro-zone crisis and Standard & Poor's downgrade of the U.S. credit rating in August 2011 drove a sharp summer selloff. Stocks then recovered part of the loss.

The S&P 500 fell about 6% in the 2018 midterm year, and its lowest close came after the vote. Americans went to the polls on Nov. 6, 2018. The index did not bottom until Dec. 24, when it closed at 2,351.10.

The election split control of Congress. Democrats won a majority in the House of Representatives, and Republicans kept the Senate. That left a divided Congress for the second half of President Donald Trump's term.

Two forces shaped the year for investors. The first was monetary policy. The Federal Reserve raised interest rates four times in 2018 under Jerome Powell, who became Fed chair in February of that year. The hikes lifted the federal funds target range from 1.25%–1.50% to 2.25%–2.50%. Each increase raised borrowing costs for companies and households. The final hike came on Dec. 19, in the middle of the late-year slide.

The second force was trade. The U.S. and China imposed several rounds of tariffs on each other's goods during 2018. The dispute between the world's two largest economies weighed on sentiment. Companies with supply chains or customers in China drew particular scrutiny. Investors had to judge how far the conflict might spread and how long it might last.

The index began the year at about 2,674 and finished at about 2,507. That full-year figure hides how uneven the path was. The heaviest losses came in the final quarter, in October and again in December. A partial federal government shutdown that began on Dec. 22 added to the pressure heading into the holiday.

The rebound started almost as soon as the Christmas Eve close was in. Stocks rallied in the final days of December and ended the year above that low. The S&P 500 then gained about 29% in 2019.

Monetary policy changed course during that recovery. The Fed stopped raising rates in early 2019 and began cutting them in July. In 2018, the vote itself did not mark the turning point. The bottom came about seven weeks after Election Day, and the gains followed in the next calendar year.

The S&P 500 fell about 19% in 2022 as the Federal Reserve raised interest rates seven times. The index hit its closing low for the year on Oct. 12. That was nearly four weeks before the Nov. 8 midterm election.

The index began the year at about 4,766, close to its record high, and ended it near 3,840. Inflation was the central problem. Price increases that took hold during the pandemic recovery kept building into 2022. Russia's invasion of Ukraine on Feb. 24 pushed energy and food prices higher still.

The Fed responded with its most aggressive tightening in decades. Its first hike came in March and lifted the fed funds target off near zero. By December, after seven increases, the target range stood at 4.25%–4.50%. Four of those moves were 75 basis points each. The Fed had not raised rates by that much at a single meeting since 1994.

Higher rates hit stocks and bonds at the same time. Bond prices fall when yields rise. That left a mixed portfolio of stocks and bonds with little of its usual cushion. Technology and other growth shares were among the hardest hit. Much of their value rests on profits expected far in the future. Higher rates shrink what those future profits are worth today.

The S&P 500 closed in bear market territory in June, more than 20% below its peak. It rallied in the summer, then slid again into the early autumn. The Oct. 12 close of 3,577.03 left the index about 25% below where it started the year.

By Election Day, the index had already climbed off that low. The Fed's fourth straight 75-basis-point hike landed on Nov. 2, six days before the election. Republicans won control of the House, and Democrats kept the Senate.

Stocks ended the year about 7% above the October low, though still well below their starting level for 2022. The Fed raised rates once more in December, by half a percentage point. The S&P 500 was higher a year after the election.
