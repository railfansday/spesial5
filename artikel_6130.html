Privatize Air Traffic Control

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

A plane takes off from Hollywood Burbank Airport in Burbank, Calif., October 7, 2025.

Thank goodness we’re still two weeks away from Thanksgiving. Otherwise, Steve Martin and John Candy’s long trek home would have become a near-universal experience.

Over the weekend, more than 18,000 flights were canceled when the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA), the agency that oversees America’s air traffic control system, ordered a nationwide ground stop. No plane was allowed to take off from a U.S. airport for over four hours. The stoppage was necessary when the FAA’s telecommunications network suddenly failed, apparently caused by a faulty update to its decades-old tangle of copper wiring.

This was the second nationwide ground stop since 9/11. The previous one happened less than four years ago, in January 2023, and was also caused by technical difficulties. Republican lawmakers blamed the Biden administration last time; now, Democrats are faulting the Trump administration. But the FAA’s antiquated infrastructure isn’t a partisan issue. It’s the institutional consequence of having an unaccountable bureaucracy manage the skies.

Unlike almost every other major country, the U.S. federal government funds and operates air traffic control directly. Congress must appropriate its budget each year, and controllers must work without pay when the government shuts down. Infrastructure investments are subject to political timetables. Meanwhile, the FAA is expected to regulate the very system that it runs.

Usually, it wastes no time in dodging responsibility. Immediately after the grounding, Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy — who oversees the FAA as part of his department — placed the blame on Congress and past administrations for not adequately funding upgrades to the agency’s aging infrastructure. Yet the FAA received $12.5 billion from last year’s reconciliation law for that purpose. Earlier this month, it requested $30 billion more. Now, Duffy claims that he really needs $50 billion to get the job done. And we thought airlines’ add-on fees were bad.

Improvements don’t have to be so costly. The FAA has spent a quarter century trying to switch its network from copper wires to fiber-optic cables. Its counterpart up north, Nav Canada, completed most of the same transition in just 18 months.

The fundamental difference? Nav Canada was spun off from the national government in 1996 as a nonprofit corporation. The organization is funded entirely by user fees from the airlines and private aircraft that rely on its systems, not general taxes, and it’s structured to be accountable to both private stakeholders and the government. When it needs to finance capital projects, Nav Canada doesn’t have to wait for approval. It can simply issue bonds and recoup the cost over the long run.

The results are impressive. Research finds that privatization lowered costs and boosted productivity for the aviation sector, especially compared with the FAA. Safety has greatly improved, and Nav Canada leads in technological advancement. Dozens of countries have since followed Canada’s lead toward an independent, commercial operator.

Passengers wait at a terminal after the FAA issued a national ground stop following the agency’s earlier report of serious air traffic control communications issues, at Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International Airport in Atlanta, Ga., November 13, 2026.

America almost joined that list in 2017. That year, legislation gained traction in Congress that would have transferred air traffic control to an independent entity while retaining the FAA’s oversight role. Regrettably, the plan was shot down by the labor union representing controllers, which feared losing the federal government’s generous protections and benefits. Private aircraft owners also opposed it to avoid paying user fees they are currently exempt from, keeping the revenue burden on commercial flyers.

Aside from placating these entrenched interests, there is no good reason to keep air traffic control in government hands. The public utility model has proven safe and effective elsewhere, and the FAA has shown once again that it can’t measure up. Free the aviation system from political gamesmanship, and watch dependability soar.
