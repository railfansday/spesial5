Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is highly heritable, and mutations in hundreds of genes have been implicated as individually rare causes of ASD1,2,3. Understanding how disruptions to these functionally diverse genes lead to the core features of ASD remains a major challenge4. Moreover, ASD is three- to four-fold more common in male than in female individuals5, and female autistic people tend to carry more autosomal risk alleles for ASD compared with their male counterparts6,7; however, the biological basis of this ‘female protective effect’ (FPE) has remained unknown8,9. Here we show that individual perturbations of 18 ASD genes in human neural progenitor cells converge on shared effects on gene expression, including widespread downregulation of other ASD genes. De novo reconstruction of a gene regulatory network enabled identification of central transcriptional regulators that drive this transcriptomic convergence, including prominent ASD gene CHD8 and candidates including REST. Furthermore, X-linked transcription factor ZFX, which is expressed from both the active and the inactive X chromosomes in female individuals10, emerged as a key activator of many ASD genes: we propose that the higher ZFX expression level observed in the female brain can buffer damaging mutations in diverse ASD genes, contributing to the female protective effect. Together, these results show how key gene regulatory networks can become broadly and similarly dysregulated upon disruption of individual ASD genes and provide molecular insight into the female protective effect in ASD.

Large-scale genetic studies have implicated hundreds of genes in autism spectrum disorder (ASD), with many identified through rare mutations that result in loss of function of one allele1,2,3. This suggests that gene dosage plays an important part in ASD11 and makes it critical to determine how appropriate expression levels of ASD genes are maintained. Characterization of gene regulatory networks (GRNs) could provide important insights into mechanisms mediating the proper transcriptional dosage of these ASD genes. In addition, ASD is significantly male-biased in prevalence, and although some ASD genes have been identified on the X chromosome12, X-linked mutations alone cannot fully explain this sex bias8,13. Moreover, it has been consistently demonstrated that female individuals require greater autosomal genetic risk to manifest ASD compared with male individuals6,7, but the genetic mechanisms of this female protective effect (FPE) are unknown8,9.

Here we use GRN reconstruction to identify key drivers of expression of ASD genes, including CHD8 and REST. These analyses also identify female-biased transcription factor ZFX, which is expressed from both the active and inactive X chromosome in female individuals10, as a key regulator of ASD genes, suggesting a direct transcriptional mechanism that could contribute to the FPE. This study provides a crucial framework for determining how variants in diverse genes can cause convergent molecular effects that ultimately result in ASD.

Functional interrogation of ASD genes revealed strongly overlapping effects on downstream targets in human neural progenitor cells (NPCs). Knockdown (KD) studies were performed for 20 ASD genes, including 18 genes from the Simons Foundation Autism Research Initiative (SFARI) gene database14 and two long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) previously implicated in ASD (NR2F1-AS1 (ref. 15) and SOX2-OT16). RNA-degrading CRISPR enzyme CasRx17 and an array of three guide RNAs (gRNAs) per target (Supplementary Table 1) were used to induce individual KD of each target gene relative to non-targeting controls (NTCs) in NPC cultures from a control human male (XY) induced pluripotent stem (iPS) cell line (Fig. 1a,b and Extended Data Fig. 1). RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis after 24 h of KD demonstrated that nearly all perturbations led to substantial numbers of significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs; adjusted P (Padj) < 0.05), with a median of 920 DEGs per perturbation (25th percentile: 644, 75th percentile: 1,485) (Extended Data Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 2). To ensure sufficient statistical power for downstream analyses of these DEGs, we focused on the 18 perturbations (18 of 20, 90%) that resulted in more than 20 DEGs, excluding the ARHGAP5 and ARID2 KD samples from further analysis. The remaining perturbations each led to a minimum of 287 DEGs, which enabled robust characterization of these samples.

Transcriptome-wide analyses showed that ASD gene KDs caused remarkably widespread disruption of other ASD genes. Most sets of DEGs exhibited significant enrichment for known ASD genes in the SFARI Gene database (15 of 18, 83.3%) (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Table 3). By contrast, there was no significant enrichment for genes implicated in intellectual disability but not ASD (intellectual-disability-only genes) (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Table 3) (Methods), despite their comparable expression in NPCs (intellectual-disability-only genes: 1,062 of 1,242, 85.5% expressed; SFARI genes: 797 of 1230, 64.8% expressed). This suggests that perturbation of ASD genes particularly affects other ASD genes, as opposed to neurodevelopmental genes more broadly.

We observed a striking pattern of transcriptomic convergence across perturbations, which we defined as statistically significant overlap of the same DEGs (Extended Data Fig. 3). This was particularly evident for high-confidence SFARI (HC-SFARI) genes, the SFARI genes in the highest evidentiary tier for association with ASD (Fig. 1d). Moreover, HC-SFARI genes tended to shift in a concordant direction as a result of different perturbations even when they were not significant DEGs within a particular KD sample (Extended Data Fig. 3c). Such genes included prominent ASD risk gene CHD8 (ref. 18), which was significantly downregulated by most (11 of 18, 61.1%) of the KDs. Notably, 27 HC-SFARI genes were DEGs in at least 10 of the KDs (27 of 183, 14.8%, P = 4.1 × 10−9), and all were consistently downregulated (Fig. 1e), indicating convergent dysregulation of many of the strongest risk genes for ASD.

High levels of CasRx have been reported to cause non-specific RNA degradation of a magnitude that is correlated with the expression level of the KD target, whereas use of lower CasRx levels avoids this problem19. In our NPC transcriptomic studies, CasRx was expressed from only a single safe-harbour locus (AAVS1), which limited its expression. We also performed several further experiments to confirm that non-specific RNA degradation did not affect our results. First, we found no relationship between expression levels of KD targets and numbers of resulting DEGs when considering either the whole transcriptome (P = 0.990, R2 = 8 × 10−6, linear regression) or SFARI genes specifically (P = 0.784, R2 = 0.0043, linear regression) (Extended Data Fig. 4a). We next generated NPCs expressing fluorophores mKO2, mScarlet and iRFP670 and targeted each individually with CasRx (Supplementary Table 4); flow cytometry showed no collateral effects on the fluorescence intensity of non-targeted fluorophores (Extended Data Fig. 4b and Supplementary Table 5). Finally, we confirmed strong transcriptomic convergence on shared downregulated DEGs by performing further KD studies of a subset of our target genes using short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) in NPCs (Extended Data Figs. 4c,d and 5 and Supplementary Table 6). Re-analysis of data from a study that used Cas9 to knock out ASD genes in iPS cells and iPS cell-derived excitatory neurons (ExNs)20 similarly demonstrated strong transcriptomic convergence on shared downregulated DEGs both within each cell type and between cell types (Extended Data Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table 6). Recent studies published since our work was submitted have also noted convergence across diverse ASD models21,22. Together, these data confirm that disruption of ASD genes results in transcriptomic convergence across various cell types and experimental contexts.

The marked transcriptomic convergence we observed in our KD studies suggested that candidate ASD risk genes could be nominated by the following features: (1) consistent downregulation following ASD gene KDs; and (2) dosage sensitivity, as indicated by high genic constraint scores (Supplementary Table 7 and Methods). This approach yielded 397 genes (Supplementary Table 8), including 113 SFARI genes (odds ratio (OR) = 5.87, Padj = 1.85 × 10−39) and 49 HC-SFARI genes (OR = 11.17, Padj = 1.11 × 10−29), demonstrating that these criteria effectively prioritize known ASD genes. Notably, this set of genes showed no enrichment for intellectual-disability-only genes (OR = 1.01, Padj = 0.509). All of the remaining 284 non-SFARI genes exhibited significant co-expression with SFARI genes (all Padj < 0.05), which has previously been shown to help prioritize novel ASD risk genes4, further supporting their relevance to ASD. Moreover, at least 40 of these genes showed some association with ASD in at least 1 major sequencing study (Supplementary Table 9). For example, mutations in AKT3, although classically associated with brain overgrowth disorders such as megalencephaly, have recently been associated with ASD; other genes including KIF11, KIF2A and SOS1 have also been associated with ASD in multiple studies (see references in Supplementary Table 9). Therefore, the remaining genes merit consideration as candidate ASD risk genes.

Many ASD gene perturbations also caused similar phenotypic effects in NPCs. Proliferation analysis using heritable dye (CytoTrack) showed that 9 of 18 (50%) of the KDs led to a statistically significant decrease in proliferation (Fig. 1f, Extended Data Fig. 6a, Supplementary Table 10 and Supplementary Fig. 1), including several genes that have been implicated in microcephaly (SETD1B23, SETD2 (ref. 24), SETD5 (ref. 25), KMT2E26 and WAC27). By contrast, only 1 of 18 (5.6%) of the KDs led to a significant increase in NPC proliferation rate. This suggests that reduced NPC proliferation is a common phenotype that results from disruption of several different ASD genes, consistent with previous findings28. Deciphering the specific relationships between these molecular phenotypes and abnormal head size in ASD is complex, as reports implicate increased rates of both microcephaly and macrocephaly across individuals with autism29,30. Indeed, two KD targets that we found to decrease proliferation in NPCs, SETD2 (ref. 24) and KMT2E26, have been reported to be associated with either microcephaly or macrocephaly depending on the specific variants studied in these genes. Thus, although disruption of NPC proliferation is a convergent phenotype observed across many of our perturbations, how this relates to downstream changes in head size remains to be determined.

Inducible KD in cerebral organoids also revealed shared neurodevelopmental phenotypes. For these organoid studies, we created the FLEx-based inducible CRISPR knockdown (FLICK) construct. FLICK enables control over the timing of KD through doxycycline-inducible genetic recombination that results in constitutive expression of CasRx and an EGFP reporter (Fig. 2a), providing robust KD (Extended Data Fig. 7a). For long-term studies, we targeted ASD genes whose KDs were not found to impair NPC proliferation in our CytoTrack experiments (Supplementary Table 10). FLICK constructs targeting these genes or expressing NTC gRNAs were each separately transposed into iPS cells. We then used fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) to isolate cells that had integrated the FLICK constructs on the basis of their expression of fluorescent reporter TagBFP. Following FACS, we pooled an equal number of FLICK+ iPS cells for each KD target and for the NTCs, and cultured this mixed iPS cell population for 4 days to allow the cells to recover before generating mosaic organoids (day 0). Recombination of the FLICK construct, which leads to constitutive expression of CasRx and an EGFP reporter, was induced in a subset of the cells (approximately 10%) by treatment with doxycycline from days 14–15, when the organoids predominantly consisted of PAX6+ NPCs (Extended Data Fig. 7b). These organoids were subsequently grown until either 1 or 2 months of development (Fig. 2b) and analysed by scRNA-seq; this analysis identified all expected cell types (Fig. 2c,d and Extended Data Fig. 7c,d) including radial glia, intermediate progenitor cells, inhibitory neurons and ExNs. Further scRNA-seq analysis confirmed that the FLICK construct could produce efficient KD (Extended Data Fig. 7e).

Long-term organoid KDs revealed shared phenotypes across several perturbations. Several KDs, particularly those targeting BAZ2B, CLASP1 and EHMT1, were depleted from the organoids compared with NTCs (Fig. 2e), even though these KDs did not impair proliferation in NPC cultures (Extended Data Fig. 6a and Supplementary Table 10). This discrepancy between our NPC cultures and organoids suggests that long-term organoids, which support progenitor proliferation as well as differentiation, can be used to identify neurodevelopmental phenotypes that are not apparent in short-term NPC cultures. Notably, microcephaly has been associated with pathogenic variants in these genes31,32,33, consistent with the organoid phenotypes. Furthermore, these KDs also altered cell type proportions in organoids, producing relatively greater numbers of ExNs and fewer radial glia (Padj = 0.021 for BAZ2B, 0.018 for CLASP1 and 0.012 for EHMT1; ordinary one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test) (Fig. 2f and Extended Data Fig. 7f). Together, these results indicate that disruption of these genes may result in increased ExN differentiation at the expense of self-renewal of radial glial progenitors. Most of the other KDs also resulted in a trend towards an increased proportion of ExNs by the 2-month time point, suggesting that this is a convergent neurodevelopmental phenotype, in line with previous observations34,35.

Depletion of ASD genes also resulted in prominent transcriptomic convergence in cerebral organoids (Fig. 3a and Extended Data Fig. 7g). Radial glia with different KDs exhibited highly overlapping DEGs, further supporting our findings from NPC cultures. Indeed, the DEGs identified in organoid radial glial progenitors strongly overlapped with the DEGs identified from KD of the same target in NPC cultures (Extended Data Fig. 7h), suggesting that these effects on gene expression are not specific to a particular experimental context. Transcriptomic convergence was also observed in other cell types, including intermediate progenitor cells and ExNs (Fig. 3a and Extended Data Fig. 7g), demonstrating that the convergence of ASD genes on shared downstream effects is not unique to neural progenitors. This was consistent with findings of studies that identified shared ASD DEGs in postmortem brain tissue from individuals with distinct causes of ASD36,37,38, as well as with widespread observations of convergent ExN phenotypes such as disordered synaptic structure or activity upon disruption of distinct ASD genes20, suggesting that convergence does not reflect merely a shared alteration of cell type or fate of progenitor cells. Furthermore, we found that detection of overlapping DEGs using scRNA-seq was heavily dependent on the number of cells sequenced for each perturbation, as demonstrated by downsampling analysis (Fig. 3b). This sample-size dependence may partially explain the lack of robust transcriptomic convergence observed at the individual gene level in some previous studies with smaller sample sizes34,39. Our use of an isogenic background is also likely to have contributed to our detection of transcriptomic convergence, as different genetic backgrounds have been found to significantly affect neurodevelopmental phenotypes resulting from the disruption of ASD genes in organoids34. Finally, although some convergently disrupted genes were cell-type specific, many genes were consistently disrupted across several cell types, including SFARI DEGs (Fig. 3c). This suggests that although cell-type-specific features affect the transcriptomic consequences of ASD gene perturbations, certain effects could be broadly shared by diverse cell types.

Acute disruption of ASD genes in 2-month organoids revealed even greater convergence on shared DEGs. A further set of mosaic FLICK organoids was grown for 2 months before induction of KD with doxycycline treatment from days 61–62; this was followed by scRNA-seq 2 days later, to determine the acute effects of disruption of these genes in organoids that had developed normally. This approach resulted in similar numbers of cells per KD (Fig. 3d and Extended Data Fig. 7i), enabling deeper analysis of the KDs that had been depleted from the organoids with long-term KD (Fig. 2e). As expected, these organoids contained a mixture of cell types (Fig. 3e and Extended Data Fig. 7j), with ExNs by far the most abundant cell type at this developmental time point (Extended Data Fig. 7k). Many KDs resulted in ExN DEGs that were enriched for the DEGs identified from NPC cultures (Extended Data Fig. 7l), again suggesting shared changes in gene expression across cell types upon KD of ASD genes. Furthermore, acute KD of the target genes that had been depleted from organoids over the course of long-term KD (Fig. 2e) allowed us to obtain much greater cell numbers for these perturbations, which showed that these KDs also resulted in strong transcriptomic convergence (Fig. 3f).

Transcriptional regulators that could mediate transcriptomic convergence in ASD were identified through MAGIC analysis (Methods), which uses chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP–seq) data from ENCODE to identify transcription factors and cofactors whose binding targets are enriched within a gene set of interest. This identified transcriptional regulators whose target genes were recurrently differentially expressed across many of the ASD gene KDs in NPCs (Extended Data Fig. 8a and Supplementary Table 11), including HC-SFARI genes such as CHD2 and SIN3A. Of the recurrent MAGIC regulators not previously implicated in ASD, several were both highly constrained (Supplementary Table 7) and co-expressed with known ASD genes (Extended Data Fig. 8a), features that have been demonstrated to be predictive of novel ASD risk genes4.

MAGIC analysis also identified consistent drivers of convergence in organoids (Extended Data Fig. 8a and Supplementary Table 11). Of the top recurrent transcriptional regulators identified in NPCs, half (13 of 26, 50%) were also identified in organoid radial glial progenitors (Extended Data Fig. 8b). MAGIC also identified recurrent ExN regulators shared across developmental stages and across long-term versus acute KD timescales, including many SFARI genes. Nine of these genes were also identified as top transcriptional regulators in neural progenitors, indicating that they may be key drivers of convergence in various cellular contexts.

Of particular interest, the RE1 silencing transcription factor (REST) was significant according to MAGIC analysis in 17 of 18 (94.4%) NPC perturbations (Extended Data Fig. 8a) and consistently emerged as a top MAGIC regulator across all tested conditions (Extended Data Fig. 8b). Although direct mutations in REST have not previously been associated with ASD, REST has critical neurodevelopmental roles and functions as a master negative regulator of neurogenesis40,41. Dysregulation of REST has also been reported following disruption of ASD genes including CHD8 (ref. 42) and Rett syndrome gene MECP2 (refs. 43,44). Re-analysis of human and mouse REST ChIP–seq binding sites (Methods) identified strong enrichment of SFARI genes (Padj = 3.6 × 10−67 and 7.7 × 10−50, respectively), including HC-SFARI genes (Padj = 2.2 × 10−13 and 3.7 × 10−8, respectively), among REST targets (Extended Data Fig. 8c), supporting a key role for REST in regulation of ASD-relevant gene expression.

De novo GRN reconstruction from our NPC RNA-seq data using ARACNe enabled association of further transcription factors and chromatin modifiers (together referred to as ‘regulators’) with their downstream targets (regulons) (Methods). We validated this approach by separately constructing ‘held-out’ GRNs that excluded data from samples in which the KD target itself was a potential regulator, thereby preventing circularity in regulon inference. Regulons predicted from these held-out GRNs strongly overlapped with the DEGs observed following KD of the corresponding target genes in NPCs (Padj < 3.98 × 10−116 for each, combined P = 4.08 × 10−937) (Fig. 4a and Extended Data Fig. 9a). Thus, ARACNe effectively captures bona fide regulatory relationships.

The full NPC GRN revealed a high degree of interconnection between ASD genes and their upstream regulators (Fig. 4b). Indeed, ASD genes were strongly enriched among central genes of the NPC GRN (Padj = 9.81 × 10−6 for SFARI genes, 1.99 × 10−4 for HC-SFARI genes, centrality assessed using the PageRank algorithm, Methods) (Fig. 4c). By contrast, intellectual-disability-only genes did not exhibit enrichment among the central genes of the GRN (Padj = 0.19), consistent with the lack of enrichment that we observed among the DEGs resulting from ASD gene KDs (Fig. 1c). In addition, central GRN genes were enriched for pathways associated with the G2M checkpoint (Padj = 6.0 × 10−4) and mitotic spindle (Padj = 3.6 × 10−5), consistent with the NPC proliferation phenotypes we observed (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Table 10).

We next identified GRN regulators that acted as key drivers of ASD-associated transcriptomic convergence. This analysis revealed 70 top drivers of convergence whose activity was consistently altered by ASD gene KD (Methods) and whose targets were both enriched for SFARI genes and central to the GRN (Supplementary Table 12). Nearly half of these top drivers of convergence were SFARI genes (31 of 70, OR = 6.35, Padj = 3.9 × 10−11), and nearly one-third were HC-SFARI genes (22 of 70, OR = 10.61, Padj = 6.7 × 10−12). By contrast, only 7 of these top drivers of convergence were intellectual-disability-only genes (7 of 70, OR = 2.06, Padj = 0.078). Moreover, the top drivers of convergence were consistently downregulated across nearly all ASD gene KDs (Extended Data Fig. 9b). They were also highly interconnected in the GRN (P < 1 × 10−6) (Fig. 4d), as well as in the STRING database of protein–protein interactions (P = 2.0 × 10−9) (Methods). Taken together, these findings suggest that these genes both act as central regulators of many ASD genes and cross-regulate each other, driving transcriptomic convergence in ASD.

Iterative walktrap clustering identified 4 gene modules among the 70 top drivers of convergence (Fig. 4e), one of which (the blue module) was especially strongly interconnected. This module exhibited very high cross-interaction through STRING analysis (P < 1 × 10−16) and included REST (Extended Data Fig. 8) and several notable ASD genes including CHD8, one of the strongest known risk genes for ASD18. Notably, the inferred CHD8 regulon from the GRN was strongly enriched (first-degree regulon Padj = 4.6 × 10−7; second-degree regulon Padj = 3.9 × 10−20) for direct CHD8 target genes previously identified through ChIP–seq from NPCs45 (Fig. 4f), further supporting the validity of ARACNe-predicted regulatory relationships. Moreover, consistent with previous reports45,46, CHD8 ChIP–seq targets were also highly enriched for NPC-expressed SFARI genes (Padj = 2.6 × 10−8) and HC-SFARI genes (Padj = 8.5 × 10−8). The CHD8-containing module also included three transcriptional regulators (SETD2, SETD5 and KMT2E) that we had directly perturbed, and DEGs from these KDs exhibited significant enrichment for CHD8 ChIP–seq targets (Padj = 1.2 × 10−5, 7.8 × 10−8 and 2.6 × 10−4, respectively) (Fig. 4f), further demonstrating convergent regulation of shared targets by critical ASD genes. This analysis highlights CHD8, a high-confidence ASD risk gene, as a key driver of transcriptomic convergence in ASD.

Previous studies have found that genes downregulated in ASD tend to be more highly expressed in the neurotypical female versus male brain47,48, raising the possibility that this baseline female-biased expression may contribute to the FPE8,47,48. We identified 82 genes that were consistently downregulated by ASD gene KD and exhibited female-biased expression in the brain (Methods); 28 of these were highly constrained (Extended Data Fig. 10a), including 12 highly constrained SFARI genes (OR = 2.79, P = 8.65 × 10−3) (Fig. 5a). Re-analysis of single-nucleus RNA-seq (snRNA-seq) data (Methods) from the frontal cortex of neurotypical male controls and autistic male participants—including individuals with 15q duplication syndrome and genetically idiopathic ASD—revealed that many of these highly constrained genes were significantly downregulated in ExNs from autistic male individuals, further supporting their relevance to ASD (Fig. 5a and Extended Data Fig. 10a).

ZFX is of particular interest because it is expressed from both the active and inactive X chromosome in female individuals10, making it one of the most highly and consistently female-biased genes across diverse tissues49, including the developing cerebral cortex (Extended Data Fig. 10b,c). Moreover, a recent report has implicated pathogenic ZFX variants in ASD50, and other coding variants in the ZFX gene have been identified in ASD probands3,51,52. ZFX encodes a potent transcriptional activator of genome-wide targets53, and we identified many regulatory relationships between ZFX and ASD-relevant gene sets in our GRN (Fig. 5b), including the top drivers of transcriptomic convergence in ASD that we had previously identified (OR = 9.8, Padj = 2.2 × 10−6). We also found that ZFX was a downstream target of many of these top drivers, with several reciprocal regulatory relationships (Fig. 5c). The strong enrichment of ZFX targets for ASD-relevant genes was further confirmed by re-analysis of ZFX ChIP–seq data (Fig. 5d). As ZFX is recurrently downregulated upon disruption of ASD genes, its female-biased expression, in combination with its role as a regulator of ASD-relevant genes, could result in cascading and broad sex-differential impacts on ASD gene expression, potentially contributing to the FPE in ASD (Fig. 5e).

Here we have shown widespread transcriptomic convergence following depletion of ASD genes and identified key chromatin modifiers and transcription factors, including CHD8 and REST, as critical drivers of this convergence. These drivers were highly interconnected with each other, often through mutual regulatory relationships; these findings demonstrate how disruption of a single regulator can lead to changes that propagate throughout a GRN. We also identified the transcription factor ZFX, which is expressed from both the active and inactive X chromosome in female individuals, as a key transcriptional regulator of ASD genes; the female-biased expression of ZFX could thus buffer the effects of ASD gene disruptions in female individuals, contributing to the FPE in ASD. Taken together, these findings provide a model for how diverse genes can converge on core transcriptomic changes and generate shared ASD phenotypes, as well as how sex-differential activity of these genes can lead to male-biased risk for ASD.

Our detection of widespread transcriptomic convergence probably benefited from several experimental design choices that were intended to minimize variability. For instance, to avoid confounding effects of different genomic backgrounds, we perturbed the ASD genes in an isogenic background. In addition, we performed these perturbations by introducing KD constructs in a polyclonal manner instead of creating different cell lines for each ASD target gene, as the process of clonal selection can introduce line-to-line variability54. For our cerebral organoid studies, we created the FLICK KD construct so that we could generate mosaic organoids containing all KD conditions as well as the NTCs, minimizing any effects from organoid-to-organoid variability. By reducing variability across our experimental designs, we probably enhanced our sensitivity to detect convergence.

We also optimized our RNA-seq approaches for the different experimental contexts. Initially, we used highly homogeneous NPC cultures, enabling us to perform bulk RNA-seq on this single cell type. This allowed deep transcriptomic profiling, avoiding the problems of ‘dropout’ that affect scRNA-seq55. However, for samples that contained a heterogeneous mix of different cell types, such as cerebral organoids, bulk RNA-seq results could reflect differences in relative cell type abundance as well as changes in gene expression. Thus, we used scRNA-seq to analyse our organoids and sought to compensate for the decreased sensitivity of this approach by sequencing a large number of cells for each KD condition. As our downsampling analysis demonstrated (Fig. 3b), these large cell numbers were critical for detection of transcriptomic convergence through scRNA-seq. Furthermore, for our NPC experiments, we performed RNA-seq 24 h after introducing the KD constructs. We selected this short timeframe to focus our analysis on the initial effects of each KD, rather than long-term consequences that could be more strongly influenced by compensatory responses and stochastic effects. This short timeframe may have been particularly well suited for detection of transcriptomic convergence, which would have further benefited our GRN reconstruction.

Building an understanding of the complex relationships in GRNs is critical for identification of key ‘rate-limiting’ genes that affect the GRN as a whole and lead to particular biological outcomes. Previous studies of Hirschsprung disease have identified an analogous GRN centred on rate-limiting gene RET, which can be affected by direct mutations in RET itself or disruptions to upstream regulators of RET56. Here we similarly identified critical genes, including CHD8 and REST, the effects of which can propagate throughout the GRN and drive transcriptomic convergence in ASD. Our findings are consistent with those of previous studies identifying CHD8 as an important regulator of ASD gene expression45,46,57. Moreover, haploinsufficiency of CHD8 has been found to result in dysregulation of REST in the mouse brain42, consistent with the highly interconnected relationships we found in our GRN. In addition, disruption of Rett syndrome gene MECP2 affects REST43,44, and REST is dysregulated in ASD brain tissue relative to neurotypical controls42, further supporting its relevance to ASD. Given that REST is a master negative regulator of neuronal genes40,41 and that CHD8 affects proliferation and neurogenesis58, it is plausible that the convergent dysregulation of CHD8/REST targets could directly underlie the altered proliferation we found in NPCs (Fig. 1f), as well as the increased relative abundance of ExNs that we observed following some of the ASD gene KDs in organoids (Fig. 2f). Several previous studies have also identified imbalances in neural proliferation versus differentiation following ASD-relevant perturbations28,58,59; thus, future work is needed to further characterize the molecular mechanisms linking our observed convergent changes in gene expression to these shared neurodevelopmental phenotypes.

Our results also provide insight into the male bias of ASD. Downregulation of ZFX by KD of many different ASD genes in NPCs and lower ZFX levels in ExNs from individuals with ASD (Fig. 5a) suggest that decreased ZFX expression may be a recurrent transcriptional feature of ASD. The higher baseline level of ZFX in the female brain due to its expression from both X chromosomes could thus buffer against this ASD-associated downregulation. Consistent with this, a recent study of sex-biased gene expression across cell types in the cortex found that promoters of autosomal genes exhibiting female-biased expression were enriched for ZFX transcription factor motifs, whereas promoters of male-biased genes were not60. Male individuals have a Y-linked homologue of ZFX called ZFY; however, this encodes a weaker transcriptional activator than ZFX53, and the GRN regulon of ZFY showed no enrichment for SFARI genes (OR = 1.1, Padj = 0.36), suggesting divergent roles with respect to ASD. Further functional characterization of ZFX and other genes expressed from the inactive X chromosome may provide critical insights into how female-biased expression affects ASD risk13. Moreover, it will be valuable to directly compare the transcriptional effects of ASD gene perturbations between male cell lines, as used in this study, and female cell lines. Indeed, our use of a male rather than female founder cell line may have contributed to the striking changes in gene expression we observed, as female cells may be able to better tolerate the same ASD gene KDs owing to higher baseline expression of ZFX and/or other factors that contribute to the FPE.

As many genes that have been implicated in ASD show rare heterozygous loss-of-function mutations1, we prioritized ASD genes with a high probability of loss-of-function intolerance in our study. We also focused on genes that exhibited a ‘head-to-head’ configuration with an adjacent lncRNA; several lncRNAs in such a configuration have been found to fine-tune the expression of their neighbours61, meaning that this configuration could be used to putatively identify particularly dosage-sensitive genes. We further included two lncRNAs that had been implicated in ASD through heterozygous loss-of-function mutations15,16. Our Cas13-based KDs on average produced a 48.2% decrease in expression of the target genes (Supplementary Table 2); thus, this experimental approach seems to be suitable for study of how heterozygous loss of function of haploinsufficient genes can contribute to ASD.

Although our perturbations were focused on genes associated with ASD through rare single-gene mutations, it is likely that other classes of ASD-associated variants disrupt the same convergent network. Supporting this, studies of postmortem brain tissue have demonstrated that idiopathic ASD cases share core transcriptomic changes38 and have overlapping DNA methylation signatures62 with cases of ASD caused by rare mutations of large effect size, such as chromosome 15q11–13 duplication. Moreover, reports suggest that common alleles of smaller effect size that have been implicated in ASD can also drive changes that converge with those caused by rare ASD variants63. This raises the possibility that distinct causes of ASD, including rare single-gene mutations, large copy number variants and common genetic variation, could result in similar phenotypes by converging on disruption of shared ASD-relevant GRNs. Further characterization of these GRNs has broad potential to clarify our understanding of the genetic architecture of ASD across a wide variety of genetic variant classes and to identify potential therapeutic targets that could benefit individuals with diverse causes of ASD. More broadly, although this work was focused on ASD, the approaches used here are widely applicable to different cell types and conditions. Thus, this study provides a framework for identification of critical transcriptional regulators across diverse contexts, from development through disease.

Transcriptome-wide scRNA-seq libraries were prepared using Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3′ Reagent Kits v.3.1 (Dual Index) from 10x Genomics, following user guide CG000316 Rev. A.

Full details of protocols are available from protocols.io (https://doi.org/10.17504/protocols.io.5qpvoj5pzg4o/v1). Briefly, for quantification of FLICK constructs from the initial pool of iPS cells used to generate mosaic organoids (day 0), RNA was extracted from triplicate samples of 42,000 cells using a Direct-zol RNA Microprep Kit (Zymo Research, R2062). For generation of cDNA, 50–250 ng of RNA was reverse transcribed using a SuperScript IV First-Strand Synthesis System (Invitrogen, 18091050) with an oligo(dT)VN primer containing UMIs. Sequencing libraries were then generated using Q5 Hot Start High-Fidelity 2X Master Mix (NEB, M0494L) and custom primers.

To generate FLICK barcode libraries from scRNA-seq libraries, we used 5 μl of amplified and cleaned cDNA (produced by following the 10x User Guide CG000316 Rev. A to the end of step 2). Barcode-containing cDNA was amplified for sequencing using Q5 Hot Start High-Fidelity 2X Master Mix (NEB, M0494L) and custom primers. Libraries were cleaned using SPRIselect DNA Size Selection Reagent (Beckman Coulter, B23317) and assessed using TapeStation High Sensitivity D1000 reagents (Agilent, 5067-5603 and 5067-5584).

We aligned the scRNA-seq data to the prebuilt Cell Ranger (v.9.0.0) hg38 reference using cellranger-count in feature-barcode mode to perform simultaneous gene expression and guide abundance quantification. Introns were not included in gene expression quantification (--include-introns=false) to avoid quantification of partially degraded transcripts that had been targeted by CRISPR–CasRx. CRISPR guide sequencing library FASTQs for 2-month samples were downsampled to 1.25% of reads using seqtk (v.1.4) to match CRISPR library sequencing depth for 1-month samples and to minimize background for guide assignment using the cellranger-CRISPR guide quantification pipeline. All other parameters were set to default values. Cell Ranger guide assignments were used to filter to only cells with exactly one assigned guide.

Long-term and acute KD samples were processed separately but equivalently. CellBender (v.0.3.2) was used for detection and filtering of ambient RNA contamination, whereas scrublet (v.0.2.3) was used for automatic doublet detection and filtering. All other downstream analysis was performed with Seurat (v.5.0.0). High-quality cells were retained according to the following quality control metrics: (1) percent mitochondrial (percent.mt) gene expression < 20% and (2) 500 < number of expressed genes (nFeature_RNA) < 6,000.

Cell type annotation was first performed on only NTC cells. NTC cells were extracted to create a control-only Seurat object. This object subsequently underwent normalization (NormalizeData), variable feature identification (FindVariableFeatures), scaling (ScaleData; regressed covariates of percent.mt, percent_ribo, nCount_RNA and nFeature_RNA) and dimensionality reduction (RunPCA and RunUMAP). Integration was performed using harmony (v.1.2.0) across organoid batches, and the integrated object was used for unsupervised clustering (FindNeighbors and FindClusters, resolution values of 0.6 and 1.2). A small number of unsupervised clusters (fewer than 1,000 cells) were filtered owing to low cluster-wide quality control metrics (exceptionally high or low nCount_RNA, high percent.mt). All subsequent two-dimensional visualization of this control-only Seurat object was performed using a UMAP representation of the integrated data. Cell type annotation was performed using canonical brain marker genes. A Seurat object was next constructed for all cells, following the processing pipeline (for instance, normalization, scaling, dimensionality reduction and integration) described above. For cell type annotation, label transfer was performed from the control-only Seurat object (TransferData) to the full Seurat object. Expression patterns of canonical brain marker genes were then used to refine and finalize these cell type annotations.

The relative abundance of cells for each KD target was determined for each time point (day 0, day 30, day 60 and day 61). For day 0, relative abundance was determined from bulk RNA-seq libraries (‘Construction of FLICK barcode sequencing libraries’) using a custom pipeline (‘Code availability’). For days 30, 60 and 61, relative abundance was determined from scRNA-seq data using the CRISPR guide assignments generated by Cell Ranger (‘Alignment and quantification of organoid scRNA-seq data’). For each sample, results were normalized to the starting proportion at day 0 and displayed relative to NTCs from the same time point. Results were plotted as the mean ± s.e.m. Statistical analysis was performed using repeated measures one-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test (GraphPad Prism), considering all long-term samples (days 30, 60 and 61).

The relative ExN/RG proportion was determined for each KD target at day 30, day 60 and day 61. Results were normalized to the NTC sample from the same time point. Statistical analysis was performed using ordinary one-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test (GraphPad Prism), considering all long-term samples (days 30, 60 and 61).

Differential expression analysis was performed between perturbed cells per guide and cells that received NTCs, stratified by main cell types (radial glia, intermediate progenitor cells, ExNs and inhibitory neurons). Seurat FindMarkers was used to perform differential expression with logfc.threshold = 0, min.pct = 0.10, min.cells.group = 3 and all other parameters set to default values. Enrichment analyses of DEGs were performed using one-sided Fisher’s exact tests, and P values were adjusted using Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple hypothesis testing. The universe was defined as all genes expressed in both comparisons of interest.

We visualized shared DEGs between organoid cell types (Fig. 3c) using R package igraph (v.2.1.4). The Fruchterman–Reingold layout algorithm (layout_with_fr) was used to determine final node placement.

MAGIC91 (v.1.1) was used to identify transcription factors and cofactors whose targets were enriched in the DEGs from individual perturbations. The strategy of ‘gene body (promoter to the end of the last exon) plus 1Kb flanking sequence either side of the gene body’ was used. For NPC analyses, the universe was set as all genes with baseMean > 10 in NTC samples. For organoid analyses, the universe was set as all genes expressed in at least 10% of cells of that cell type (Seurat min.pct = 0.10; no log2 fold-change cutoff), as these were the genes assessed for differential expression.

Data was extracted from Table S2 from ref. 92. Mouse genes were mapped to human genes using Orthology.eg.db (v.3.20.0), org.Mm.eg.db (v.3.20.0) and org.Hs.eg.db (v.3.20.0). Enrichment analysis was performed using a one-sided Fisher’s exact test with all BrainSpan genes with normalized expression ≥ 1 as the universe (version ‘RNA-Seq Gencode v10 summarized to genes’; https://brainspan.org; accessed 6 January 2024). P values were adjusted using Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple hypothesis testing. Results are displayed as bar plots, with main bars indicating the OR from a one-sided Fisher’s exact test and error bars indicating the lower bound of the 95% confidence interval on the OR.

A GRN was constructed using bioinformatic tool ARACNe-AP93. The input comprised the RNA-seq data from our 3 NTCs and the successful KD samples with at least 50 DEGs. We used curated lists of transcription factors94 and epigenetic regulators95 and selected genes expressed in NPCs (baseMean > 10 in NTC samples) as the candidate genetic regulators. To create the GRN, we used default parameters and 100 bootstraps that were then aggregated to form the final network. We then used VIPER96 (v.1.36.0) to identify differentially active regulons following standard operating procedures. In brief, we identified regulons with significant differential expression between NTCs and KDs using a t-test, with a null distribution defined by sample permutations.

To benchmark the predictive power of the ARACNe-derived GRNs, we generated further ARACNe networks after individually holding out perturbation samples for 5 target genes that were identified as regulators of at least 250 genes (range: 478–1,219 genes) in their second-degree regulons (that is, their direct targets, which constituted their first-degree regulon, as well as the direct targets of those genes). For each held-out regulator, we assessed the overlap between the predicted positively regulated genes within its second-degree regulon and the set of downregulated DEGs resulting from perturbation of the same regulator, using a one-sided Fisher’s exact test. P values were adjusted using Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple hypothesis testing. P values across the five held-out perturbations were combined using Fisher’s method.

To identify central regulators in our GRN, we converted the GRN to a graph using R package igraph (v.2.1.4) and ran the PageRank algorithm97 using igraph method page_rank. We then ordered genes by their PageRank score and ran gene set enrichment analysis98 using R package fgsea (v.1.32.4), testing the following ontologies: SFARI genes, HC-SFARI genes, intellectual disability genes (exclusive of SFARI genes) and the 50 Molecular Signature Database Hallmark Pathways99 (obtained from https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb).

VIPER96 (v.1.36.0) was used to identify GRN regulators whose regulons were significantly differentially active across the KDs compared with NTC controls. From these, we prioritized 70 regulators whose regulons were significantly enriched for both SFARI genes (one-sided Fisher’s exact test with Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple hypothesis testing) and high PageRank scores (gene set enrichment analysis with Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple hypothesis testing).

We calculated the mean normalized degree for the indicated set of regulators and their first-degree regulons (Fig. 4d) using R package igraph (v.2.1.4) by running the function mean(degree(graph, normalized = TRUE)). We then created an empirical null distribution of mean normalized degrees for each indicated set of regulators by creating 1 million subnetworks of the same set size of randomly selected GRN regulators. The relative mean normalized degree was calculated as the observed mean normalized degree for a given set of regulators divided by the mean of the values for the corresponding empirical null distribution. A one-sided empirical P value was then calculated as the proportion of null values greater than or equal to the observed mean normalized degree, with a detection limit of P = 1 × 10−6.

To evaluate known or predicted protein–protein interactions between the top 70 regulators, we used the STRING database GUI100. For the universe, we used the full set of genes that were considered as candidate genetic regulators in the GRN: transcription factors94 and epigenetic regulators95 that were expressed in NPCs (baseMean > 10 in NTC samples). All other parameters were set to default values. For clustering of our 70 regulators into modules, we performed iterative walktrap clustering using R package igraph (v.2.1.4) by running the function cluster_walktrap with default parameters.

For enrichment analysis of CHD8 targets, we used Supplementary Data 1 from ref. 45.
