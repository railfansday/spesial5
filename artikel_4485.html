Our Long-Term 2026 Honda Passport Is Still a Hot Commodity

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

With its plush interior and a commodious cargo hold, our boxy Honda has become the car-board-lottery jackpot for those planning a road trip.

“I’ve finally won!” exclaims technical editor Austin Irwin in response to snagging our long-term 2026 Honda Passport TrailSport for the weekend. Typically, it’s sports cars, rolling palaces, or vehicles with a manual transmission that elicit that type of reaction during our weekly sign-out process, not mid-size two-row SUVs. But the Passport really does have that effect on folks around here.

While it’s nowhere near the most engaging vehicle at our office at any given time, our sunburnt Honda’s allure comes from its ability to effortlessly eat both cargo and miles in large sums. Depending on your weekend plans, that can be far more valuable than the Hattori Hanzō–sharp steering precision of a 911 or the rewarding snick of a Miata’s six-speed shifter.

In the 10,000 miles since we last checked in, a handful of editors have taken full advantage of the Passport’s strengths. In just a few short months, it checked Pennsylvania, Wisconsin, and Ohio off its regional-visitation checklist, with one sentiment echoed by the three staffers upon returning from their respective excursions: The Passport is a damn good road tripper.

“Taking the Passport on a trip through Ohio to Hocking Hills was very enjoyable,” said online production assistant Sarah Taylor, who specifically called out its smooth ride and competent handling on winding mountain roads. Senior social media editor Carter Fry also joined in on praising the Honda’s on-road demeanor, noting that the SUV “is so well balanced considering its size and all-terrain tires.” A plush ride and confidence-inspiring handling are important traits on long-distance trips, but the Passport’s comfortable and expansive interior is what really cements it as our resident road-trip hero.

“Cavernous,” “spacious,” and “generous” have all appeared in the Passport’s in-car logbook to describe its cargo accommodations, and it is indeed very roomy back there by mid-size-SUV standards. In our testing, we managed to stuff under its liftgate 16 carry-on suitcases with the rearmost seats in their upright position and 38 carry-ons with the rear bench folded down. That’s 13 and nine more than will fit in a Toyota 4Runner, respectively. Managing news editor Eric Stafford did his best to stretch the Passport’s people and stuff-hauling capabilities to their limit on a ski trip to northern Wisconsin and reported that there was more than enough room to comfortably accommodate three guys and all their gear. So far, no one has been able to find anything that doesn’t fit in the Passport's cargo hold, but we’ll keep brainstorming.

As far as grievances are concerned, there haven’t been many. Several editors have reported software issues related to phone pairing, Google Maps connectivity, and Apple CarPlay glitches. On one occasion, the infotainment screen went blank but continued to play music from the grave. On another, the GPS display within the digital gauge cluster got confused and gave up despite that Android Auto was running with no connectivity issues. Both of those incidents were resolved with a simple restart. Aside from that, there’s an ambiguous logbook comment likening the Passport’s interior to “something you’d order out of an L.L. Bean catalog,” but it's unclear whether that was meant as a compliment or a dig. Depends on how much you like cable-knit sweaters, we suppose.

There isn’t much to report from a service or repair standpoint, either. Since we took in the Passport for its 10,000-mile oil service, which was covered under Honda’s one-year, 12,000-mile complimentary maintenance coverage, the sole out-of-pocket expense we’ve incurred was for the Passport’s 20,000-mile service, which included an oil and filter change, a tire rotation, and an inspection, for a total of $184. The only change to the TrailSport’s hardware was a swap back to its original 275/60R-18 General Grabber A/T Sport tires after the shedding of its Continental VikingContact 8 winters, but we handled that ourselves.

While it’s uncommon for a family SUV to be a first-round pick in an office full of performance-pilled enthusiasts, it’s clear why our long-term Passport is so highly coveted. With its coddling seats, plush but poised ride quality, and seemingly boundless cargo hold, the Passport is a hard act to beat when you’re staring down a thousand-mile road trip. As long as C/D staffers remain adventurous, we’re sure that the Passport’s key fob will continue to be fought over in the 20,000 miles to come.

The first few months of a new job can be stressful, especially when you have more than 20 professional scrutinizers judging you at all times, constantly scheming new ways to push you until your wheels fall off. Some might call that a hostile work environment, but that’s daily life for the vehicles in the Car and Driver long-term test fleet. While some of our long-termers are progressively eased into their 40,000 miles of hard labor, our Honda Passport TrailSport Elite was afforded no such luxury. Soon after it got acquainted with its new handlers, we threw it headfirst into the ultimate utility litmus test as a support vehicle for our most recent Lightning Lap event.

For those unfamiliar with our annual gantlet of speed, here are the CliffsNotes: We round up the newest street-legal performance cars and set course for Virginia International Raceway in Alton, Virginia. Once there, our editors attack the 4.1-mile Grand Course configuration with as much aplomb as they dare, reporting back with lap times and harrowing tales of grit and grandeur from behind the wheel. It is quite the production, and with 16 vehicles in need of attention at this year's event, a capable gear hauler was as important as ever.

We decided that a 12-foot U-Haul box trailer would best suit our needs, so we rented one and loaded it with items crucial for the operation, including jacks, jack stands, scales, test equipment, tires, a grill, and a hot-dog rotisserie. After filling the trailer well short of the Passport's 5000-pound maximum towing capacity, we backed our rig up to its new companion for the 600-plus-mile journey to the Virginia–North Carolina border. Mating the two was easy with the TrailSport Elite's multiview backup camera. Our only gripe was the position of our Passport's hitch receiver, which was a little low for our use case, but we made it work. With the supplies loaded and a full tank of gas, we hit the road.

Road warrior Christi VanSyckle was tasked with piloting our traffic-cone-colored Honda across multiple state lines and the Appalachian Mountains. She arrived at the track with a positive report card for the Passport, saying that “stability was confident at highway speeds through the mountains.” She added that the cabin was surprisingly quiet and the seats provided enough support that she never felt the need to stop and stretch. Gold stars for comfort and capability. Nothing changed on the return trip, so we'd say that the Passport aced its doozy of an initiation test.

Back home, the Passport was finally given the chance to settle into a more mundane daily rhythm. It continued to draw praise as more editors had the chance to slide behind the wheel. In the logbook comments that trickled in, one comparison continued to pop up: "It used to be the Accord sedan, but the V-6 family Honda is now an SUV. I'm surprisingly okay with it," said editor-in-chief Tony Quiroga. That thought was echoed by staff photographer Michael Simari, who mused, "The Passport's Accord-like agreeableness continues to grow on me."

Being likened to the 40-time 10Best-winning Honda Accord is quite the compliment, and the comparison makes a lot of sense considering the Passport shares the Accord's practical, ergonomically satisfying, and well-rounded demeanor. Its softly sprung ride makes long-distance travel comfortable and effortless, and its voluminous rear cabin grants back-seat passengers an especially generous amount of stretch-out space. Up front, the 12.3-inch touchscreen is quick to respond to inputs, and the physical steering-wheel buttons and climate-control toggles are positioned just right. Some drivers have experienced Apple CarPlay connectivity issues, but that is likely due in part to the dozens of phones constantly cycling through our car's system.

While our time with the Passport has been mostly smiles so far, a couple of sore spots have surfaced. The 3.5-liter V-6 under the hood has received mixed reviews, with the main pain point being its lack of low-rpm grunt. Its full 262 pound-feet of peak torque doesn't arrive until 5000 rpm, which, combined with our test vehicle's 4702 pounds, leads to a notable delay in gumption after kicking the go pedal. One comment also mentioned the 10-speed automatic's indifferent attitude toward downshifting, which contributes to the malaise, especially during city driving and highway merging. These gripes have led some staffers to contemplate what the Passport would be like with an alternative powerplant, with video deputy editor Carlos Lago positing that "a hybrid system would make this feel much punchier, à la Grand Highlander Hybrid."

A hybrid powertrain would also work wonders for the Passport's fuel efficiency, which has nonetheless been reasonable thus far. After more than 10,000 miles, we're averaging 19 mpg, which is 1 mpg less than the TrailSport's EPA combined rating yet a decent figure considering it includes the round trip to VIR with a trailer in tow.

A recent swap to Continental VikingContact 8 winter tires, which set us back a little under $930 with installation, also is likely impacting our fuel economy, but with southeastern Michigan doing its best snow-globe impression over the past few months, it's been a welcome switch. The slightly narrower winter rubber—sized 265/60R-18 versus the OE-spec 275/60R-18s—has made drudging through the polar-apocalyptic wasteland easy, providing nearly undefeatable grip on glassy interstates and unplowed side roads. Road warrior Jacob Kurowicki pointed out that the new tires are a double-edged sword, saying, "This thing is too damn capable in the snow on these winter tires. Being a hooligan requires some commitment." While ride quality hasn’t been impacted to any noticeable degree, cabin noise levels certainly have. Managing testing editor Dave Beard isn't a fan of the racket, proclaiming, "These tires are already getting annoying. How soon can we install some BFGoodrich KO3s?"

So far, the Passport has made two trips to the dealer. The first was at around 6200 miles, when a TD02 code was triggered for a supposed over-the-air software update failure. Resetting the system resulted in a fresh S404 (update unavailable) code, for which the dealer had no solution. We're hoping a future software update will resolve the matter. The second stop was for an oil change, tire rotation, and rear differential fluid change at roughly 10,200 miles. Both services were free under Honda's one-year or 12,000-mile complimentary maintenance coverage, and we haven't encountered any other issues that would force us to fork over some dough.

Despite the abhorrent weather, our time with the Passport has been mostly sunny. Even faced with a particularly difficult challenge right out of the gate, our long-termer has proved its worth as a formidable hauler, a practical errand-runner, and a comfortable road-trip companion.

Since the Honda Passport was revived for the 2019 model year, it has racked up some significant Car and Driver accolades. Upon its return to the market, the Passport promptly conquered its contemporaries in a five-car two-row mid-size-SUV comparison test. After its strong showing, we invited a 2019 Passport EX-L to live with us for a while, where it proved to be a comfortable, practical, and (mostly) unproblematic companion for 40,000 miles. The Passport has also earned a spot on our Editors' Choice list every year since its revival, and now it has joined the ranks among the industry's best as a 2026 10Best laureate.

While the outgoing generation was certainly deserving of the praise it received, one thing was desperately missing from its arsenal: allure. The Passport had utility mastered, but its anonymous styling and drab interior made it as appetizing as boiled chicken. Honda has addressed that point with the new 2026 Passport, which now looks much more appealing with its brawny, cubist sheetmetal and enhanced capabilities. With it now more flavorful, we're going in for a second serving, inviting the Passport back for another 40,000-mile stress test.

When it came time to spec our new long-termer, we had an important decision to make. While every 2026 Passport comes with all-wheel drive, a 3.5-liter V-6, and a 10-speed automatic transmission, there's a sizable difference between the base RTL and the off-road-focused TrailSport. Plenty of C/D staffers aren't afraid to get dirty, so we decided to pull the trigger on a TrailSport Elite to ensure that no mud pit would go uncrossed.

While the Passport is still not quite as capable as some of the body-on-frame options in the segment, such as the Toyota 4Runner or the Ford Bronco, it's clear that tough-terrain capability was a top priority for the newest Passport. As with all other TrailSport models, our TrailSport Elite comes with plenty of gear to aid in our off-pavement antics, including skid plates that provide genuine underbody protection, an off-road-oriented suspension tune, a torque-vectoring rear axle within the all-wheel-drive system, easy-access recovery points that are rated for twice the Passport's weight, and 18-inch wheels wrapped in all-terrain tires. An integrated trailer hitch rated for 5000 pounds of towing capacity comes standard, as do amber daytime running lights, which are less about function but really add some unique character to the Passport's facia.

While we left the boxes on our long-termer's rather short options list largely unchecked (other than the one for Sunset Orange paint at $455), the TrailSport Elite is jam-packed with its fair share of luxuries as a premium trim in the Passport model hierarchy. Standard features include heated and ventilated leather front seats, a panoramic moonroof, heated side mirrors, a Bose audio system, a power liftgate, puddle lights, a heated leather 60/40-split rear bench seat, and a 12.3-inch infotainment touchscreen with wireless Apple CarPlay and Android Auto. Our final tally: $54,355.

Regardless of trim, the Passport is powered by a 285-hp 3.5-liter V-6 mated to a 10-speed automatic transmission. There is no other engine available. While the 2026 Passport's double-overhead-cam V-6 makes five more horsepower than the outgoing generation's 280-hp SOHC 3.5-liter V-6, the new model's additional weight obviates any gains made by the updated powertrain from an acceleration standpoint. The verdict on the Passport's gumption is split, with some noting that it feels quick enough and others musing if it feels underpowered.

Regardless of our subjective opinions, numbers don't lie, and our TrailSport Elite is much slower than the previous-generation Passport. At the test track, our new TrailSport Elite took 7.1 seconds to reach 60 mph—1.2 seconds slower than a 2024 Passport TrailSport that we tested. While a 7.1-second 60-mph time is far from blistering, it's still quicker than a nonhybrid Toyota 4Runner or a nonturbo Subaru Outback.

Lateral grip is one area where the new Passport made significant gains on its predecessor, with our TrailSport Elite circling the skidpad at a respectable 0.84 g compared with 0.74 g for the previous version. Its larger 275/60R-18 General Grabber A/T Sport tires (compared with 245/60R-18 Grabbers on the 2024 TrailSport), in addition to its suspension upgrades and a significantly stiffer chassis, can be mostly credited for the significant increase in grip between generations. Tipping the scales at 4702 pounds, our 2026 rig marginally improved upon its 4237-pound predecessor's braking performance, requiring 177 feet to come to a stop from 70 mph; the 2024 model took 181 feet to stop from the same speed.

The Passport didn't pull off any groundbreaking feats at the track, but its on-road comportment, well-fitted interior, and commodious cargo space have made it a growing favorite among the staff. The TrailSport Elite's muscular new look seems to be a hit—a stark contrast to the last generation, which had as much visual intrigue as a bowl of nonfat Greek yogurt. Several editors have also complimented its comfortable ride and agreeable, if somewhat dull, on-road dynamics. By far the most common praises have been about the Passport's roomy interior and luggage-eating cargo hold, spaces which feel almost geometry-defying within a mid-size-SUV envelope.

After a few months with the Passport, the collective outlook for our sun-kissed Honda is optimistic. As we venture deeper into what is turning out to be a frigid Michigan winter, our Passport will be subjected to grueling Midwest snowstorms, icy roads, and any wintertime shenanigans our editors can dream up. We'll check in again at the 10,000-mile mark to let you know what those shenanigans entail as the miles add up.
