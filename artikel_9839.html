Weird licenses and the new open source

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Somewhere on an IRC channel, I ended up in a conversation about the The Filthy Human Hands (FHH) License. It wasn't the first time weird licenses were discussed there: Reticulum's sparked some interesting discussions as well.

If you don't want to read the Wikipedia article on Free Software, a couple of years ago, I gave a small presentation at school as part of the "Oral communication in English" course, which is a (shoddy) cake-based explanation of the topic. Long story short, a Free Software is a software with 4 freedoms:

There is also the term Open Source, which is more or less Free Software but without the sociopolitical aspect of it, which is like a pizza without olives, or art without context: something devoid of meaning, a business-oriented product instead of a creation. Like in every sociopolitical endeavour, people sometimes disagree, leading to subtle but important variations. One of the most important one in the Free Software movement has been the question of the copyleft: should derivatives of Free Software be licensed under the same license? Some licenses, like the GPL says yes and this includes everything built on top of GPL code, the LGPL says that only code under LGPL needs to be released as LGPL, and others, called permissive licenses, like the BSD and MIT don't impose such restrictions.

The Free Software movement started in 1986, so we're around 40 years later now, and the picture is pretty bleak: technically, one could argue that it won, as every computer-using company in the world depends on Free Software one way or the other: Firefox, Linux, VLC, Chrome, Ubuntu, Apache, nginx, Wikipedia, Open Street Map, Open Food Facts, â¦ as well as more fundamental software components like curl, libxml, busybox, OpenSSL, GCC, clang, â¦

Yet virtually none of them do contribute back to those commons, some of them being thanklessly maintained by a random person in Nebraska. Worse, a handful of computer-related companies ("cloud providers") are making ungodly amount of money renting infrastructure running Free Software, most smartâ¢ devices are running Free Software, every single software producing/selling company depends on Free Software, and again, almost none contribute back meaningfully. A small number are trying to sponsor some projects they're depending on, but it often feels like a crass move for software that are maintained as a hobby: "Could you please garden a bit more efficiently, and add 150 apple trees to your small garden? We need it for our business, here is a 1000â¬ one-time payment"

With AI making everything worse, Free Software maintainers are now wasting their time dealing with unreviewable useless pull-requests, drowning into a sea of low-quality no-fix-provided bug reports, being demoralized by vibe-coded forks adding shiny features that are never going to be upstreamable and thus upstreamed. It really feels as if nobody is even contemplating making the pedestrian effort of trying to avoid murdering the golden goose.

Copyleft does help to mitigate those behaviours a bit, as companies aren't usually big fans to having to release their secret source code. The Asahi Linux project recently re-licensed its software from MIT to LGPL, to make it, if only symbolically, harder for people to profit from the labor without giving anything back. Unfortunately, there are a handful of tricks to bypass copyleft: don't use GPL as all, use strong boundaries (like Android is doing: Linux for its kernel, but everything running on top of it can have whatever license), rent the software to the users (every Cloud is doing that, and most ISP too), provide a context-free dump of the modifications and prevent users from modifying the code their devices are running, simplify ignore the license as the odds of somebody suing you are low, â¦

If copyleft isn't the answer, maybe it's time to find better licenses? In my opinion, the freedom 0, being able to run the program for whatever purpose, might be the crux of the problem. Reticulum's license has the following paragraph, because of course a decentralised secure communication network could help killing people more efficiently:

The Software shall not be used in any kind of system which includes amongst its functions the ability to purposefully do harm to human beings.

This sounds reasonable in theory, but it means that it's not Free anymore. Do we care? Well, Linux distributions usually have strict policies (debian, fedora, â¦) when it comes to what software they package, with non-Free ones being either forbidden, on living in their own not-enabled-by-default dedicated repository.

One step further is the The Filthy Human Hands (FHH) License, with:

No part of a work under this license may be used or reproduced in any manner by organizations or businesses with more than fifty (50) employees. An exception is granted to allow ISPs and other hosting services to reproduce the work for the purposes of display and distribution.

No part of a work under this license may be used or reproduced in any manner which promotes bigotry of any kind. This includes but is not limited to fascism, racism, sexism, misogyny, ableism, transphobia (including erasure and/or denial of nonbinary identities), homophobia, ageism, sane-ism (including denial of multiplicity and/or plurality), and discrimination against non-human identities.

What's interesting here, from a technical point of view, is that this isn't really enforceable, legally speaking, as most terms are pretty vague. I think it's by design, and must be taken as a statement. One could use any other open-to-interpretation terms and obtain the same effects, namely that any entity with a legal department won't use it, and those small enough to not have one won't be able to sell/rent to those who do. What's beautiful about it, is that it does fit perfectly into the idea of the small web, of small communities with bespoke governance, of bricolage and of excluding authoritarianism/capitalism/morons/â¦ by design.

Given the current dire state of the world, I think it's time to realise that Free Software isn't enough, and that not wanting to have code ending up in whatever war machine bombing kids and families is less radical than the idea of Free Software at its inception 40 years ago. I'd love a weird-license repositories in Linux distributions with lovely, quirky, useful, useless, handcrafted software!
