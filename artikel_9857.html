NIST: Solo Shouldn't Mean Less Secure

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Cybersecurity In-Depth: Feature articles on security strategy, latest trends, and people to know.

New guidance provides a basic framework for less-technical solo business owners, but it may miss the mark on how to secure technology that requires no human oversight.

Threat actors always target the path of least resistance, and for years that has been small to midsize businesses (SMBs). Now, new guidance from the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) aims to help companies with possibly even fewer resources: "solopreneurs."

Cybersecurity White Paper 50 (CSWP) is a series of recommendations on how to curb cyber-risks for a population that makes up 81.9% of all US businesses. NIST is trying to offset challenges with guidance for password hygiene, ransomware threats, and adhering to regulations requirements — areas where solopreneurs typically lack the time, money, or people to build out a security program on their own.

The guide translates its Cybersecurity Framework into lay language for non-employer firms, the agency states. But with 33 pages to work through, is it worth the investment?

Most of the practical core is within reach for solopreneurs, says Martin Zugec, technical solutions director at Bitdefender. People just need to scroll to the short list on page 2, which calls for phishing-resistant multifactor authentication (MFA), a password manager, a standard (non-admin) account for daily work, automatic updates, and tested backups with one copy kept offline.

"None of that needs a security background, and you can do it in an afternoon," Zugec tells Dark Reading.

Compared to other NIST technical publications, Zugec says the CSWP is much easier to digest. Like NIST advertised, the language is plain and every action comes with a reason, a first step, and a note on what changes as businesses grow, he adds.

NIST outlines how businesses can respond to growing contractual agreements and an increasing number of suppliers and third parties as they expand operations and it becomes even more crucial to know third-parties' cyber postures. That means staying current with regulations and establishing a formal process to manage risks that relationships could introduce.

While Bryson Byrd, cybersecurity advisor at Huntress, applauds NIST for the guidance, he says terms like "least privilege," "risk register," and "data sensitivity classification" aren't the language of someone running a bakery or a consulting practice.

"For too long, solopreneurs have been an afterthought in cybersecurity guidance," Byrd tells Dark Reading. "The intent is right, but the closer guidance like this gets to the language of everyday business owners, the more likely it is to actually get implemented."

But it's not just the language, Byrd says. It also assumes the owner knows how to turn on logging, review account sessions, and work though a separate spreadsheet of worksheets. And he points to a recommendation on obtaining legal counsel's input before notifying customers following an incident as an extra cost that often is not in the budget.

The guidance doesn't appear to take into consideration that "nobody goes into business to build the most secure company that exists," he adds, but to build something over time. Every hour a solopreneur spends on asset inventories, backup testing, tabletop exercises, and compliance tracking is an hour taken away from running the business.

"Asking a single person to do all of this is extraordinarily cumbersome and, quite frankly, unrealistic," Byrd says.

Byrd notes gaps in the guidance in places "we see hurt small businesses in the real world." The first is domain registrar; attackers regularly go after small business domains to launch impersonation attacks and siphon money from customers and partners. Control of the domain means threat actors assume control over email and the business's website.

"Locking down the registrar account with multifactor authentication, enabling registrar lock, and turning on auto-renewal should be part of any baseline for a solopreneur," Byrd recommends.

The second gap he addresses is email authentication. The three core protocols — Sender Policy Framework, DomainKeys Identified Mail, and Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting, and Conformance — are becoming more important to protect a business from being impersonated and to ensure its legitimate email is delivered, Byrd explains.

The third and perhaps most impactful gap for a business of one is what happens when the owner is unavailable for an extended period of time, whether due to an illness, family matter, or simply a well-deserved vacation.

Ransomware groups target businesses during off-hours, weekends, and holidays. If something does happen that demands an immediate response, it's essential for someone to be there. It could mean the difference between an incident and a breach.

"A break-glass plan that gives a trusted person or partner emergency access to critical accounts is still needed, and it belongs in any cybersecurity plan for a solopreneur," he advises.

Previously, SMBs and the self-employed adopted the mindset that they were too small to be considered a target. That has since been disproven and is only worsening.

Advancements in autonomous tools and artificial intelligence (AI) have lowered the barrier to entry and the cost of attacks, letting threat actors target that population at scale. They realized they had a choice: go after large enterprises with strong defenses for one big payout or target thousands of small shops with weak security that are far more likely to pay up.

As the struggles evolved, so must the guidance. Daniel Eliot, lead for small business engagement at NIST, says the new whitepaper comes nearly a decade after its predecessor, NISTIR 7621 rev.1. After the initial call for comments, "that feedback was instrumental in moving the project forward."

AI is forcing a lot of security guidance to be updated and making that guidance easier to write, says Zugec, adding that new CSWP additions include a section on AI and a warning that AI makes phishing more convincing.

NIST urges businesses to take a second or third look at any message that asks people to click a link, download a file, transfer funds, or log into an account.

But contrary to popular belief, AI hasn't brought any new or novel attacks, Zugec says. It is changing who runs businesses and what they rely on to operate.

Solopreneurs are using AI assistants to read their mailboxes, run commands, write code, and use accounts under the owner's identity, he explains. While it enables one person to do the work of a small team, it also expands the attack surface.

"Many solopreneurs will use agentic AI to grow faster than their security does," he says. "The guide gives no advice on what these agents should be allowed to reach or on securing the code they write."

The biggest gap Zugec says he found in the guide is "AI that acts." NIST treats AI as something passive that businesses need to inventory and review outputs for, but agentic AI has increasingly removed humans from the equation.

As agentic AI enables more people to run a business alone, the security of those businesses matters more than ever, Zugec says. That will warrant outside help, as people increasingly turn to vibe coding to build their own applications. He also urges solopreneurs to have their code reviewed by someone with coding and security experience before anything ships.

If anything is public-facing, an attacker can reach it. That warrants protection.

"AI is good at producing software that works but not necessarily software with a small attack surface," he says. "The guide doesn't cover this, yet it's often the first risk solopreneurs run into."

Zugec points out other weaknesses, too. For example, NIST pushed for continuous updates, but a solopreneur can’t realistically track every legal and contractual security requirement, negotiate security terms with a cloud provider, or monitor their systems around the clock.

Second, it never ranks tasks, which makes prioritization difficult for a one-person team. For example, it opens with governance, the least actionable task, while MFA and backup advice only starts on page 14. It also fails to cover two common attack routes: ClickFix, which tricks people into pasting a command themselves, and a malware that steals their logged-in session, which bypasses MFA

"It lists around 25 actions and says outright that it isn't a checklist, so a busy owner has to decide what comes first," Zugec says.

The guide also sets its scope by head count, which is problematic. Technically the head count would be one, but risk follows what information businesses hold and who targets them. But it's not just about size; it's about what's exposed. Risk heavily varies between someone who sells candles on Etsy as a side gig versus someone in the finance industry trading billions in cryptocurrency.

"A solo crypto operator or a consultant with client secrets needs far more than the guide’s baseline," Zugec says.
