Your client paid less than the invoice because they withheld tax. Here's how to record it

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Posted on Nov 19 • Originally published at ad.miniaccountant.app

You invoiced $1,000. The money arrives and it's $900. No note, no dispute, and the client's accounts team says it's "tax". If you work for clients in other countries, this happens sooner or later, and the first time it does it's easy to book it wrong.

This page explains what's going on in plain words and how to record it so your books still make sense. It isn't tax advice. Whether you owe anything, and whether you can get the withheld amount back or set it against your own tax, depends on your country and on any treaty between it and the client's country. That part is a question for your tax authority or an accountant.

Some countries make the payer, not you, collect part of the tax on certain payments. The client takes a slice off before paying you and sends that slice to their tax authority in your name. They don't keep it. In the IRS's own words, the person who does this is a withholding agent.

So the $100 isn't a discount and it isn't a bank fee. It's tax that was paid on your behalf, out of money you earned.

If your client is American and you aren't, the paperwork usually starts with Form W-8BEN. You fill it in and give it to the client, not to the IRS. The IRS instructions for Form W-8BEN say to give it to the person asking for it before the payment is made, and that without it the withholding agent may have to withhold at the 30% rate or the backup withholding rate.

Where does 30% come from? The IRS page on NRA withholding describes it as the general rule for US-source income paid to a foreign person, and says a lower rate, or none, may apply under a tax treaty between your country and the US. That's what the treaty section of the W-8BEN is for.

The word that does the work there is US-source, and it surprises people. The IRS's summary of source rules puts the source of pay for personal services where the services are performed. Royalties for copyrights and similar property are sourced where the property is used. So, roughly:

If the client did withhold, they report it on Form 1042-S. The Instructions for Form 1042-S say it must be filed with the IRS and furnished to the recipient of the income by March 15 of the following year. That copy is your proof of how much was withheld. See also the IRS page About Form 1042-S.

If a US client took 30% off and you think your treaty rate is lower, or that the income wasn't US-source at all, raise it with the client and your adviser. Don't fix it in your books by pretending the money never existed.

The US isn't special here. In India, a client deducting TDS gives you a Form 16A, the TDS certificate for income other than salary. In the Philippines, the certificate is BIR Form 2307, the Certificate of Creditable Tax Withheld at Source. The names change and the logic doesn't: the client pays part of your tax, and hands you (or should hand you) a certificate that proves it. Your own tax authority's site is the place to read what the certificate means for you.

Write the $100 off as a discount. Now your income says $900. But you earned $1,000, and the certificate the client files says so too. Your records and theirs disagree, and the tax that was paid for you has vanished from your books.

Leave the invoice as partly paid. It sits there as $100 overdue forever, and a month later you're chasing a client who already paid in full.

Record $900 and move on. Same problem as the discount, without even a note explaining the gap.

The version that holds up has three parts: the invoice is closed, income is the full $1,000, and the $100 is visible somewhere as tax withheld, next to the certificate that backs it.
