Apple fires back at Epic over claims it withheld 122,000 documents

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

Apple has responded to Epic Games’ filing from last Friday, pushing back against accusations that it improperly withheld more than 122,000 documents related to its proposed App Store commissions. Here are the details.

While the Supreme Court is set to review whether the civil contempt designation against Apple should be upheld in its lawsuit against Epic Games, the two companies have also been battling in the district court over what commission, if any, Apple should be allowed to charge on purchases made through external payment systems.

Apple submitted its proposal to the district court on August 13, calling for commissions ranging from 5% to 15%, but Epic is disputing not just the basis for those proposed rates, but also Apple’s handling of document production in the proceedings.

As part of the proceedings, Epic had submitted 16 categories of documents it wanted Apple to produce (including “internal analyses of the financial or business impact” of its proposed commissions and “communications relating to any strategies to dissuade developers from offering link-outs”), along with a list of current and former Apple employees whose records should be searched.

Apple agreed to use Epic’s proposed search terms and include all employees, and began producing documents it determined were responsive to Epic’s requests.

Epic, however, said last Friday that Apple was improperly withholding more than 122,000 documents matching those search terms from the records of the seven initial employees, and asked the court to order Apple to produce all matching non-privileged documents, regardless of its relevance assessments.

Which brings us to Apple’s newest filing.

In its filing, Apple argues that contrary to Epic’s claim that its proposed search terms were “tailored” to the proceedings over link-out fees, they were actually “overbroad,” capturing large numbers of unrelated documents.

For instance, Apple notes that while intended to identify documents related to its proposed commission rates, Epic’s “commission*” search term also “captures documents that refer to government and private commissions, as well as documents hitting on the totally irrelevant term ‘commissioner,’ such as documents discussing the NFL Commissioner.”

“[…] the use of a wildcard in a search string is designated by the asterisk symbol, which means that the search string will hit on any term that includes the prefix before the asterisk.

In the same vein, Apple notes that “Epic’s use of ‘fee*’ in its search strings related to litigation and Apple’s link-out-related projects pulled in documents containing words like ‘feel,’ ‘feed,’ ‘feedback,’ ‘feet,’ and ‘feeble’ without the word ‘fee’ or ‘fees’ appearing at all.”

Apple’s overall argument is that these unrelated matches account for the vast majority of documents identified by Epic’s searches, and it cites legal precedent to argue that it should only be required to produce documents responsive to Epic’s requests, rather than every document matching the agreed-upon search terms:

In fact, at least 95% of the Initial Custodian Document Population hit on generic terms like these and did not include any reference to the term “link-out” or its variations, despite that concept being central to the Proffer and related discovery. These sweeping terms do not just increase the total volume of the Initial Custodian Document Population, they also captured highly sensitive and irrelevant documents, such as Apple employees’ personal tax or financial records, medical records, documents discussing unannounced products, documents containing passwords, investor-relations materials, and privileged or protected legal communications.

The law is clear: parties are only required to produce responsive documents, not every document returned by a search string. […] (“The Court will not compel defendants to produce any document simply because it contains a search term whether or not it is responsive to the discovery request.”). Epic has not shown, and cannot show, why the regular discovery process should be disrupted here. Speculation based on hit counts, particularly where the producing party can explain low responsiveness rates, does not justify the extraordinary and unprecedented remedy Epic seeks.

Apple goes on to argue that complying with Epic’s demands would require extensive additional work, including reviewing documents for privileged or sensitive information and redacting protected material, making it impossible to meet Epic’s proposed December 2 deadline.

Finally, Apple asks the court to reject Epic’s motion but proposes that, should the court decide that some intervention is warranted, a randomized, statistically significant sample of the documents it deems nonresponsive be submitted to a Special Master for independent review, rather than producing all of them to Epic directly.
