AI technology identified teen’s bag of Cool Ranch Doritos as gun, lawsuit claims

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Updated: In 2025, artificial intelligence technology used by the Baltimore County Public Schools to screen for weapons identified a bag of chips as a gun, resulting in local police cuffing and searching students, according to a lawsuit filed Tuesday by the Lawyers’ Committee for Civil Rights Under Law.

Besides the county, the police department and the school district, Omnilert, which made the technology, is named in the Baltimore County Circuit Court complaint, filed Nov. 17.

A spokesperson for the police department said in a statement it could not comment on pending litigation, and the school district told the ABA Journal that it did not have a comment. It did reference an update shared with its school community after the incident happened.

According to the update, an Omnilert alert was issued at 7:04 p.m., and following a review by school safety personnel, it was canceled at 7:06 p.m. At 7:17 p.m., the school principal “contacted the school resource officer (SRO) and reported the alert. Because this alert was received after school hours, the SRO called the local precinct,” according to the update.

All the plaintiffs were Black teenage boys, and they were waiting for one of their moms to pick them up from football practice, according to the complaint. Taki Allen, one of the youths, was eating a bag of Cool Ranch Doritos, and the Omnilert technology, which scans school security camera footage for firearms, identified the bag as a gun, according to the suit.

At least six police cars arrived, some officers exited their vehicles with guns drawn, and they ordered the students to their knees. The youths were handcuffed and searched without being told what they were suspected of, according to the suit.

No weapons were found, and one of the police officers reportedly muttered, “stupid AI cameras,” according to the complaint.

And another officer, when he left, reportedly told the students, “Hey, thanks for not running away, that would have been a real problem.”

“We need a reckoning on how AI is being used, especially concerning public safety and kids,” said Damon Hewitt, the president and executive director of the Lawyers’ Committee for Civil Rights Under Law, in a Nov. 17 news release.

Additionally, the suit alleges that some of the officers made light of the situation, with texts describing what happened, followed by laughing emojis. Causes of action listed in the complaint include unreasonable search and seizure, false arrest and imprisonment, gross negligence and improper conduct.

A spokesperson for Omnilert said they are “truly sorry” for what the students and their families experienced. According to the company, the Maryland inspector general for education investigated their system and found no bias.

“Our system is built to send anything that could be a firearm to trained people for review because missing a real gun is the worst possible outcome. In this case, the school’s designated safety staff reviewed the alert and canceled it within about two minutes, and everyone who received it was notified. That is exactly why human review matters and why a canceled alert must stay canceled,” the spokesperson said.

Updated Nov. 19 at 11:44 a.m. to include a response from Omnilert. Updated Nov. 19 at 1:05 p.m. with more information from the school district.
