Forget Taylor Sheridan, This is the Master of the Modern Western

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

Westerns have continuously trickled down in numbers as a genre for film and TV through the decades. While neo-Westerns seem to get a lot of traction, with a filmmaker like Taylor Sheridan having a filmography filled almost completely with entries in the genre—including his epic American Frontier Trilogy—there are few traditional Westerns that still get released to a wide audience. An argument could be made that there isn't much of a market anymore, or maybe that a lot of people just don't like Westerns, but that's about as much a blanket statement as saying you don't like action movies or chick flicks. That's fine if you don't like Nicholas Sparks knockoffs, but if you besmirch The Princess Bride or When Harry Met Sally by lumping them in with that sort, you best prepare to have a donnybrook on your hands. Rather, when it comes to traditional Westerns, short of homages or parodies, very few directors seem to have a deep desire to make them. That is, of course, except for the man with no first name given, and that's S. Craig Zahler.

The character actor of directors, S. Craig Zahler continues to work under the radar of general audiences, even though the movies he's made have been widely well-received by critics. He's a writer/director with a succinct style—bordering on niche—that's recognizable for its lockdown visual style, methodical pace, and engrossing, stage play-like dialogue, contrasting against viciously brutal violence and inhuman villains. All of this was established with his debut film, Bone Tomahawk, which is about a group of cowboys in the Old West, led by Sheriff Hunt (Kurt Russell), working to save the town doctor, Mrs. O'Dwyer (Lili Simmons), from savage, cannibalistic troglodytes. To put it into context, if you've ever heard the Mark Twain quote where he compared masterful writing to wine, and his to water...well, Zahler's movies are like the film-making equivalent of a White Monster Energy Drink (i.e. not for everybody, but the ones who like them will REALLY like them).

Aside from Bone Tomahawk, Zahler has also quietly flexed his Western skills by both being the uncredited screenwriter of last year's underrated Frontier Crucible, and by being a prolific novelist, with his two most famous being A Congregation of Jackals and Wraiths of the Broken Land, which, combined with Bone Tomahawk, are thematically linked to make a twisted Western trilogy. Wraiths of the Broken Land will also forever live as a "What could have been" scenario since it was announced back in 2016 to be adapted by Ridley Scott and Drew Goddard, but has since languished in the fires of Development Hell. Presumably because of how dark the novel gets and Zahler's distinct style being hard to sell to general audiences.

If spaghetti Westerns were considered subversions of traditional Westerns, Zahler's stories are subversions of spaghetti Westerns. Zahler's biggest strength as a storyteller is his ability to craft his stories into twists and turns that you're not expecting, and to go hard with wherever the story leads him. His method is to write each day until he shocks himself with what comes out, working from the idea that if he surprises himself while writing, the audience won't see it coming, either. That's probably how he came up with the horrid things that can be done with fishhooks, dead baby turtles, rifle barrels heated over an open fire, and everything about the "Wishbone" scene in Bone Tomahawk.

There's a blunt harshness to his action and violence, and especially the depiction of life on the frontier. He doesn't play favorites, and isn't afraid to shock and abhor his viewers or readers under the guise of telling the stories the way he wants to tell them. Sometimes, that means the lead characters are just as cruel as the villains, and almost without fail, that means the villains are pitiless and verge on being inhuman entities. It's like Zahler read Blood Meridian before writing A Congregation of Jackals and thought Judge Holden was too affable and normal looking, so he came up with Quinlan and his gang. And then he put Blood Meridian down because Cormac McCarthy's lack of quotations and speech tags around dialogue can be hard to follow sometimes . . .I'm guessing.

It's a different kind of Man With No Name.

What makes Zahler's stories so good is that even though their content might be too disturbing for some, his characters are consistently very fleshed-out and captivating to follow. And for every morally gray character, there are a few, like Sheriff Hunt in Bone Tomahawk and Merrick Beckford (Myles Clohessy) in Frontier Crucible, who feel like they could've come right out of a John Ford Western just for how noble they are. And in some cases, what actually makes the more disturbing things even more upsetting is it's happening to characters you like. Bone Tomahawk in particular is interesting, because—unlike something like From Dusk Till Dawn, which does a complete genre flip from crime film to vampire movie—it gradually devolves from a traditional-looking Western into a cannibal horror film. Almost as if the characters are traveling from one genre and entering into another (which is made clearer through the changing landscapes).

S. Craig Zahler is a unique writer and filmmaker who's able to tell stories across different mediums, but one never usurps the other when it comes to quality. When it comes to his takes on Westerns, the stories feel uniquely his own, but they don't seem like he's trying to deconstruct the genre or emulate the style with a modern twist (or have them take place in a modern setting). They feel like stories that he wanted to tell that just happened to take place in the Old West and didn't feel the need to go the way of paying homage.
