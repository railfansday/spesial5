A Grocery Price Book Shows Which Sales Are Real – Track Your 20 Regular Buys

Start a grocery price book with the 20 or so items you buy most and what each costs at every store you use. Six to eight weeks of entries show the lowest price each one reaches and how often it comes back. Stock up when a sale hits that price, and pass on the ones that don’t.

A sale tag tells you the price dropped, not whether it’s as low as that item gets.

Federal Trade Commission economists who studied supermarket prices found that stores tend to keep a regular price on each item, and that most changes from it are price drops. They also found that the same items go on sale again and again, while others rarely, if ever, do.

A price book records that pattern for the things you buy. For each item, the lowest price you’ve logged is your buy price, and the book shows how many weeks pass between its sales. Skip a sale that stays above your buy price, since you’ve seen the item cheaper. For an item that seldom goes on sale, the book shows which store charges least.

Set it up in a notebook, a spreadsheet, or a notes app, with one line for each of the 20 or so items you buy most. For six to eight weeks, check each item’s price at two or three stores and log its unit price, the cost per ounce, pound, or count, so you can compare different package sizes. Note the date and whether the price was a sale. Where a sale price needs a digital coupon from the store’s app, log the price you’d pay with the coupon.

At the buy price, stock up with enough to last until the item’s next sale, but no more than your pantry and freezer hold or you’ll use before it goes bad.

Say your household goes through a bag of coffee every two weeks, and your book shows it dropping from $12.49 to $8.99 every six weeks at one store. Three bags bought at each sale last until the next one, so all 26 bags you go through in a year cost $8.99 instead of $12.49, a $91 saving on coffee alone.

Start with your last two grocery receipts, which already list most of your 20 regular buys and what you paid for each.
