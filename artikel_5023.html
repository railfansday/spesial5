Bitcoin ETFs shed $4 billion — How did one fund hide the cracks before leading the exit?

U.S Spot Bitcoin ETFs lost $4.14 billion over three weeks, with BlackRock’s IBIT accounting for roughly 84% of the withdrawals. However, the same fund had been concealing weakness elsewhere just weeks ago. The story behind the numbers is considerably more complicated than the headline.

In the week leading up to October 19, U.S Spot Bitcoin ETFs appeared to be doing rather well. The funds collectively attracted $301.2 million in net inflows. That’s quite a reassuring development for a market that had become accustomed to treating ETF demand as a barometer of Bitcoin’s institutional appeal.

There was one small complication, though. BlackRock’s iShares Bitcoin Trust (IBIT) had brought in $565.7 million on its own. Every other fund combined lost $264.5 million. So, how healthy was that $301.2 million gain if it depended entirely on one fund?

Three weeks later, the answer became harder to ignore.

Between November 2 and 20, Bitcoin ETFs suffered a staggering $4.14 billion in net withdrawals, according to Farside Investors. By the way, IBIT ALONE accounted for approximately $3.49 billion, or 84% of the exodus.

And the selling gathered pace as the week wore on. Nearly $1.79 billion left in the final week, around 1.7 times the previous week’s withdrawals. From November 17 through November 20, each trading session ended with a larger outflow than the previous one.

October’s biggest source of support had become November’s biggest source of pressure. The reversal was striking enough. In fact, what was happening beneath it was more revealing!

BlackRock’s influence on the industry’s performance was hardly a one-week curiosity.

The pattern continued into October 26–30. IBIT attracted another $176.9 million while competing funds collectively lost $379.3 million. Even though the industry finished that week in the red, BlackRock’s inflows effectively absorbed nearly half the damage.

Spot BTC ETFs recorded roughly $643 million in withdrawals in a single session, with IBIT contributing approximately $234 million. The fund that had been cushioning the industry’s losses was now participating in them.

November made that reversal unmistakable.

IBIT accounted for approximately 94% of the industry’s net outflows during the first week, 85% during the second, and 76% during the third. Curiously, its share declined even as the third week brought the industry’s biggest outflow yet.

That was the more consequential development. Other funds, including Fidelity’s FBTC and products from Grayscale and ARK, were contributing increasingly to the selling. What had initially been concentrated overwhelmingly in BlackRock’s product was beginning to spread.

A market once held up by its largest fund was now being dragged down by it, with competitors increasingly joining the retreat.

Now, IBIT was already by far the industry’s biggest fund. Perhaps its dominance in the withdrawals was simply a consequence of its size. Hence why that possibility deserved a closer look. However, a closer look at this position revealed another complication.

A fund holding most of the industry’s assets will naturally account for a significant share of its movements. A billion dollars leaving a $60 billion product is hardly equivalent to the same amount leaving one worth $5 billion.

By October 30, IBIT held approximately $62.7 billion in Bitcoin, according to iShares holdings data. Looking at it broadly, the Bitcoin ETF market held roughly $98.14 billion, based on SoSoValue.

BlackRock therefore controlled about two-thirds of the industry’s assets entering November. And yet, it accounted for 84% of the subsequent three-week net withdrawals.

The disparity only becomes sharper when adjusted for size.

IBIT’s $3.49 billion outflow was equivalent to approximately 5.6% of its starting holdings value. For all the other funds combined, the equivalent was just 1.8%.

In relative terms, BlackRock’s fund experienced roughly three times the withdrawal pressure of its competitors. Its scale explained part of its dominance, but hardly all of it.

The underlying Bitcoin inventory tells a similar story. IBIT held approximately 811,817 BTC on October 30. By November 20, that had fallen to roughly 760,926 BTC, a reduction of nearly 50,900 BTC or 6.3%.

Bitcoin’s declining price could reduce the dollar value of those holdings, but it could not account for the disappearance of the coins themselves. IBIT’s inventory had genuinely contracted.

The departures were measurable. The identities of those departing were another matter.

A $4 billion ETF outflow makes it tempting to declare that institutional investors are abandoning Bitcoin. Yet the available data cannot distinguish an asset manager reducing exposure from an individual investor selling shares, or a market participant unwinding a hedged position.

Nor does it establish whether the capital left Bitcoin altogether or found another route back into the market. That uncertainty matters because the industry has grown comfortable treating ETF inflows as evidence of institutional conviction. Applying the opposite interpretation to withdrawals may be tempting, but it is no more conclusive.

What November demonstrated was a substantial withdrawal of capital from these investment products, led disproportionately by BlackRock’s IBIT.

Whether it represented a broader loss of faith in Bitcoin was a considerably larger question though.

And while investors were heading for the exits, there seemed to be little indication that the fund itself had stopped functioning as intended.

There is an understandable tendency to interpret large ETF outflows as a sign that the investment product itself has somehow failed. However, IBIT’s trading data tells us a more measured story.

According to iShares, IBIT closed at $33.02 on November 20 against a net asset value of $33.05, a discount of about 0.10%. Its 30-day median bid-ask spread was 0.02%. In other words, the fund traded almost exactly at the value of the Bitcoin it holds, even as markets were falling.

Trading was heavy. About 109.2 million IBIT shares changed hands on November 20, more than twice the fund’s 30-day average of about 50.1 million.

That is not the same as money leaving the fund though because trading volume is not net flow. A 30-day median spread also doesn’t show that trading stayed cheap at every volatile moment.

Still, nothing here suggests investors were fleeing a broken product. They were trading a working fund whose underlying asset had dropped sharply. IBIT’s NAV fell $2.08 (5.92%) on November 20 to $33.05, and its NAV total return for 2026 stood at −29.06% as of November 19.

Wall Street made Bitcoin easier to buy. That was never the same promise as making it safer to hold. That distinction matters for the question that comes next — how much of the fall in ETF assets was money leaving, and how much was the price?

According to SoSoValue, combined Spot Bitcoin ETF assets reached approximately $110.8 billion on October 5. By November 20, they had fallen to roughly $71.3 billion.

A decline approaching $39.5 billion sounds extraordinary beside three weeks of approximately $4.14 billion in net withdrawals.

However, assets under management represent the market value of what these funds hold. That value changes both when investors create or redeem shares and when Bitcoin itself rises or falls.

Using Coinbase Bitcoin prices recorded by the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis, Bitcoin fell from approximately $85,821 on October 5 to $58,831 on November 20 — A fall of 31.4%.

Over the same period, reported ETF assets fell by approximately 36%.

Such a comparison strongly suggests that Bitcoin’s price decline accounted for much of the reduction in ETF asset values. It is not a precise decomposition — flows occurred throughout the period, holdings changed, and the dates and valuation methodologies must be matched before assigning an exact proportion to each driver.

Still, the broad conclusion is difficult to miss.

Tens of billions of dollars can disappear from reported fund asset values without an equivalent sum being withdrawn by investors. A falling portfolio value and a redeemed investment are two different financial events. Treating them interchangeably gives a dramatic headline considerably more authority than it deserves.

Bitcoin’s ETF market was not the only place seeing withdrawals either.

From November 9 to 13, U.S Spot Ether ETFs lost about $398.9 million, according to Farside. BlackRock’s iShares Ethereum Trust (ETHA) accounted for about $311.1 million of that, or 78%. The following week, Ether funds lost about $306 million, with ETHA responsible for about $230 million or 75%.

That’s the same pattern we saw in Bitcoin — one BlackRock fund carrying most of the headline outflow.

Stablecoins told a slower story. According to DefiLlama, their combined market capitalisation was about $295.3 billion on October 30. It fell to $290.2 billion by November 6, $285.6 billion by November 13, and $281.3 billion by November 20. That is a contraction of about $14 billion, or 4.8%, in three weeks.

Unlike the ETF flows, which peaked in the final week, stablecoins shrank at a fairly steady pace — about $5.1 billion, $4.6 billion, then $4.3 billion a week.

Stablecoins are the dollar-pegged tokens traders use for settlement, trading balances, and collateral. So, a falling supply suggests liquidity may be tightening beyond the regulated ETF market. The two trends overlapped, but they moved at different speeds.

Bitcoin’s own trading schedule adds a complication too.

FRED’s Coinbase series showed Bitcoin falling from about $68,178 on November 13 to $62,891 on November 14, a drop of 7.8%, after that week’s ETF sessions ended.

However, after the much heavier withdrawal week ending November 20, Bitcoin went the other way. It fell from about $58,831 on November 20 to $60,992 on November 22 — A gain of 3.7%.

Two weekends prove nothing on their own, and FRED’s prices are taken at 5 p.m Pacific, not at the ETF close. But they do show that a record ETF outflow and a falling price don’t move in lockstep.

There is no need to minimise the withdrawals. About $4.14 billion in three weeks is a major reversal, particularly when the final week was the worst of the three.

Nor should IBIT’s concentration be brushed off as a consequence of its size. Its 84% share of the net outflows exceeded its roughly two-thirds share of starting industry assets.

However, context changes the interpretation.

SoSoValue’s November 20 figures still showed about $52.8 billion in cumulative net inflows since January 2024. That is down roughly $4 billion from where November began, but nearly three years of accumulated inflows were not erased by three difficult weeks.

October offers a useful warning about reading any headline in isolation. The same fund that masked withdrawals elsewhere in two October weeks went on to account for most of November’s exits. The industry totals recorded both developments accurately, yet told very different stories depending on whether you looked at the funds behind them.

The ETF structure broadened access to Bitcoin. It also gave investors a familiar, liquid way to reduce exposure. Neither is a guarantee about Bitcoin’s price.

Perhaps institutions were cutting risk. Or, retail investors may have been heading for the exits. Others could simply have been unwinding complex positions that had little to do with long-term conviction in Bitcoin. All three are plausible explanations.

What they do establish is narrower but more useful — the withdrawals were substantial, heavily concentrated in one product, and increasingly shared across competing funds as November went on.

So, the next time a billion-dollar ETF outflow makes the headlines, the interesting questions begin after the headline. Which funds accounted for it? How large were the withdrawals relative to their assets? Is the number money leaving a product, or is the market value of its holdings falling?

And one more, which no flow table can answer — Who was actually selling?
