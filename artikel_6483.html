When eco-friendly advertising backfires: More green claims can make a product less appealing

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

Advertising both eco-friendly ingredients and sustainable packaging can lower a product’s appeal among shoppers who care less about buying green.

A product made with eco-friendly ingredients and sold in sustainable packaging sounds like an easy sell.

But a new study suggests that advertising both benefits can make it less appealing to some shoppers.

People who care a lot about buying green rated a product higher when its ad mentioned both. People who care less rated it higher when the ad stuck to just one.

At least with these ads, more green information wasn’t automatically more persuasive.

Miyuri Shirai, a professor at Keio University, tested this in three experiments with 1,729 people in Japan. The products were made up for the study, and people rated ads instead of buying anything.

Soap, cereal, detergent and other things people buy over and over are called consumer packaged goods. A growing number of them are sold as better for the planet.

In 2023, 18.5% of these goods sold in the US were marketed as sustainable, according to the NYU Stern Center for Sustainable Business. That’s up 4.8 percentage points from 2015.

McKinsey & Company and NielsenIQ found that products with sustainability claims grew their sales by 28% over five years, compared with 20% for products without those claims.

Growth like that leaves companies with a choice. They can make the product itself greener by changing what goes into it or how it’s made. Or they can work on the packaging, using better materials, a new design or a package that can be recycled.

Sustainable packaging gets a lot of attention because these products are bought and thrown away so often. All that packaging uses up raw materials and energy and adds to pollution.

Doing both often costs more and makes production harder. So it helps to know whether shoppers actually reward the extra effort.

At first glance, two green features should beat one. Researchers call this the more-is-better pattern, where the more good things an ad says about a product, the better people tend to rate it.

A much older finding suggests otherwise. In a 1992 study, Daniel Kahneman and Jack Knetsch looked at how much people were willing to pay for environmental services.

People tended to value something less when it came as part of a bigger package than when it was judged on its own. This is called the embedding effect, and it’s where Shirai’s question started.

“My interest in this research began when I read articles about the embedding effect, which suggest that adding more positive information does not always make an option seem better,” Shirai said.

“I became curious about whether communicating multiple sustainability benefits might sometimes be less effective than highlighting just one, depending on the consumer,” she added.

At Keio’s Faculty of Business and Commerce, Shirai studies the psychology of how shoppers judge products. For this project, she recruited people through Freeasy, a Japanese online survey panel.

Each person saw one ad for a made-up product. Some ads talked only about what the product was made of, others only about the packaging, and the rest about both.

People then rated the product. Shirai also measured how much each person cares about buying things that are good for the environment, which researchers call green consumption values.

The first experiment used laundry detergent. Of the 750 people recruited, 124 failed a check meant to catch careless answers, which left 626.

The second used organic granola, a food that’s widely sold and already seen as sustainable.

Switching from detergent to food helped test whether the pattern held across different product categories.

This time, 600 of the 750 recruits made it through the same check.

Body wash was the product in the third experiment, which included 503 people. Those people also answered extra questions to help explain why they reacted the way they did.

In all three experiments, the results came out the same way.

People who cared more about green buying rated the product higher when the ad covered both the product and its packaging than when it covered only one.

People who cared less did the reverse. They rated the product higher when the ad stuck to a single feature.

That second group’s response was consistent with the embedding effect.

For them, one green feature may have seemed less impressive when it came bundled with another.

For the first group, Shirai had a different explanation. It’s hard to tell how green a product really is from an ad or a label, so people look for small clues.

For someone who cares a lot about the environment, details about the product and details about its package may work as two clues that back each other up.

“Emphasizing sustainability in both the product and its packaging may not always be the most effective strategy,” she said.

In the body wash experiment, people also rated how useful they found the green information and how sustainable they thought the product was.

Both ratings were linked to how people felt about the product, and those links depended on each person’s green values.

The analysis couldn’t show which came first, though, so this is a possible explanation rather than proof.

There’s one catch in how the ads were built.

The ads that mentioned both the product and its packaging had four green statements, while the single-focus ads had two each.

So the results can’t show for sure what people were reacting to. It could have been the number of claims, or whether the claims covered one feature or two.

Everyone in the study was in Japan, and participants rated fictional products without spending real money.

Whether tailoring ads to shoppers’ green values would increase sales or benefit the environment remains untested.

The full study was published in the Journal of Environmental Psychology.
