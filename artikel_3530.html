As MAGA Melts Down, MAHA Is Also Breaking Apart—Starting With the Moms

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

In August 2024, Robert F. Kennedy Jr. suspended his presidential campaign and endorsed Donald Trump, bringing his ragtag bunch of supporters—crunchy mamas, anti-vaxxers, and conspiracy theorists; testosterone bros, peptide enthusiasts, and people who put a “COEXIST” bumper sticker on their car in the 1990s—along for the ride. But now that the blue wave or tsunami—pick your tortured water metaphor—has come to pass, the fractures within MAHA threaten to break the movement apart, driven by bitter disappointment with the Trump administration.

“I think we are seeing a fracturing of the MAHA movement as those concerns start pulling people in different directions,” says Sandro Galea, dean of the Bursky School of Public Health at Washington University in St. Louis. Groups that are interested in healthier food are not necessarily the same as groups opposed to vaccines, he points out; it’s not a sure thing that those groups will continue to find common cause.

The crack-up didn’t start with the midterms, though the fact that most MAHA-backed candidates lost certainly accelerated it. Earlier this year, Trump had to withdraw his nomination of MAHA darling Casey Means for surgeon general after her stance on vaccines made it impossible for Republicans to get enough votes to support her confirmation. In May, MAHA ally Marty Makary, the commissioner of the Food and Drug Administration, resigned, reportedly because of pressure from the administration to authorize fruit-flavored vapes. And in October, Calley Means—Casey’s brother—resigned from his high-level post under Secretary Kennedy at Health and Human Services, with MAHA outrage on unmet promises growing.

Three days after the midterms, Joe Rogan released a bomb-throwing, names-naming, two-plus-hour interview with Calley Means, in which the former Kennedy adviser used the word “demonic,” possibly in the literal sense, eight times.

The big takeaway was that Susie Wiles seemed to have told Kennedy to go sit in a corner and play by himself. All roads to Trump went through her office, and according to Means, she slow-walked, watered down, or outright quashed many MAHA priorities. According to Means, she did this primarily because of her and her family’s financial ties to lobbying on behalf of big business.

Wiles did not respond to a request for comment, but a spokesperson for Wiles previously told Axios that these allegations were “outrageous, defamatory, and provably false.”

Means argued that MAHA managed some wins: Trump signed an executive order to boost research on the use of psychedelics for treating mental health conditions. The new food pyramid instructs Americans to eat more steak than oatmeal. (In this economy?) There have been other MAHA victories too: The administration made it easier for Americans to get a handful of unproven injectable peptides, including the “Wolverine stack.” Trump signed an executive order in support of regenerative farming. The administration requested changes to the labels on testosterone products in an effort to make the hormone easier to prescribe—good news for Pete Hegseth’s promise of a “high-T Department of War.” It asked food manufacturers to voluntarily phase out some food dyes. It has sought to reduce the number of recommended childhood vaccines.

Means did not cite the United States recently losing its measles-elimination status as a win, though that was also a result of MAHA talking points.

The MAGA-MAHA mind meld provided plenty for the wellness bros, but precious little of what MAHA moms were expecting from their alliance with Trump. Tiffany Cianci, a high-profile MAHA advocate who goes by @thevinomom on Instagram, says the results were “small things,” not the massive systemic changes that had been promised.

In an emailed statement, a spokesperson for HHS pushed back on this narrative, writing, in part, “HHS has delivered the most sweeping public health reforms in modern history…Across the federal government, the administration has transformed food and nutrition policy, invested more than $1 billion in regenerative agriculture and farm modernization, and taken action to address harmful chemical exposures.”

Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. delivers remarks during an event at HHS in August. The event highlighted a series of 'landmark actions' to improve the nation's food.

For the MAHA moms, there are two original sins of Trump 2.0. The first is the administration’s outright support for glyphosate, an herbicide that has been classified as a probable carcinogen by a World Health Organization agency. The second is its failure to put out a definition of ultra-processed foods, which the movement sees as the first step toward curbing those foods, particularly in school lunches. One was never released; Means alleged to Rogan that ultra-processed-food initiatives such as this were stymied by White House officials including Wiles, who he said told him, “I need sugar money.” Reporting found that food CEOs leaned on the White House to make the definition go away.

A White House official said on background that the definition for ultra-processed food remains a priority and is actively being worked on by relevant agencies. White House spokeswoman Allison Schuster said that “the MAHA coalition has found a champion in President Trump…The administration has already secured extensive victories for the MAHA agenda, including overhauling the dietary guidelines for Americans, axing artificial ingredients in our food supply, and investing in regenerative farming.”

However, the Trump administration has arguably hindered research on ultra-processed foods in other ways. A leading scientist who was studying ultra-processed food’s link to diabetes and obesity took early retirement from the National Institutes of Health last year after NIH officials, he said, censored the reporting of some of his study results because the study didn’t prove the hypothesis of ultra-processed-food addiction as they had hoped it would. If he’s to be believed, one of our best hopes for understanding the health risks of ultra-processed foods exited due to the actions of MAHA officials. (Vanity Fair has reached out to HHS for comment on the allegations of censorship; an HHS spokesperson previously told The New York Times, “NIH scientists have, and will, continue to conduct interviews regarding their research through written responses or other means. We remain committed to promoting gold-standard research and advancing public health priorities.”)

“We do have a food supply that’s nearly 70% ultra-processed food,” says Jessica Knurick, a registered dietitian and science communicator on social media. She counters that many of MAHA’s alleged priorities are aligned with the hopes of public health workers and nutritionists—yet the movement writ large has eroded trust in the very professionals who do that work. “They really want fewer environmental toxins. If you want that, you need strong regulation on corporations,” she says. “They voted in an administration that is decisively deregulatory.”

“All of the things that the MAHA movement claimed to have wanted,” she adds, “this administration has legislated in the exact opposite direction.”

See, for instance, the glyphosate debacle. Instead of curbing the herbicide, something for which he’d long been an advocate, Kennedy got on board with Trump’s support of it, including an executive order that actually prioritized production of the chemical under the bizarre guise of national security. Then the administration supported Roundup weed killer manufacturer Monsanto, now owned by Bayer, in a case before the Supreme Court, which resulted in a decision shielding the company from state-level lawsuits brought by those who believed they’d been harmed by the glyphosate-containing product. (Glyphosate-based Roundup products are no longer produced for the US residential market.)

This flew in the face of everything MAHA believed about Kennedy’s priorities. Vani Hari, a MAHA influencer who goes by the Food Babe, has been called both the Jenny McCarthy of food and a Democratic infiltrator. She thinks that the last nearly two years resulted in some positive change. She also tells me that she’s become somewhat disillusioned: “There have also been deeply disappointing decisions, particularly when it comes to glyphosate and pesticides.”

Despite it all, the HHS secretary continues to be deified by the vast majority of MAHA leaders, who don’t blame Kennedy for these setbacks. Cianci, however, blames just about everyone involved. “What I see within MAHA right now is a fracturing into one very, very small sliver that loves the proximity to power and the fundraising. They unfortunately tied their hitch to the wrong horse, which is partisanship,” she says. “And you have the rest of us that actually want our kids to be healthy.”

Supporters of the Make America Healthy Again movement are seen at a news conference on removing synthetic dyes from America's food supply, at the Health and Human Services Headquarters in Washington, DC, 2025.

If there is a road forward for MAHA, it might be best exemplified by Elizabeth Frost, a community health advocate and the leader of MAHA Ohio. She comes by her skepticism of the pharmaceutical industry honestly, after growing up in Appalachian Ohio during the height of the opioid epidemic. She now runs a political consultancy in the state, which she says works with both parties.

Beginning last year, Frost partnered with epidemiologists from Yale to find out how the people of East Palestine, Ohio, were still being affected by toxic exposures after the 2023 derailment of a train filled with hazardous materials. Frost went to bat for the scientists, lending her local grassroots credibility to their study on water quality in the town.

“I think there is a broader anti-corporate movement in America that hasn’t really been given a home with either established party because both parties have their own pet corporate interests,” she tells me. After Kennedy’s ascent, she had hoped for a focus on the MAHA issues that enjoy broad support. “Vaccines are not the most popular issue even for MAHA. Most people in MAHA would prefer for the majority of time to be spent talking about issues that are a little bit more unifying”—rather than something like Steak ’n Shake’s now using beef tallow for its french fries. “It’s just not where people’s hearts are.”

Speaking of no heart: Stephen Miller has been promoted to chief of staff after Wiles was forced out in the wake of the midterms. Both Calley Means and Secretary Kennedy have celebrated the move. “Stephen Miller is the strongest MAHA supporter in the administration. It’s time to unleash the Kraken,” Kennedy wrote on X.

But some MAHA moms are not so sure about the kraken. Cianci says that within MAHA, “people are very, very, very uncomfortable” with Miller. “If you’re just going to do what you’ve been told to do and take it to the extreme—he’s like the human embodiment of the paper clip problem,” she says.

Frost is similarly concerned about how things might go under Miller. “I don’t expect that on environmental exposures, industrial contamination, a strong streak against corporate influence—I doubt we’re going to get those things,” she says. She is also dismayed by MAHA leadership’s embrace of convicted rapist Russell Brand. “What the hell are we doing?” she wonders.

A person familiar with MAHA thinking who spoke on background counters that while it’s fair to say that Miller might be polarizing, he is also not beholden to corporate interests the way Wiles was, making him far less likely to block the scrutiny of pesticides and healthy food policies.

Cianci, Frost, and Hari all hope that MAHA will survive by focusing on policy over partisanship. “I also believe MAHA’s future must be bipartisan,” Hari wrote in an email.

Cianci sees opposition to data centers as the next big issue on which MAHA can lead. And she hopes that the anti-corporate tilt she sees in MAHA will spread. “We had a record number of candidates get elected to Congress this year that turned down all corporate PAC money. That is indicative of the MAHA movement. That is indicative of anti-corruption. And I think they’re going hand in glove.”

Frost would like to see Republicans work with progressives like Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez on shared goals, like getting pesticides out of the food supply. She wants legislators to realize that “the way to hold their seat is to work across aisles on issues that their constituents care about.”

Perhaps MAHA can reinvent itself by leveraging some of its most popular issues to make common cause with Democrats. After all, 30% of midterm voters told pollsters that they support the MAHA movement, while many more—the vast majority of Americans—say that food additives and pesticide use should be more regulated. A plurality of Americans do not want data centers in their communities. The movement’s best hope for staying relevant is probably the (currently very pissed) MAHA moms, whose concerns about food and pollution were always the most broadly resonant parts of the MAHA platform—rather than a yen for peptides, beef tallow, or testosterone.

Most people want to be able to afford healthy food and effective health care. Trump’s rule has not made those things more accessible, with high cost of living, massive cuts to Medicaid, the ending of certain Affordable Care Act subsidies, and reductions in federal funding for SNAP benefits.

The challenge with a rebrand is that the connection to MAGA is right there in the name. But Trump’s presidency clearly has a shelf life, and MAHA wants to outlast him. “If anyone has any ideas on how to change the name of a political movement,” Frost says, “let me know.”
