Weekend Warm-up: Did Bayern Munich do the right thing in signing Jamal Musiala and Alphonso Davies to massive deals?

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

There is an uncomfortable question hanging over Bayern Munich's recent contract strategy — did the club make the right investments in Jamal Musiala and Alphonso Davies, or did it allow the fear of losing two exceptional talents to dictate financial decisions that could eventually become difficult to justify?

Both players received lucrative, long-term extensions in February 2025. At the time, Bayern Munich viewed them as essential building blocks for the future. Musiala was arguably the most exciting young attacking talent in German football, while Davies remained one of Europe's most sought-after left-backs. Losing either player, particularly on a free transfer, would have been a significant sporting and financial setback. At the time that each signed, it was reported that neither had a serious suitor.

But there was another consideration that could not be ignored — neither player had an entirely clean injury history. Bayern Munich was committing enormous money to two footballers whose availability had already become a recurring concern.

That concern has become considerably harder to dismiss since the contracts were signed. The numbers from Transfermarkt provide some perspective on why Bayern Munich's decision deserves scrutiny. Its seasonal injury-history summaries include both days unavailable and matches missed, although the records also include some non-injury absences, such as illness, quarantine, and fitness-related issues.

Musiala's injury record became increasingly concerning during his final two seasons before the new contract and immediately afterward. A hamstring injury in November 2023, recurring muscle problems, and a torn muscle bundle in April 2025 interrupted his progress. Then came the devastating broken fibula sustained at the Club World Cup in July 2025, which kept him out for 174 days and 31 matches.

Davies' record is arguably even more concerning in terms of cumulative absences. His 2021/22 season was interrupted by heart-related problems, while his 2024/25 campaign ended with an anterior cruciate ligament tear suffered on international duty. That injury alone cost him 221 days and 39 matches.

Davies subsequently experienced additional muscular problems, making his return to consistent availability another challenge.

These figures do not mean either player is destined to remain injury-prone. They do, however, demonstrate that Bayern Munich was not unaware of the availability risks when it committed to these contracts. In Davies's case, the question about investing that much into the left-back position for a player with that kind of injury track record is likely causing a lot of internal Monday-morning quarterbacking.

The reported salaries make the debate considerably more interesting. According to Sport Bild, the estimated annual salaries for the two players are listed below:

Musiala: More than €25 million per year, including potential bonuses. Per Capology, Musiala's potential bonuses total €12,450,000.

Davies: Up to €20 million per year, including potential bonuses. Per Capology, Davies' potential bonuses total €5,000,000.

Those are reported figures, not officially disclosed salaries. There is also a distinction between base salary and performance-related compensation. Capology, which publishes estimated salary data, currently puts Musiala's gross fixed annual salary at approximately €22.83 million and Davies's at €15 million, excluding bonuses. As you can see, there are some discrepancies because figures are not publicly released as you might see in North American sports.

That distinction between salary and total financial package matters. The headline figures are not necessarily guaranteed annual payments, and performance incentives can make a significant difference. Some recent salary coverage also suggests Musiala's maximum compensation is linked to playing-time thresholds. The exact contractual structure has not been officially confirmed.

There is another particularly interesting detail from the latest Sport Bild reporting (via @iMiaSanMia) — both players reportedly negotiated special provisions for continued salary payments during extended injury absences. Musiala's protection allegedly extends to six months, while Davies's is reportedly limited to three or four months. Bayern is also said to have taken out additional insurance to mitigate the financial exposure.

In other words, Bayern was not simply agreeing to pay two players enormous salaries. It was reportedly agreeing to additional financial protection even if injuries kept them away from the pitch for extended periods.

There is a very good argument that Bayern could not afford to lose either player in 2025.

Musiala was 21, already a Germany international, and had developed into one of Europe's most exciting attacking midfielders. He was producing goals, assists, and moments of individual brilliance while becoming increasingly central to Bayern's identity. He was also approaching the final year of his previous contract.

The danger of allowing a player of that caliber to enter the final year of his deal without an extension was obvious. Premier League and La Liga clubs were interested, and Bayern risked either losing him for nothing or being forced into a sale on unfavorable terms.

The BBC reported that Musiala had been granted a release clause as part of his new agreement, but the overall decision to retain him was understandable. Bayern needed a player around whom it could build its next generation.

Davies presented a different but equally difficult situation.

At 24, he was an established Champions League winner with extraordinary pace, athleticism, and experience at the highest level. He was also attracting considerable interest from elsewhere, particularly Real Madrid, and was approaching the end of his existing contract.

Losing a player of his quality without receiving a transfer fee would have been a significant blow. Finding a comparable replacement would also have required a substantial investment.

Bayern Munich ultimately secured both players through 2030. That gave the club long-term security, protected the value of two important assets, and avoided the possibility of watching them leave for nothing.

There was also a symbolic component. Bayern wanted to demonstrate that it could compete financially with Europe's wealthiest clubs and retain its most valuable young players. Letting Musiala or Davies walk would have sent precisely the wrong message.

The strongest argument against the contracts is not that Musiala and Davies were undeserving of significant pay raises. Both had demonstrated their value. It is that Bayern may have negotiated as though retaining them was more important than protecting the club against foreseeable risks.

Musiala's talent was never in question, but his availability was already becoming an issue. His muscular problems during 2023/24 and 2024/25 should have prompted serious consideration of how much guaranteed money Bayern was willing to commit.

Davies had an even longer history of interruptions, including the heart-related problems in 2022 and subsequent muscular injuries. Bayern knew it was investing in a player whose availability had been inconsistent.

There is a difference between paying a premium for exceptional ability and paying a premium without adequately accounting for the possibility that the player cannot consistently deliver that ability.

The reported injury-protection clauses make this especially interesting. They suggest the club recognized the risks during negotiations but was nevertheless willing to accept additional financial obligations to secure the signatures.

There are also broader concerns about Bayern's wage structure. Reports in 2025 and 2026 have described internal criticism of Max Eberl's negotiating approach, with some supervisory board members reportedly believing that the club was too accommodating and agreeing to unnecessarily expensive contracts. Musiala and Davies have been among the examples cited.

That criticism becomes more understandable when considering how many other players have subsequently received substantial extensions or entered the highest salary brackets.

Bayern Munich cannot have every important player earning €15–25 million annually without creating significant pressure elsewhere in the squad. It also makes future negotiations with players such as Michael Olise considerably more complicated.

There is, however, a danger in judging these decisions exclusively through the lens of injuries that occurred afterward.

Bayern did not sign two ordinary squad players. It secured two footballers with the potential to define the club's next generation.

Musiala offers qualities that are exceptionally difficult to replace. His dribbling, creativity, ability to operate between the lines, and capacity to produce something from nothing make him a unique attacking weapon. When healthy, he changes the way Bayern plays.

Davies provides a similarly unusual profile. His speed, ability to recover defensively, and willingness to attack space give Bayern tactical options that few left-backs can offer.

Replacing either player would have been expensive, and there was no guarantee a replacement would deliver comparable performances.

There is also an important distinction between an injury being unfortunate and a club making an inherently bad investment. Musiala's broken fibula and Davies's ACL injury were serious setbacks, but neither could have been predicted with certainty when the contracts were negotiated.

Furthermore, both contracts run until 2030. Bayern is not evaluating these investments over a single season. There is still considerable time for both players to deliver the returns the club anticipated.

The question is whether they can establish the consistency required to justify those commitments.

One could argue that Bayern Munich was right to prioritize retaining Musiala and Davies. Allowing either to leave in 2025 would have created enormous sporting problems and potentially cost the club even more money in replacement transfers.

However, there is a legitimate argument that FC Bayern also overpaid, particularly when considering the players' existing injury histories and the additional protections reportedly negotiated into their contracts — plus the fact that neither might have had an actual suitor pursuing them.

Musiala earning more than €25 million annually is a massive commitment for a player who has struggled to maintain uninterrupted fitness. Davies earning up to €20 million is similarly difficult to justify if his availability continues to fluctuate. Due to questions about Davies's ability to stay healthy, Bayern Munich also had to acquire a high-end backup in Nathaniel Brown, who did not come cheap.

The important distinction is that neither contract was necessarily a mistake when signed. The danger is that Bayern Munich may have underestimated how much availability would influence their value.

Ultimately, these decisions will be judged by what happens between now and 2030. If Musiala becomes the world-class centerpiece Bayern Munich believes he can be and Davies returns to being a consistently available elite left-back, the investment will look considerably more sensible.

If the injuries continue, however, Bayern Munich could find itself paying superstar money for players who cannot consistently provide superstar performances. The point remains — Bayern Munich was probably right to keep both players. Whether it was right to pay quite so much to do it is an entirely different question.

While the project has been bandied about for years, it looks like it will come to fruition this time. During an appearance on The Dan Patrick Show on Thursday, director Peter Farrelly confirmed that the project was moving forward, but offered very little else on it. Peter would be reuniting with his brother Bobby for this project, as they worked on the original together.

Kingpin, of course, was a slapstick, somewhat dark comedy about bowling, a couple of degenerate pro bowlers, an Amish fella, and a lot of hijinks. The plot revolves around a washed-up former bowling prodigy who tries to reclaim his glory by mentoring a naive Amish prodigy.

In 1979, rising bowling star Roy Munson (Woody Harrelson) is hustled by his rival, Ernie "Big Ern" McCracken (Bill Murray), leading to a confrontation where Roy loses his right hand. Seventeen years later, a down-on-his-luck Roy discovers Ishmael Boorg (Randy Quaid), a sheltered Amish man with an extraordinary natural talent for bowling. Roy convinces Ishmael to leave his community and enter a $1 million tournament in Reno, Nevada, hoping the prize money will save Ishmael's family farm — and revive Roy's career — if they can beat Big Ern along the way.

Naturally, the biggest question about the sequel is whether the original cast members will return (which is not a certainty). There have not been any announcements on that as of yet.
