Amid recent banking outages, should you start carrying cash again? Top advisors weigh in

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

For years, Americans have moved away from using physical U.S. currency in favor of credit, debit and digital payments. But amid the recent outages affecting some consumer banking services, experts say it's wise to revisit cash.

JPMorgan Chase on Monday reported a technical issue that disrupted some consumer banking services, with ripple effects extending to several major U.S. banks and the peer-to-peer payments network Zelle. A day later, a separate outage affected Apple, temporarily disrupting Apple Pay and iCloud, the service that keeps data available across various Apple devices.

Those reports came on the heels of separate, widespread issues with Microsoft Outlook and communication systems used by air traffic control centers.

As technology outages expose vulnerabilities across parts of the U.S. economy, some top advisors say consumers should reconsider keeping physical money on hand.

"This is about preparedness, not panic," said David La Placa, CEO of Intellectus Partners in San Francisco, which ranked No. 92 on CNBC's Financial Advisor 100 list for 2026. "I think keeping some cash at home is a reasonable starting point for many households."

La Placa advises consumers to "build redundancy" into their financial lives.

"Maintain access to at least a second financial institution, keep a backup credit card from a different issuer and make sure you have more than one way to access liquidity," he said.

A client's cash management should be diversified, said Seth Fierston, a certified financial planner and co-president of Fierston Financial Group in West Hartford, Connecticut. The firm ranked No. 22 on CNBC's Financial Advisor 100 list.

Having multiple accounts at brokerages or banks and various ways to move money, including Automated Clearing House, or ACH, wire, check, Venmo or Zelle, "can help mitigate technology risk," Fierston said. "One of our guiding principles is flexibility."

When it comes to everyday transactions, Americans are increasingly relying on digital forms of payment.

These days, only about 46% of Americans carry cash with them always or most of the time, according to a survey of nearly 10,000 adults conducted between May and June by the Pew Research Center, a nonpartisan research group.

Roughly 42% of U.S. adults said they don't use cash for any purchases in a typical week, up from 24% in 2015, Pew found.

Young adults are the least likely to carry physical currency. Among adults under 30, 51% said they go completely cashless, according to the survey.

"I recommend to my clients that they always keep three days of average cash spend on hand given the uncertainty of life in general," said Tim Lootens, CFP, managing director at Chilton Capital Management in Houston, which ranked No. 7 on this year's Financial Advisor 100.

Considering the recent technical troubles, Lootens said consumers should keep even more cash at the ready.

"We've had the Microsoft Outlook outage, the FAA ground shutdown and now banking disruption," he said. "This makes me think five to seven days may be a better number."

The right amount is as psychological as it is financial, he added. "The ability to access cash and having comfort knowing it is there when needed is so important," he said.

Brian Vendig, president and chief investment officer at MJP Wealth Advisors, which ranked No. 42 on CNBC's Financial Advisor 100, also said he recommends holding a dollar amount that corresponds with a household's discretionary living expenses. He advised having enough for at least one to two weeks, depending on their needs.

While there is little precedent for a banking or payment system outage lasting more than a few business days, Vendig said, a one- to two-week cash reserve provides a "reasonable buffer" for essentials such as groceries, gas and other necessities, if electronic payments become unavailable.

"We apply this range because it is also an emotional concern for most people, and they might want to have a little extra saved at home due to the size of their household and having a little extra 'peace of mind' guarantee," he said.

Vendig said he recommends stashing extra cash in a secure location at home for emergency needs.

Vendig also said consumers should be vigilant in ensuring their electronic devices have the most recent software security patches installed.

Criminals will try to exploit moments of uncertainty, experts say.

"Consumers should assume they will be targeted," said Craig Timm, senior director of anti-money laundering at ACAMS, an anti-financial crime group.

"[This week's] reported bank outages are likely to cause widespread confusion and fear among consumers about losing access to their money," he said. "Criminals will try to exploit that anxiety by pushing victims to take urgent action to protect their funds or restore account access."

As always, "stay alert, never click on links in unexpected bank messages and never share personal information such as passwords or PINs," he advised. "If you need to reach your bank, use a trusted, official channel," Timm said.
