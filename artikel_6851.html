I Went to the World's Biggest Furniture Trade Show—These 6 Trends Are About to Be Everywhere

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

The furniture profiles, patterns, and palettes you’re about to see everywhere.

Twice a year, High Point, North Carolina, becomes the home industry’s giant showroom, where furniture and design brands unveil new collections for retailers, interior designers, and buyers to see and source. At House Beautiful, we’re your boots on the ground, looking for the ideas that may travel from showroom to store—and into your home next.

This fall, Market made a case for decorating with more character and muddier hues: green moved from the forest floor to the showroom floor, florals claimed entire sofas, and upholstery played with everything from a flash of leg to a full fabric wrap. Add wood-backed furniture as an accent, garden-inspired lighting, and a generous swish of fringe, and the message was clear: The details are ready to take center stage.

This year, green was the unofficial color of Market. Benjamin Moore and Behr had already put the hue on the radar with their 2027 Colors of the Year: Webster Green, a confident mid-tone balancing blue’s coolness with yellow’s warmth, and Grounded, a mossy olive softened by earthy brown undertones. Then came the showroom echoes. Versions of green, from earthy vegetable shades to deeper, moodier tones, appeared nearly everywhere. Kathy Kuo even created a “Music Room” (shown in our opening image) for Rowe Furniture inspired by the verdant shade. The appeal is easy to understand: Green brings the natural world indoors, lending a sense of calm and grounding a space without draining it of personality.

The design industry’s obsession with prints is now a love letter to botanicals. Florals erupted across Market in a myriad of scales, from small, almost sun-bleached botanicals to overscaled, tapestry-adjacent blooms. Even the tiniest prints made a surprisingly bold statement when carried across an entire sofa; the larger blooms brought the feel of a faded heirloom into full view. Both have that grandmotherly, another-era quality, but the impact is anything but timid. Yes, it’s a major design commitment, but why play it safe with a solid, barely-there pattern? It may not be for the faint of design, but we are all in a sofa fully dressed in flowers.

Here’s a furniture detail that changes the whole silhouette: Brands are letting wood take over the outside back—or sides—of upholstered seating. Baker’s sofa makes the move hard to miss, with a broad wood shell framing the piece. It’s a little like “inside-out” upholstery, where the outside gets its own treatment, but here the wood gives the piece architectural shape and presence. As floor plans open up and sofas float in family and living rooms, the back is no longer hidden against a wall. It’s part of the view, and part of how the seating area takes shape, making it all the more important that it feels worth looking at.

Upholstery continues to take its cues from fashion, with a focus on mixing silhouettes: Mini skirts that let those gams show; full, ballooning skirts with all the best parts of an ’80s prom dress; and what we lovingly call body-con upholstery, where the entire chair is wrapped head to toe in one fabric.

The appeal goes beyond a good fashion metaphor, though. Each treatment changes the chair’s proportions: a flash of leg keeps it light, a generous skirt adds volume, and an all-over wrap turns both seat and base into one continuous form, with no leg finish to choose or worry about. Each has its own distinct style POV, making your shopping choices that much more complicated, but in the best way.

If garden motifs aren’t your thing, don’t look up. The floral revival isn’t limited to upholstery and wallpaper; it’s reaching the ceiling, too. Visual Comfort & Co.’s Fairoaks collection, designed by Fisher Weisman, includes a chandelier, sconce, and mini flush mount. Sculptural branches and twigs cleverly disguise the chandelier’s ceiling cord, preserving the romance of the design. At Hudson Valley Lighting, a tulip-inspired chandelier brings a more literal bloom overhead. Both offer a way to bring the garden indoors through shape and silhouette, without committing an entire room to pattern.

Sometimes, all it takes is a little bullion fringe. The thick, tightly twisted cords fall in a dense, weighty row, adding plush texture and a satisfying swish to a skirted chair or sofa. It nods to the Victorian parlor, but stops short of period-drama excess; against a cleaner silhouette or in a spare room, the old-world trim feels playful and unexpectedly fresh. Consider it the design equivalent of a great pair of earrings: a little extra, in exactly the right place.
