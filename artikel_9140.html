Frieze Abu Dhabi Debuts to Brisk Sales and Confident Dealers

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

On the morning of Frieze Abu Dhabi’s inaugural edition, Saudi Arabia’s financial center in Riyadh was reportedly attacked by Yemen’s Iran-backed Houthi rebels. The crowd in Abu Dhabi, at least, had other things on their minds: the fair was about to open, with millions of dollars’ worth of art ready to be sold. Members of the royal family were back after getting a sneak peek yesterday, alongside politicians and celebrities including tennis star Venus Williams and architect Kulapat Yantrasast.

“It’s about time. Frieze is bringing its A-game to Abu Dhabi, where they have the best museums,” Yantrasast told ARTnews, cheekily mentioning that he’s been putting dots on artworks from The Third Line, Taymour Grahne Projects, Experimenter and Pearl Lam Projects. The dealers were in high spirits too, as booths started to get busy an hour after the opening. At Thaddeus Ropac, three of Imran Qureshi’s Water Bodies sold for $60,000, each to a UAE-based collection, though the gallery declined to specify whether this was a private or institutional collection.

“There is a great energy around the fair in Abu Dhabi this week. We’re having good conversations with collectors who are well-informed about the artists we are presenting, and you can feel the impact of the institutional scene here,” Xenia Geroulanos, director at Thaddaeus Ropac, told ARTnews.

The fair is under the umbrella of Frieze and has rebranded from Abu Dhabi Art fair, and attendees are noticing a difference. The exhibitor list has been streamlined to 89, down from 142 last year. Dealer Asmaa Al-Shabibi told ARTnews that when she walked through the fair’s new entrance, it felt “a little bit like going into Frieze London, and it feels like a different fair.”

Samy Ghiyati, co-founder of art advisory NG Partners, agreed. “The quality is definitely higher, and galleries made an effort to bring very good inventory. Usually fairs in the region are slower and sales happen over the course of several days,” he told ARTnews.

One of Ghiyati’s clients placed three orders on pieces ranging from $10,000 to $400,000. Within an hour of opening, dealer Sundaram Tagore had already placed two works on hold, one by Hiroshi Senju and another by Chun Kwang Young, ranging from $40,000 to $400,000. By late afternoon, Timothy Taylor had sold its entire solo presentation of paintings by Hilary Pecis to institutional and private collections in the region and internationally, to the price of $65,000-$205,000. The London-based antiquities gallery David Aaron sold a 2,000-year-old funerary stele for a seven-figure sum to a private collector, while Dubai-based Taymour Grahne Projects also sold out its booth of mosaic works depicting 70s-era architecture in Abu Dhabi.

Regional collectors reportedly spent big at Frieze Abu Dhabi. Iris Projects sold two works by Shamsa Al Omaira, a sculpture acquired by a new foundation in the UAE for $32,000 and an acrylic on canvas by Juma Al Haj for $20,400. UAE-based collectors also bought two ceramics by Eman Al Hashemi, priced at $17,000 and $15,000, an oil pastel on linen by Dhabia Al Qubaisi for $10,500, and a photograph by Ammar Al Attar for $12,500.

By then, Lawrie Shabibi had sold seven pieces by artists Hamra Abbas and Shaikha Al Mazrou, priced from $10,000 to $120,000. “People are recognizing the work when they’re coming here,” said the gallery’s cofounder Asmaa Al-Shabibi, referring to a new client who bought a work by Al Mazrou, who has a solo exhibition at the Cultural Foundation.

Rayan Ben El Mlih, sales and marketing manager at Waddington Custot, told ARTnews that a large contingent of collectors with reserves hailed from Abu Dhabi, Dubai, Qatar and Bahrain. The gallery brought works by Etel Adnan, Sophia Vari and Fabienne Verdier.

Dealers noted that local collectors, some of whom began their collections by acquiring Western artists, are now deepening their collections with more historical pieces by regional artists.

“We’ve been seeing European collectors diversifying their collections, too,” said Ghiyati. Dubai’s Carbon 12 sold a painting and a sculpture by 28-year-old artist Sarah Al Mehairi, as well as two works by Mexican artist Edgar Orlaineta, priced between $17,570 and $35,000 (€16,000-€32,000).

“We’ve engaged mostly with collectors that we’ve cultivated relationships with for a while, and this is our home fair,” Judy Karkour, associate director of Carbon 12, told ARTnews.

Collectors are branching out beyond SWANA this year, with notable interest in art from the African continent and its diaspora and Latin America. Meanwhile this year’s Frieze Masters section is dedicated to trade across the Indian Ocean, taking its cues from the Louvre Abu Dhabi’s current exhibition “Spices and Wonders: Sailing the Indian Ocean.” An hour into the fair, Efie Gallery had placed two works by Malian artist Abdoulaye Konaté on reserve, both priced between $45,000 to $65,000, in addition to two works by Cuban artist Maria Magdalena Campos Pons ($27,500-$55,000).

“Frieze Abu Dhabi has got off to a strong start for us with a high volume of enquiries in the first few hours from both institutions and private collections,” Kwame Mintah, cofounder of Efie Gallery, told ARTnews, which specializing in artists of African descent. “One of the more encouraging developments across the Gulf has been the gradual maturation of its collecting community, accompanied by a growing appreciation for artistic practices beyond established geographical and institutional frameworks,” he added.

At Daniel Crouch Rare Books, a private collector bought the first map to name Abu Dhabi, dated to 1832. While dealer Daniel Crouch declined to say how much the map sold for, he priced the booth’s total value between $10,073 and $1.19 million (AED 37,000 to AED 4.4 million).

“Abu Dhabi is becoming a cultural hub in the region with a lot of development,” Crouch said. “It’s the logical place to come to for dealing in the larger Indian Ocean region and the Louvre Abu Dhabi exhibition has attracted quite a lot of attention—we’ve obviously jumped into it with alacrity.” London-based antiquities gallery ArtAncient, carrying pieces ranging from $5,000 to $5 million, sold a collection of shipwreck jewels for an undisclosed sum—the gallery also had a visit from Venus Williams, who was admiring the Graeco-Roman mummy portrait Portrait L, which had recently been dubbed the “Mona Lisa of the Middle East.”

Throughout the opening, the blue-chip-leaning powerhouses, including Gagosian, Pace Gallery, Thaddaeus Ropac, and Victoria Miro, boasted crowded booths. Gagosian’s dedicated Roy Lichtenstein booth became the step and repeat for many visitors—the gallery said it “placed many” orders without revealing specifics.

Judging by its debut, Abu Dhabi has become a no-brainer stop on the art fair calendar. The emirate is slowly integrating into the art circuit. Earlier this month, Christie’s hosted its first fine art auction there, with Sotheby’s own version due next month, joining a colorful lineup of events that includes the Formula 1 Abu Dhabi Grand Prix, Abu Dhabi Finance Week and Culture Summit Abu Dhabi.

Several dealers cited the Guggenheim Abu Dhabi’s upcoming December opening with the strong pull to participate in Frieze Abu Dhabi, characterizing the museum as a bridge between West and East.

“The Guggenheim is going to open now in the next few weeks, it’s literally where contemporary art gets space to breathe and to grow,” said Milo Gatti, fair coordinator at Galleria Continua.
