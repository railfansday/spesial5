Building Confidence From Failure

There is something about summits that enthralls the human spirit. We build careers, companies, and identities around reaching our personal peaks or the summits of our industries. We put mountaintops on posters and gaze longingly at their beauty and remoteness. Yet in life, as in mountaineering, the summit may be the goal, but it is never the real story. Few people understood this better than Tenzing Norgay.

Tenzing grew up in the shadow of the Himalayas, herding yaks in the Khumbu, the craggy highlands below Everest. The mountain that loomed over his childhood—known to the locals as Chomolungma—would come to define his life.

As a teenager, Tenzing ran away to Darjeeling to look for work with the foreign expeditions that used the Indian city as a staging post for their journeys into the Himalayas. He started as a porter and rose to become a sirdar, the head Sherpa who recruits and leads an expedition's support team. In all, he spent nearly two decades carrying loads in pursuit of other people's dreams.

Inevitably, many of these foreigners were drawn toward Everest, and so Tenzing found himself working his way up the mountain again and again. He joined his first Everest expedition in 1935. In 1952, alongside the Swiss climber Raymond Lambert, he came within a few hundred meters of the top. The next year, on his seventh attempt, he finally reached the summit with the New Zealand climber Edmund Hillary.

Tenzing was by then in his late 30s. After nearly 20 years on the mountain, the difference between one more failed attempt and the chance to stand at the highest point on Earth came down to a single morning's climb. In truth, the journey had taken a lifetime.

In the West, people spoke of the conquest of Everest. The mountain had not simply been climbed. It had been overcome, taken possession of by adventurous humans on an expedition mounted, significantly, by a great European empire. Tenzing, a devout Buddhist, knew better.

Chomolungma means "Holy Mother," and one does not conquer one's mother. Sherpas ask her for permission to climb, and when they succeed, they give thanks. When he reached the summit with Hillary, Tenzing's celebration was characteristically modest. He buried a few small offerings in the snow, among them sweets and a pencil his daughter had given him. For Tenzing, a mountain was not something to be overcome. One should approach it, he said, “as a child climbs onto the lap of its mother.”

It is easy to assume that Tenzing's humility and deference left no room for the striving ambition of the Western adventurers he so often accompanied. But it is a mistake to think that modesty and ambition are incompatible.

Those other people's dreams were also Tenzing's own. He wrote that climbing Everest was what he had wanted most in his life, and six times the mountain turned him back.

For some people, that repeated experience of falling short of a deep personal goal would have undermined their confidence and self-belief. But failure does not have to be damaging. What matters is how we choose to interpret it. People with resilient confidence treat a failure as a learning experience, as evidence of a gap in their effort, knowledge, or skill set that can be closed. People who treat a failure as a verdict on who they are rarely learn anything from their setbacks except how to condemn themselves.

This is where Tenzing's way of holding his ambition mattered. If a mountain is an enemy to be conquered, then every retreat is a defeat, and six defeats amount to a judgment. If a mountain is a mother whose permission you ask, then a retreat means only that permission has not yet been given. Tenzing came down in 1952 knowing the route, knowing his own body at 28,000 feet, and knowing that the summit could be reached. He went back the following spring and asked again.

I grew up in Bangladesh, on the flat green delta where the great rivers of the Himalayas meet the sea. The water that fed our fields began as snow on those peaks, and Darjeeling, where Tenzing made his home, lies only a short distance beyond our northern border. For a boy on the delta, the mountains were a horizon, close enough to imagine yet too far to reach. Tenzing's story was part of that horizon: a poor boy with no formal schooling who kept going back until the mountain let him through.

I left Bangladesh at 17 and arrived in America with very little. In the late 1990s, I was building a startup, and like Tenzing, I teamed up with people who had the means to get me up the mountain. The venture capitalists who backed me saw the market as something to be conquered, and conquered quickly. They pushed for expansion at a rate the company could not sustain, and I went along with it, even as the pace forced compromises to the vision I had started with. Eventually we fell out, and I was pushed out of the business I had built. Over the next several years, I watched it fall apart.

For a long time, I treated that failure as if it was a defeat. It took me years to learn to view it in the same way Tenzing viewed his retreats from Everest. Tenzing had spent 20 years climbing beside people who spoke of conquest without ever taking on their view of the mountain. I had taken on my investors' view of the market and let it shape my own approach to business.

When I started again, my ambition was intact, and I still wanted to reach the top. What was different was how I climbed. Where I had once seen building a company as a battle to be waged against outside forces, I now saw it as a conversation with myself, with my teams, and with the markets and clients I hoped to serve. My part in that conversation was to ask and then to listen, even when the answer was no. Tenzing's mountain turned him back six times before it let him through. Mine have turned me away many times since, but I know now how to return.
