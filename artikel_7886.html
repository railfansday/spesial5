Some foods are fine to share in small amounts, while others can cause anything from an upset stomach to life-threatening illness. Knowing which is which makes it easy to include a dog in the day without risking an emergency.

Turkey is safe and healthy for dogs, with a few conditions. The skin is fatty and usually coated in bastings and spices, and too much fat can lead to obesity and pancreatitis. Turkey bones can splinter and damage a dogâs mouth and digestive tract, so they should be avoided. Seasonings, including salt, can cause toxicity, so processed turkey should be skipped too. Plain meat is the safe choice, with no sauces or gravy.

Plain pumpkin is considered healthy, which is why so many dog foods contain it. Canned or fresh both work, provided the canned kind isnât pre-seasoned and nothing has been added. Pumpkin pie, with its spices and sugar, is a definite no.

Sweet potatoes are full of nutrients and vitamins, but they should be cooked and served in small amounts, without marshmallows, seasoning, or butter. Green beans offer vitamins, fiber, and manganese. They can be steamed, raw, canned, or cooked, as long as they are plain and not part of a casserole.

Corn is healthy without butter or salt, but only if it's been taken off the cob. Corn cobs are a relatively common source of intestinal blockage in dogs. Carrots, celery, and peas are fine cooked or raw and unseasoned, and carrots and celery should be cut into bite-sized pieces to prevent choking.

Plain cranberries are safe in moderation, though cranberry sauce and juice contain too much sugar. If they come from a dried fruit pack, only the cranberries should go to the dog. Apple slices are healthy and tasty, but the core and seeds should go in the trash. The seeds contain small amounts of cyanide that wonât harm a dog unless it eats too many, but itâs best to be cautious. The pie is for the people.

Cheese makes a tasty treat unless a dog is lactose intolerant, and low-fat versions suit dogs watching their weight. A small piece of plain bread is safe unless the dog has a grain allergy. It should have no raisins, seeds, or nuts, and since bread offers few nutritional benefits, it should only be an occasional snack.

Onions are highly toxic to dogs, along with everything in the onion family, including shallots, chives, and leeks, as they can cause anemia and liver damage. Garlic should also be avoided, and garlic powder is especially potent. It can also cause anemia, among other problems, so it shouldnât be anywhere near your pet.

Stuffing is also off limits. It usually contains onion, and it often carries a large amount of butter, which makes it too fatty for a dogâs diet. Mashed potatoes are fine if completely plain, but most are made with plenty of salt, butter, and other ingredients that arenât good for dogs, so itâs best to skip them. Gravy is a problem because of its high sodium, and it usually contains onions or garlic.

Chocolate is toxic because of two chemicals, theobromine and caffeine. It can lead to death if left untreated, and the signs can appear relatively quickly. Caffeinated drinks are risky for the same reason. Dogs are smaller and lighter than people, so it doesnât take much caffeine to cause trouble, and that rules out coffee, tea, and soft drinks.

Yeast-based bread dough is dangerous even though small amounts of baked bread are fine. The yeast expands in a dogâs stomach and can lead to bloat, which can progress to a life-threatening condition called gastric dilatation-volvulus. Alcohol of any kind causes poisoning in dogs, whether itâs a drink, rubbing alcohol, or hand sanitizer.

A final but deadly ingredient to be aware of is the artificial sweetener, xylitol. Sometimes listed as birch sugar, even in small amounts, this seemingly minor ingredient causes severe hypoglycemia, and even if dogs survive this, irreversible liver damage can finish them off. Always keep an eye out for this one, commonly included in sugar-free mints, gums, candy, and peanut butter, to name a few.

A dog caught eating something toxic needs to go straight to a vet or the nearest emergency animal clinic. Bringing the packaging, along with a sample of whatever the dog ingested, helps the vet treat that particular substance. When itâs unclear whether something was toxic, the ASPCA Animal Poison Control at (888) 426-4435 and the Pet Poison Helpline at (855) 764-7661 can help. Both charge a fee, but they can guide the next steps and get a pet immediate help.

When it comes to ingested toxic ingredients, timing is everything. If you know your dog has eaten something potentially dangerous, or even if you just suspect it, donât wait for clinical signs to appear. By then, it may already be too late. Far better to pay for an overcautious detox than risk the life of a four-legged family member.

Be familiar with ingredients that are harmful to pets, particularly anything toxic. A little care with what gets shared keeps the day safe and lets everyone relax.
