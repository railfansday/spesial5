In other words, the days of box-ticking and boilerplate are finally over.

Paperwork isn’t compliance, said Courtney Cantor, deputy general counsel at PubMatic, who regularly fields a steady stream of due diligence requests.

“It’s no longer about just putting the right contracts in place,” Cantor said, “but actually having evidence to show if a regulator came knocking on your door.”

Although that’s assuming contracts reflect reality, which often isn’t the case.

Take the standard clause requiring a vendor to delete everything when a customer agreement is terminated, said Gary Kibel, a partner at Davis+Gilbert. Not only do most companies not remember to do that; it’s often impossible.

“I can’t delete everything” is the correct response to any deletion request, Kibel said, because vendors have legal and record-keeping obligations of their own. On top of that, if businesses purge all of their data, they can’t do business. If you delete a client’s email address, for example, then how do you chase an unpaid bill?

The fact is, managing retention – which Turner referred to as “the ‘R’ word” – is borderline impossible, he said.

“Everybody puts it in their agreements; everybody says how long they’ll keep things,” Turner said. “But I guarantee that if I went to every single person in this room’s company and looked at your data, I’d find retention violations everywhere.”

It’s not malicious or anything; it’s just incredibly difficult to do. “I don’t mean to inspire any attorneys general,” Turner said, “but I think it’s an area we all have to be really careful about going forward.”

Although there’s a heck of a lot of low-hanging fruit to go after first. You’ve gotta get the basics sorted or you could be screwed.

“I had a client about a year ago that got a letter from the Delaware attorney general’s office and they raised that issue,” he said.

“The simple, easy things,” Turner said, “don’t let them be a problem.”

And also don’t forget that regulators are also just people, he said – people who read the news. When one company gets sued or fined, other states take notice and start wondering whether the same thing is happening in their backyard.

Not that getting a letter from a regulator is necessarily a DEFCON 1 situation.

These types of inquiries can feel scary because they often contain detailed questions, Kibel said. Regulators do their homework before reaching out. But a letter isn’t a formal legal demand, and companies don’t technically have to respond.

It’s always a good idea to engage, though, Kibel said.

Ignoring outreach from a regulator is a surefire way to turn a voluntary back-and-forth into a subpoena.

Kibel has put clients on the phone with regulators in the past, and the vibe was usually as pleasant as could be expected. “It wasn’t treated like a deposition,” he said, “and it wasn’t a hostile setting.”

Because regulators do “understand that every system is not 100% perfect,” Kibel said.

“But they also do want to see that you’ve thought about these issues in advance,” he said, “and that you’re being forthright with them in terms of answering their questions.”

Ideally, the whole thing gets worked out over a few phone calls and never escalates.

Likewise, the purpose of Delaware’s HB 380 isn’t for the AG’s office to chase down and investigate every single third party, Eakins said. The hope is that companies and their counsel do the policing themselves.

“The goal is for the industry, for you all, to be saying, ‘You know what, this counterparty is just too much of a high risk,’” Eakins said. “I don’t trust what they’re doing with the data, so we’re just not going to take that reputational risk.”
