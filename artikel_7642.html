Some Miscellaneous SCOTUS Scoops From Woodward's "Secret"

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

There is not much new information revealed beyond what is in The Brethren, but we learn how Woodward obtained the information.

Bob Woodward's new book is a bit unusual. There is no coherent narrative or flow. Each chapter simply tells the story of another one of his sources. Chapters 36 through 43 walk through his interviews with Supreme Court Justices, law clerks, and others for The Brethren. The book is a collection of his recollections, mostly from recorded memos, and what all of his source said.

Beyond any particulars about the Justices, I found most insightful his craft in getting people to speak. He invariably starts by finding someone low on the totem pole, building trust, and working his way up.

Scott and I were intensely pursuing interviews with clerks all over the country. The names of all the law clerks, past and present, was readily available public information. I cannot underscore enough how important it is in journalism to focus on the low end of the totem pole. Presidents, justices, cabinet officials are all important interviews.

What began as a somewhat random "catch-as-catch-can" for Scott and me helped us get inside, as we used what we collected to dig deeper. Quoting information or documents we already possessed helped give confidence to other clerks who would then sometimes reach into their own archives to add details and documents. . .  .

Most of the information in our book, The Brethren, is based on interviews with more than 200 people, including five justices who talked, 170 former and current law clerks, and several dozen former employees of the court. Virtually all the interviews were conducted on deep background, meaning that the identity of the sources would be kept confidential for their lifetimes but all the information could be used in the book. Since all of the justices are now deceased, I am lifting the lid on our interviews with them.

This sort of journalism takes time and time and more time. But eventually, he has enough information that principals feel comfortable talking to him. And even if he learned what happened from multiple sources, asking yet another source opens up the possibility for even more insights to be gleaned.

Chapter 36 recounts Woodward's interactions with Justice Stewart, who invited the reporter to his home for an interview. Stewart spoke very critically of Chief Justice Burger. That interview convinced Woodward there was enough to write a book. The publisher gave him and Scott Armstrong a $300,000 advance. That is more than $1.5 million in present-day value.

Chapter 37 recounts Woodward's interview with Justice Powell. Powell invited him to the Court—right away. Woodward arrived at 4:30 p.m. that day. Powell said, "Intellectually there is no Brandeis or Frankfurter or Cardozo on the court now." I've made similar comments since Justice Scalia's passing. I wonder if any members of the current Court think they are at the intellectual level of Holmes or Story? I appreciate Powell's humility.

Woodward would interview Powell two more times. I found these comments about Roe striking:

I asked him about the Supreme Court's landmark 1973 abortion decisions in Roe v. Wade and Doe v. Bolton, which legalized abortion nationwide. "It was hard for me and I was frankly strongly result oriented," Powell said of the decision. "I would support abortion funding by the states if I was in the legislature," he added. This caught me off-guard and seemed contrary to the opinions he had written saying there was no right to a state-funded abortion.

"I thought the abortion laws were atrocious," Powell continued, referring to the state abortion laws prohibiting abortion. "If you wanted to pay for an abortion you could get one." I noted his selection of the word "wanted" instead of "could afford" which was a big part of the issue as I saw it.

There should be no doubt that Roe was an outcome-driven decision. There was no law—just policy.

Chapter 38 focuses on how he interviewed more than 170 law clerks. Powell authorized his law clerks to speak to Woodward. Burger's former clerks were "at times the most helpful," especially since Woodward never bothered asking Burger to speak. I already recounted the story in which he "borrowed" Justice Brennan's history of the Watergate tape case.

Chapter 39 explains how Justice Powell thought the Justices should talk to reporters to minimize the bad press. Powell wrote in a draft letter to Burger that he never sent: "I had rather someone on the Court discussed this with him accurately than leave him to reach his own conclusions with the right to say that he was stonewalled by the Justices."

Chapter 40 discusses Armstrong's interview with Justice Blackmun, and the resulting "19-page single-spaced memo." There were a few useful nuggets. Blackmun appreciated how he wasn't Nixon's first or even second choice for the seat.

"I am humbled by the fact that I was number three, not the number one choice," Blackmun said of his appointment to the Supreme Court. Nixon's first choice, Clement Haynsworth, a federal judge from South Carolina, and second choice, G. Harrold Carswell, a federal judge from Florida, were rejected narrowly in brutal public fights, largely over concerns that Nixon was attempting to shift the court's ideology, which of course he was. Blackmun had been confirmed unanimously in May 1970 in a 94-to-0 vote. "A scarecrow could get nominated after what had happened," Blackmun continued. "It was a difficult period for the Senate. A number of [senators] told me that they wanted to get it over with. They had a real sense of relief that an average guy had come along with whom they couldn't find much wrong."

I wonder if Justice Kennedy ever had similar marks of humility, as a fellow third-choice?

Blackmun was also assigned the Roe opinion because Chief didn't want to take the heat:

Blackmun said he was well aware that no matter what he wrote, the opinion would be controversial and he would be hated and vilified. The chief could not afford to take on such a controversial case himself, particularly from the minority. "That's why I was assigned the abortion memo. We were scattered on it," Blackmun said. Douglas was the court's mischievous liberal, the rebel, and couldn't be the author. Any abortion opinion Douglas wrote would be widely questioned outside the court. Brennan was a firm vote for striking down the state abortion laws, but he was the court's only sitting Catholic. As such, Brennan could not be expected to take the heat from the Catholic anti-abortion groups. Marshall could not author for similar reasons: An opinion by the court's only Black man could be perceived as specifically designed for Black people or incite race-fueled blame for the decision. Stewart would have trouble going very far… That left one option: Blackmun.

By Chapter 41, the first draft of the book was complete. Now, Powell declined to talk further with Woodward.

Chapter 42 recounts a fairly gruff interview with Justice White, who gave up virtually nothing.

Chapter 43 relays how Justice Powell wrote to Chief Justice Burger about the new book. Powell, of course, did not disclose how he spoke with Woodward, and how he authorized his clerks to do the same. I can get Burger's frustration.

Chief Justice Warren Burger erupted in a letter back to Powell, squarely blaming the clerks and noting "there are hazards in talking with certain 'categories' in this life"—meaning me and Scott, or reporters in general. He criticized the clerks for seeming to have succumbed to the "glamour" of rubbing shoulders with "print names!" . . .

I wondered if Burger knew that five of the seven justices in his ranks had talked to us and several had given their clerks permission to assist us.

If only Burger knew that Powell and others blabbed. I suspect Chief Justice Roberts can relate.

It also turns out that Woodward asked a young Stephen Breyer to fact-check the book.

Another secret that has a certain historic value today was that one weekend before The Brethren went to the printers, a young man named Stephen Breyer came over to my home. He sat in the living room and read the book cover to cover. At that time he was chief counsel to the Senate Judiciary Committee chaired by Senator Ted Kennedy. Scott had insisted we have the book's use of legal terms checked for obvious errors. After reading, Breyer told us it was "a hell of a book."

I would note there is no discussion of interviewing Justice Rehnquist, even though Rehnquist is named as a source. Something is off.

That's it. Nothing earth-shattering. But it is a shame Woodward decided to relay non-public communications with Breyer and Kagan, who are both alive and well.
