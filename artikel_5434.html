How retired oil platforms become artificial reefs, and why Europe mostly says no

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Rigs-to-reefs programs leave retired offshore oil platforms in the sea as artificial reefs, and these five points explain the methods, laws, marine life and debate behind them

An offshore oil platform is built to pull oil and gas from beneath the seabed. By the time its wells stop producing, its steel legs can be coated in sponges and corals and surrounded by fish. That leaves operators and regulators with a practical choice. The structure can come out of the water, or it can stay where marine life has already settled on it.

Rigs to reefs is the name for the second option. Under this approach, some or all of a retired offshore oil or gas platform's steel stays in the sea as an artificial reef. It is not hauled to shore for scrapping. The steel can be cut off below the surface, moved to a new site or tipped over where it stands.

Decommissioning is a major engineering and policy question for any region with aging offshore fields. Every platform eventually stops producing, and each one needs a plan for what happens next. The choice touches shipping safety, fisheries and cleanup costs. It also settles who is responsible for the steel once production ends.

The answer differs sharply by region. In the U.S. Gulf of Mexico, a 1984 federal law and state programs in Louisiana and Texas allow retired structures to become reefs. In the North Sea, an international agreement covering the North-East Atlantic generally requires full removal. Off southern California, researchers have studied fish on platforms for decades. The state passed a law in 2010 that created a framework for conversion.

The same steel frame can be treated as waste in one ocean and as habitat in another. The reasons show how governments weigh ecology, cost and long-term liability at sea.

Retired offshore oil and gas platforms become artificial reefs through one of three main methods: partial removal, tow-and-place or topple-in-place. Each one keeps some or all of the platform's underwater steel in the sea.

An offshore platform has two basic parts. The topsides sit above the water and carry the deck, the production equipment and, on many platforms, the crew quarters. Below them stands the jacket. This is a steel lattice of legs and cross-bracing that reaches down to the seabed and holds the topsides up. In a reef conversion, the topsides come off and go to shore. The jacket is the part that can stay in the water.

Partial removal leaves the jacket in its original spot. Crews cut the structure off at a set depth below the surface and take away the upper section. The lower part keeps standing where it was first installed.

The cut depth follows a navigation clearance rule. U.S. practice for partial removal keeps at least 85 feet of water above whatever steel remains. That clearance is meant to keep the submerged structure out of the path of passing ships. The standard follows the National Artificial Reef Plan.

Tow-and-place moves the jacket instead of leaving it where it stood. Crews free the whole jacket from its original position, tow it to a designated reef site and sink it there. The reef ends up at a chosen location, not at the spot where the platform once produced oil or gas.

Topple-in-place keeps the jacket at its original site but changes its orientation. The jacket is tipped over and comes to rest on its side on the seabed. Laid down this way, it rises less far off the bottom than it did when upright.

Full removal, the alternative to all three, takes the entire structure out of the water. The choice among the reef methods comes down to two questions. One is where the jacket ends up. The other is how much of it remains standing.

Rigs-to-reefs conversions in the U.S. rest on a federal law, the National Fishing Enhancement Act of 1984, and on state programs in the Gulf of Mexico. Louisiana and Texas each run a program that accepts retired offshore platforms as reef material.

Congress passed the National Fishing Enhancement Act to set up a national framework for artificial reefs. The law also called for a National Artificial Reef Plan to guide how reefs are sited, built and managed. Retired oil and gas platforms fall within that framework as one type of reef material.

Louisiana created its Artificial Reef Program in 1986, two years after the federal act. The Louisiana Department of Wildlife and Fisheries manages it. The program takes in retired platforms and keeps their steel in the water as reef.

Texas has its own Rigs-to-Reefs program, run by Texas Parks and Wildlife. It began in 1990, six years after the federal law.

The Bureau of Safety and Environmental Enforcement, known as BSEE, oversees the decommissioning of offshore platforms in federal waters. BSEE sits within the U.S. Department of the Interior. Its rules generally require an operator to remove a platform once the structure is no longer needed. Reef conversion is one approved exception to that requirement. A state reef program enters the picture when the operator donates the structure as reef material.

The state programs work through donation. An operator retiring a platform hands the structure over to the program. The operator also typically donates a share of the money it saves by not removing the platform entirely. That money goes to the state program and helps pay for its reef work.

Full removal means pulling the whole structure out of the sea and hauling it to shore. Leaving steel in place as reef skips that step for the part that stays underwater. Each conversion involves three parties: the operator that owns the platform, BSEE as the federal regulator and the state agency that accepts the steel.

Offshore oil and gas platforms can host dense communities of fish, corals and sponges. Steel built for drilling off southern California and in the Gulf of Mexico has become habitat for marine life in both regions.

A 2014 study by Claisse and colleagues, published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, measured fish production at oil and gas platforms off southern California. The authors found that these platforms had some of the highest secondary fish production per unit area of any marine habitat they compared.

Secondary production describes how much new fish tissue a habitat generates through growth over a period of time. It differs from a head count of the fish present on a single dive. Expressing it per unit area lets researchers compare habitats of very different sizes on equal terms.

Milton Love of the University of California, Santa Barbara has studied fish on California platforms for decades. His work documents juvenile rockfish living on the structures. Rockfish belong to the genus Sebastes, a large group of species found along the Pacific coast of North America. Many rockfish species are targeted by commercial and recreational anglers on the West Coast. The presence of young fish shows that platforms serve these species early in life.

In the Gulf of Mexico, platform legs become covered with encrusting animals, including corals and sponges. These organisms need a hard surface to settle on. A platform's submerged steel provides one. Once that living layer forms, it draws fish to the structure. A platform's legs run from the seafloor up to the surface. That gives encrusting animals room to grow through the full depth of the water at the site.

California has taken a legal step toward keeping some of these habitats in place. A state law created a framework for converting retired platforms into reefs. That framework lays out a legal path for conversion. It does not by itself turn any platform into a reef.

OSPAR Decision 98/3 bans dumping or leaving disused offshore installations in the North-East Atlantic. For retired oil and gas platforms in the North Sea, that makes full removal the default.

The decision sits under the OSPAR Convention, the agreement that protects the marine environment of the North-East Atlantic. The North Sea falls inside that area. Its offshore oil and gas fields, including those in the U.K. and Norwegian sectors, come under the same ban.

Decision 98/3 allows limited exceptions. These apply mainly to two categories: the footings of very large steel structures and concrete installations.

Those exceptions are the main route by which any platform material may stay on the North Sea seabed. For most installations, the rule points to removal and disposal on land.

The Brent Spar episode of 1995 was a high-profile dispute over disposal at sea in the region. Brent Spar was a storage buoy, not a production platform. Shell $SHEL planned to sink it in the deep Atlantic once it was no longer needed.

Greenpeace campaigned against the plan. Shell dropped it and had the buoy dismantled onshore instead.

The rules produce a sharp contrast with the U.S. In the North Sea, rigs-to-reefs conversions are rare. In the U.S. Gulf of Mexico, the practice is established.

The difference lies in regulation, not engineering. Under OSPAR, the starting assumption is that a disused installation leaves the sea. In the Gulf of Mexico, leaving part or all of a platform's structure in the water as an artificial reef is an accepted option.

Scientists have not settled whether reefs built from retired oil platforms produce new fish or mainly gather fish that already lived nearby. The evidence differs by species and by region, and no single answer covers every rigs-to-reefs site.

The distinction shapes how these structures are judged. A reef that adds to production increases the number of fish in the sea. A reef that only pulls fish in from surrounding waters moves them to a new spot without changing the total.

The issue is known as the attraction-versus-production debate. Production is more plausible for species whose numbers are limited by a shortage of hard habitat. In those cases, extra structure gives young fish more places to settle and survive. Where food or spawning success caps a population, added steel is more likely to redistribute fish than to multiply them.

Attraction carries its own risk. A reef that concentrates fish in one known location can make them easier to catch. If fishing pressure rises at the site, a structure that gathers fish without producing more could speed the depletion of a local stock.

In the Gulf of Mexico, red snapper sits at the center of this argument. The species gathers in large numbers around platforms. It is also one of the most heavily fished reef species in the region.

The steel can also host species that do not belong there. Orange cup coral, Tubastraea coccinea, is native to the Indo-Pacific. It has spread into the western Atlantic and colonized platforms in the Gulf of Mexico, where it competes with native corals and sponges for space. Platforms left standing can serve as stepping stones that help it reach new areas.

Long-term responsibility is another point of contention. Full removal takes the steel out of the sea. When part or all of a platform stays offshore as a reef, responsibility for that structure over the following decades shifts as well.

Cost draws the sharpest criticism. Leaving a structure in place is cheaper for an operator than hauling it ashore. Critics argue those savings, rather than habitat value, are a major force behind rigs-to-reefs programs. Supporters point to the marine life already living on the legs.
