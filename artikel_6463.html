Not All Physical Activity Protects Your Brain, Scientists Warn

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Across 30 countries, researchers found that exercising during free time was linked to lower dementia risk, while physically demanding work was linked to higher risk.

After spending an entire day on your feet, going for a walk or bike ride might seem unnecessary. Yet new research suggests that being physically active at work may not provide the same protection against dementia as exercising in your free time.

A major analysis involving more than 4.2 million people found that those who were most physically active during their leisure time had a 24% lower risk of dementia compared with those who were least active. In contrast, physical activity performed on the job was associated with a 20% higher risk.

The findings, published in The Lancet Public Health, raise an important question about the familiar advice to move more. If physical activity is good for the brain, why might its benefits depend on where and how it takes place?

Researchers at the University of Southern California (USC) examined this question by combining evidence from 74 cohort and case-control studies conducted across 30 countries. The participants ranged in age from 45 to 93 and were followed for a median of 10 years. During that time, researchers tracked diagnoses of dementia from all causes, including Alzheimer’s disease and vascular dementia.

The analysis was led by David Raichlen, a professor of biological sciences and anthropology at the USC Dornsife College of Letters, Arts and Sciences. It is the first systematic investigation of whether the “physical activity paradox,” previously documented in cardiovascular research, also applies to dementia. The paradox describes how physically demanding occupations may fail to provide the same health benefits as recreational exercise and may even be associated with greater disease risk.

“The findings challenge the long-held assumption that ‘every move counts’ for brain health,” said Natan Feter, the study’s first author, a postdoctoral fellow in Raichlen’s Evolutionary Biology of Physical Activity Lab at USC and a researcher in the Graduate Program of Epidemiology at Universidade Federal do Rio Grande do Sul in Brazil.

One possible explanation lies in what happens around the physical activity itself. Walking, cycling, running, swimming, and playing sports during leisure time are generally activities people can choose and adjust to their comfort level. They may also provide opportunities to socialize, relax, and reduce stress.

A physically demanding job often presents very different circumstances. Workers may spend hours standing, repeatedly lifting heavy objects, or completing strenuous tasks with little opportunity for recovery. Unlike a recreational workout, these activities may occur alongside workplace hazards and pressures that extend well beyond the physical effort involved.

Manual occupations, for example, can expose workers to greater psychosocial stress, less control over their working conditions, and higher levels of air and noise pollution. These factors have independently been linked to dementia risk. Workers may also struggle to find time or energy for recreational exercise, particularly when access to safe places for physical activity is limited.

For these reasons, the researchers believe the higher dementia risk associated with occupational activity is unlikely to result from movement itself. Instead, the working conditions and broader social environments surrounding physically demanding jobs may help explain the difference.

The relationship also changed depending on how much activity people performed. Higher levels of leisure-time exercise were associated with progressively lower dementia risk, although the association eventually leveled off. Occupational activity followed the opposite pattern, with dementia risk increasing alongside greater activity before reaching a similar plateau.

Not every form of daily movement could be evaluated with the same confidence. Household physical activity appeared to be beneficial, although that finding came from just one study. Active commuting was associated with a modestly higher dementia risk, but only two studies examined this relationship. The researchers emphasized that more evidence is needed before firm conclusions can be drawn about either activity.

There are also important limits to what the larger analysis can establish.

Because the studies were observational, the findings cannot prove that physically demanding work causes dementia or that leisure-time exercise directly prevents it. Nearly all the studies relied on participants reporting their own physical activity, which can introduce errors in memory and reporting. Most of the evidence also came from high-income countries, despite expectations that low- and middle-income countries will experience the largest increases in dementia cases over the coming decades.

Even with these limitations, the contrasting associations remained after researchers accounted for factors including age, education, and chronic diseases. Multiple sensitivity analyses, which test whether findings hold under different analytical assumptions, produced consistent results. Feter said this consistency strengthens the case for taking the relationship seriously.

The importance of context may extend beyond physical activity. In a related study published in Alzheimer’s & Dementia the previous month, Raichlen, Feter, and their colleagues examined how different sedentary behaviors relate to brain structure.

They found that mentally engaging activities performed while sitting, such as working at a desk, were associated with greater volume in regions of the brain’s cortex compared with more passive activities, such as watching television. These regions included areas involved in executive function, which supports abilities such as planning and decision-making. Like the findings on physical activity, these associations do not establish cause and effect.

The researchers stress that their findings should not discourage people from working physically demanding jobs. Instead, they suggest that dementia prevention strategies need to account for the conditions under which people are active, including the opportunities they have to exercise for enjoyment outside work.

“In other words, when it comes to protecting the brain, the context of physical activity may matter just as much as movement itself,” Raichlen said. “Improving access to safe parks, walking trails and recreational facilities, ensuring people have adequate leisure time for exercise, and reducing harmful workplace exposures may ultimately prove as important as promoting physical activity itself.”

Reference: “Domain-specific physical activity levels and risk of dementia: a systematic review and meta-analysis” by Natan Feter, Paula Iso-Markku, Tiladia Markarian, Dylan M Luong, Jack Gunnink, Natália Schröder, Eduardo Lucia Caputo, Jayne Feter, Anamika Nanda, Sarah Hourihan, Gustavo S da Silva, Ashley Ventura, Daniela Benzano Bumaguin, Yann C Klimentidis, Gene E Alexander, Daniel Umpierre, Pedro C Hallal, Airton José Rombaldi and David A Raichlen, September 2026, The Lancet Public Health.
