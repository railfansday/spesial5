The Secret Shelter

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

After a grueling ascent, I reach the formidable summit of New Jersey, battling thin mountain air at a staggering 1,803 feet above sea level. I check out a location known as the Secret Shelter.

Don’t get too excited, I couldn’t get my lazy bones out of bed before sunrise, so there will be no pictures of a sunrise from Sunrise Mountain. There would also have been the small matter of the 5.3 miles I’d need to hike to capture such a photo. I’ll take my beauty rest, thank you very much.

The plan for the day is to hike north to Carnegie Road, turn around a find a place to camp. I say “find a place” intentionally because there is some question about the openness of the nearby “Secret Shelter” to hikers. More on that later. Also on tap is a short side quest to the New Jersey High Point, which you may recall has a large memorial tower marking the site.

Getting underway just before 8:00AM, the trail makes its way up Sunrise Mountain, a long, tree-lined ridge with occasional views West and East. Along the way the trail passes by the Culver Fire Tower, which is currently in use and not accessible for hikers. The best views of the morning were indeed right at the summit of Sunrise Mountain, which features an open pavilion positioned perfectly for watching sunrises. Oddly the nearby parking area doesn’t open until 8:00AM, more than half an hour after the latest sunrise of the year (tomorrow!).

Early morning view from Sunrise Mountain.

Early morning view from Sunrise Mountain.

Continuing north, the trail remained decidedly wonderful. After days of twisting my ankle on leafy rocks, the flat ride was just what I needed. I crossed the Deckertown Turnpike, captured a few more photos from the ridge, before reaching the High Point State Park HQ. The park office has a handy water bottle refilling station, restrooms, a gift shop, and rangers available for questions. I felt this was a good time to sit at the picnic table and scarf down my trail snack before the next stop, the New Jersey High Point. There was a high wind advisory for the area, and it was showing. I had to keep close tabs on my various bags to ensure they didn’t blow away which I chomped on my peanuts and sunflower seeds.

Preceding the observation tower is an observation deck provided a great view of the towering obelisk. I clambered down briefly before turning off the AT and up the steep trail to the tower. I was beat by the time I reached the monument, which had been built in 1930 as a war memorial/observation tower. It is possible to go inside the tower and up to the top. You simply need to open the front door and climb 291 stairs. After trekking up the steep trail to the tower I simply couldn’t wait to take on those steps. The information I had said the tower was open until 3:30PM, but when I reached the metal door it was latched and locked closed. I was positively dismayed, as you can see from the picture I took to capture my strong emotional reaction to such a brutal letdown.

The wind at the tower was incredible, I hadn’t felt this much wind since I was in the Whites. I tried to escape the wind by finding a safe side of the tower, but the wind was strong on all 4 sides, how does that work? I decided the best idea was get off the top of the mountain and continue my journey for the day.

Once I was safely out of the wind I descended from the ridge that I’d spent all day on to hike in a valley for the rest of the day. The AT tracks northeast close to the Pennsylvania/New Jersey before turning to the southeast to follow, quite precisely, the New Jersey/New York border. The trail was mostly flat and passed through a few pastures. Just before the turn for the Secret Shelter, the trail passes over a ridge with a view of the high point tower. After a marshy area and several local roads stemming from the town of Unionville, New York, I reached Carnegie Road and turned south to retrace my steps.

This time around I would turn off the trail at a dirt driveway to scope out the Secret Shelter. The shelter was erected by James Macklin Murray, an avid hiker who completed the Appalachian Trail in 1989. After his hike, decided to give back to the trail community by building the shelter on his property. Rather than follow the model of traditional AT shelters, which are quite rustic, he hooked up electricity and running water. Sadly, Jim Murray, aka Backpack Jim, passed away on November 5th, 2025. The property is in jeopardy, but efforts are underway for the Trust for Public Land to acquire the property thereby allowing the shelter to continue operating. A commenter on FarOut, posting 2 days earlier, noted that there was currently a chain on a gate with a keep out sign at the entrance to the property. I was considering staying at the shelter, but obviously wanted to see for myself if it was still open first.

I turned down the driveway toward the shelter, keeping a sharp eye out for any deterring signage or chains, but saw none. The same path that hikers had taken for decades was still there leading to the grouping of buildings. I walked up to the building and found it unlocked with the trail logbook as well as several maps of the area still up on the walls. Even the hiker box had a few useful items. It certainly seemed to be operating as normal to me. I skimmed the log book and recognized plenty of trail names – Squirrel, Wit, Lancelot, 2Bears, and Quilt.

I knew it would be getting cold tonight and I was feeling increasingly swayed to stay at the shelter. I figured it would be warmer than my tent, and probably make it easier to get going in the morning. In the end I decided I’d rather be closer to my van and returned to the trail to hike south a little further.

View of the memorial tower from down in the Hudson Valley.

A view of the memorial tower from near the Secret Shelter.

On my way back to the trail another hiker was making his way toward the shelter. He goes by Rebar, and knew about my hike from fellow southbounder, Pebbles. I shared my findings on the shelter, including the fact that he would have it all to himself if he chose to stay there. We parted ways and I set a course for some streams I had passed on my initial trek northbound. I wanted to get as far south as I could manage in the fading light (and fading muscles). Conveniently, the furthest stream was reachable in that time and also had the most flat area for tenting. Despite some not so clean water flowing nearby, this stream was clear when I passed it earlier. Inconveniently, the water had completely changed. Something had dumped quite a lot of extra water in the time since I passed by. The water was a translucent gray, which sometimes results from farm runoff, or could be actual clay in the water. I decided not to risk and turned around to hike a few tenths of a mile north, I was pretty sure that water still looked good. It did, and I found a fine spot to pitch my tent for the night. By the time the day began getting dark I was climbing into my tent to rest up for the next day.
