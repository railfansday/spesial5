Long Day and Disgusting Dinner — John Muir Trail Day 18

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

I slept through the night.  OK, I got up to pee several times.

Maybe an apology is in order.  I write a lot about my bodily processes.  Yet on the trail you think about your legs – are they tired or strong;  your lungs – are you gasping for air or breathing clearly and steadily;  your stomach – are you hungry, or do you need a snack;  your feet – are they blistered and hot or cool and comfortable;  your back – is the pack sitting right, or is it aching;  and yes, your bladder, is it full or empty, is your urine a light yellow or is it dark and concerning.

The answers to these questions, and what you do about those answers, determine what your experience is on the trail.  That is the mental element, which is no doubt the most critical of all.  How do you respond to keep the whole organism moving forward successfully?  When do you need to stop and rest, or drink, or eat?  When do you need to push on through pain or discomfort?  So, let me run through the mental process of feeling the need to pee in the middle of the night.

First of all, you’re warm and toasty in your sleeping bag.  You don’t want to leave it for the cold night air outside your tent.

You decide, “I don’t really have to go very badly, let me wait awhile.  No point getting cold just to void a half a bladder, right?”

At a certain point you can’t fall back to sleep and your bladder says to you, “Don’t be an idiot.  You’ve got to go, so go.”

Reluctantly, but a little urgently, you unzip your sleeping bag and pull yourself out.  Then you unzip the tent flap and stick your feet in the shoes you have sheltered in the vestibule.

Then off you go to whatever tree you have designated to fertilize on this particular night.

When you are done, it is a relief.  You are so glad you roused yourself.

Then you look up.  Perhaps the stars are splashed in an ethereal glow across the inky sky?  Perhaps the moon is blasting the mountaintops in a bluish light?  In either case, you are doubly glad you roused yourself, and you pause before returning to your tent.

(Or course, if it is raining, you hightail it back into the tent as fast as you can!)

Back in the tent, your bladder feels empty and your heart feels full.  You fall back to sleep.

I would add that this is the routine for a man.  I recognize that for a woman it is harder and, if she is experiencing her period, it may be much, much harder.  I acknowledge that.

But even when I’m feeling reasonably efficient, I’m just not.  So, getting up at 6:30  means I start hiking at 8:30.  I’m not proud of it, but if I haven’t fixed it by Day 18, it seems unlikely I will at all.

As a result, Ninja Queen left ahead me along with others camping in the area.

After digging – and using — a new cat hole and washing my socks, I finished packing and hit the trail.  Without any major passes to cross, I thought I could log pretty big mileage (for me), to leave a short stretch into Red’s Meadow the next day.  That would be my last resupply.

Immediately the trail went downhill, and I realized it was going to drop down about a thousand feet.  At the bottom was a beautiful mountain stream, splashing gaily over the rocks as it descended.

This was Fish Creek. I didn’t see any fish, but it was a delightful stream.

I also took a video of it and then began an ascent back another thousand feet.

No, I was not climbing a pass, but it was a considerable uphill slog.  Most of it was on an exposed hillside traversing on switchbacks.  I certainly appreciated the traverses!  It would be so much harder without them.

Eventually I caught up to Ninja Queen and we chatted briefly.  Then I passed her, because I’m quite a bit faster than she is on the uphill, probably because she carries such a heavy pack.

Frankly, I feel slow on the uphill, but the discipline of my walking-and-breathing technique maintains my momentum.  By the time I reached the ridge line I was breathing hard, convinced that this was basically a pass, just not a named pass like other more famous ones.

The good news, according to the FarOut app, was that I had completed fully half of the day’s ascent profile, and it was still just mid-morning.

Below the ridge, the trail dropped into a shallow valley where there was a stunning lake with a dark blue color to it.  Lake Virgnia seemed a scenic spot to break for my early snack.  As I sat and nibbled, I watched a person ahead of me follow the trail across the base of the lake.  They seemed to have a problem at a certain point, but they were too far away for me to tell what it was.

Back on the trail, I found the first stream entering the lake to be an easy crossing with steppingstones.

At the second crossing I discovered what the problem was – there were no steppingstones.  Seeing no other option, I dropped my pack.  It annoyed me, because I had just stopped for lunch, and now I had to stop and take everything off all over again to put my water shoes on.  But I did not want to hike in wet shoes and let them squish as I walked until they dried on my feet.

As I waded, the water felt nice and cool on my feet, but the bottom felt sort of muddy and slimy.  I was glad not to be in bare feet.

Once on the other side, I put my shoes and socks back on.  About the time I was ready to go, Ninja Queen had caught up and was facing the same crossing dilemma, except that she didn’t have water shoes.  So, she went through in her bare feet.

I greeted her and refrained from asking if her feet were slimy. Then I set off up the little hill behind the lake and down the other side.  It felt like it was going to be a long day.

From there, there was a long descent to a lake called Purple Lake.  I thought, “Oh, if I only had time, I’d swim in it, because I like the name Purple Lake.”  Purple is, after all, my favorite color.

…and very clear water. If I had the time, it would have been a great swimming spot!

Instead, I just refilled my water bottles there and no, the water wasn’t purple!  But there was a climb above Purple Lake and quite long dry stretch, so I was glad to have the water.

I didn’t have my second lunch until the next stream crossing that I encountered.  It was about 3:00 p.m. and by then I was hot and utterly exhausted.  Some folks I met at the stream, coming the other way, warned me that there was no more water for the next five and a half miles.

So, I filled everything up with water again, but I felt like my legs could barely even move.  I was lethargic and the extra water weight seemed onerous.  I wondered how much further I could go.

Then the calories and nutrition from the lunch kicked in.  My attitude changed completely.  I started going pretty fast and, after another small ascent, the path was smooth and level.  These were ideal conditions for me to make really good time.

I said there were not many views here, but some of them were outstanding!

I just put my head down and hiked and hiked.  There were not many views on this stretch, so there was not a lot of temptation to stop and look around.  At one point though, I finally got a picture of a little chipmunk.

Usually they dashed off too fast, but this one was standing on a rock, and I slowly took out my phone camera, waited until the moment was right, and took some pictures.

There were always so many rodents.  I saw them all the time.  Chipmunks and squirrels and ground squirrels and probably others that I misidentified.

At VVR, one of them even attacked Amy’s granola.  She left it unattended for 10 seconds.  Then there was a chipmunk or a squirrel munching away at it!  (Can’t remember which it was.)  Amy’s response was funny.  She said, “Oh, I got that out of the hiker box, and I didn’t really like it.  So, it’s okay.”

As promised, there was no water for five and a half miles and then there was a stream.  When I was so exhausted before, I intended to stop at the first tent site after the next water source, but now I was ready to continue.

I thought, “Let me fulfill my original plan and get closer to Red’s Meadow.”  I scratched a note in the ground for Ninja Queen, saying that I was going on, but I did not see her again that day.

I pressed on and on.  At one point I found a hat on the trail.  I wondered who — the owner?  someone else? — would pick it up.

Does the rock suggest a deliberate effort to prevent it from blowing away?

At length it was late afternoon, getting towards dusk.  I thought, “Why don’t I just continue and go all the way to Red’s Meadow tonight?  I could pitch my tent, and it could stay in place for two nights, and I would have a true Zero at Red’s Meadow.  It might be wonderful.”

“What is more, it is a full moon, or near a full moon, right now tonight.  It would be easy to see my way, without relying entirely on my headlamp.”

I seemed it was only a little more than four miles further on.

Why didn’t I do that?  What made the difference was a check of the elevation profile.  I saw that Red’s Meadow was more than a thousand feet below where I was then standing.  I thought to myself, “If the descent is a treacherous, rocky downhill hike, and I’m doing it by the moon or by my headlamp, and something happens, no one’s coming.”

During the day, if you had an accident on the trail, another hiker would be sure to come along within an hour, or maybe 90 minutes, tops.  But if that accident happened in the middle of the night?  You would most likely be found the next morning.

Late afternnon on the flat and sandy trail

At the next camping place I saw, up on the hillside above the trail, I pulled off and called it a day.  I had still done almost 19 miles; it was my longest distance yet on the trail.

Up on top there were several camping spots, but I was blissfully alone.  There was little light left, so I quickly set up my tent and began soaking my food.  The freeze dried “Beef in Marinara Sauce” seemed to have congealed inside the package, making me suspicious of the packaging process.  This was my last dinner before resupply and it had a ton of calories, but it was quite disgusting.

Even after I added water, used my utensils to try to break it up, and shook it vigorously in the Nalgene, it remained a semi-solid mass with globules floating around it.

I was worried that I might get food poisoning.  On the other hand, it was my last meal before resupply, I had completed a really long day, and I needed to take in those calories.

I took my chances and began to eat it.  It tasted as terrible as it looked.  Like a prisoner forced to eat his gruel, I forced myself to get it down.  This was my third bad meal on trail, and it moved into first place in the “Worst Meals” competition.

On FarOut, it said that there was a Verizon signal near Red’s Meadow.  I tried it and, to my surprise and delight, I got my wife.  While I waited to begin puking or having diarrhea or something, we had a lovely conversation.

By the time we hung up I was reasonably sure the “dinner” was going to stay down.  I set up my electronics to charge off the block in my tent and snuggled into my sleeping bag.
