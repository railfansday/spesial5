Here Are the 7 Best Long-Sleeve Shirts for Cold-Weather Workouts

FALL IS HERE, and with it, the chill is starting to hit the air. This change in temperature shifts the spotlight to what we think is the unsung hero of your fall and winter workout wardrobe, especially when it comes to your workouts: the long-sleeve T-shirt.

Whether you’re trying to ward off goosebumps or protect your skin from the sun, long-sleeve shirts are a solid choice for training. Many are made from the same performance fabrics as our favorite workout shirts; they can wick moisture and keep you cooler than a wool layer while offering more coverage than a short-sleeve tee.

Collectively, the Men’s Health editorial team has trained in dozens of long-sleeve shirts and pooled our experience to bring you seven picks for different types of training.

The long-sleeve sibling of our best overall workout shirt also snags the top spot in our roundup of long-sleeve workout shirts. After all, it’s virtually the same shirt, just better suited for fall and winter workouts.

The Strato Tech Tee is among the softest shirts I’ve ever worn, and after a year of consistent use, mine still looks, feels, and performs much like it did during my first week of testing. Its four-way-stretch fabric contributes to its excellent comfort, and despite the shirt’s paper-thin feel, it was surprisingly effective at wicking moisture during our sweatiest workouts.

Because it’s lightweight, keeps sweat at bay, and is versatile enough to double as an everyday shirt, the Strato Tech earns its near-$70 price tag. It’s the kind of staple worth investing in.

The skin-tight fit of compression gear doesn’t appeal to everyone, but compression shirts offer plenty of utility. You can wear one on its own, under a short-sleeve tee for a fall or winter workout, or as a base layer for skiing and hiking.

Dick’s Sporting Goods’ DSG compression crew is a good option for skeptical buyers. It costs $30 per shirt and comes in more than 15 color options. The trade-off for that affordability is slightly less comfort. In particular, the seams that run along the arms can be a little annoying. It also runs a bit longer than I prefer, so I opted to tuck mine in.

When people think of Under Armour, they often think of compression gear, so its HeatGear line—a collection designed to provide coverage while keeping athletes cool—is a natural pick for anyone who wants to keep their arms covered during spring and summer workouts.

Athletes who wear pads, trail runners looking for protection from brush, and even people with sun-sensitive skin can all benefit from the extra coverage. HeatGear fabric is ventilated and described by Under Armour as “ultra-light,” so the shirt offers protection without adding much bulk or warmth. And at $40 with 16 color options, it’s an affordable, versatile choice that works as either a standalone training shirt or a base layer.

At this price, that makes it a slightly less proven pick, but its simple design and performance-focused construction give it plenty of appeal for runners and lifters alike.

Most workout shirts are made primarily from synthetic materials such as polyester, nylon, and elastane. These fabrics tend to dry faster than cotton and can be engineered to move moisture away from your skin, which helps keep the shirt from feeling soaked during a workout.

Cotton, by comparison, absorbs and holds onto more moisture, which can leave a shirt feeling heavy and damp and may contribute to chafing or irritation. That doesn’t mean cotton has no place in workout gear. Some shirts blend in a small amount to soften the fabric and improve comfort. An all-cotton shirt may be less appealing for sweaty sessions, but a little cotton in the blend isn’t necessarily a drawback.

Odor control generally comes down to two things: how quickly the fabric dries and whether it has been treated to limit odor-causing bacteria. Moisture-wicking, quick-drying fabrics can help keep sweat from sitting against your skin for as long, but the fabric itself doesn’t automatically prevent odor.

Some manufacturers also treat fibers with antimicrobial technologies designed to slow bacterial growth. Silver, copper, and zinc-based treatments are common examples. Other brands use different chemical or bio-based treatments to achieve a similar effect. If odor control matters to you, look for a specific antimicrobial or anti-odor claim rather than assuming every synthetic shirt has it.

You probably don’t need to obsess over seams, but they’re worth paying attention to if you’re prone to chafing. Look for flatlock seams, which sit flatter and feel less bulky against the skin, as well as thoughtful seam placement in high-friction areas.

The armpit is one of the more annoying places to have bulky stitching, which is why some workout shirts use raglan sleeves. Instead of a traditional sleeve seam that starts at the shoulder, raglan sleeves extend up toward the collar and create diagonal seams across the upper chest and back. That construction can reduce irritation and allow for more freedom of movement.

At the time I wrote this article, I was also logging 15 to 25 miles of running per week across the treadmill, road, and local trails, so I rotated several of these shirts through those sessions to see how they held up.

We take notes on each shirt’s style, fit, comfort, price, versatility, and performance, including stretch, moisture management, and breathability. We also pay attention to sizing—whether a shirt fits true to size or runs large or small—and how the fabric holds up after repeated workouts and washes. What we don’t do is chase trends. Our goal is to recommend shirts that perform well now and should still make sense years from now.
