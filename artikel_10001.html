Anthropic says Claude solved an age-old math problem, but the written proof is 250,000 pages of slop

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

If the AI can't explain what it's proven, are we actually any closer to understanding?

The leaders of the great model houses, Anthropic's Dario Amodei and OpenAI's Sam Altman, desperately want the world to believe AI is the skeleton key which will unlock the secrets of the universe.

But if the AI can’t explain what it’s “proven,” are we actually any closer to understanding anything, or have we simply built another black box?

That question is top of mind among mathematicians and computer scientists this week after Anthropic researcher Levent Alpöge claimed to have solved a mathematical conjecture so elusive it's gone unproven for more than 150 years. The fact that this note came out one day after Anthropic's record-setting IPO is a coincidence, we're sure.

In a four-page post to his website, the researcher described how with the help of Claude, he believes he has proven the Riemann Hypothesis, one of seven Millennium problems so complex and so important that the Clay Mathematics Institute has offered a $1 million prize to anyone who is successful in solving them.

The Riemann Hypothesis, conjectured by German mathematician Bernhard Riemann in 1859, contends that all nontrivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function – those with a real part between zero and one – have a real part equal to 1/2.

Confused? Don’t worry. We’ve linked to an explainer that goes into more detail here. What's important is that, if the Riemann Hypothesis is right, mathematicians can place tighter bounds on the difference between the actual distribution of prime numbers and mathematical estimates of that distribution.

Researchers have previously attempted to disprove the conjecture through computational brute force, and therein have shown the hypothesis holds to at least 10 trillion zeros. There’s reason to believe that the Riemann Hypothesis is accurate, but the possibility remains that over a sufficiently long series the pattern might diverge. And so, more than 150 years after Riemann published his hypothesis, it has remained just that.

Alpöge’s proof, if it can even be called that, is now the subject of great debate.

The four pages in which Alpöge remarks on the discoveries he and Claude made aren’t exactly a proof, but rather 14 tarballs worth of Lean code totaling roughly 240 MB. Lean is an open source programming language commonly used to help validate mathematical proofs.

The Clay Mathematics Institute, which oversees the seven Millennium Problems, has promoted the Riemann Hypothesis from “unsolved” to “active” status, but it is not yet considered solved. For Clay to consider the problem solved and award the prize, the proposed solution must be published in a qualifying outlet, remain available for scrutiny for at least two years, and receive broad acceptance from the mathematics community.

As it stands, Alpöge describes the 250,000 pages of text generated by the model for its written proof as a “massive unreadable travesty of ‘slop.’”

Not long after Alpöge's remarks and Lean code were published, Stanford mathematician Ravi Vakil released a nearly 1,000-page draft proof by Alessandro Maria Masullo, who earned his PhD at Stanford in 2020. Masullo has reportedly spent more than a decade pursuing the problem.

Frameworks like Lean make mathematics a prime candidate for AI-assisted discovery for many of the same reasons that the technology has proven so successful in software development: code either compiles and runs or it doesn’t.

Code can also be audited and could, with great effort, be deciphered. But a proof written in Lean isn't a mathematical proof in the traditional, human-readable sense. Although its logical validity can be checked by a computer, understanding the reasoning behind it can be another matter. Traditional proofs ideally explain their arguments in a way that other mathematicians can follow. Cantor's Diagonal Argument offers a comparatively accessible example.

While the clock hasn’t even started for the Clay Math Institute, the Lean Focused Research Organization and Mathlib Initiative have already declared the Riemann Hypothesis solved.

“We have full confidence that Alpöge's proof of the Riemann Hypothesis is correct, and we congratulate him on this achievement,” the orgs wrote in a joint statement. “We hereby certify that both the statement and the proof of the Riemann Hypothesis are correct.”

Even if the code ends up being right, if we still don’t know why it’s true, have we really learned anything meaningful?

"At the minute, we don't even know why the theorem is true," Kevin Buzzard, professor of pure mathematics at Imperial College London, said in a recent Al Jazeera interview.

Alpöge’s proof may simply be the product of a black box. To quote the late Arthur C Clarke, “any sufficiently advanced technology is indistinguishable from magic.”

The implications of such a technology aren’t lost on researchers. Months ago, several prominent researchers called for greater scrutiny and oversight regarding the application of AI in mathematics.

“Mathematics produces not only a body of results, but also understanding, clarity, and judgment among the communities of mathematicians who have shaped them, often in the context of their own autonomously guided research,” the Leiden Declaration reads. “This expert knowledge is essential, both to effectively use mathematics, and to continue to articulate new and significant research questions.” ®
