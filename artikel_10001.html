NVIDIA uses fuses for product segmentation, security features, and other fun access control features - and crucially, there are several functions where blowing a fuse on an NVIDIA die can grant you more access, not less. In a properly secured design, a blown fuse should always induce a more restrictive state, because a blown fuse cannot be unblown in an economically feasible way. NVIDIAâs design allows one to relax restrictions by blowing more fuses!

My work builds on prior research by Jon Pry and numerous others who have dedicated much of their time towards better understanding the GA100 die. The GA100 is an NVIDIA die that has been used to power almost 20 different SKUs - the A100 being the most well-known variant. I wonât delve into every SKU, but I will present the GA100 manifestations that matter to my work, covering the different versions of the CMP 170HX and the A100.

As fabbed, the GA100 die has 128 SMs, 12 FBPs, 5 NVDEC units, a PCIe Gen4 x16 controller, and depending on the framebuffer size, comes with 4-6 stacks of HBM co-packaged with the die. NVIDIA uses eFuses for yield-control and product segmentationâundifferentiated GA100s turn into more restrictive variants based on defect testing and manufacturer demand.

In one of the most extreme manifestations of product segmentation, the CMP 170HX, an Ethereum mining card with nominally 8 or 10 GB of application-exposed framebuffer capacity, actually comes with, respectively, ~80 and ~40 GB of HBM onboard! This work chiefly centers around how to fully unlock and use that capacity. Previous work has made great strides in unlocking the GA100 via software methods; VFI takes us across the last mile.

So, how does NVIDIA utilize fuses to produce both a datacenter A100 and a mining CMP card from one die? To understand this, we need to take a closer look at the fuse controller located on the GA100.

The fuse controller is a block that contains 16,384 eFuses (512 rows of 32) and a bunch of logic surrounding that raw fuse macro. Its job is to perform fuse readout and programming, protect the underlying fuses, and provide usable âoption wordsâ to the rest of the die. These option words subsequently decide how the die functions and what features are enabled.

Before we name some of the FF-encoded option words, weâll have to first understand the FF record format in a bit more detail, then talk about the die blocks that govern the features we are interested in.

Each FF row is a 32-bit record. Bits 0â4 select a chain, bit 5 distinguishes FF from IFF (0 for FF), bits 6â15 select a 16-bit block, and bits 16â31 supply that blockâs new value. The JTAG-chain picture from the patent is quite literal here: the controller builds the option data by replaying these records into chains. Fixed locations in each chain correspond to individual option words.

If two records address the same chain and block, the later record wins. For example, 0x0fe805c1 means chain 1, block 23, data 0x0fe8. Appending that record can clear an effective bit set by an earlier FF record. No physical fuse is unblown; a new row replaces the old 16-bit block. This works for FF-backed options, not the regular control fuses in rows 0 and 1.

FBP is the internal NVIDIA term for a âframebuffer partitionâ, with 2 FBPs to one HBM stack. FBPAs are subpartitions, 2 FBPAs to one FBP. PES/ROP are blocks (seemingly graphics-related) that are required for an SM to function.

With this knowledge in hand, what if we could write new FF rows that set the _DISABLE words to be all zeroes, or at least, as permissive as possible given die defects? If we look at how the _DISABLE options are used in conjunction with other elements, weâll quickly see that these _DISABLE words are the primary lever we have in preventing parts of the die from being âfloorsweptâ. After illuminating the âwhat-ifâ, Iâll talk about the âhowâ: the exact application of voltage fault injection and the fuse controller safeguards we can defeat with VFI.

NVIDIA puts these options in charge of setting floorsweep signals, which will render a die element unreachable (and likely powered down). From experiments, I believe the floorsweep lines are specified in RTL, emanating from the fuse controller and terminating in individual functional blocks and controlling some master power/clock domain switch within the block. I have observed the floorsweeping behavior to correspond with very little latency with the value of the fuse controllerâs effective status words for each functional block.

Letâs take FBP floorsweeping, for example. On one of my 8 GB cards, OPT_FBP_DISABLE was 0x852: FBPs 1, 4, 6, and 11 were masked. The separate OPT_FBP_DEFECTIVE mask was 0x840, covering 6 and 11. After I reduced the FF-backed disable mask to 0x840 with a replacement FF row, the extra masks on 1 and 4 were gone, but the defective 6 and 11 stayed disabled. That leaves 10 of the dieâs 12 FBPs in the effective mask. Functionally, FBPs 1 and 4, which refused register reads and writes when OPT_FBP_DISABLE was 0x852, became responsive after this fuse write.

Although the functional blocks can be âunfloorsweptâ with FF writes, this does not fully suffice to render the block fully available to an end application - the default framebuffer geometry configuration uses only a portion of each available HBM stack, for example.

To bypass that, we can utilize what is essentially a small modification to cmpunlockerâs method. The usual driver patch for the 8 -> 64 GB unlocks writes to a particular set of feature configuration words (FBPA_CFG, SM_SPEED_SELECT, etc.). The default values are chosen to succeed for the commonly found variant of the 170HX, which has the _DISABLE FF-derived option words preventing certain functional blocks from being used (any attempt to use them will return floorswept errors). With the unlocking FF burns in place, we can write even more permissive words into the configuration registers, resulting in what is functionally an A100-80G.

With the information we have, it seems straightforward that if one could blow arbitrary FF fuses, any GA100 could be fully unlocked to its maximum capability, as determined by die yield and the populated memory stacks. But how do we blow FF fuses? Letâs take a look at the fuse programming functionality of the fuse controller to understand more.

To request a fuse blow, one must write to the FUSEADDR, FUSEWDATA, and FUSECTRL registers belonging to the fuse controller. Although not nominally accessible to the host or to unprivileged requesters, with the Canary in the Crypto Mine ROP, opening up the responsible privilege level masks is trivial. The specific option word I am referring to is OPT_SECURED_BURN_FEATURE_ENABLE (âSBFEâ), and it is set to be 1 in all production cards. When this option word is clear, fuse programming is allowed in any address. When it is set, only FPF fuses may be blown. To date, no one has found any way to burn FF fuses with SBFE=1, even when operating the fuse controller from HS contexts. It is not clear what âsecured burnâ truly means, I speculate that there must be a software-only way to perform non-FPF burns even with SBFE=1, and we just donât know what the âsecuredâ adjective really means here.

Importantly, as people have figured out on more permissive cards, it is possible to perform FF burns with SBFE=0! And as it turns out, achieving this state is how we achieve the GA100 unlock. After much trial-and-error, I have resorted to inducing this state via voltage fault injection, and I was able to achieve it. Letâs talk some more about voltage glitching.

Much has been said about VFI, and Iâll leave the in-depth explanation to other sources. In short, applying brief pulses of low supply voltage will often cause unexpected behavior in integrated circuits. I asked GPT-6 Astra to design an STM32G4 based board that taps into the PWMVID signal on the CMP PCB. PWMVID is an NVIDIA standard for voltage regulators, and my glitcher intercepts the PWM signal emitted by the GA100 die, and replaces it with its own waveform fed into the MP2888B VRM driver IC. With my setup, I can induce the cardâs own VRM to produce timed glitches for me, which is simpler than MOSFET-based setups and enables faster glitching than attempting to directly drive the PMBus.

The glitcher requires two connections to the PCB, ideally done with magnet/enameled wire. One wire taps NVVDD for voltage sensing, and another wire taps the PWMVID signal. The only other required step for the glitcher to function is the removal of a 0402 0ohm resistor, which will break the connection between the GA100 output PWM pin and the VRM IC. Finding where to place the taps is trivial by tracing the pinout of the MP2888B, specifically its PWMVID and VOSEN pins (the MP2888B shares a footprint with its sibling public chip MP2888A). Test points directly adjacent to the MP2888B enable relatively easy installation of the PWMVID tap.

After S0, the host inspects the card state and decides what to do next; if it involves another glitch, the glitcher board will be commanded over USB with a glitch pattern. Then the host induces S3 with an RTC timer to wake back up in ~3s (empirically derived period for decent stability).

Sometimes the host will fail to return to S0, Iâve remedied this with relegating bookkeeping and glitch campaign preparation to an SBC (Radxa Cubie A5E) which also can drive the motherboard power/reset button pins via GPIO if the glitch target host is unresponsive.

Some glitches make almost every published option word read as zero. In one captured state, 669 of 672 named option reads were zero; the other three were error responses. This state is consistent with a catastrophic failure on behalf of the fuse controller. The card comes up basically unusable, because almost all boot functions are disrupted by the all-zero state. Reading the RIR data on the CMP 170HX 8GB reveals its exact effect: 15 noop repair instructions, and one write that changes the value of a fuse in row 18, whose option word we can monitor. Further, because the RIR sense is consumed internally by the fuse macro instead of directly by the fuse controller, we can observe a disrupted RIR latch by attempting to read the raw row - the observed bitflip remains consistent across the flipped option word as well as the raw fuse row. At this stage, we are still not yet capable of issuing FF writes, because ENABLE_FUSE_PROGRAM=0. I am honestly not sure why ENABLE_FUSE_PROGRAM would ever be zero, given itâs 1 in retail boards, so this almost reads to me like a way to implement a glitch-detection canary (or at least it would be elegant to do so).

Fuse sensing happens one row at a time, with all 32 fuses powered for sensing with one line, meaning it is much more challenging for an attacker to make a single fuse appear as cleared or set. It would make sense, then, to include a âcanaryâ fuse that disables security-critical features (like fuse burning) when it is cleared, because usually the attacker can only manipulate fuse sensing at the row granularity at most. So, when the row0/1 option words come up as all cleared, ENABLE_FUSE_PROGRAM=0 stops us from burning any fuses.

However, take a look at what we have in this state: DISABLE_SW_OVERRIDE=0. This word is normally 1, and it stops us from setting the EN_SW_OVERRIDE register to 1, which then lets us âshadowâ most of the option words exposed by the fuse controller. Doing so involves writing to a register CTRL_OPT_*, corresponding to option word OPT_*, which then determines the effective value STATUS_OPT_*. This is what lets us shadow SBFE to be 0. But, even though we have DSOV=0, ENABLE_FUSE_PROGRAM turns out to be one of the option words that are impossible to shadow, even with DSOV=0. So it seems weâre stuck.

At least thatâs what I thought, and I banged my head against the wall in this state for a while. Then I noticed that there is one fuse controller command I havenât tried issuing yet: SENSE_CTRL. I had refrained from issuing this, because on the face of it it seems like all of the row0/1 (control fuse) derived option words would match their fused values, which would reset SBFE, turn off software option word shadowing, and leave us unable to fuse. To my surprise, when I issued this command, all of the control-fuse-derived options went to their fused value, except DISABLE_SW_OVERRIDE, which remained 0!

With DSOV=0, we can trivially set SBFE to 0, ENABLE_FUSE_PROGRAM is 1 because it matches its fused value, and we are off to the races! One wrinkle remains in that FUSEWDATA may have a locked down PLM, but with DSOV=0, we can just write 0 to OPT_SECURE_FUSE_WDATA_WR_SECURE to set the PLM to be all-ones, opening host access for fusing.

At this point, all thatâs left to do is to calculate _DISABLE words that match the _DEFECTIVE values for the features we are interested in, and issue FF burns. Note that the occupied FF space and defective bits differ per die, so each burn must be die-tailored.

On 8 GB cards with 80 GB intact (seems to be around ~90% of 8 GB cards from my limited sample size), the unlock yields the full 80 GB as a usable framebuffer. Multiple synthetic tests (writing pseudorandom words throughout the full framebuffer and reading them back after L2 invalidation) as well as real workloads (such as vLLM and SGLand) are fully functional. As mentioned, one of my cards has only 64 GB intact. In this case, the hardware unlock yields no framebuffer advantage.

The 170HX is limited to 70 SMs out of the box. In practice, there are almost always >120 SMs non-defective on the die, and the unlock allows using them all. This results in a significant performance uplift, ~1.7x compared to the software-only 170HX unlock, and even an impressive ~1.2x over a retail A100!

The only thing that stops cmpunlocker from advancing beyond PCIe Gen2 are the FF option words. With relevant bits cleared, Gen3/4 train and are confirmed to be fully functional. When combined with the PCIe decoupling capacitor mod, up to PCIe Gen4 x16 is attainable, matching a retail A100 PCIe variant.

Zeroing out the OPT_NVLINK_DISABLE word is only one of the required mods for NVLink to actually work. Because the CMP 170HX PCB is missing the U11 IC (which connects PCIe 3.3v to an NVLink bridge pin), a stock board fails to bring up the reference clock generator on attached NVLink bridges. Simply bridging two of the pins on the footprint of U11 resolves this issue, although it removes whatever regulation or protection was provided by U11. Be careful if youâre bridging GPUs powered by different power supplies, as this will connect the +3.3V rails together unless you leave U11 unpopulated on some of your GPUs! The NVLink bridge works fine if you just patch U11 on one of the boards in the pair.
