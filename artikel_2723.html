Celtics fans from Maine have room in their hearts for Cooper Flagg

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

About 95 minutes before tipoff Sunday night, a group of kids sitting behind the Dallas Mavericks bench were doing everything in their power to get Cooper Flagg's attention.

"WE'RE FROM MAINE!," they yelled toward the court, where Flagg was working through his pregame routine.

I had wandered over after spotting a large Maine state flag draped across the first row of seats. As someone who grew up in Maine, I was naturally curious. And as someone still getting comfortable with the whole credentialed-media experience, I figured hanging around some fellow Mainers might help settle my nerves.

Flagg was not far away, working through jump shots and post moves while the kids continued their campaign to get him to acknowledge them. A woman standing nearby, who appeared to be one of the children's mothers, turned to me.

"It'd be a lot easier if you guys just got him over here so they'd stop yelling," she joked.

I laughed. Sure, it would. But kids yelling at NBA players during warmups is about as common as overpriced beer at TD Garden. Usually, the player keeps working and maybe signs a few things near the entrance tunnel before disappearing into the locker room.

But a few minutes later, Cooper Flagg started walking toward us.

He stepped off the court, made his way over to the first row behind the Mavericks bench, and began signing everything the kids put in front of him. Basketballs, cards, jerseys. And, of course, that giant Maine flag that had caught my attention in the first place.

The kids looked absolutely stunned. I think I did, too.

A TD Garden usher standing beside me watched the interaction with what appeared to be equal astonishment.

He was referring to how unusual it was to see a visiting player walk away from his usual exit route to spend time with fans in the stands.

I'd of course seen players sign autographs before, but this was different. There was something surreal about watching Flagg cross that little boundary separating the court from the rest of us, the place where a kid's basketball dreams come true from the place where most of us spend our lives watching other people live them.

And here he was, standing right in front of a group of kids who'd been shouting that they were from the same state he grew up in.

Kids who just met their idol are then asked to pose for a picture taken by an obscure writer who they've 100% never heard of or care for.

The mother who'd joked about getting Flagg to come over was now thanking him repeatedly, her voice noticeably shaky as he signed her custom jersey.

Her jersey was half Celtics green, half Mavericks blue, with Flagg's name crossing the green and blue divide on the back. I don't know if she had the jersey specially made as some sort of elaborate voodoo ritual to bring Flagg to Boston someday, but I fully support her play at dark magic if that's what was happening.

The jersey perfectly captured the unusual position basketball fans from Maine find ourselves in these days, myself included. We've spent our lives rooting for the Celtics. And now, somehow, one of the brightest stars in the entire NBA is a kid from our humble little state. Someone we can proudly call our own, even when he's wearing the wrong colors.

Turns out, there's enough room in our hearts for both.

I've been a Celtics fan for as long as I can remember. I grew up playing basketball in Maine, eventually winning a high school state championship, and spent much of my childhood imagining what it would be like to play on the same court as the guys I watched on television.

For most kids from Maine, the NBA might as well have been located on another planet. We could drive down to Boston, buy tickets to a Celtics game and watch our favorite players from the stands. But the idea of somebody from Maine actually becoming one of those players, let alone a superstar? Good luck.

A kid from Newport, Maine, went to Montverde, became a superstar at Duke, was selected first overall in the NBA Draft, and suddenly one of the best young basketball players in the world was someone who'd grown up in the same state as us.

I wrote about Flagg's first appearance at TD Garden back in March, which happened to coincide with Jayson Tatum's return from his Achilles injury. It was my first credentialed Celtics game, and I spent much of the night watching Flagg try to navigate a building that was simultaneously celebrating him and hoping he'd lose by 40.

Sunday was his second NBA appearance in Boston, and the novelty clearly hadn't worn off. When the Mavericks were introduced before tipoff, the usual boos greeted the first four Dallas starters. Then the PA announcer called Flagg's name.

The reaction changed immediately. Cheers poured out of the stands. You could practically hear the collective decision being made across TD Garden.

The massive Maine flag wasn't the only sign of Flagg's popularity. Mavericks blue dotted sections of the crowd, mixed in with plenty of Celtics green. Some wore Duke gear, a convenient loophole for fans hoping to support both Flagg and fellow Blue Devil Jayson Tatum.

And then there was that remarkable half-and-half jersey. Celtics green stitched together with Mavericks blue, a combination that felt mildly sacrilegious at first glance. Somewhere, a lifelong Celtics fan sitting behind that woman was probably experiencing mild chest pains.

But the more I thought about it, the more the jersey made perfect sense. You could spend your entire life rooting for Boston and still feel an enormous sense of pride watching someone from your home state succeed elsewhere.

Of course, all that goodwill was about to be tested when Flagg started trying to score against the Celtics.

Sunday's game was supposed to be an opportunity for Flagg to show New England just how much he'd grown since his first appearance in Boston. Instead, the Celtics spent the opening quarter making Dallas look like a group of guys who'd accidentally wandered into the wrong gym.

The 33-point advantage was the largest first-quarter lead in Celtics franchise history. Dallas made two field goals in the entire period.

Derrick White had 11 points by himself. Jordan Walsh was flying around the floor, attacking the basket and helping set the defensive tone. The Celtics looked fresh after several days off, while the Mavericks, playing the second night of a back-to-back, looked like they'd have gladly accepted an earlier flight home.

Flagg missed his first few attempts and went scoreless in the opening quarter. Meanwhile, I was having a wonderful time watching the Celtics.

Because once Boston built a lead of approximately one billion points, I found myself feeling comfortable secretly rooting for one particular Mavericks player.

Cooper, buddy. Come on. Take the shot. Show these people what you can do.

I was surrounded by Celtics reporters, doing my best to look like a normal, impartial member of the media while silently begging a Mavericks player to score. I know we're a humble state. Mainers generally aren't the type to kick down the door and announce their arrival. But really, Cooper. You're supposed to be OUR GUY!

He finally got on the board in the second quarter, backing down Payton Pritchard for a basket and a foul. I swear I could feel the collective exhale of hundreds of Mainers pass through the arena. A few minutes later, he threw down a dunk in transition, drawing another audible reaction from the crowd.

Boston still led by more than 30 points. Everything was fine. I could enjoy watching Flagg do Flagg things without worrying that he'd ruin the Celtics' night. If anything, this was starting to feel like the ideal arrangement for a Celtics fan from Maine.

Boston wins comfortably. Flagg provides a few highlights. Everybody goes home happy. Well, maybe not everybody wearing a Mavericks uniform.

What impressed me was that Flagg kept trying to make the right basketball play, even as the deficit grew increasingly ridiculous. He continued finding open teammates and celebrating their baskets, including a third-quarter alley-oop to Daniel Gafford that earned a little fist pump from Flagg despite Dallas still trailing by around 30.

Mavericks coach Dusty May noticed it, too.

"I do think Coop made a lot of really good decisions with the ball in his hands tonight," May said afterward.

Flagg was playing through an illness, which became noticeably apparent when he addressed reporters after the game. But he didn't seem particularly interested in talking about excuses.

"I need to step up and be a lot better," he said.

That's the kind of answer you want to hear from a player carrying the expectations of an entire NBA franchise. And, apparently, a good portion of Maine's 1.4 million residents.

By the end of the third quarter, Boston led 104–71. Tatum had piled up 32 points, including 21 in the third, and the Celtics were preparing to rest their starters with another game scheduled for Monday.

Flagg was on the bench to begin the fourth quarter, too. I figured that was probably it for his night. And I was a little disappointed.

I'd watched those kids spend their pregame screaming for his attention. I knew people had traveled from Maine to be here. Depending on where you're coming from, getting to TD Garden can be a pretty significant undertaking.

I live in Vermont now, and the drive takes me about three hours. Plenty of the Mainers in attendance had traveled just as far, if not farther, to watch him play. All those miles, all that excitement, and the guy everyone came to see might be finished after three quarters of a game that had never really been competitive.

Then, with roughly eight minutes left in the fourth quarter, something unexpected happened.

I mentioned how surprising the decision seemed to fellow CelticsBlogger Trevor Hass, who was sitting next to me in press row.

"Must be for the fans who made the trek to see him," Trevor suggested.

I have no idea whether Dusty May was thinking about the fans when he sent Flagg back onto the floor. But I liked the theory, and Flagg gave those who stuck around a few more highlights.

He knocked down a beautiful stepback three that drew oohs and ahhs from the crowd, then delivered a lob to a teammate and emphatically celebrated the finish. Keep in mind, the Celtics were still winning by approximately 40 points.

For a few minutes, those of us who'd been quietly hoping to see more from Flagg got our wish. I wondered whether the same kids who'd spent pregame yelling "WE'RE FROM MAINE!" had started another campaign directed toward the Mavericks bench.

Given how well their strategy worked the first time, I wouldn't have put it past them. Maybe that mother had finally figured out the secret to influencing an NBA team's decisions.

Either way, Flagg got a brief fourth-quarter curtain call before checking out for good. He finished with 17 points, three rebounds and four assists in Boston's 131–94 victory. The Celtics had won their sixth straight game. Their starters got plenty of rest, the crowd got Gino Time, and everyone in green had reason to celebrate.

But I couldn't stop thinking about the people who came to see Coop.

After the game, Flagg was asked about the support he receives from the fans who travel to see him play in New England.

"It's special," he said. "Just being able to come here and play and feel that love and support every time is going to be really special. It's something that I'm not going to take for granted. It's such a blessing."

He also acknowledged that playing close to home means something extra, even when he isn't feeling his best.

"Obviously, being back home with a lot of people showing up to watch, it means a little bit more," Flagg said.

I thought about those comments and the people I'd watched throughout the night. By the time the fourth quarter reached its final few minutes, fans were already streaming toward the exits. Fair enough. The Celtics had been winning comfortably since approximately six minutes after tip-off, and getting out of TD Garden after a game can be its own competitive event.

But the fans gathered near the visitors' bench? A lot of them were still there.

Some had come wearing Celtics green, others Mavericks blue. Regardless of which team they wanted to win, they'd come hoping to watch Cooper Flagg do something special.

Even before the final buzzer sounded, fans began gathering around the Mavericks tunnel, holding out jerseys and phones, hoping for one last glimpse of Flagg. The basketball game had been decided ages ago, but plenty of people weren't ready for the Cooper Flagg experience to end.

Cooper Flagg makes his way to the visitors' locker room, but not before a few hundred fans say goodbye.

That felt familiar. After Flagg finished signing autographs before the game, I'd asked the kids with the Maine flag if I could take their picture. They happily obliged, still riding the high of just meeting their idol, though they seemed a little less certain about what exactly I intended to do with it.

I explained that I wrote for CelticsBlog. Blank stares.

I told them they could follow CelticsBlog on Twitter. More blank stares.

Right. Of course. Forgive me for speaking the ancient language of a civilization that apparently no longer exists.

Whatever they thought of the strange CelticsBlog writer asking for their picture, I doubt I did much to diminish the excitement of what they'd just experienced.

And somewhere along the way, a Celtics writer from Maine had wandered over hoping to feel a little more comfortable in an unfamiliar environment, only to find himself witnessing something he wouldn't soon forget.

Maybe that's part of what makes Flagg so special to people from Maine. We've spent our lives traveling to Boston to watch basketball players we admire. Now we get to make that same trip to watch somebody who grew up where we did.

He's wearing the wrong uniform, sure. We'll work on that.

But for now, I'll happily settle for watching the Celtics win by 37, Cooper Flagg get his moments, and a group of kids take home a Maine flag bearing the autograph of a player they'll probably spend the rest of their lives talking about.

For Mainers, Cooper Flagg is worth the traffic.
