Miles Below the Pacific, Rocks Litter the Abyss. Something Is Alive Inside Them—Thousands of Somethings.

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Up to 245 lifeforms can live on a single nodule, but mining could wipe them out before we even name them.

At first glance, polymetallic nodules don’t look like much. To the uninitiated, these strange potato-sized rocks look relatively unremarkable, but everything about them defies such a description—and that’s also why they’re central to a deep-sea controversy that’s been brewing for years.

The problem is twofold. First, these nodules contain metals vital to emerging technologies, including manganese, cobalt, copper, zinc, and iron. Second, most of them sit in the middle of the Pacific Ocean, in a region called the Clarion–Clipperton Zone, roughly 4,000 to 6,000 meters (about 2.5 to 3.7 miles) below the surface. Life in estuaries and tidal zones near the coast is adapted to constant change, from daily temperature swings to human interference. The deep ocean is far more fragile. That’s a problem, because there’s immense interest in mining these nodules, and doing so could be far more destructive down there.

Now, a new study published in the journal Scientific Reports has found that in addition to being part of a delicate ecosystem, the nodules are ecosystems themselves, home to dozens or even hundreds of tiny animals. That’s because of their porous internal structure. Each nodule begins as a bit of ancient debris on the seafloor, and dissolved metals slowly build up around it in layers, adding only a few millimeters of rock every million years. One scientist, speaking with Popular Mechanics in 2024 said the process is “like a super slow game of Tetris.”

The new study analyzed a total 244 nodules that contained more than 4,000 individual animals on their surfaces alone. While on average 17 animals lived on each nodule, some contained as many as 245 lifeforms, ranging from single-celled species to nematodes and small crustaceans. The various crevices found throughout the nodules provide the perfect habitat to harbor all these species.

“The crevices in the polymetallic nodules function as small shelters and provide home to animals that we do not encounter in the soft sediment of the seafloor,” said Coral Diaz-Recio Lorenzo, a co-lead author of the study from the Royal Netherlands Institute for Sea Research (NIOZ), in a press statement.

The surrounding sediment held more life overall, but nodules hosted their own distinct communities. They were similar from one nodule to the next and noticeably different from those in the mud. And some of those residents seem to live nowhere else. One species, a single-celled organism called Thurammina sp. 1, appears perfectly adapted to life on the nodules. If the nodules were mined, it could disappear along with an ecosystem that took millions of years to build.

“Our findings point out that commercial mining may result in an irreversible loss of undescribed and unique biodiversity associated to nodules,” Sabine Gollner, also from the Royal Netherlands Institute for Sea Research and senior author of the study, said. “This needs to be considered in environmental management of deep-sea mining.”

But the creatures that depend on the nodules aren't the only reason to think twice about mining them. In 2024, a team of scientists from the Scottish Association for Marine Science and Northwestern University found possible evidence that the nodules produced “dark oxygen,” a rare form of oxygen production that doesn’t rely on sunlight. The Mining Company—which initially funded that research—and other scientists refuted those findings, while the initial research team stood behind their work. They’re currently undertaking another expedition to the CCZ to study this oxygen-producing phenomenon.

Dark oxygen or no, it’s increasingly clear that these nodules are part of a long-standing ecosystem whose history began long before the first hominin appeared on Earth—let alone Homo sapiens. Will this unique habitat fall prey to our insatiable hunger for progress? Well, that remains to be seen.
