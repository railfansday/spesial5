How the Dylan Tombides Foundation is educating footballers about testicular cancer

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

15 years ago, a random drugs test at the Under-17 World Cup revealed that West Ham striker and Australian youth international Dylan Tombides had testicular cancer. He would pass away three years later at the age of 20.

Originally misdiagnosed by his GP as a cyst, Tombides’ mother, Tracy, and brother, Taylor, are now on a mission to minimise the likelihood of such a mistake happening again by getting people to know what to look for.

Testicular cancer is the most common cancer in males aged 15 to 44, but highly treatable and in most cases curable when detected early - so education is key to saving lives.

That is why the DT38 Foundation was set up in Dylan’s name and why Tracy and Taylor, in partnership with FIFA Medical, are explaining to players at this year’s U17 World Cup in Doha why regular self-examination matters and how to carry it out.

“Getting the call from FIFA was a massive opportunity and an amazing thing for us and for the charity,” Taylor tells Standard Sport.

“Obviously, it’s where Dylan last kind of played healthy before his diagnosis. So to come back to where it all started for us as a family is a grateful honour.”

Tracy and Taylor have spoken to more than 1,200 players across the tournament, and their story has really hit home.

“One of the young players stood in front of me and said, ‘I’m so sorry for your loss. I’m sorry your son isn’t coming home to you. We are so grateful that you are telling us all about it’,” Tracy says.

“(We are) very grateful to have this opportunity to educate 1,200 players out here this fortnight, that hopefully will go home and pass on the message of the signs and symptoms of testicular cancer and the importance of an ultrasound scan.”

The DT38 foundation have a campaign called ‘Check One Two’, encouraging men to check themselves on the first day of every month.

Dylan’s nickname - ‘Didge’ - also provides an acronym and hashtag for the foundation: Delay Is Deadly, Get Educated.

FIFA medical director Andy Massey has worked with the DT38 Foundation to get Tracy and Taylor talking to players at the U17 World Cup and says it is an initiative that he hopes to continue year on year.

“What I would love to do is carry this on next year for the Under-17s,” Massey says.

“It’s an annual event now (the World Cup) for Under-17s. We’re going to be in the same place until 2029, and it would be fantastic to do it (talk to players) again for next year and the year after.”

We’re not just affecting one life, we’re affecting a whole generation of lives, which is the biggest part of it

Massey continued by underlining the value of the work Tracy and Taylor are doing, with free scans now on offer to players during the tournament.

“We don’t want this to be a box-ticking exercise. We don’t want this to be a, ‘Let’s pat ourselves on the back, we’re working for a charity’.

“This is one of the biggest things that I think I’ve ever done in my sports medicine career because it has such a reach.”

The DT38 Foundation are starting with the U17 World Cup, but ultimately Taylor hopes their work will have an impact down the generations.

“The effect is lasting,” he says. “If we can teach these kids now, we know the next generation, they’ll teach their kids and they’ll teach their kids and it’ll be continuous.

“We’re not just affecting one life, we’re affecting a whole generation of lives, which is the biggest part of it. Without FIFA, we couldn’t touch as many people as we have.”

Visit dt38.org to find out more about the DT38 Foundation, Dylan's story and advice and guidance about testicular cancer.
