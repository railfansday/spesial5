Forget 'Stranger Things,' These 10 Sci-Fi Shows Have Better Mysteries

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

After being mentored by their hero, M. Night Shyamalan, Matt and Ross Duffer felt that they were ready to write and create their own show. After reading the pilot and the pitch book they prepared, Netflix purchased the entire first season of Stranger Things, which would go on to become one of the streaming giant's most successful pop-cultural sensations so far.

There are plenty of reasons why Stranger Things is a masterpiece, including its many enrapturing mysteries; but there are 10 particular sci-fi shows which are even more effective in their approach to mystery. Full of lingering questions that are only occasionally given cathartic answers, these are some of the greatest sci-fi series that the small screen has ever seen.

The incredible Alex Garland began his career as a novelist, which earned him his first screenwriting gig in Danny Boyle's 28 Days Later. Soon enough, he would make the jump to the director's chair with Ex Machina, and six years ago, he dipped his toes into television with Devs, about a software engineer investigating the secretive quantum computing division of her employer after her boyfriend's disappearance.

One of the best-ever sci-fi shows with eight episodes or less, Devs is a miniseries built entirely on mystery, elevating its premise by tying its core narrative puzzle to high-concept physics and existential philosophy. It's thematically layered and full of fascinating characters, but its many complex mysteries are the main reason why it's such a riveting series from beginning to end.

Originally designed by creator Justin Marks as a feature film script about a cross-dimensional story, Counterpart soon evolved into a TV series starring J. K. Simmons in top form. Its narrative follows Howard, a low-level UN bureaucrat in Berlin who discovers that his agency is guarding a gateway to a parallel dimension, forcing him to team up with his own ruthless spy counterpart.

As far as sci-fi spy shows go, it doesn't get much more riveting or complex than Counterpart, one of the most near-perfect yet forgotten thriller shows of the last decade. Like all the best stories about Cold War espionage — a concept that should sound attractive to any Stranger Things fan — Counterpart blends its cleverly constructed sci-fi elements with an exquisitely slow-burning sense of mystery.

Far more than just one of the most fun shows of the 1990s, The X-Files is one of the most groundbreaking and revolutionary series in the history of network television. A pioneer of online fandom culture, it tells the story of two FBI agents, Mulder and Scully. One is a believer in the paranormal, the other an ardent skeptic, and the two investigate bizarre cases while uncovering a vast government cover-up of extraterrestrial life.

The X-Files is the quintessential blueprint for mystery box science fiction, a form of television that's built entirely on constant mysteries, twists, and surprises. Brilliantly episodic but injected with just the right doses of serialization, it's hugely atmospheric and full of mysteries worthy of endlessly theorizing about — which makes it unsurprising that it continues to have one of the most legendary fandoms in modern television history.

Based on the series of novels of the same name by James S. A. Corey (the pen name of Daniel Abraham and Ty Franck), The Expanse is one of those perfect sci-fi shows worth watching over and over again. It's set in a star system full of Cold War tensions between Earth, Mars, and the asteroid belt, where a group uncovers an alien biotech conspiracy that threatens to tear human civilization apart.

This galactic neo-noir is space opera sci-fi through and through, which makes its target demographic fundamentally different from that of Stranger Things; but those whose favorite part of the iconic Netflix original is the escalating mystery and cosmic horror should absolutely love The Expanse. The whole thing is driven by a plot hook that very much feels like it belongs in a classic hardboiled detective novel, and the vast mystery that comes from that hook is incredible.

Based on Graham Yost's trilogy of novels of the same name, Silo is set in a dystopian future where the last few thousand survivors of humanity live in a sprawling underground bunker, where an engineer uncovers dark secrets about their confinement. What ensues is proof that few streaming giants are doing science fiction quite as well as Apple TV right now.

This is one of those Apple TV sci-fi shows where every episode's a masterpiece, and that's largely thanks to how marvelously the writers were able to translate the many mysteries of Yost's books to the small screen. Much like Stranger Things, Silo relies on the premise that the whole world is a lie and our heroes' attempts to discover the truth only keep putting them in further danger, which makes for a naturally enthralling narrative throughout.

Far ahead of its time, The Prisoner is one of the best forgotten shows of the 1960s, a show so auteur-driven and masterfully surreal that it feels like something that was made just a few years ago. Its story follows an unnamed British intelligence agent who abruptly resigns from his post and is then taken to an inescapable coastal village to be interrogated about the exact reason for his departure.

Often described as one of the most brilliantly bizarre and Kafkaesque shows in the history of British sci-fi television, The Prisoner is a must-see for people who loved it when Stranger Things went all in on the natural oddness of its many sci-fi elements. This is the most foundational template for mystery box sci-fi, a psychologically nuanced masterpiece that truly has no equal.

In Fringe, an FBI agent, an institutionalized mad scientist, and his estranged son team up to investigate unexplainable phenomena that trace back to a dangerous parallel universe. What at first started out as more of an episodic and somewhat generic procedural inspired by The X-Files and The Twilight Zone soon transformed into something far more special and uniquely serialized.

As a result of the many transformations that it saw throughout its run, Fringe is not only one of the most brilliant sci-fi shows of modern times, but also one of the most rewatchable. Under the facade of its "monster-of-the-week" format lies a slow-burning mystery with some of the most richly layered world-building of any multiversal sci-fi show in history, and the whole thing remains consistently entertaining throughout the series' entire run.

Back during the 2000s, it felt like everyone and their grandmother was watching and constantly talking about Lost. It was the series that picked up The X-Files' torch as Hollywood's most prominent mystery box show, telling the story of the survivors of a plane crash as they struggle to survive on a dangerous island while uncovering its many mind-bending mysteries.

Even die-hard fans of the show might agree that it jumped the shark a bit toward its conclusion, but it's still one of the most fascinatingly twist-filled shows in the history of sci-fi. Many shows have tried to replace Lost's spot in the pantheon of mystery box sci-fi, but none have quite managed. Every time you think that you're getting more of a feel for what the show is trying to do, it pulls the rug from under your feet with yet another jaw-dropping reveal.

It's a testament to the talent of creators Baran bo Odar and Jantje Friese that Dark, Netflix's first attempt at a German-language series, ended up being one of the most perfect thriller show masterpieces in history. It's set in a small German town, where four interconnected families embark on a desperate search for answers after a child goes missing, uncovering a multi-generational time-travel conspiracy.

When we're talking about the many great time travel and parallel universe sci-fi shows of the streaming era, it doesn't get much better than Dark. Sure, it's a show so filled to the brim with puzzles and mysteries that it's easy to lose track of everything that's going on, but patient viewers will be treated to some downright cathartic reveals. Well before it reaches its finale, Dark makes a rewatch immediately tempting.

If there's any one show worthy of being considered Lost's successor in the space of pop culture phenomena that have redefined the mystery box sci-fi space, it's Apple TV's Severance, a workplace dramedy unlike any other. It's about a group of office workers at a sinister biotech corporation who have undergone a surgical procedure that divides their memories between their work "selves" and their personal lives.

Just as much as it is a deep thematic study of identity and a scathing critique of unchecked capitalist greed, Severance is also one of the most entertaining—and weirdest—sci-fi shows in recent memory. Every time the writers feel kind enough to feed viewers an answer to one of the show's mysteries, they bring up at least a couple more. The result is a show that, so far, has been absolutely impossible to stop watching.
