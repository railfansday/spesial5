How Asking Teams to Flag Problems Earlier Can Create a New Challenge for Leaders

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

You're viewing the US website. Looking for the Site edition?

Identifying risks early isn’t enough if no one has the authority to act.

Opinions expressed by Entrepreneur contributors are their own.

A large company was preparing to move a group of customer-service teams onto a new order-management platform. Two weeks before the first wave, the program director saw a pattern in the integration testing: Orders were still processing, but the time between the customer system and the back-end platform was getting less predictable.

The issue hadn’t caused a failed launch checkpoint, though.

The program director escalated it anyway because resolving it might require the technology team to redirect shared engineering capacity from another priority.

The senior team reviewed the issue and returned it for monitoring because there were more immediate launch risks on the table, and this one had no confirmed business impact yet. The program director kept it visible inside the program, but the engineers who could change the shared capacity remained committed elsewhere.

Nine days later, the same integration delay began appearing often enough to disrupt final practice sessions.

Training teams had to repeat exercises, supervisors lost preparation time, and the technology team had to pull engineers back into work that could have been addressed earlier with more room to move.

By the time the integration problem returned to senior attention, the program had fewer choices.

The delay itself had started consuming time across the rollout. Practice sessions had to be repeated when response times changed from one exercise to the next, and technology specialists were pulled into additional troubleshooting calls to determine whether the variation came from the integration or normal test activity.

Program leaders were now coordinating repeated work across training and technology while the launch window kept narrowing. That repeated work also displaced other readiness activity for the same rollout wave.

Technology specialists who were rechecking integration behavior had less time for remaining validation tasks, while program leads spent more of the day coordinating exceptions instead of closing other open launch items.

The issue was now consuming capacity meant for other launch work. The issue also consumed senior attention at a worse moment.

Earlier, the decision had been whether to free a small amount of shared engineering capacity to investigate a developing risk. Now the decision included training disruption, launch timing and the impact of taking engineers away from other committed work.

The problem hadn’t appeared “suddenly.” The fact is that the cost had grown while the issue sat below the level where the cross-functional authority to change the resource commitment existed.

After the first wave, leadership asked teams to raise developing issues sooner. The program added earlier warning triggers around integration stability and asked workstream leads to bring forward concerns before they threatened a formal milestone.

The next rollout wave produced more early warnings, including smaller variations in system response that would previously have stayed inside the testing team.

The same integration issue also remained under observation. Whenever the delay moved outside its expected range, the program director raised it sooner rather than waiting for the pattern to become persistent.

The volume of developing issues now reaching the senior team was higher. Some required action immediately. Others, including the integration concern, still depended on how the pattern developed over several days.

The strongest issues stayed active because their impact was already clear. A confirmed system defect, a missed supplier commitment or a customer-facing problem was easier to keep in the senior discussion because the consequence was visible.

The integration issue sat in a harder category. It had been raised early, but the team still couldn’t say whether the delay would stabilize, worsen or stay within the range the launch could absorb.

So, the senior team returned it for continued monitoring while keeping attention on issues with clearer impact.

That’s where the “Hidden Problems” leak appears. The issue was surfaced, understood and still lost senior visibility before the organization had enough evidence to treat it as urgent.

Some developing problems belong below the senior team. Local leaders should solve them when the work, resources and authority sit within their control.

The testing team could observe the integration delay, and the program director could adjust local sequencing, but neither could move engineering capacity from another enterprise priority.

That distinction matters because earlier escalation only helps when the issue remains visible at a level with enough authority to act on it. If the issue is sent back to a level that can watch but can’t remove the constraint, the organization has improved detection without improving its ability to respond while options are still open.

In the next rollout wave, the same pattern became easier to see earlier. That didn’t mean it became easier to carry in the senior discussion.

Leadership responded to the first wave by adding earlier indicators. That made the program better at finding developing problems before they became formal failures. It also increased the number of issues competing for the same senior attention.

As more concerns arrived earlier, triage became stricter. Issues with immediate consequences stayed active. Developing issues with uncertain timing or impact were more likely to be returned for monitoring until the picture became clearer.

The integration problem therefore moved through the same cycle again. It was raised sooner, reviewed sooner and returned sooner. Earlier detection improved while the amount of senior attention available to carry every developing issue forward stayed fixed.

The response had strengthened one part of the system and exposed the limit in another. More early warnings entered the senior layer, but the organization still had to decide which ones remained there.

The usual question is whether teams are escalating early enough, and in this case, they were.

The harder test is what happens after a developing issue reaches senior attention while it’s still uncertain. If it requires cross-functional authority, shared resources or a choice that local leaders can’t make, returning it for monitoring leaves the need for senior action unresolved. It delays the point at which that action becomes unavoidable.

By then, the issue usually carries more consequence and fewer options.

The company improved the speed of detection after the first rollout wave. What it hadn’t changed was the amount of leadership attention available to keep unresolved, developing issues active before their cost became obvious.

Earlier escalation works only when the organization can keep the right uncertain problems visible long enough to act before they become urgent.
