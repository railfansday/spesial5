Lufthansa Unveils Spacious Business Class Seats And Starlink For Select A320 Flights

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Lufthansa is finally adding proper business class recliners to a small portion of its Airbus A320 fleet. The new seats will cost extra, even for passengers already booked in business class, but I welcome the option. After years of selling an economy seat with a blocked middle as business class, this is an upgrade worth getting excited about. It is a welcome change for an airline more accustomed to following competitors than setting the standard.

Lufthansa has opened bookings for its new “Premium Business Seat,” which will initially be installed on six Airbus A320ceo aircraft.

Each aircraft will receive eight recliners in the first two rows, arranged 2-2. Features include:

The six aircraft will also receive Starlink Wi-Fi during the retrofit.

These are recliners rather than flat beds, but they will represent a substantial improvement over the standard European business class seat, particularly on longer flights, which for those unaware is simply a regular economy class seat with the adjacent seat blocked.

The first modified aircraft is scheduled to enter service on December 16, 2026, initially operating various routes.

Dedicated deployment begins in February 2027, with seat reservations now available for flights from March 1, 2027:

Look for the Premium Business Seat designation when booking:

Lufthansa is retaining its standard business class offering behind the recliners. Business class passengers can select the larger seat during booking or subsequently through seat reservations, subject to the buy-up price: buying business class will not automatically get you a business class recliner on these routes.

I can already hear the objections: passengers are paying a premium for business class and then being asked to pay again for a better seat. But this is quite different than what intra-Europe business class has been for as long as I can remember on Lufthansa (except for the old A300s) and I would still rather have this option than continue with the blocked-middle arrangement as the only choice.

Lufthansa calls this a “commercial test.” Demand and the response to its booking options will determine whether the concept expands, but I think this concept is going to do great.

I wrote about the Eurowings Premium BIZ concept when it was announced last year. It was rather amusing that the budget carrier would introduce proper narrowbody business class seating before its “full service” Lufthansa Group siblings.

That project was intended to test the concept for other airlines within the group. Now we are seeing a similar approach at Lufthansa itself. The conventional European model has an operational advantage: airlines can move the curtain to adjust the size of business class. Dedicated recliners make that space less flexible.

Installing a small permanent cabin while retaining conventional business class behind it strikes me as a reasonable compromise. Lufthansa can sell the larger seats to passengers willing to pay for them and still expand the rest of business class when demand warrants. I would particularly like to see this spread to longer routes across the group. An empty middle seat is welcome, but it becomes a rather thin comfort proposition when you are spending several hours onboard, especially with those uncomfortable Recaro slimline seats.

One Mile At A Time suggests that Air France should introduce proper business class seats throughout its Airbus A220 fleet. I love that idea…SWISS should do the same.

The A220 offers an especially compelling opportunity because its economy cabin is arranged 2-3. With adjacent seats blocked in business class, Air France sells only three seats per row.

A proper 2-2 business class cabin would allow it to sell four seats across while giving passengers a wider seat. Additional legroom would consume more cabin length, of course, so the calculation is not quite as simple as adding another paying passenger in every existing row, but I think the math would work.

For illustration, replacing three conventional business class rows with two recliner rows would reduce capacity from nine passengers to eight. That is a modest reduction in exchange for a much better product, though the actual economics would depend on the seat dimensions and final layout.

I have already experienced what this could look like on my Bulgaria Air A220-300 flight from Sofia to Amsterdam. The airline offered proper recliners in a 2-2 layout, with leg rests and power outlets. I found it an excellent way to spend three hours flying within Europe.

Air France and SWISS have invested heavily in its premium reputation. A small recliner cabin on the A220 would extend that investment to the European flights that often begin or end a longhaul business class journey.

We do not need a flat bed from Paris to Amsterdam or Zurich to London, but on longer European routes, a comfortable recliner would give me another reason to choose one carrier over another.

Lufthansa will install eight Premium Business Seats on each of six Airbus A320s, with bookings now open for select Frankfurt flights to Athens, Nice, and Cairo from March 2027.

The surcharge will bother some passengers, understandably, but I think it makes sense and am pleased to see Lufthansa offer a better seat and hope the test succeeds.

Eurowings demonstrated a willingness to try something different. Lufthansa is following. Perhaps Air France, SWISS, and others will see an opportunity too. European business class has room to improve, and a seat that is actually different from economy is a very good place to start.
