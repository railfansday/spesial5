The best way to avoid getting scammed is to take advantage of our carefully curated Black Friday Deals. We put the work in to find you the best deals on stargazing equipment, cameras, tech and even toys, double and triple-checking that they're genuine savings from reputable retailers.

And if you're hunting for deals on your own, you should do the same; shop with known and trusted retailers such as Amazon, Best Buy, Target and others. Ideally, you'll want items that are sold and shipped by that retailer; you can usually tell if that's the case by looking at the buy box, the area with 'Buy Now', 'Add to Cart ', or similar.

Some sites, Amazon included, let third-party companies sell through their sites. That's not a scam, and most are legitimate. However, I'd still recommend you check out the seller's feedback and if you see a lot of negative feedback, you should probably steer clear.

Even if the website isn't an imitation, it's important to be wary of sites that pop up offering massive discounts. While lightning deals are absolutely a thing, some of these sites offer fake countdowns in an effort to pressure you into buying goods. If you see one of the best smart telescopes for a quarter of its normal price, for example, you should immediately be suspicious.

Your credit card or debit card may offer some protection if you are scammed but you should always be wary. Other warnings include misspelled product names, stock text, buttons that don't work and other oddities. You can also use a domain name checker to find out how old a website is. If the website was registered just a few months ago, that's a big red flag.

If you're shopping for a Black Friday bargain for the space or sci-fi fan in your life, it's important to keep an eye out for off-brand and fake goods. Not all building block sets are Lego, for example, and there's nothing illegal about other companies making their own sets, but it could lead to disappointment come Christmas Day. And there are also shady companies who will sell direct copies of existing Lego sets. We have a guide to imitation Lego, but the shortcut is to just look out for the Lego logo.

You may come across unfamiliar brands of telescopes, binoculars and other gear. Often, companies will take a generic product, add their own branding, and sell it at a markup. There's no guarantee that product will last and support may well be lacking. Reading a product's feedback can help, but I've also stumbled across many suspicious reviews.

Then there are some products that are just copies of branded goods; forgeries, essentially. I've seen knock-off MicroSD cards, bootleg chargers, and even Celestron has had to warn against fraudulent websites and fake telescopes. The best advice in this regard is to use trusted websites and remember that if a deal is too good to be true, it probably isn't.

Hackers aside, phishing scams are another risk to your online safety and security. If you get an email that claims to be from a retailer, there's the possibility it's a phishing scam, looking to steal your details. Here are some warning signs:

A common phishing scam I've seen, and one which I almost fell for, is fake emails from a shipping company, telling you there's an extra charge on a package. Click through and the scammer will steal your money and/or credit card details. The best option, if you genuinely have ordered something, is to go to the official website you ordered from and click on your order history. And above all, never, ever share your passwords with anyone, friend or otherwise.
