Never Got What You Paid For? Dispute the Credit Card Charge Within 60 Days

If your credit card statement shows a charge for something that never arrived, a purchase billed twice, or the wrong amount, federal law makes your card issuer investigate. Send a written dispute early enough to arrive within 60 days of the first statement showing the charge.

Say you put a $1,800 sofa on a credit card, with delivery promised in six weeks. The delivery date comes and goes, and the store stops returning calls.

By then, your 60-day clock has been running for weeks. It started when the statement listing the charge went out, not when the sofa failed to show.

The Fair Credit Billing Act doesn’t make you settle things with the store first. A promise of a new delivery date is no reason to let that clock run out.

Give the store one written chance to fix it, and save every receipt, email, and chat. If it stalls, go to your card issuer:

The issuer has 30 days to answer your letter in writing and two billing cycles, never more than 90 days, to resolve the dispute. While it investigates, it can’t report you as late for the amount you hold back. The issuer must remove a charge it finds was an error, along with any interest on it, and credit back anything you already paid toward it.

Debit cards don’t carry this right. Debit purchases fall under the Electronic Fund Transfer Act, which covers unauthorized and incorrect transfers but not a seller that never delivers, and the FTC warns you may not get that money back. When you pay now for something that arrives later, use a credit card you pay off in full.
