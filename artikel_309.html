A Power Provider for Meta Urged Regulators to Exempt Data Center Plants From Acid Rain Rules

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

Williams, a provider of gas power to several Meta data centers, met with Environmental Protection Agency leaders in the period leading up to the agency’s recommendation to waive acid rain regulations for data-center power plants, documents obtained by WIRED show.

The oil and gas infrastructure company’s private discussions with the agency come as the Trump administration loosens a number of environmental requirements for data centers and the natural gas plants being built to power them.

“The administration is trying to relax, if not suspend for all intents and purposes, a range of regulatory requirements for these data centers,” says Joseph Goffman, the former assistant administrator at the EPA’s Office of Air and Radiation. “What this administration is trying to do, either explicitly or de facto, is take layers [of regulatory protections] away.”

In response to a list of questions from WIRED, Williams spokesperson Alex Schott said in an emailed statement that the company “is committed to developing energy solutions to enhance America’s position in the global competition for AI, while prioritizing environmental protection—including addressing legislation and regulations to modernize construction and implementation timelines.” Meta declined to comment.

In July, the EPA announced new federal guidance for states around participation in the Acid Rain Program, following inquiries from “companies and States.” The decades-old program was designed to reduce harmful emissions of chemicals that cause acid rain. Power plants that fall under the program need to submit a permit application two years before a facility comes online, and then regularly monitor emissions to ensure compliance.

That month, the EPA said that it recommends that “islanded” power plants—which are increasingly being adopted by data centers across the country—be exempt from the program. Because the program was designed for facilities that sell electricity to the grid, the EPA said, power plants that exclusively power data centers wouldn’t need to comply.

Documents obtained by WIRED from the EPA show that Williams was one of three private companies that asked regulators for guidance on the program.

In a February letter sent to the Utah Department of Environmental Quality and obtained by WIRED, Williams director of permitting Steve Hall wrote that the “current regulatory uncertainty” around power plants being built solely to power data centers has created “obstacles … for data center development in the United States.” The EPA confirmed to WIRED that Williams sent the agency this letter.

Hall wrote that the company believed its power plants “are not subject to the Acid Rain Permit program requirements, as this program was never intended to apply to sources that do not sell power to the public grid.” Getting federal guidance, he wrote, would “accelerate deployment of critical infrastructure” and “support national goals for digital infrastructure and energy reliability in the global AI race.”

The EPA said in its July announcement that the new guidance was in keeping with orders from the White House to encourage data center developers to build their own power, in order to avoid increasing utility rates for consumers. However, data center developers are also increasingly turning to private power because it’s often quicker to build their own infrastructure than wait for a grid connection. These private power plants often create more emissions than many of their on-grid counterparts.

Williams, one of the top oil and gas infrastructure companies in the country, has emerged in recent years as a power provider for data centers. As of November, Williams had at least six data-center power plant projects in states across the country. Meta is the client for at least four of those projects: four in Ohio, and one in Utah.

Calendar invites provided by EPA show that Hall and other executives were invited to at least two meetings with EPA officials, including at least one with Aaron Szabo, then the chief of the agency’s Air office, earlier this year. In an external meeting request form filed with the agency in April, Williams indicated that its request to discuss projects in Ohio and Utah—sites where it is building power plants for Meta—was “urgent.”

The EPA confirmed to WIRED that Williams “requested regulatory guidance for both projects” in Ohio and Utah, and that the meetings “broadly discussed air regulations.”

“EPA program offices regularly meet with stakeholders in their areas of jurisdiction,” the agency added.

Williams’ materials submitted to regulators did not specifically reference its relationship with Meta. But in its official letter on the rule recommendations, the EPA used an example provided by an unnamed company that is building a planned 500-megawatt power plant of simple-cycle natural gas engines “to exclusively power an adjacent, third-party-owned data center.” Williams’ project in Utah and two of its projects in Ohio to power Meta data centers are around 500 megawatts each.

Williams first publicly proposed the exemption to the Acid Rain Program in September 2025 in testimony to the House of Representatives from Mark Gebbia, then the company’s vice president of environmental, permitting, and regulatory affairs. In the testimony, Gebbia made several suggestions as to how EPA should change or clarify the implementation of the Clean Air Act, including exempting behind-the-meter data centers from Acid Rain Program requirements. He noted that the long timelines for the permits are “not feasible given the urgent need to install and operate gas-fired generation for AI data centers.”

The Acid Rain Program has long been considered a regulatory success that’s ensured coal plants reduce emissions of sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides, pollutants that help create acid rain. The law created a cap-and-trade program for power plants, allowing them to buy and sell credits for emissions to keep national levels of the pollutants low.

Natural gas plants emit lower levels of these pollutants than traditional coal-fired power plants. The permit application for Williams’ facility to power Meta’s data center in Utah, for example, shows that it could emit a maximum of around 100 tons of nitrogen oxide and 11 tons of sulfur dioxide each year. A 500-megawatt coal plant, meanwhile, can emit thousands of tons per year of each of those pollutants.

Other local and national restrictions may limit how much of these pollutants come from natural gas plants. But Acid Rain Program regulations also created crucial reporting requirements that are now in jeopardy for data-center power plants. In the early 1990s, Congress passed an amendment to the Clean Air Act mandating that power facilities monitor and report various emissions in order to establish the program. The reporting requirement covers sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides, as well as carbon dioxide, the main driver of climate change.

The Obama administration later set up a program that would separate CO2 reporting requirements, though the Trump administration has made moves to repeal that program.

Now, exempting data-center power plants from complying with the acid rain regulations may jeopardize how much information regulators eventually get about data center emissions—including CO2 emissions, Goffman says.

“If the Trump administration was not deranged, and the greenhouse gas reporting program was operating, I think the EPA would be either interpreting that program’s existing requirements to cover these gas plants, or they would be promulgating new regulations to ensure these new gas plants were reporting their greenhouse gas emissions,” he says.

Both Goffman and Carrie Jenks, who directs the Environmental and Energy Law Program at Harvard Law School, agree that the EPA’s guidance on the Acid Rain Program for data centers seems to stick to the letter of the law. The program, they say, was explicitly intended to apply only to power plants that sold power to the grid. (Some environmental groups disagree with Goffman and Jenks: Earthjustice and the NRDC sent a letter to the EPA in September challenging the agency’s interpretation of the law in its July recommendation.)

When the program was created in 1990, though, wealthy tech companies weren’t rushing to build hundreds of megawatts of private fossil-fuel power that may not ever be connected to the grid. “No one expected it to be economical to develop a bunch of power plants that wouldn’t sell the product they were creating. No one envisioned this,” Jenks says. The program “was never designed to address behind-the-meter generation,” she says.

Some estimates show that there’s more than 110 gigawatts of planned behind-the-meter gas projects in the US, with some facilities—like Amazon’s 7-gigawatt facility in Texas, and SoftBank’s 9.2-gigawatt project in Ohio—on track to be some of the largest natural gas plants in the world. Even if those facilities don’t technically qualify for the Acid Rain Program because they’re not on the grid, lawmakers could move to change requirements to make sure that these massive facilities are covered by the program.

“Congress should have the ability and the authority to say, we have this situation, it wasn’t anticipated, here is the law,” Jenks says. “If the world were working, that’s what should happen.”
