What do we mean when we talk about news deserts?

The term has become a shorthand for areas devoid of local news coverage. But a new essay from the Independent Journalism Atlas recalls that when Penny Abernathy popularized the term through her research, she used it to describe a more nuanced picture:

Things grow in a desert, she told Medill’s Tim Franklin in 2024, just not as abundantly. It runs off the surface, sinks underground and comes up wherever someone digs.” The team found that “in more places than the desert map shows, someone has picked up a shovel.” (That might help explain why people who live in news deserts don’t feel deprived of local news.) The team identifies a few patterns in how information looks and travels in news deserts, including “surface” information, wells, and springs. Posts from the local sheriff, weather alerts, and obituaries from the funeral home are all examples of “surface” information that “runs off,” Bank writes, typically telling you what happened without telling you why. Wells are newsrooms that form when a local paper shutters. The team points to the Anaconda Weekly Review in Deer Lodge County, Montana, as an example of a well appearing in a county Medill identified as a news desert. Springs, meanwhile, are “harder to spot” than wells because they’re smaller and more ad hoc. The Independent Journalism Atlas applies this label to efforts like the Gallatin County Board Illinois Unofficial Facebook page, which livestreams the board’s public meetings and posts its agendas. The team also paid attention to coverage of news deserts by outlets in neighboring counties. They found high school sports are often a gateway to coverage, pointing to a channel covering southern Illinois high school sports that interviewed the principal in Harrisburg, the seat of Saline County, another news desert county.
