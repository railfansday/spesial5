Data center policy dominated the midterms. The 'Virginia of Europe' is the latest battleground

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Spain's government is seeking to tighten restrictions on one of Europe's fastest-growing data center booms, as the country's Socialist prime minister prepares to fight an election. Investors like Amazon, Microsoft and Blackstone-backed QTS are on tenterhooks.

The measure, which doesn't need parliamentary approval, could be approved by Pedro Sánchez's cabinet by next week, and is widely expected to pass ahead of the Nov. 29 elections.

It could potentially cost one of Europe's fastest-growing tech regions billions of dollars in investment.

The government first introduced the decree in August, partly in response to concerns over energy demand and prices. After fierce opposition, it put forward a revised version on Oct. 26, but the proposal still includes strict renewable-energy requirements.

"These requirements could pose insurmountable barriers and act as an effective moratorium," S&P said in an October report. The proposed rules would require 80% of electricity used by data centers to come from renewable sources matched hour by hour, with projects potentially losing grid access if they fail to comply.

Projects already in development or awaiting grid connections would also be subject to the rules, with a five-year transition period to comply.

Data center build-out has quickly become a prominent political issue around the world. In the U.S. midterm elections, CBS exit polls found data centers were a major influence for more than half of voters.

In Spain, concerns over the water and energy demands of data centers are increasingly part of the election debate as the country faces mounting pressure to expand its electricity grid to meet the demand.

CBRE estimates that while developers have announced 10.5 GW of data center capacity across Spain and Portugal, only about 4.5 GW is currently considered realistically deliverable because of grid, permitting and infrastructure constraints.

Alberto Nadal, the opposition center-right Popular Party's deputy secretary for the economy, has said a conservative government would repeal the decree. Recent polling shows the Popular Party leading, with several seat projections putting the PP and far-right Vox together above a parliamentary majority.

Aragón, a landlocked region in the country's northeast, governed by the Popular Party, has emerged as one of Europe's fastest-growing digital infrastructure hubs and is at the center of opposition to the draft rules.

The region has attracted investors with fast-track permits, abundant renewable energy and land, and international cable connections. Aragón president, Jorge Azcón, wants to turn it into the "Virginia of Europe," referring to the U.S. state with the world's highest density of data center sites.

Aragón is set to receive more than 60 billion euros ($66 billion) in data center and digital infrastructure investment over the next decade.

But concerns are growing among residents over noise, water use and pressure on the electricity grid.

Pilar Alegría, leader of the Socialist Party in Aragón, told a Nov. 15 rally that the election would be decisive for the region's future and for protecting its natural resources.

She highlighted the Ebro River, which runs through Aragón, and raised concerns about the resources used by data centers in a region vulnerable to drought.

Popular Party leader Alberto Núñez Feijóo, speaking the same day, sought to reassure investors.

"It would be a mistake to squander that advantage through poor planning or regulation that creates uncertainty for investors," Feijóo said.

Eva Valle, Aragón's economy minister, has also called for the decree to be scrapped.

"It is necessary to halt the approval of this decree, which abounds in harmful conditions placed on the sector," she said at an AWS-organized event on Oct. 27, accusing the government of pursuing "a political agenda."

Amazon Web Services and Microsoft have said their investment plans in Aragón remain unchanged, while urging Spain to provide long-term legal certainty and regulatory predictability.

Google has already put a multibillion-euro data center project on hold pending greater regulatory certainty, according to a report in Spanish newspaper El Mundo, which cited anonymous sources. Google did not immediately respond to a request for comment from CNBC.

SpainDC, which represents the data-center industry in the country, said the decree would give the group's members the most restrictive data center framework in the European Union and could put 80% to 90% of investment at risk.

"We are going to use all the institutional, regulatory and legal avenues at our disposal" to challenge the decree, said SpainDC president Emilio Díaz.
