What do these Supreme Court plaintiffs have in common?

Last term in Little v. Hecox, which concerned whether states could bar transgender female athletes from participating in girl’s and women’s sports, Justice Sonia Sotomayor asked Idaho Solicitor General Alan Hurst: “Do you dispute that having a case named after you makes your infamy ... live forever? ... You don't think that Brown and any of the other named plaintiffs that we have in famous cases dr[ew] … attention to those people as people?” Sotomayor asked the question in the context of Hecox requesting that the Supreme Court dismiss the case, citing, in part, “negative public scrutiny ... because of this litigation.”

On Tuesdays for the past few months, we have dedicated the “Closer Look” section of SCOTUSblog’s daily newsletter to covering the story of some of the most famous plaintiffs behind landmark Supreme Court cases.

Now 16 installments in, we thought it would be a good time to take a step back and assess what’s been learned. In this article, we offer a glimpse at the lives of a baker, a farmer, an executive director at Planned Parenthood, two students, and a leader of the Ku Klux Klan. But besides having interesting (and sometimes disturbing) life stories, what do they all have in common? Each asked the Supreme Court to answer one of the most fundamental questions of all: Where does government power end and personal liberty begin?

Let’s start with Joseph Lochner. Lochner immigrated from Bavaria, Germany, to the United States at the age of 20 in 1882, and settled in Utica, New York. He sought employment in the bakery industry; around 1890, he opened his own small, family bakeshop in Utica.

During this time, efforts emerged across New York to regulate the conditions of bakery workers after an article written by Edward Marshall, a muckraker, revealed their unsanitary conditions. In 1895, the state passed The Bakeshop Act, which aimed to prevent biscuit, cake, and bread bakers from working more than 10 hours a day or 60 hours a week.

Twice charged with violating this statute, Lochner decided to challenge the law on the grounds that it violated the 14th Amendment’s Due Process Clause, which he argued protected the economic liberty to enter into such a contract with one’s employees.

Lochner ultimately made its way to the Supreme Court and presented the court with the question of whether New York’s law violated Lochner’s fundamental liberty interest. Everyone agreed that, under the powers reserved to the states, New York certainly had some powers to regulate general welfare. The question was whether New York had gone too far in restricting an employer’s freedom to contract with his employees.

For some, the New York statute was perfectly legitimate and a function of the democratic process. As then-future Judge Learned Hand argued, “we should not have the inconsistent spectacle of a government, in theory representative, which distrusted the courage and justice of its representatives, and put its faith in a [judiciary] which was, and ought to be, the least representative of popular feeling." Put simply, if there was a legitimate legislative purpose for the law, then according to Hand, courts should defer to what the elected representatives decided.

In what was seen even at the time as a surprising decision, the Supreme Court sided with Lochner five to four. “The general right to make a contract in relation to his business is part of the liberty of the individual protected by the Fourteenth Amendment of the Federal Constitution,” wrote Justice Rufus Peckham in his majority opinion.

While Peckham conceded there could be grounds for the legislature to implement regulations to improve workplace safety, he also held there was not sufficient evidence that the Bakeshop Act’s hour-limits would actually improve worker health. And if the statute was upheld, Peckham argued that it would open up almost every aspect of life to state regulation.

In dissent, Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes lampooned the majority, writing that “a constitution is not intended to embody a particular economic theory.”

Lochner has since been abandoned and its reasoning rejected by most legal scholars. Yet defenders remain. Law Professor Randy Barnett, for example, argues for a theory that if the government wants to institute a regulation of liberty, “the burden of proof is more or less on the legislature.” This gets at a fundamental debate in constitutional law: Why do certain civil liberties not explicitly mentioned in the Constitution get heightened scrutiny while economic liberty does not?

As for Lochner himself, he returned to private life and continued to run his bakery. He died on December 14, 1939.

Few cases are more detested by constitutional conservatives than Wickard v. Filburn. Roscoe Curtiss Filburn was born on August 2, 1902, in Dayton, Ohio. A fifth-generation farmer, Filburn grew crops and raised livestock. The crops included winter wheat, which was, in part, for personal use.

In a New Deal effort to help prop up the struggling agriculture industry, Congress enacted the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938. The secretary of Agriculture would create a national allotment of acres that could be used to produce wheat. The allotment would then be appropriated to states and ultimately to individual farmers. Filburn’s allotment in 1940 was for 11.1 acres of wheat, but he planted 23 acres of it. Administrators of the AAA fined Filburn 49 cents per excess bushel that he grew and placed a lien of $117.11 on his entire wheat yield.

Filburn challenged the excess penalty in court on the grounds that the statute was passed under the Commerce Clause, which states that Congress shall have the power to “regulate Commerce ... among the several States.” Filburn contended that his activities did not implicate interstate commerce.

If the winter wheat was grown by Filburn and never crossed state lines, could Congress then regulate it?

The Supreme Court sided against Filburn, holding unanimously that his economic activities, taken in the aggregate, would have a substantial effect on interstate commerce. The logic went as follows: If Filburn grew his own wheat, then he would not need to purchase wheat on the open market; and if many farmers did this, it would undermine Congress’ efforts to stabilize the price of crops. “That [Filburn’s] own contribution to the demand for wheat may be trivial by itself is not enough to remove him from the scope of federal regulation where, as here, his contribution, taken together with that of many others similarly situated, is far from trivial,” explained Justice Robert Jackson.

The decision remains controversial to this day (to put it mildly). Critics of Filburn argue that the decision gives Congress near unlimited power to enact regulations under the Commerce Clause, effectively allowing Congress to regulate every aspect of people’s lives and largely mooting the powers reserved to the states. In the late 1990s and early 2000s, the Supreme Court appeared ready to reduce the Commerce Clause’s reach, striking down two statutes as exceeding Congress’ power. But it has not invalidated a law on these grounds since. (Although the Supreme Court in National Federation of Independent Business v. Sebelius held that Congress lacked the power to enact the Affordable Care Act’s individual mandate under the Commerce Clause, it ultimately upheld the mandate under Congress’ power to tax.)

Filburn returned to private life after his Supreme Court case and rarely spoke about it. During the 1950s, he purchased Beverly Shop, a woman’s beauty store, and, in 1966, Filburn convinced the other successors to his grandparent’s 640-acre farm to collectively sell the land to developers. He died on October 4, 1987, at the age of 85.

Estelle Griswold: Griswold v. Connecticut

Estelle Griswold was born on June 8, 1900, in Hartford, Connecticut. A talented singer, she attended The Hartt School of Music, a music conservatory in Hartford. In 1935, she moved to Washington, D.C. for her husband’s job, where she volunteered at a doctor’s office and worked at George Washington University as a lab instructor.

In the 1950s, Griswold moved to New Haven and a few years later was offered the role of executive director of the Planned Parenthood League of Connecticut. In 1961, she opened a birth control clinic in New Haven, along with a doctor, but was convicted and fined $100 in violation of the Barnum Act, a Connecticut law that prohibited the use of “any drug, medicine, article, or instrument” to prevent conception (and, yes, named after that Barnum).

Soon after the decision was announced, the PPLC clinic reopened, but Griswold stepped down as executive director as she had fallen out of favor with some members of the organization due to its continued financial struggles. Griswold passed away on August 13, 1981, in Fort Myers, Florida at age 81 and was posthumously inducted into the Connecticut Women’s Hall of Fame in 1994.

Another major area of constitutional controversy has involved the limits of free speech in the classroom context. Although such cases continue to crop up (BONG HiTS 4 JESUS, anyone?), the modern foundation for them was laid in 1969’s Tinker v. Des Moines. Which brings us to the Tinkers.

John Tinker was born in 1950, and his sister, Mary Beth, was born in 1952. They grew up in Atlantic, Iowa, where their father was the pastor of a Methodist church. In November 1965, John, then a high schooler, attended a rally in opposition to the Vietnam War in Washington, D.C. On the way back to Iowa, John and other protesters agreed to wear black armbands to protest the war and to mourn the deaths on both sides.

More than 50 students across public schools in Des Moines planned to wear black armbands to school on December 16, 1965, in support of Sen. Robert Kennedy’s proposed Christmas truce. However, local principals instituted a policy that all students wearing black armbands would be asked to remove them.

On December 16, 1965, Mary Beth wore a black armband to Warren Harding Junior High School and was suspended after she refused to take it off. The next day, John wore a black armband to North High School and was also suspended after declining to remove it.

In March 1966, the Tinkers sued, arguing the schools violated their constitutional rights. Specifically, the Tinkers contended that the public schools were an arm of the state and they were therefore bound by the First Amendment.

Allan Herrick, arguing for Des Moines at the Supreme Court, contended that the school district must be in a position to “preserve order and to avoid this disturbance and disruption in the school room.” As Justice Thomas would argue decades later in Morse v. Frederick, “through the legal doctrine of in loco parentis [in the place of a parent], courts [historically] upheld the right of schools to discipline students, to enforce rules, and to maintain order.”

In February 1969, the Supreme Court sided with the Tinkers, holding that students do not “shed their constitutional rights to freedom of speech or expression at the schoolhouse gate.” In its decision, written by Justice Abe Fortas, the court established the substantial disruption test, which sets the necessary conditions for regulating student speech. To do so, the school must be able to reasonably foretell “material and substantial interference” with other students’ learning or the school’s operations. Fortas held that the school district failed to show that the Tinkers’ protests resulted in any material disruption.

To this day, Tinker receives mixed reviews. Supporters of an expansive interpretation of the First Amendment tend to support the principles in Tinker and argue that they should be even more broadly applied; critics, such as Justice Thomas, are skeptical that students have First Amendment rights at all. (As Justice Hugo Black put it in his Tinker dissent, “It may be that the Nation has outworn the old-fashioned slogan that ‘children are to be seen, not heard,’ but one may, I hope, be permitted to harbor the thought that taxpayers send children to school on the premise that, at their age, they need to learn, not teach.”).

After the Supreme Court’s decision, John Tinker remained active in anti-Vietnam war demonstrations and later became the manager of KPIP-LP 94.7 FM, a local radio station, which he manages to this day. Mary Beth Tinker pursued a career in medicine, working as a pediatric and emergency room nurse and a nurse practitioner. In 2013, Mary Beth left the nursing field and embarked on the “Tinker Tour,” where she travels the country telling her story and promoting civic education to young people.

Clarence Brandenburg: Brandenburg v. Ohio

Let’s conclude with our least likable plaintiff, who nevertheless established one of the most enduring Supreme Court precedents. Clarence Brandenburg was born on March 3, 1916, in Lee County, Kentucky. A Marine Corps veteran, he worked at a General Electric plant and operated a television repair shop upon returning to civilian life. After declaring personal bankruptcy in 1959, Brandenburg began getting involved with white supremacist organizations and sought to become a leader of the KKK.

In June 1964, Brandenburg spoke at a Klan rally, which was shown on a local television station. Brandenburg said “We're not a revengent organization, but if our President, our Congress, our Supreme Court, continues to suppress the white, Caucasian race, it's possible that there might have to be some revengeance taken. ... We are marching on Congress July the Fourth, four hundred thousand strong.” He was then charged with violating Ohio’s criminal syndicalism statute prohibiting advocating “sabotage, violence, or ... terrorism ... as a means of accomplishing ... political reform,” and later convicted.

Brandenburg appealed, arguing that his speech was protected by the First Amendment. Specifically, he argued that Ohio could not punish him for advocating for unlawful action without showing that his speech was directed towards producing such action.

Leonard Kirschner, arguing on behalf of Ohio at the Supreme Court, asserted that Brandenburg’s actions were not conducted in isolation and could lead to violence, contending that “the statements [made by Brandenburg] to 10 or 20 people can cause one heck of a lot of crime and violence and terrorism in any community. It only takes one person to cause it.”

The Supreme Court reversed Brandenburg’s conviction in 1969 on First Amendment grounds. According to the court, the Ohio statute in question failed to distinguish between an abstract endorsement of unlawful activity and speech directed towards producing imminent, lawless action. In doing so, the court established what became known as the Brandenburg test, which required that speech sufficient for incitement be “directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action” and “likely to incite or produce such action.”

In 1971, Brandenburg was found guilty of harassing his Jewish neighbor by repeatedly calling him and uttering antisemitic slurs. He died on March 29, 1986.

They came from different backgrounds, had very different worldviews, and dealt with different issues, but each of these plaintiffs had one thing in common: pushing up against the limits of state power. How that power manifested itself differed considerably, as did the court’s willingness to reign it in. For example, while the court has advanced a highly protective understanding of the First Amendment, it has been more hesitant to advance protections of economic liberty.

The nature of the plaintiff also mattered less in these cases than one might presume. Filburn was a farmer who grew wheat for his own personal consumption but did not prevail at the court. Brandenburg, on the other hand, expressed white supremacist ideas yet proved successful before the justices, becoming responsible for an entirely new free speech doctrine.

Some may view the court blinding itself to the actual facts on the ground as too simplistic; others as an ideal to aspire to. On that note, we’ll give Justice Elena Kagan the final word: “the question is not do you like this party or do you like that party ... the question is what the law requires.”
