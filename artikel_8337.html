Red’s Meadow Via Rabid Coyote — John Muir Trail Day 19

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

The previous night I spoke with my wife Lee.  I will admit to a little ambivalence.  On the one hand, of course it was great to hear her voice and learn what was happening in the outside world.  On the other hand, the usual lack of communication (except for very short Garmin texts) is a kind of shelter from the problems and worries of that outside world.  It means you can really just focus on putting one foot in front of the other in your thru-hike bubble.

On this occasion she asked me what I was thinking and feeling about the rupture with my sisters.  The honest answer was that it had not been in the front of my mind.  Her raising it, however, pushed it back into my consciousness in a way that led to a more restless night.  This was not necessarily a bad thing.  I had some insights that were useful for going forward.  But it did mean my sleep was not as restful as it might have been, waking in the middle of the night as the bright, bright moon shone down.

It was actually great to have that knoll above the trail all to myself.  For a reason I do not understand, I was a little bit paranoid about bears, so I was extra careful to keep my bear cannister well away from my tent.  As far as I know, no bears visited in the night.

In the morning, the light began to shine, but it was cold, so I just stayed in my sleeping bag, rehearsing the lines for the play I was cast in.  I knew Red’s Meadow was not far, and I could easily make it there well in advance of lunch, even if I had a lazy morning.  Eventually I did get up and did all those things that I do in the morning.

Tent stowed and pack packed. If you look carefully, you can see the trail down below, beyond the boulder.

Ninja Queen told me about her practice of putting on hiking clothes first thing and I have adopted it.  It links to the technique I already used of stuffing my sleeping bag in its sack right away, to ensure that I’m not tempted to go back inside it.  I call these motivational tricks “outsmarting my own stupidity.”

After breakfast it started to warm up a bit, I finished packing, and I hit the road.

The night before, when I considered a 4-5 mile night hike to Red’s Meadow Resort, I worried in case the trail was difficult or dangerous to navigate in the darkness.  In the daylight it was actually a very cruisy trail.

So, I need not have worried, though there was at least one stream-crossing bridge that left a little to be desired!

Despite the damage, this was still the best option for crossing this little stream!

In addition, at night I would have missed the red cones.  I had seen that marking on the map and wondered if it referred to a forest whose trees produce red pine cones.  On the contrary, they were two red cinder cones, a testament to the recent volcanic activity in this area.  Geologically, they were extremely recent, having erupted less than 10,000 years ago.   Seeing them left me with the question, “What gives lava or cinders or basaltic rock a reddish color, as opposed to the black color, which we more usually associate with vulcanism?”

I got no answer then, but now AI tells me:

Basaltic lavas are rich in iron and magnesium. When this iron-rich material is exposed to oxygen and water vapor—either while erupting into the air, interacting with steam, or weathering over time—the iron oxidizes and forms iron oxide minerals (such as hematite), turning the rock red, orange, or rust-brown.

By contrast, the classic black color of fresh basalt and volcanic glass (obsidian) happens when iron- and magnesium-bearing minerals cool quickly with limited oxygen exposure, or while the rock is still in its dense, un-oxidized state.

Most of the trail was downhill, so I was making fast time even without pushing it.  It went down steadily, but not steeply.

View toward rocky crags across the valley

Two male southbound hikers slowed as they approached me and gestured me to stop.  They said, “You should know there’s been a coyote behaving very oddly down at the river where you are headed.  It was unafraid of people, it was out in the open during the daytime, and it seemed to be hanging around for no apparent purpose.  We banged our hiking poles together but that did not chase it away.  It was acting very strangely.”

I replied, “Well, I am not afraid of coyotes, but I’m very afraid of the virus that may be in that coyote’s saliva, because it sounds like it may be rabid.”

I put a rock in my pocket, “just in case,” and continued on.

Shortly, another couple came up the trail, and I asked them whether they had seen anything.

“Oh, we think we saw a fox,” the guy said and he pulled out his phone to show me a picture.  It was definitely a coyote, but I could tell they were Europeans, and surely didn’t know what a coyote was.  It was concerning that it was close enough and tame enough that they could take a picture of it so readily.

Dropping down further, the trail broke out of the forest into a large area where there must have been a significant forest fire.  There were a large number of standing dead trees and a certain amount of underbrush, a sort of desert chaparral kind of vegetation.

Neither the first, nor the last, fire-damaged landscape

Notable was a bushy shrub that was putting out thick clusters of small, dark ash-blue berries.  I did not know what they were, but I  was not about to eat a few berries to find out!

Do leave me a comment if you know what these berries are!  (I suspect Blue Elderberry, Sambucus Cerulea)

In this exposed terrain I encountered yet another couple, two women this time, who also reported seeing this coyote, and saying how seemingly tame it was.  I said, “He didn’t lick you, or, or put any saliva on you, did he?”

They assured me that had not happened, and I was relieved.  They also told me the coyote was at the river crossing, where I would not be going as I was about to turn right off the trail to go to Red’s Meadow.  That was a relief, as a rabid coyote worried me a lot more than a healthy bear!

These women also seemed better informed about the weather report, and that news was not good.  They said, “It looks like this afternoon there’s going to be rain, becoming very steady and maybe even heavy tonight.  Then tomorrow, all day long, there’s a danger of lightning and thunderstorms, possibly severe.”  Yikes!

That sounded a bit ominous.  Now, I don’t mind being in a tent when it’s raining.  In fact, it can be a little bit cozy to be dry and warm while the rain patters on the rain fly above you.

The next morning, however, is different.  Having to pack up when everything is muddy, and the tent is wet?  That I do not enjoy at all!  That’s a major headache, because what do you do?  Do you wait until the sun comes out later in the day, to dry everything out?  Or do you just slog on carrying your wet tent?  Especially with the next day’s forecast being so ominous, spending the night tenting in the rain was unattractive.

Then I remembered that I had cell service the night before.  I thought, “Maybe I can find the phone number for Red’s Meadow and make a booking?”  So, I looked it up and called them from the trail.

London answered.  No, not England, that was just the name of the young woman I spoke to.  She looked it up and reported they still had lodging for that night.  She said the PCT Cabin was available and would be the best deal for a single hiker.  I was set!

After further descent, I saw the sign marking the turn off from the main stem of the John Muir Trail.  I was almost there.

The first thing I saw was a sort of livestock area, and then there was a signpost that indicated the distance in miles to Canada, Mexico, the Devil’s Postpile, and many other places, near and far.  The arrow pointing to Red’s Meadow store was ambiguous, however, and I read it to indicate a path that goes down to a lower parking area.

After a descent of 100 feet or so, I found more signage in the lower parking lot.  This time it showed Red’s Meadow Resort to be up a black, brand new, paved tar road, pointing right back in the direction from which I had just come.  So, I huffed and puffed back up the hill and turned left into Red’s Meadow.  I must admit, the sign had seen better days (see the featured image above).

It is interesting how expectations get set.  I went first to the Muir Trail Ranch with no expectations, and I took that as the resupply stop norm.  Then I went to Vermilion Valley Resort, and I reset that as the new norm.  Now, in Red’s Meadow, it dawned on me that there is no “norm.”  Each is its own separate thing, different and unique.

I found London in the store and paid her the $179 for a night in the cabin.  It was oddly intermediate between the $375 I paid at MTR and the $25 I paid at VVR.  The cabin was not ready yet because they were cleaning it, so I dropped my pack under an awning by the restaurant and explored the place.

There was a sort of central area with picnic tables.  Around it the store was on one side, and the restaurant on the other.  In the surrounding area, there were three stalls for men’s showers, ditto for women’s, and a laundry in between.  Separately, there were latrines for men and women.  The lodging (or at least mine) was just outside this central area.

View across the picnic tables towards the restaurant at Red’s Meadow

As I wandered around, I discovered that Ninja Queen was there!

I thought she was planning to overnight, but she had a very specific date targeted to complete the trail, and was worried about missing that mark.  For that reason, she had picked up her resupply but had packed it up right away.  Now she was shouldering a much heavier pack and continuing on her way.  In no time she was gone.

I realized that I probably would never see her again.  I had her phone number and email, however, for sending photos taken on the trail.

Idly wandering, I went in the store, hoping there might be some boot socks, because mine were getting ever-larger holes in them.  There were none.  What they did have was a good selection of beer at a very reasonable price. I got a local brew to drink later.

Outside the rain was coming in occasional drips, a harbinger of a storm moving in.  When the restaurant opened at noon, I was first in line.  Lunch was a double cheeseburger with a huge chocolate milkshake, and a nice little bowl of potato salad.  The shake was perfect; the rest was fine.

After lunch, I was given the keys to my cabin.  It was clean and appeared well constructed.  It had a small settee and a bunk bed and electricity to charge electronics.  At half the price it was still a nicer space than MTR.

I wore my rain poncho while I did my laundry.  This allowed me to wash everything.  Of course, within three hours on the trail, it’ll be as dirty as if I’d never used the laundry at all; but for those three hours, it will feel blissful.  I took a shower but did not even touch the rat’s nest that was once my ponytail.

Afterwards I did a little more grooming.  I trimmed my mustache, so it is not dragging in my food.  Most important, I cut my nails and filed them with an emery board so there are no jagged, loose edges.  The toenails are key, because hiking with long toenails can lead to all sorts of problems, especially a subungual hematoma, also known as “black toe.”

My only real criticism of Red’s Meadow is that they are open for both lunch and dinner from 12 to 6 p.m.  I had lunch right at 12:15 and then I went for dinner at 5:30, so that I wasn’t ordering the second before they closed.  But that’s not normally a wide enough window for two meals.  Fortunately, with hiker hunger, it was manageable.  I had a tuna melt because I had eaten enough hamburger, and then I had another ginormous milkshake, because you can never have enough chocolate milkshake.

Back in the cabin, I drank my local brew. It was a Yosemite pale ale, produced by the Mammoth Brewing Company from Mammoth Lakes, California.  Mammoth is accessible by bus from Red’s Meadow, and big buses arrive and leave several times a day, but I was not tempted to explore town life.

After laundry, it looks so nice all folded!

I spoke to son Ben and found out some great news: his student loan was canceled, and he was loving his new job.  Less great was that the next day he would start the second session of his new chemotherapy.  He was getting infusions at the hospital every two weeks as he battled his recurrence of colon cancer.

With time to spare, I learned another page of my part in the play “Almost, Maine” that I will begin rehearsing when I get off trail.  I thought learning it on trail would be easy, but most days I have been too tired to give it much attention.  This Nero (“Near Zero”) Day gave me some room to do that.

I also unpacked my resupply bucket and separated what I needed from what I wanted to leave behind.  I would add to their free hiker’s bucket in the morning as I headed out.

Looking ahead, I realized that the end of the trail was not so far off.  Red’s Meadow was my last resupply after all.  The next two days would include some serious elevation gain, culminating in my entering Yosemite when I cross Donahue Pass.  I hoped the residual milkshake effect would power me through!  After that, I had two or three long mileage days to the end in Happy Isles, but the elevation change would be mostly downhill.  The only wild card was the possibility of getting a permit to climb the Half Dome in Yosemite.

The horse (me!) was starting to sniff the barn a little bit.  I hoped to stay healthy for the next several days.  When people on the trail asked me whether I was hiking the whole John Muir Trail, I always answered, “I am trying to.”  Maybe it was superstition, but I never wanted to assume success before achieving it.  Even on the very last day on trail, that would be my answer.

As I prepared for bed, the sound of the raindrops on the roof reminded me that the next day might be wet.  It was also disappointing because, on this night of all nights, a total lunar eclipse would occur.  On a clear night I would have made plans to check it out.  On this night that was hopeless.

Instead, I snuggled in my nice dry cabin and fell asleep.
