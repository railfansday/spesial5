Ravens’ 2026 season has come to a crashing halt

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

It took just one play. As optimistically as it began, the 2026 Ravens season is all but finished. On the first day of November, on his first snap back from a high-ankle sprain, Lamar Jackson injured his right thumb when his throwing hand struck the wrist of Bills pass rusher Greg Rousseau. Jackson knew something was wrong immediately, sprinting straight to the sideline and then to the locker room.

The two-time MVP has since had surgery on his thumb with a late-season return technically in play – if it even matters.

At this point, Jackson is the Baltimore Ravens, and his contract reflects that. Many want to bash the supporting cast, the coaches and the front office, but it just isn't realistic for a team paying a star quarterback that much to also have stars at every other position. What did the Chiefs look like without Patrick Mahomes last year? What did the Bengals look like without Joe Burrow? What would the Bills look like without Josh Allen?

On top of losing Jackson, the Ravens have suffered a rash of injuries elsewhere, primarily on the offensive line. A position group that typically thrives on continuity is operating with a new offensive coordinator, a new offensive line coach, and a rotating door of new starters. This year, the unit has had five different centers and four different right guards. Has the offensive line been good enough? No. But we never got to see the vision. Heck, we never even got to see Jackson and Zay Flowers play a full game together.

The full extent of the Ravens' woes came to a head five days after the Jacksonville Jaguars. With undrafted rookie quarterback Joe Fagnano making his first start, the Ravens were once again trounced at home, this time in a downpour. We all expected the offense to struggle, and predictably, it did. But once again, the defense let the dam break. Is that partly a product of the offense's struggles? Probably. Still, it looked more like a group of unmotivated, aging players struggling to adjust to a new defensive system. Marlon Humphrey and Malaki Starks were both burned for big plays, and the middle of the field — an area that has plagued this defense for years — was wide open all night for Trevor Lawrence.

And yet, Monday night's loss to the Chargers might have been worse. Jacoby Brissett performed admirable in his Ravens debut, but an anemic running game and a defense that once again could not tackle gifted a winless team their first victory.

Unfortunately, the near future doesn't look much brighter, largely because of some questionable decisions by the front office. The Ravens' so-called "star" veterans by salary have underperformed this year. Marlon Humphrey, Roquan Smith, Ronnie Stanley, Mark Andrews and John Simpson make up more than 25% of the Ravens' salary cap. That's not a winning formula.

It's easy to engage in revisionist history on contracts. Humphrey signed his extension in 2020 and Smith signed his in early 2023. Since then, both have followed the natural arc of an NFL second contract: some very good years, and now the back end of their careers. Humphrey was an All-Pro in 2024, and Smith was an All-Pro in 2022 and 2023. Their decline isn't surprising. What's surprising is that the Ravens have forgotten one of Ozzie Newsome's favorite phrases: 'better to move on from a player a year too early rather than a year too late.' It might be two years late for Humphrey now. Smith's decision point should be this offseason. His oversized contract is arguably worse. Fred Warner is probably the only middle linebacker in the NFL worth that kind of money. Humphrey has virtually seen himself out the door in the last week; if the Ravens are committed to change, Smith should be gone this offseason, too.

Now let's talk about Mark Andrews. In my opinion, re-signing him was the worst contract DeCosta has ever handed out. Andrews has been terrible. So bad that he's actively hurting the team. From 2019 to 2025, the Ravens consistently had one of the best tight end rooms in the league. Now it's one of the worst. Meanwhile, as many of us suspected he would, Isaiah Likely is looking like one of the best tight ends in football. For his entire tenure in Baltimore, we assumed he'd be Andrews' successor.

That said, I don't think it's fair to claim the Ravens have simply punted on the trenches. On defense, Trey Hendrickson, Nnamdi Madubuike and Travis Jones all got paid. On the offensive line, the Ravens re-signed Ronnie Stanley, traded picks for center Cam Jurgens, spent their 2026 first-round pick on a guard and drafted Roger Rosengarten in the second round. The problem is that those players aren't playing up to their price. Stanley and Rosengarten are both playing like average tackles. On the interior, Jurgens has been fine, but he hasn't looked like he is worth a second-round pick. John Simpson hasn't been much better than what the Ravens had at guard last year, though he's dealt with a groin injury for most of the season.

The Ravens trust their development process to a fault. Sometimes it's paid off, but there have been far too many instances where it hasn't, especially on the offensive line. Emery Jones Jr., Andrew Vorhees and Daniel Faalele have been abject developmental failures.

Some of it is just bad luck. Ronnie Stanley was one of the best tackles in the NFL before his gruesome ankle injury. Since then, he's played just well enough to earn another contract. But that's the issue: the Ravens have been fine with "just good enough." Rather than proactively building a dominant offensive line, they've settled for adequate. Look at the elite teams in the NFL right now. The Bills, 49ers, Lions, Bears, Broncos and even the Steelers all have top offensive lines. Obviously, if it were that easy, every team would do it. But when you have a superstar quarterback, protecting him is paramount.

In a way, the 2024 season did them in. Despite two below-average guards, the Ravens put together a record-setting offense, so naturally, DeCosta thought it prudent to run it back. Let's face it: when Jackson is healthy, the Ravens have typically had great offenses regardless of who's blocking for him. Even this year, the Ravens ranked second in EPA per play with Jackson and this current group.The downside is that when Jackson is injured or hobbled, the team falls apart because the line can't impose its will on opponents.

If the Ravens are serious about building a championship team, they need to pour as many resources as possible into the trenches. Teams don't fear playing the Ravens anymore because they've become largely a finesse team, with undersized corners, offensive linemen, linebackers and receivers. As a result, they can't salt away games like they used to. In the second half, when opponents know the run is coming, the Ravens get shut down. Their status as one of the league's worst short-yardage teams reflects that.In the early stages of the Lamar Jackson era, the major question was whether the Ravens could play from behind. They were that good at putting opponents away. Now it's the opposite.

DeCosta also has a habit of overcorrecting for past problems. The Ravens struggled to draft and develop receivers, so he spent multiple first-round picks trying to find one instead of trading for an established star, the way the team did with Anquan Boldin. The Ravens went through a few years where they couldn't keep cornerbacks healthy, so he overcorrected again, spending pick after pick on corners to make sure it wouldn't happen again. The better approach: trade for stars at the positions you've historically failed to develop.

DeCosta also has a habit of being too loyal to his players. In one of his first press conferences as general manager, he talked about wanting to stop letting players walk out the door. The result has been long contracts that haven't lived up to their value. Compare that with Eagles GM Howie Roseman, who's an expert at trading players at peak value before they decline. A.J. Brown, whom the Eagles acquired for a first-round pick, was dealt to New England for a first-rounder. Roseman did the same with Jordan Davis and even Cam Jurgens, whom the Ravens probably overpaid for. DeCosta has done the opposite. Perhaps seeing the roster through purple-tinted glasses, he has consistently held on to players too long. Fittingly, the best move of his career may have been trading away Hollywood Brown for an extra first-round pick, which he used to select Tyler Linderbaum.

The Ravens desperately need a reload, along with more juice and playmaking. Aside from Jackson, their only true playmakers are Zay Flowers and Kyle Hamilton. Even Derrick Henry, as great as he's been, needs good blocking to get going. He isn't great at making defenders miss at the line of scrimmage or creating something out of nothing. Obviously, once he gets past the first level, he's devastating. But he's 32,and I'd love to see the Ravens pair Jackson with a different style of running back — one with three-down ability who can create yardage on his own. I'm not saying they should show Henry the door, but they should start building a contingency plan.

The rest of this season will be about the development of players like Mike Green, Malaki Starks, Ja'Kobi Lane, T.J. Tampa, Zion Young and Trenton Simpson. All have shown flashes, and if a few of them ascend to stardom, the Ravens' future will look much brighter.

The defense also has to improve as a whole. There have been stretches where it's looked good, but too often we see the same issues that have plagued it for the last three years: an inconsistent pass rush, an inability to get off the field on third down and receivers getting too much separation. DeCosta has poured resources into the secondary with all four starters being first-round picks. Unfortunately, the result has been a massive letdown. Humphrey has been a liability for two years, but his running mate, Nate Wiggins, has been far from a star, with a 61.6 grade from Pro Football Focus that currently ranks 53rd out of 110 corners (min. 150 snaps). Malaki Starks, the 2025 first-rounder, has also been inconsistent. Coming out of college, Starks was billed as a versatile chess piece, but he's looked bad in coverage. Ironically, he looks far more comfortable as a box safety, the role Kyle Hamilton typically occupies.

DeCosta and the Ravens have frequently preached that they want a "positionless" defense built on players who can line up all over the field. Unfortunately, very few NFL players are good enough to do that. Hamilton is obviously one of them, but it's becoming clear that Starks isn't, and he should be put in a role that maximizes his skill set.

The 2026 season may be over, and the next few years look bleak right now, but there are some positives as the Ravens move into their next era. First, many of these underperforming veterans will be gone next year, giving the Ravens a chance to get younger. They also have a solid nucleus of players in their prime: Jackson, Ioane, Hamilton, Flowers, Hendrickson and Madubuike. However, there will be questions about some of the other established veterans. Will Ronnie Stanley be in Baltimore next year? What about Derrick Henry?

Lastly, there's the issue of Lamar Jackson. When healthy, Jackson is arguably the best player in the NFL. The problem is that, whether because of shaky protection or otherwise, he simply hasn't been on the field as much as the Ravens need him. This team is built around him. He is the Baltimore Ravens, and the team goes as he goes. Jackson carries a massive cap hit next year, and his no-trade clause gives him all the leverage in negotiations. Heading into next year, DeCosta will probably have to hand him a blank check. We've seen this dynamic play out before: in 2023, Jackson waited until Jalen Hurts signed his extension before agreeing to his own market-setting deal in Baltimore. Owner Steve Bisciotti was adamant at the start of this season that he wants Jackson to be the highest-paid player in the NFL. Unless Jackson doesn't want to be in Baltimore, which would contradict nearly every report about his re-energized demeanor this year, a deal will get done.

It's a disappointing way for this season to go. In the one game this team was truly healthy, it steamrolled the Colts in Week 1. Since then, it's been a revolving door of quarterbacks, receivers and offensive linemen. People will point fingers, and there will be constant speculation about Lamar Jackson's future in Baltimore. Ultimately, though, I view this as a season that had potential but never came together. Hopefully, Eric DeCosta can quickly reload the roster, shed some dead weight and put the Ravens back in contention by the start of 2027.
