Inside "The Tupac Tapes" With Radio Legend Angie Martinez

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Going back to her early days on New York’s Hot 97, longtime radio host and veteran interviewer Angie Martinez has been getting rappers to drop their guard and engage in revealing, insightful conversations for over 30 years. Hot 97 was where you went to see Angie, Funkmaster Flex and the like if you had a new freestyle to get off, news to break, drama to address. But Martinez didn’t just get scoop; she facilitated dialogues that would often show some of the game’s most furtive rappers entirely at ease. Her show was a destination, her reporting was crucial, and her stature was reflected in a moniker: The Voice of New York.

In the years since, Angie has migrated to Power 105.1 while also moving into the podcast space. But there was always one piece of lore that loomed over her career: she was sitting on an unreleased interview with Tupac Shakur conducted shortly before his death in 1996. Now, 30 years later, she’s ready to pull back the curtain with a four-part podcast on Netflix where she plays the tapes for contemporary hip-hop luminaries like Joe Budden, Charlamagne and Big Boy, as well as figures more directly related to the events in question, like Outlawz member Edi I Mean or Biggie’s right-hand-man Lil Cease.

As the story goes: Pac became a fan of Angie and her work while incarcerated in New York, so much so that after his release he invited her to appear in the music video for his song “I Ain’t Mad at Cha.” Angie refused, and when Pac asked why she explained that a figure so closely associated with the New York rap scene appearing in that video as East Coast-West Coast tensions reached a boiling point could only be a bad look. Pac then invited her to his home to air it all out. So the Voice of New York spent over two hours with Pac hoping to come back with an interview that defused any negative energy. What she got instead was a Pac at his most turnt, angry, hurt, and vengeful, with the rapper saying things about Big, Puffy, and other New York rappers that would’ve only escalated those tensions. So Martinez sat on them, until today.

After viewing all four episodes of the podcast, I connected with Angie to find out why this was the right moment to release the interview, how she assembled such an all-star team of contributors, what she makes of the backlash to her decision, and how the The Tupac Tapes might help our fractured media landscape today.

Yeah, yeah. It's wild, right? You'll have one of those, hopefully. You’ll have a streak.

Yeah, for sure. I wasn't expecting how nostalgic... so much came up for me in [this process] because it's like a time capsule. It's not just Pac's story, but it's my story, it's about media, it's about culturally what we were doing, what we cared about [back then], how we were thinking. Nobody was thinking about healing or fucking trauma. We weren't talking about that type of stuff back then, ever.

It's just interesting to look at these tapes through a modern lens. This little blip, this little moment in history, So anyway, that part was really interesting to do with voices of the culture and people who are actually there. It's just trippy. The best word I can say is “trippy.”

Maybe, and people say that a lot, but back then we honestly weren't thinking about that. At that point in culture, we were just starting to pay attention to SoundScan numbers, we were just starting to pay attention to artists going platinum. There was no understanding of, "I'm going to be an icon." Nobody was thinking about that. We loved this shit. We were happy to be in it. We were trying to figure it out. There was no, “If I put this out, it's going to go viral.” So that's the interesting thing to me.

Yes, it could have been a big moment, but we didn't think like that. And that's the conversation to be had now, in terms of media and what we care about and what's in the front of our brain as we're making decisions and telling stories. How does this land? What's the headline? What are the clips? What’s the spin? Back then it was just like, "I'm going to go there and have this conversation, see what's up." End sentence, end thought.

It was. And the game changed so much and people care about different things now. And I get it. That's what happens with evolution, right? I really went there just to try to get people to hear what Pac was saying to me on the phone: "I don't have a problem at all with New York. That's crazy." And I was like, "Oh, shit. Nobody's heard you say that. I could go get you to say that. It would be so great." And then I go there and, obviously, it's not what he said, and that was not his temperament on that day at that time. So it wasn't a hard decision to make because I didn't want to make this worse when I was actually trying to do the opposite. Also, nobody knew what I had. So it wasn't like a public declaration of, "I'm not going to air it." It was just me quietly deciding [not to].

Well, you heard the tapes, right? The answers are there. It's like, you can hear those tapes and you can understand what would've happened, in that climate and at that time especially, but really at any time. It was sensitive. Things were happening. I mean, nobody had died, but violence was happening, gunfire was happening. So it's not too hard to figure out after you’ve heard the tapes.

Yeah, it's hard to hear. I hate that part so much, but it's what was happening at that moment.

And I'm grateful that I was able to share it with Havoc before it came out and get some context from [Outlawz member] Edi I Mean before putting this out. And that was the whole purpose of me doing it in this type of format. If you put that out with no context, everybody who's looking for a hundred clicks this week now has something. So that's why it was so important for me to do it in this manner.

There's been once or twice where I thought about it and I just didn't see it. I just didn't know how. Quietly, I always knew that it was like an artifact, a piece of history. And Tupac is such an important figure, and this moment in time in hip-hop culture was such an important one that it's part of our history. I'm watching museums go up and stories get told or recounted. And then I'm seeing some narratives about the tapes, things that were not true. And I'm like, “You know what? History is history at the end of the day. So here you go, here's the full history.”

It just took me a little while to figure out how to put them out in a respectful and responsible way. And then once I figured out what that format would be, it happened really quickly, honestly. There was a conversation I had with Charlamagne on my podcast about the tapes. And then it was me sitting down with Brittany, my production partner, like, "All right, if we put these out, who could we have conversations with to give people some understanding?" And then I called Netflix. "I'm going to put these tapes out, but before that I want to have some conversations." They got it right away, and they do a lot of stuff with [production company] All the Smoke, so they paired us with them to help. We made a couple calls, got some people in some chairs, hit play on the tapes, and here we are.

This wasn't, like, five-years-in-the-making. It's not a documentary of Pac's life. I keep saying that because it's important to me for people to know, I am not telling the story of Tupac's life. There's plenty of that in the world and you should go down those rabbit holes. But this story that I decided to tell is about these specific tapes in this very specific time capsule and my experience making them, holding them and then releasing them.

I actually wasn't very close with everyone on the panels. These were just people that I've come across in the business or have had different conversations with and, for different reasons, I thought they had a perspective that would be interesting to hear. Wallo has spent so much time in prison—Pac had just come out of prison and he could speak to what that must have felt like for Pac in that moment. Joe Budden and I have had many conversations about Tupac and he's a prominent voice of our culture. Charlamagne as well. Erica Ford helped Pac come up with the Code of the Streets for Thug Life and still works with his family, but she speaks to that grassroots kind of work also threaded into the conversation as well. E.D., I didn't really know well at all. I had met him the day we did these tapes 30 years ago and I really have had no interaction with him since then. So I was really grateful that he agreed to do it because, number one, he was in the room. And it was also because he was close to Pac, so he could really speak to what was going on with him and how Pac was already changing in real time. Monie Love was important to me and my storytelling because what happened to her when she was in Tha Dogg Pound video had a lot to do with why I didn't want to be in [2Pac’s] “I Ain't Mad at Cha” video and why I ultimately wound up with this interview in the first place.

Other than that, it's people who respond to some of the things he was talking about. Pac was talking about building with groups like Wu-Tang Clan. So I called RZA like, "Did you know that Pac was thinking of this?" And he really didn't. Lil' Cease, there's some things he says here about Big. It really was a labor of love from everybody. This is 30 years later. A lot of forgiveness has happened. A lot of, I don't know, growth has happened. Some of it's uncomfortable, and there was some uncomfortable energy.

Crazy, right? I hear that from people who knew Pac well, that that happens often—that there's a lot of godwinks. The lights went out twice. Through this whole process, there's so many things that have happened. I just left LA last night. I'm sitting in the airport and I just had my appendix taken out. I'm tired, I'm getting prepared to drop this and it's been a crazy couple of weeks making this, creating it, releasing it. And then fucking “I Ain't Mad at Cha” starts playing over the fucking speaker of the fucking airport.

I just sat there by myself laughing. Even the timing: we started [working with Netflix] and then the trial date happened.

Yeah. I kind of separate that stuff, and also that's a documentary. This is different. I really was in the little bubble of this story. Honestly, I didn't include any parts of the trial 'cause that's not the story we're telling. So even though there were things going on, it wasn't part of the tapes. We tried to stay focused on what was on the tapes, which was hard because there are so many interesting things about Pac where I wanted to veer off at different times.There's so many ways you could go. But that was my rule of thumb as a creator.

It was important for me to have other people's opinions, people that put time in, people who have history, people who have earned the right to have opinions.

Thanks, man. I'm always my worst critic. I will always be like, “I could have gone two steps higher. Why didn’t I ask him this?” Or, “I should have gone more into what was going on with Pac emotionally. But I didn't even have the tools to do that at that time.

And I know, because of the nature of the way the media is set up now, that people are going to pick the headlines, and the headlines are going to be the tough stuff. But there's a lot of nuance in there. There's a lot of nuance about him saying he was teaching himself the game, how he didn't have a father figure to show him how to do all this stuff. When I hear him say, “I would be sitting in the cell banging my head against the wall, and not figuratively, but literally banging my head against the wall,” that he has a condition where he was grinding his teeth. I hear that differently now. When I was young, I just wanted him to say, "I don't got beef for the whole coast.” I wasn't hearing what was happening to him as a traumatized man. Imagine—he had been shot five times and he had bullet wounds in his head, and he's in prison banging his head against the wall while he's listening to people on the radio popping champagne and living their best life and going on without him.

But because I'm a crazy person, I'd be like, "Damn, there's 10 other people I would've loved to talk to. I need more time. I need more money. I need more resources." I'm always going to want to tell the story bigger. But I'm just going to have to tell another story next. And that is the plan for IRL Productions. I got a battery in my back right now, for real, a different type of battery in my back. And I know I attribute that to the conversations that we've had and to just being able to get this story out in the manner in which we did.

We could have easily stuck you in one of the chairs. [You and I] did that Sprite panel [earlier this year], all those folks are great. There's plenty of voices of the culture that are doing good work and should be lifted up. So it's not that it doesn't exist. The problem is that the floodgates have opened, and we give the same amount of leverage and the same amount of voice to somebody who puts time and effort into a story and tells a story with care and responsibility as we do some guy who gives no fucks, who's looking for a click.

So that's the problem, but we can't let that stop us from pushing forward and doing what we want to do. I'm not somebody that addresses dumb shit, and I have been very tempted throughout this to jump in and be like, "First of all, you, sir, sound ridiculous." People will say things with absolutely zero truth. And some of them know there's zero truth, but they don't care.

Because you say the word “Tupac,” and you're guaranteed clicks. There were plenty of narratives about what I've done with the tapes, that I’ve sold the tapes. Why would I sell the tapes? I would've made people pay for them if I was trying to get a money grab. There's so many people who just rip it apart. It's the nature of the beast right now. So do I think that there are still people that care? Absolutely. I just hope that the other side of this that makes it so hard doesn't make people not want to do this. It's easy to just be a blood sucker. And I get that, if somebody doesn't know me and my history, they don't know me as a human being, their immediate first thought is to think the worst.

There's a lot of reason to be cynical out here. People do a lot of dumb things for dumb reasons. It's not my business or my job to inform or correct people. It's my business to carry on the way I've been carrying on and to continue to try to tell good stories with responsibility and some integrity.

I love hearing about what his visions were for the future, his plans for hip-hop. But it's not just that. It's him laughing. Sometimes he's on one, and every now and then he'll stop and he'll start laughing and he'll be like, "Nah, you know I didn't mean that like that." When you see him crack a little bit. Like, he’ll be yelling at me, and then he says, "Listen, I'm not mad at you.” Just seeing different sides of him was so interesting and meaningful.

Even when he's saying something that I maybe don't agree with, I really am inspired by his conviction. I am really inspired by his willingness to say what he said, stand on what he believes, tell you exactly how he feels. But he wasn't so far gone that it was this one way. There's a little glimpse into how he was evolving in real time, even inside those tapes. It makes me wish I'd have interviewed him a month later, or two months later, or six months later.

The apartment was lovely. It was brown leather couches. He had a sign that said “Thug Mansion” in his bedroom. I think he also had a home in Malibu, but this was his apartment. He had a picture of him and Janet Jackson from Poetic Justice on the table that was right behind us the whole interview. He was a showman, boy, in every sense of the word. He was a full showman who had a lot of heart, who had a lot of big ideas and passion, and man, I'm grateful to have had that little moment in time with him, for sure.
