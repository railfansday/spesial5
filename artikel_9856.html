SpaceX Teams Up With Qualcomm to Build a Nationwide 5G Network

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

The company that put thousands of routers in orbit is now coming down to earth: steel towers, rooftop leases, zoning boards and all. Here's what's planned, how 5G works, and why the hard part isn't the rockets.

Back in February 2020, I wrote a parody called "SpaceX Starlink Master Plan", in the voice of a certain CEO. It copied the "trickle-down" structure of Tesla's master plan, in four steps:

Fake-Elon also bragged that Starlink could beat 5G because the carriers "need to deploy a lot more cell sites," while Starlink doesn't "need to pay site rentals and backhaul." For the astronomers, he promised to paint every satellite chroma green "so they can be easily removed in photoshop."

Six and a half years later, steps 1 and 2 happened, if not quite in that order (consumers came first): millions of pizza-box dishes, on roofs and on boats. Step 3 took a turn I didn't see coming. SpaceX never built a StarPhone; it made every phone a StarPhone, beaming signals straight to the one already in your pocket. And now comes the twist the parody never imagined: SpaceX is about to pay site rentals. Lots of them.

For about 30 years, American mobile service has worked like this: three giant companies own the networks (the airwaves, the radios bolted to the towers, and your monthly bill), and everyone else rents from them. Not even the giants own most of the towers anymore. Over a decade ago they sold or long-term leased them to landlords like American Tower and Crown Castle, and now pay rent like everybody else. Upstarts have tried to break in. Most have ended up as footnotes, or as a Boost Mobile ad.

Now a rocket company wants a go. On November 4, 2026, SpaceX and Qualcomm announced a multi-year deal under which Qualcomm supplies the core radio silicon and software for a nationwide terrestrial 5G network under the Starlink Mobile brand. SpaceX already has phone service coming down from orbit. This time the plan is phone service coming across, from tens of thousands of ordinary cell sites. Construction starts in 2027; when you can actually get service from them, nobody has said.

So: what exactly was announced, how does the technology work, and how many billions of dollars and zoning hearings stand between SpaceX and your SIM card?

Malladi called it "huge," and agreed with Light Reading that it's Qualcomm's biggest network deal since 3G. For a company best known for the modem in your phone, that's a big deal.

This gear has been deployed before, though not at this scale. Vietnam's Viettel runs Qualcomm-based radios across "a few thousand sites," and Qualcomm chips have shown up inside radios from NEC and Mavenir (which has since quit making radios). SpaceX is about to become its first customer at continental scale.

Qualcomm handles the physical layer, the hard real-time signal math. SpaceX is expected to write its own Layer 2, Layer 3 and core network software on top. As Malladi put it, "They still have to do the Layer 2, the Layer 3, and so on." That's very SpaceX: buy the chips and own the software, the same way it treats rocket engines and satellite buses.

Radios are useless without airwaves, and in about 13 months SpaceX has agreed to spend roughly $28 billion on spectrum (not all of it paid yet):

SpaceX is also reportedly (per the Washington Post) in talks to buy Boost Mobile from EchoStar. That would bring an existing customer base, retail distribution, and wholesale roaming deals with AT&T and T-Mobile, two carriers that have so far shown little interest in signing anything directly with Elon Musk.

The U.S. market is a mature three-way oligopoly. Verizon, AT&T and T-Mobile together serve the overwhelming majority of American phone lines, each with roughly 120 to 147 million connections. Cable companies (Comcast's Xfinity Mobile, Charter's Spectrum Mobile, about 23 million lines combined) have become the fastest-growing challengers, but they cheat a little: their consumer service runs on Verizon's network as an MVNO, and they push as much traffic as possible onto their own Wi-Fi (plus some CBRS small cells).

AT&T and Verizon each plan to spend $16–24 billion on their networks in 2026, and T-Mobile about $10 billion. That's the annual fee for staying in this business.

A three-way oligopoly is a comfortable place to be, if you're one of the three. Americans pay some of the highest mobile prices in the developed world:

Notably, South Korea also has just three carriers and still charges far less, so the number of players isn't the whole story. Scale, geography and decades of regulatory choices all play a part.

That pricing turns into money. The Big Three post EBITDA margins in the mid-30s percent, which makes them some of the most profitable mobile operators anywhere. European carriers, squeezed by cutthroat competition, have spent the past decade complaining about the opposite problem.

What do Americans get for the premium? A good network, but not a world-beating one. Opensignal's Global Network Excellence Index ranked the U.S. 14th in the world in late 2025, behind South Korea, among others. Ookla's Speedtest index has put U.S. median mobile download speeds somewhere between 9th and 13th in recent months, far behind the Gulf states and South Korea. And the rural dead zones are still there. In fairness, covering a huge, sparsely populated continent costs more than wiring Seoul. But that gap between price and performance is exactly the opening a fourth carrier is looking for.

The last company to try building a fourth facilities-based network was Dish (later EchoStar). Regulators made it the designated fourth carrier as a condition of the T-Mobile–Sprint merger, which closed in 2020. It built what it billed as the world's first cloud-native Open RAN 5G network and hit its 2023 coverage milestone of 70% of the population (its later deadline was pushed back). Then in 2025 it agreed to sell its spectrum to AT&T and SpaceX, and it is now moving Boost customers onto AT&T's network as a hybrid MVNO. Its wireless unit filed for Chapter 11 in mid-2026.

So the most recent attempt to build a new U.S. carrier ended with a handful of companies dividing up its airwaves, and SpaceX was one of the buyers.

SpaceX isn't going to out-Verizon Verizon in midtown Manhattan in 2028. It's going after the problems the incumbents have never fully solved:

The incumbents, for their part, sound unbothered. "The physics just don't allow direct-to-cell to be a substitute product," said T-Mobile's (outgoing) CFO Peter Osvaldik in September. Verizon CEO Dan Schulman said Starlink "just can't compete without a terrestrial network in urban and suburban." Hence the towers.

A cellular network divides the world into cells, areas each served by a base station. Your phone talks to the nearest one over licensed radio spectrum. The base station passes your data back over backhaul (fiber, or a microwave radio link) to the operator's core network, which handles authentication, billing, mobility, and the connection to the internet. When you move, the network hands you off to the next cell. Every generation since the 1980s (1G analog voice, 2G digital voice and SMS, 3G mobile data, 4G LTE mobile broadband, 5G) has been a better trick for packing more bits into the same air.

5G New Radio (NR) isn't magic. It uses three broad levers:

1. More spectrum, in three flavors. Radio physics forces a trade-off: lower frequencies travel farther and go through walls, while higher frequencies carry more data but give up at the first pane of low-E glass.

2. Denser modulation. Every radio symbol encodes bits through combinations of amplitude and phase. Since 3GPP Release 17, 5G's downlink supports up to 1024-QAM, meaning 10 bits per symbol, but only when the signal is clean enough. It's like a crowded restaurant: the quieter the room, the faster you can talk.

3. Massive MIMO and beamforming. This is the big one. An old 4G antenna is a floodlight: it sprays energy across a whole 120° sector. A 5G massive MIMO panel such as Qualcomm's 64T64R has dozens of small antenna elements. By adjusting the phase of each element's signal, the base station makes the waves add up in some directions and cancel in others. That produces focused beams aimed at individual phones.

Once you can aim beams, you can reuse the same frequencies for several users at the same time, as long as they're in different places. This is spatial multiplexing. Vendor and industry estimates suggest a 64T64R sector can carry roughly 3x the capacity of a 4T4R sector on the same 100 MHz, and give users at the cell edge a signal boost of around 9 dB.

There's a catch that matters a lot for SpaceX. Most massive MIMO deployments use TDD spectrum, where uplink and downlink take turns on the same channel, which makes it easy for the base station to learn the radio channel. SpaceX's spectrum is all FDD: paired blocks with separate uplink and downlink channels, plus an uplink-only slice, and no TDD at all. Massive MIMO is harder to do well on FDD, and the FDD deployments out there today are mostly 32T32R. "If they're spectrum constrained, which they are, then this FDD massive MIMO is the way to get the capacity but especially the coverage benefits," says Omdia analyst Gabriel Brown. Qualcomm's pitch is that it can make 64T64R work well on SpaceX's 2 GHz FDD bands. A lot depends on that being true.

Plus the 5G stuff you've heard about. A standalone 5G core makes network slicing possible (virtual private networks with guaranteed performance), lower latency, and better handling of huge numbers of IoT devices. These matter for enterprises and are mostly invisible to people scrolling TikTok.

SpaceX's existing Direct-to-Cell satellites (about 650 launched since January 2024) work on a seemingly impossible premise: talk to an unmodified smartphone from hundreds of kilometers up.

The satellites make up for it with brute force: huge phased-array antennas (several square meters) and very sensitive receivers, so they can pick up a phone's faint signal from orbit. Each satellite is essentially an eNodeB (an LTE base station) in space, backhauled through laser links to the rest of Starlink. That's the clever part. The phone thinks it's talking to an unusually distant tower in a roaming partner's network.

SpaceX's next-generation satellites, dubbed Starlink Mobile V2 and borrowing technology from its V3 broadband satellites, start launching on Starship around mid-2027. In October 2026 the FCC authorized a constellation of up to 15,000 of them. CFO Johnsen promises a "full 5G quality service," and SpaceX's target is up to 150 Mbps per user at peak. New 3GPP Release 19 phone modems with built-in satellite support, like Qualcomm's X105, should help. Even so, physics has a vote. A satellite beam covering a whole county is one shared cell. It will never match the capacity of a dense mesh of towers in a city.

That's the whole logic of the terrestrial plan: towers for capacity, satellites for coverage, one network for both.

Global RAN equipment is one of the most concentrated markets in tech. According to Dell'Oro and Omdia, the top five vendors (Huawei, Ericsson, Nokia, ZTE and Samsung) took about 95–96% of RAN revenue in 2025, and Dell'Oro says market concentration hit a ten-year high.

The traditional model was vertically integrated: the radio on the tower and the baseband box at the bottom came from the same vendor and spoke a proprietary dialect over the cable between them. Once you'd picked Ericsson, you were an Ericsson shop for a decade.

Open RAN (driven by the O-RAN Alliance) standardizes the interfaces inside the base station so operators can mix vendors. It splits the base station into three parts:

The key standard is the 7.2x fronthaul split, which defines exactly which signal-processing steps happen in the radio and which happen in the server, connected by plain Ethernet (eCPRI). That standard is the only reason a chip company like Qualcomm can sell what is effectively a full base station design to a carrier like SpaceX without Ericsson in the room.

Open RAN's track record is mixed. Rakuten in Japan and Dish in the U.S. (until EchoStar wound its network down) proved it works, though neither proved it wins. Integrating parts from multiple vendors turned out to be expensive and messy, which is why the radios and baseband in AT&T's "Open RAN" come almost entirely from Ericsson. The first third-party radio, from 1FINITY, only made its first call in 2025. SpaceX is taking a third path: one silicon vendor, its own software, and an engineering culture that would rather build than integrate.

Rockets are hard. Getting a conditional use permit from a county planning board in suburban New Jersey may be harder. Here's what's in the way.

TD Cowen says more than $80 billion, and Bernstein $50–130 billion including spectrum. LightShed thinks the initial build could be as little as $10–15 billion. SpaceX has unusual advantages here: a Starlink broadband business that throws off cash, the ability to raise money at an enormous valuation, and a satellite layer that can cover rural areas, so it doesn't have to build towers everywhere the incumbents did.

But deep pockets have a lot of pockets. Since swallowing Elon Musk's xAI in February 2026, SpaceX has been pouring money into AI data centers: capital spending hit a reported $28.5 billion in the first half of 2026, more than triple a year earlier, with most of it going to AI infrastructure such as xAI's Colossus supercomputers in Memphis. Investors knocked the stock down about 13% when that number came out. Every dollar spent on GPUs is a dollar not spent on tower leases. If AI and 5G end up competing for the same budget, it's not obvious the cell towers win.

Here's the number from earlier. SpaceX's terrestrial holdings total roughly 80 MHz, and some of that is uplink-only. T-Mobile has nearly 400 MHz. Verizon and AT&T each have hundreds of MHz too, including wide TDD mid-band channels (C-band, 2.5 GHz, 3.45 GHz) that are ideal for massive MIMO.

Spectrum is capacity. With a fifth of the airwaves, SpaceX has to squeeze far more out of every MHz, which is why 64T64R FDD massive MIMO and Qualcomm's AI-driven beam prediction are central to the plan and not just marketing. Expect SpaceX to show up at every future FCC auction, starting with the upper C-band auction (160 MHz at 3.98–4.14 GHz), due by July 2027. (An AWS-3 re-auction already wrapped up in June 2026, raising about $3.5 billion.)

There's also a band-sharing puzzle: the same 2 GHz spectrum is supposed to serve both towers and satellites. Keeping the two from interfering, especially near the edges of tower coverage where a phone could hear both, takes careful coordination, and it's a novel problem.

Your phone has to support SpaceX's frequencies. The good news is that much of SpaceX's 2 GHz spectrum lines up with 5G band n70 (which Dish got into recent phones, iPhones included) and band n66, and the 800 MHz block sits inside band 26, which nearly every U.S. phone supports. One snag: Apple's phones don't list the 4G LTE version of band 70. The less good news: carrier certification, eSIM provisioning, Wi-Fi calling, VoNR voice, emergency-911 location accuracy and roaming agreements are all tedious, unglamorous work, and any one of them can sink a launch. It helps that Qualcomm makes most of the modems in question.

AT&T, Verizon and T-Mobile are not ignoring the sky. AT&T and Verizon have backed AST SpaceMobile, a direct-to-phone satellite rival. T-Mobile is currently Starlink's own D2C partner, which makes the relationship awkward. In May 2026 the three carriers also agreed to pool spectrum in a joint satellite venture aimed at rural dead zones. SpaceX's CFO reportedly mused that they "wanted to create a cartel, maybe." And they all have one powerful tool: price cuts, funded by networks they paid off years ago.

As recently as September 2026, SpaceX's CFO was publicly hedging on whether it would build a terrestrial network at all, keeping options open for an MVNO arrangement or rooftop "femtocells" next to Starlink dishes. The Qualcomm deal suggests the company has made up its mind. Still, signing a supply contract is the easy part.

SpaceX is doing something no operator has tried: building a cellular network from the sky down, with satellites for coverage, towers for capacity, and Qualcomm silicon to make the towers cheap and power-efficient enough to deploy by the tens of thousands.

The technology is plausible. The spectrum is thin but usable. The money exists, at least for SpaceX. What remains is the unglamorous work that has humbled every challenger before it: leases, permits, crews, phone certification, customer care and price wars.

SpaceX has a habit of turning "impossible" into "late, but done." Phone networks are a slower industry, measured in zoning variances instead of launch windows. Either way, the next few years will be interesting, and probably good news for anyone's phone bill.

Even if SpaceX stumbles, the industry is better off for the attempt. In 2023 I wrote an obituary, "R.I.P. 'Innovation in Mobile' (2015–2023)", blaming three killers: mergers that wiped out independent carriers, the big three swallowing the MVNOs, and spectrum priced beyond any challenger's reach. SpaceX is taking on all three. It has committed roughly $28 billion to spectrum, and, shut out as an MVNO, it is building its own network as an independent carrier. Even if it never wins, it forces everyone else to move. Maybe the obituary was premature.

In my 2020 parody, fake-Elon bragged about never paying for cell sites or backhaul. Real SpaceX is about to pay for both, which is the most grown-up thing a rocket company has ever done. Master plan amendment, Step 3b: rent the towers. If it works, your next dead zone might just be a fond memory.

The chroma-green satellites are still pending. Don't tell anyone.
