Homes for the Holidays: Pros and Cons of Buying as the Year Turns

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

The home-buying journey, while exciting, eventful and usually enjoyable, can also be stressful for various reasons. The same can be said about the holiday season. Indeed, research from the American Psychological Association shows that 89% of U.S. adults experience stress during the holidays.

So might that make buying a home during the holidays double the fun while double the angst? While this season is historically when buyers slow down their home search, there are opportunities for those willing to push through. According to various agents and brokers RISMedia consulted, it all depends on individual expectations and potential changes for various financial criteria. Educating your clients on all these matters ahead of time can render them better glad than sad.

“Buyers who stay active during the holidays are often motivated by timing, whether that’s a relocation, a change in family needs or a desire to get settled before the new year,” says Andy Emory, global real estate advisor at Summit Sotheby’s International Realty in Utah. “Some also see the season as an opportunity, since there are typically fewer active buyers in the market, which can mean less competition.

“In many cases, the buyers who are still touring homes at this time of year are doing so with real intent, not just browsing casually. Others choose to pause until spring simply because the holidays are busy, and they’d rather wait for more inventory and better weather before resuming their search.”

Emory points out that year-end timing can sometimes create more flexibility in negotiations, especially when a seller has a specific reason for moving, such as a job change, financial planning considerations or a major life event.

“Not every seller is under pressure, but those listing during the holidays are often serious about selling, which can make conversations around price, timing or terms a little more productive for qualified buyers,” he says. “The seasonal slowdown can also work in a buyer’s favor, since fewer competing listings and a smaller pool of active shoppers can create a more focused negotiating environment.”

Pam Rosser Thistle, with Berkshire Hathaway HomeServices Fox & Roach, Realtors® in Philadelphia, agrees that buyer activity is slower during the holidays, which can create opportunities for those who are actively looking.

“It’s the second week in January when ‘spring’ market buyers surface, after making New Year’s resolutions and feeling motivated to tackle goals,” she says. “Spring market sellers think they need to wait until true spring to list their home. Consequently, the early spring market sellers are usually rewarded.”

Veronique Perrin, an agent at Christie’s International Real Estate New York, posits that timing is everything, and real estate certainly used to be a very seasonal business.

“Both buyers and sellers could expect everything to be on pause during the holidays,” she says. “That used to be a pretty reliable time of the year when everything was on hold until after the ball dropped in Times Square. But with limited inventory the past couple of years, this is no longer the case.

“When working with sellers, I always encourage them to list ASAP regardless of timing. There is so little available that if you are priced correctly, the response will be immediate. Serious buyers never take a break from their search. They are on top of it constantly, and with technology making it possible to conduct business remotely, there is no excuse. I did several virtual showings last week with investors in Europe who would love to close the year with the right buy.”

Perrin notes that timing comes into play as a year edges closer to the end.

“Buyers definitely have more leverage with listings that have been on the market for a while, as sellers see the end of the year looming,” she says. “That is especially true when the property is a house that will be very costly to maintain during winters that only get more brutal. Price improvements are definitely ramping up in the hope of securing a deal. This time of year, I make sure we are ready, willing and able to take advantage of the fact that some buyers will put their search on hold or not be around.

“When working with buyers, I also touch base with the listing agents of ‘temporarily off market’ options. Those are the best opportunities to put a deal together. Sellers love the idea of never having to get back to it the next year.”

Emory adds that conditions around the end of the year in cold climates can make it harder to fully evaluate a property because snow and ice may obscure important exterior details like landscaping, drainage, grading, rooflines or deferred maintenance.

“Weather can also create scheduling challenges for showings, inspections and travel,” he says. “That’s why buyers should work closely with their agent and inspectors to identify what can be assessed immediately and what may need a closer look once conditions improve. At the same time, winter showings can reveal how a home performs in real conditions, including heating, insulation, natural light and day-to-day livability during colder months.”

Another agent who works in a cold-weather climate is Jeffrey Decatur, with REMAX Capital in upstate New York. He says that homes on the market during holidays will often show at their best. Why? “Because most are decorated to the nines and typically smell good,” he says. “Whether something is cooking in the oven or there is a fresh tree, most people are a little tidier in case company pops by. Typically, there is less buyer competition during the holidays, but there is also less inventory.”

​Agreeing that the major caveat of buying during the holiday season is not being able to see everything outside, Decatur warns that holidays make some things a little easier to conceal.

“I once had a buyer relocating here, and we had about three feet of snow,” he says. “The seller had just listed and pictures had snow. Much to the buyer’s surprise in the spring, there was a beautiful large patio, with a fire pit and tons of landscaping. Not all snow stories work out like that, I am sure.”

Jeremias Maneiro, associate broker at the Maneiro Team at REMAX Realty Group in Rochester, New York, sums up the advantage late-year buyers have.

“The tire-kickers go home for the holidays,” he says. “Anyone out looking at houses in the middle of December is usually out there for a reason, like a job move, a lease ending or a life change. Sellers are the same way. A home on the market on December 20th tends to belong to someone who wants it sold, so there’s often more room for a real conversation.

“Quality over quantity is how I define it. You can also catch the friends and family members that come home for the holidays who are planning on moving back soon.”

Maneiro cautions that while the end of the year can be a great time to buy and sell, the timing can be crunched for several reasons if Dec. 31 is the hoped-for closing date.

“Inspectors, appraisers, lenders, attorneys and title folks all take time off, right when everyone’s trying to close before year-end,” he says. “My advice is to build in some cushion, get the appointments booked the day you go under contract, and find out who’s out and when. Treat Dec. 31st as a wish, not a promise.

“In a snowy market like Rochester, also remember that an inspector can’t fully evaluate a roof under a foot of snow, and plan for that. Working with an attorney that specializes in real estate is more important than ever because that can expedite title and survey work so that you can close on time. If there is financing, you have to meet with the bank ASAP and provide them with all your documents to meet deadlines.”

Standalone houses have their peculiarities for buyers, and so too do condos and co-ops. In New York City and other major cities, many people either own their own units (condos) or a percentage of the building (co-ops). Thus, they must work with HOAs and boards, while facing such financial challenges as assessments.

All of those things should of course be understood before making an offer, as well as what charges may be coming or increased in the new year. Having the right finances to get approved by a building’s board can also be something that must be timed.

“If anything, a buyer may wait to get another year’s earnings documented, meaning if the board asks for two years, they will want that year to be included,” says Vickey Barron, an associate broker with Compass in New York City. “It could work the other way: this year’s earnings aren’t as strong, so they race to buy and close in 2026 so they can submit 2024 and 2025. HOA charges don’t necessarily go up right after the new year; they could go up at any time.”

Morella Machado, a real estate agent at Christie’s International Real Estate New York, posits that New York is a seasonal city, so the timing of an offer for purchase can deliver very different results.

“As a real estate agent, I can see buyers waiting until the new year to obtain more information before making an important purchase decision,” she says. “Buyers nowadays, especially young buyers, are well-informed and have so much access to informational tools that they want to have all the information before moving forward. I think, however, in many instances, waiting for all the information while overlooking the right property available now can deliver mixed results.”

In Machado’s experience, real estate activity in New York substantially decreases during the holidays, which can create excellent negotiating opportunities for buyers.

“A New York seller on the market past mid-December will likely have been on the market for weeks or months and, in a typical year, has been witnessing a substantial decrease in showing traffic,” she says. “This makes sellers more receptive to a negotiation with a serious buyer than they would have been a few weeks earlier.

“I worked on a deal where the buyer initially followed my advice to seal the deal before the new year. She ultimately got cold feet, and when she tried to revive the deal, competition increased for a unit that had been quiet for months and ultimately sold for 4% more than she had offered. Not only did she lose the leverage, but she also lost her dream apartment and substantial time over the next few months waiting for a similar opportunity.”

Buyers should know that more important than the one new yearly maintenance increase is the last five to 10 year increase average. This information is easily obtainable from management and gives a better picture of a building’s financials than one year, which could represent an off year for that specific building.

“Any potential increase could be more than offset by obtaining a strong price during negotiations that favor the buyer, as is during the holidays,” says Machado.

Regarding condo and co-op building fees, Maneiro stresses that waiting for the new number can feel smart, but he thinks it’s the wrong thing to focus on.

“They go up or they don’t, and waiting until January doesn’t protect you from that,” he says. “The better move is to ask for the association’s budget, its reserves and its recent meeting minutes. A small fee increase won’t hurt you nearly as much as a surprise assessment on a building that’s underfunded. Don’t shop the fee. Shop the financial health behind it. Meeting minutes are the move that so many agents miss. You may look at a building that has an affordable HOA but has a seven-figure special assessment that’s coming up and would not be disclosed because it hasn’t passed yet, but the meeting minutes will have a record of it.”
