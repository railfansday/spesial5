Flipping a House Isn’t Paying Like It Used To. Is Renting the Better Bet?

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

The typical home flip generated just $60,526 in gross profit in the second quarter of 2026, down from $71,000 a year earlier, according to new data—before accounting for renovation costs or other expenses.

It's a shrinking cushion for investors who have long relied on a simple formula to fuel their business: Buy low, apply elbow grase, and sell high. But with elevated borrowing costs and renovation expenses eating into margins, there's much less room than there used to be for anything to go wrong.

The report, from real estate data analytics company ATTOM, found that flipping returns have been declining for two years, with the typical gross return falling to 21.5%, from 27.6% a year earlier.

"Flippers are still making money in most markets, but the typical return continues to narrow," Rob Barber, CEO of ATTOM, said via press release. "The second-quarter numbers continue the general downward trend in profit margins and gross profits we have seen over the past two years."

So, why not skip the resale altogether and rent out the property instead?

After all, the promise of passive income—collecting rent every month while building equity—helped fuel a wave of small investors buying up homes earlier this decade when borrowing costs were low and rents were on a meteoric rise.

But the economics have changed for landlords, too.

Potential rental yields declined in more than half of U.S. counties with comparable data between 2025 and 2026, according to separate ATTOM research. That's to say nothing of the work involved in managing a rental property, which, as Realtor.com® previously reported, has proved to be anything but passive.

In a market defined by high carrying costs and shrinking returns, the better strategy may come down to which risks an investor can afford to weather—and for how long.

Despite shrinking profits, an index of fix-and-flip market conditions remained in expansion territory in the third quarter of 2026, according to a November survey of roughly 280 investors from John Burns Research and Consulting and Kiavi.

Some 66% of respondents reported selling most of their properties over the previous 12 months in line with their initial after-repair value (ARV) estimates—the amount a property is expected to fetch after renovations.

“Flipping can be profitable in a hot market with limited inventory, where homes sell quickly and buyers pay a premium for updated properties,” explains Realtor.com economist Jiayi Xu.

By purchasing below market value and making improvements that buyers look for, investors can boost the home's after-repair value and pocket the difference. In the right conditions, that means faster returns than holding the property long term.

And the slowdown may be creating opportunities to buy at a deeper discount.

Competition for fixer-uppers has eased in some markets, particularly Texas and Florida, where 44% and 37% of surveyed investors, respectively, reported less competition than usual, according to the November survey.

"Fewer bidders doesn't make every deal a good one," cautions Charles Goodwin, vice president of investor mortgage sales at Figure, Kiavi's parent company. “What it does is give well-capitalized investors with a defined exit strategy more room to be patient and negotiate. That's where experienced operators tend to separate themselves."

The trouble is, it's becoming harder to accurately predict what that exit will pay.

Four in 10 surveyed flippers reported closing sales at lower prices than a year earlier, according to the November John Burns–Kiavi survey. Meanwhile, 20% said most of their properties sold below their initial ARV estimates.

Among those who fell short of their targets, 84% said they had overestimated the eventual sale price, while 57% reported underestimating their costs.

Goodwin warns against basing resale projections on comparable sales from a stronger market.

"If a deal only works with last spring's comps, it probably doesn't work," he says.

And even when investors get their resale estimates right, renovations can consume a substantial share of the proceeds.

The average renovation cost was approximately $72,000 per flipped home in the third quarter of 2026, down from $80,000 a year earlier. Yet renovations still consumed a larger share of the average sale price, rising from 16% to 17%, according to the survey.

Financing adds another layer of risk, particularly when a project takes longer than expected.

The average effective interest rate reported by surveyed flippers who obtained new financing was 9.3% in the third quarter. At that rate, an investor carrying a hypothetical $320,000 interest-only loan would spend roughly $2,480 in interest for each additional month before selling.

Just one extra month would wipe out more than three times the $800 saved over a six-month hold by securing a loan at 9.3% rather than the previous year's 9.8% rate, according to the survey's calculations.

And even when a flip does succeed, Xu notes that taxes can reduce the final profit. Properties acquired primarily for resale as part of a flipping business generally generate ordinary income rather than qualifying for preferential long-term capital gains rates.

With strong headwinds to flipping a property, renting out a fixer-upper can offer a better risk exposure, especially for investors prepared to hold the property long term, according to Xu.

“If you prefer steady income, long-term wealth building, and lower risk, renting is generally the wiser choice,” she says.

Over time, owners may also benefit from property appreciation and tax deductions associated with operating a rental, making the rental path more attractive for those focused on building wealth.

And while asking rents were in decline for more than three years, the market is beginning to stabilize. The median asking rent rose 0.1% year over year to $1,692 in October 2026, its first annual increase since June 2023, according to the latest rental report from Realtor.com

Of course, renting out a fixer upper isn't entirely without risk.

Potential gross rental yields for three-bedroom properties declined in 54.8% of U.S. counties with comparable data between 2025 and 2026, according to ATTOM's annual single-family rental report.

"Even though rents and wages are rising in many markets, record-high home prices are compressing yields," says Barber.

And those projected yields don't account for the ongoing costs of owning and maintaining the property.

Katie Lyon, a rental property owner and host of the Landlord Diaries podcast, previously told Realtor.com that even profitable long-term rentals can leave landlords with little room for unexpected expenses.

"If the water heater goes out, you're back three years of cash flow," she says.

A simple benchmark can help would-be investors gauge whether a rental deal makes sense. The “1% Rule" suggests that monthly gross rent should equal roughly 1% of the purchase price.

When it comes to fixer-uppers, seasoned investors rely on a similar guiding principle. The "70% Rule," advises paying no more than 70% of a property's after-repair value, minus estimated renovation costs.

Ignoring this benchmark can quickly erode profits, especially in today's market where flip margins are already thin, warns Ian Hart, a seasoned housing investor and founder of Barndominium.org.

And the latest returns suggest that buying at the lowest possible price isn't necessarily the answer.

Homes purchased for $100,000 to $200,000 generated a typical gross flipping return of 28% in the second quarter of 2026, according to ATTOM. By contrast, properties acquired for $50,000 or less generated a typical gross loss of $15,000 before renovation expenses.

The November John Burns–Kiavi survey offers another measure of how cautious investors have become: About two-thirds of respondents said they wouldn't pay more than 70% of a property's estimated ARV, while the average maximum was just 67%.

Perhaps most importantly, though, buyers should look for exit flexibility. The best fixer-upper deals are those that work in more than one scenario—whether as a flip in a hot resale market or a rental in an area with strong tenant demand.
