How companies pick a new CEO: 10 stages of a board's search

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Free daily briefing on global business news.

The CEO search process runs from succession planning and a board committee to interviews, pay talks and an SEC filing — these 10 stages shape how boards decide

Few decisions belong to a board of directors as squarely as the choice of who runs the company. Directors leave budgets and daily operations to management. Hiring, evaluating and replacing the chief executive is a different matter. It is one of the board's core responsibilities, and directors do not hand it to the people the CEO will lead.

The work often begins well before anyone leaves. Many boards keep succession plans ready ahead of any vacancy. One plan covers a sudden departure, when the company needs a leader on short notice. Another covers a planned retirement, when the board has time to prepare an orderly handover.

When a vacancy arrives, a small group of directors usually does most of the work. This can be a special search committee or the existing nominating and governance committee. The committee reports to the full board. The full board makes the final decision. Along the way, many boards bring in an executive search firm to build a list of candidates, approach them quietly and keep the process confidential.

The CEO search process that follows usually moves through a broad sequence. A board settles on what kind of leader the company needs, drawing on its strategy and the challenges ahead. It then weighs internal executives against outside candidates using the same criteria. If the seat is empty, it may name an interim CEO to keep the business running. Finalists typically face several rounds of interviews, followed by assessments and checks. The board then negotiates pay and employment terms. For U.S. public companies, the appointment must be disclosed in a regulatory filing within four business days.

This walkthrough breaks that sequence into 10 stages. The first six follow the core steps of a board's search, from succession planning to the interim leadership question. The last four cover what happens once finalists emerge: interviews, reference and background checks, pay negotiation and the public announcement. Each stage stands on its own. Together they show how a boardroom decision becomes a new name at the top of the company.

CEO succession planning is work a board does before any vacancy exists, and it usually takes two forms. An emergency plan covers a sudden departure. A longer-term plan prepares for a scheduled retirement.

Planning ahead follows from the board's own duty to hire, evaluate and replace the chief executive. Without a plan, directors face that duty under time pressure, with no agreed view of who could step in.

The emergency plan answers a narrow question: who runs the company tomorrow if the CEO cannot? A chief executive can leave without warning through death, serious illness, an abrupt resignation or a dismissal. The emergency plan sets out who would take charge in the short term. Its purpose is continuity rather than a permanent choice.

The long-term plan works on a much longer horizon. It looks ahead to a retirement or a planned handover that the board can see coming. Here the board considers which senior executives could one day be ready for the top role. It also considers what experience they still lack. Boards often use this period to give promising executives wider responsibilities, such as running a larger division or a new region.

Stock exchange rules put the topic on the formal agenda for many listed companies. The New York Stock Exchange's listing standards require listed companies to adopt corporate governance guidelines. Section 303A.09 of the NYSE Listed Company Manual says those guidelines must address management succession. Its commentary says succession planning should include policies for selecting the CEO and for succession in an emergency or on retirement.

Shareholders can raise the issue too. In Staff Legal Bulletin No. 14E, issued in 2009, SEC staff said companies generally could no longer exclude shareholder proposals on CEO succession planning as ordinary business.

The preparation shapes what follows. A board with a current plan already holds a view of its internal bench and of what the company needs from its next leader. That gives any later search a starting point instead of a blank page.

A small committee of directors usually runs the day-to-day work of a CEO search, and the full board keeps the final decision. That committee is often a special search committee formed for the job, or the board's standing nominating and governance committee.

A committee gives the search a working group that can meet on short notice. It also keeps sensitive discussions among a handful of directors.

The choice between the two committee types shapes who does the work. A special search committee is put together for one assignment and exists only for that search. The board can pick its members for relevant experience, such as directors who have run companies themselves.

A nominating and governance committee already exists. It already handles questions about board membership and leadership. Using it means the search begins with a group that has a set mandate and a regular meeting schedule.

For companies listed on the New York Stock Exchange, that standing committee comes with rules attached. Section 303A.04 of the NYSE Listed Company Manual requires listed companies to have a nominating and corporate governance committee. Every member of that committee must be an independent director.

The legal basis for handing work to a committee is also well established. Many U.S. public companies are incorporated in Delaware. Section 141(c) of the Delaware General Corporation Law lets a board designate committees of directors and assign them powers.

In a CEO search, that delegation has limits. The committee reports back to the full board as the process moves along. It narrows the field and brings forward a recommendation. The full board then votes on the appointment.

That split keeps accountability with the whole board. Every director shares responsibility for the choice, not only the directors who sat on the committee. Directors outside the committee can also question the reasoning behind a recommendation before the vote.

The committee also serves as the main point of contact for others involved in the search, from outside advisers to the candidates themselves. Deciding who sits on it is an early step in any search.

Before any candidate names come up, a board typically agrees on a written profile of the CEO role. That profile draws on the company's strategy, its current challenges and the skills and experience the next chief executive will need.

In executive search, this document is often called a position specification. It describes the job as the company needs it done in the years ahead. It does not simply describe how the departing CEO happened to do it.

Strategy is the starting point. A company planning to expand into new international markets may want a leader who has run operations across several countries. A business dealing with falling sales or heavy debt may look for turnaround or restructuring experience. A company shifting toward software or digital products may give technology expertise more weight than before.

Current challenges narrow the picture further. Directors weigh pressures such as regulatory scrutiny, a pending merger, labor relations or demands from investors. Each of these points toward a different kind of background.

The skills section usually covers both track record and leadership qualities. Track record can include responsibility for a profit-and-loss statement, leadership of a large workforce or experience at a public company. Leadership qualities can include how a person builds a team, communicates under pressure and works with a board.

A profile often separates essential requirements from desirable ones. That split matters when no candidate matches every line. It tells directors which gaps they can accept and which would rule a person out.

The document also gives directors a shared yardstick. Board members often arrive with different views of what the company needs. Writing those views down and agreeing on them moves the debate to the start of the process. That happens before personal preferences attach to specific people.

Every later stage of the search measures candidates against this profile. It shapes who is approached, what interviewers ask and how finalists are compared.

A profile is not fixed. If the company's plans change while the search is under way, the board can revise it. When it does, the revised criteria apply to every candidate still in contention.

Many boards bring in an executive search firm to handle the mechanics of a CEO search. The firm helps build the candidate list, keeps the process confidential and makes the first approach to potential candidates.

Four firms are well-known names in board-level searches: Spencer Stuart, Russell Reynolds Associates, Heidrick & Struggles and Korn Ferry. Korn Ferry and Heidrick & Struggles are publicly traded companies. Spencer Stuart and Russell Reynolds Associates are privately held. Some boards use smaller boutique firms instead. Others run the search entirely on their own.

In a CEO search, the client is the board rather than the management team. The search firm takes direction from the directors leading the process and reports back to them. This keeps the work independent of a departing chief executive or senior managers who may hold views about who should come next. Some of those managers may also be candidates themselves.

List building is the first of the firm's core jobs. A search firm can map executives at competitors, at companies in adjacent industries and in roles that fit the board's profile. Many strong prospects are not looking for a new job. Part of the firm's value lies in surfacing people a board might not identify on its own.

Confidentiality is the second job. Sitting executives at other companies often cannot be seen exploring a move. Internal contenders face a similar problem inside their own company. Using an intermediary lets a board gauge interest without exposing names until both sides are ready to go further.

Outreach is the third. The firm makes the initial calls and describes the opportunity in general terms. It then screens for interest before any director gets involved. This saves the board's time. It also lowers the risk for candidates who decide not to proceed.

Search firms at this level typically work on a retained basis. The firm is hired exclusively for the assignment and paid whether or not the eventual hire comes from its list. Contingency recruiting works differently. There, a recruiter is paid only when a placement is made.

Boards also ask about conflicts before signing on. Large search firms often observe off-limits arrangements that stop them from recruiting executives away from their current clients. Those arrangements can narrow the pool a given firm is able to approach. Directors check which companies are excluded before choosing a firm.

A CEO candidate slate is the shortlist of people a board will seriously consider for the top job. It can mix internal executives from the company's own leadership team with outside candidates, and boards often measure both groups against the same set of criteria.

Internal candidates usually come from the senior ranks below the CEO. Typical names include a chief operating officer, a chief financial officer or the head of a major business unit. Directors may have watched these executives work for years. They may have seen their board presentations, their quarterly results and the way each person handles a setback.

That history gives the board a body of direct evidence about an insider. It also shapes how an insider is judged. Directors know the candidate's weaknesses as well as strengths. An outsider arrives with a record the board has mostly read about.

External candidates come from beyond the company. They can include sitting chief executives at other businesses, senior leaders at competitors or executives from other industries whose experience matches what the board wants. An outside search can surface skills the internal bench lacks, such as experience with a turnaround, an acquisition or a new market.

One set of criteria for both groups keeps the comparison even. The yardstick is normally the role profile the board agreed on earlier. That profile is built from the company's strategy and the skills the next CEO needs. Each name on the slate is weighed against it, not simply against the other names.

The slate usually narrows as the search moves forward. A long list of possible names is cut to a smaller group the committee wants to meet. That smaller group becomes the finalists who go through formal interviews.

Some boards use an executive search firm to build the outside portion of the slate. The firm handles outreach to external names. Insiders, by contrast, are already known to directors and can be approached directly.

A board can appoint an interim CEO to run the company while the search for a permanent leader continues. The interim is usually either an existing senior executive or a member of the board itself.

The role exists because a company cannot pause while directors work through candidates. Someone still has to sign off on budgets, run the leadership team and speak for the business to employees, customers and lenders. An interim appointment fills the gap with a named person and a clear line of authority.

The title varies. Some companies use "interim CEO," while others use "acting CEO." Either way, the person holds the authority of the office for as long as the appointment lasts. The temporary label tells staff and investors that the board has not yet made its permanent choice.

Choosing an existing executive keeps day-to-day knowledge in place. A chief financial officer or chief operating officer already knows the company's finances, managers and pending decisions. The trade-off is that the executive's own job may need covering. Colleagues may also read the appointment as a hint about the final outcome.

Choosing a director brings a different kind of knowledge to the role. A board member knows the company's strategy and the reasons for the search. Some boards hand the job to the chair, who then holds both roles for the duration. A director who becomes an employee typically stops counting as independent. That person then usually steps off committees that require independent members, such as the audit committee.

Boards also decide whether the interim is a candidate for the permanent post. Some interims rule themselves out from the start. Others enter the search and compete against the rest of the field. Internal and outside candidates alike often ask whether the interim is a rival.

The interim's brief tends to be narrow. It usually centers on keeping operations steady and avoiding major commitments that would bind the next CEO. Large strategic moves are often left for the permanent leader to make once the board completes its choice.

Finalists for a CEO job usually sit for several rounds of interviews. These start with the directors who run the search and widen to the full board. Formal assessments of the strongest candidates typically follow those conversations.

The early rounds belong to the search committee. Directors test each finalist against the role profile the board agreed on before the search began. They ask about past results, leadership record and how the candidate would approach the company's specific challenges.

Later rounds bring in the wider board. That step matters because the full board, not the committee, casts the final vote. Directors who sat outside the search get their own chance to judge each finalist before they are asked to approve one.

Board-level meetings can take several forms. Some are one-on-one conversations. Others are small-group sessions or informal meals, where directors see how a candidate handles less scripted discussion.

Interviews run in both directions. A finalist is also deciding whether to take the job. The sessions give a candidate a view of how the board operates, what it expects from management and how directors relate to one another. What a candidate sees in these meetings feeds into the decision to accept an offer.

Internal and external finalists face different conditions. Directors often already know inside executives from board presentations and committee work. Their interviews focus on whether they can handle the top job rather than the one they hold now. Outside finalists often hold senior roles elsewhere. Their meetings are usually scheduled discreetly to protect their current positions.

Assessments give the board a second source of evidence beyond the interviews. Boards may commission structured evaluations of leadership style, decision-making and judgment. Outside specialists, such as industrial-organizational psychologists, sometimes conduct them. Some boards also ask finalists to present their view of the company's strategy and priorities.

The combined record from interviews and assessments lets the board narrow the field to a leading candidate. That person then moves on to verification before any offer is made.

Reference and background checks begin once a board has a leading CEO candidate. They test whether the person's record matches what the interviews and assessments suggested, and they come before the board makes a final offer.

The two checks do different jobs. A reference check gathers judgment from people who have worked with the candidate. A background check verifies facts on paper, such as degrees, past job titles and any legal or regulatory history.

Reference calls for a CEO role usually reach well beyond the names a candidate supplies. Directors or the search firm may speak with former bosses, fellow executives, board members who oversaw the candidate and people who reported to the candidate. Each group sees a different side of a leader. A former board chair can speak to how the candidate worked with directors. A former direct report can describe how the candidate ran a team day to day.

Searches also often use what recruiters call backdoor references. These are conversations with people the candidate did not list. They can confirm a pattern or raise a question that the supplied references left out.

A CEO search is usually confidential. That means the board has to weigh whom to call and when. An early call to the wrong person can let the candidate's current employer learn of the search.

The background check is more mechanical. It typically confirms education and employment dates. It also searches court records for litigation or criminal history. For a candidate who has served as an officer or director, the review may extend to regulatory actions. It may also look for possible conflicts of interest, such as outside board seats or business ties.

In the U.S., a company that uses an outside screening firm for this work falls under the Fair Credit Reporting Act. That federal law requires the employer to tell the candidate in writing and get written permission before ordering the report.

Boards generally run these checks late, on one finalist or a very short list. That limits how many people learn of the search. It also keeps sensitive inquiries focused on the person most likely to get the job.

The results go back to the committee leading the search and then to the full board. A clean result clears the way for pay negotiations. An unresolved issue can send the board back to its other finalists.

Once the board settles on its preferred candidate, the compensation committee usually negotiates the new CEO's pay package and employment terms. Depending on the company, the committee approves the final terms on its own or together with the board's other independent directors.

The committee's task is to turn an agreement in principle into a binding contract. That contract covers how much the CEO earns and how the pay is structured. It also covers what happens if the relationship ends.

A typical package at a large public company has several layers. Base salary is the fixed cash component. An annual bonus ties cash to goals set for the year. Long-term incentives, such as stock options, restricted stock or performance shares, link pay to the company's results and share price over several years.

Outside candidates add a complication. An executive who leaves another company can forfeit unvested equity from that employer. Boards often answer with a make-whole award. This is a grant meant to replace some or all of what the candidate gives up by moving.

The employment agreement reaches beyond money. It can set severance terms and define what counts as termination for cause. It can also spell out change-in-control protections that apply if the company is sold.

Many agreements include non-compete and non-solicitation clauses. Their enforceability varies by jurisdiction. California, for example, generally does not enforce non-compete agreements.

At U.S. public companies, the committee works within specific rules. NYSE and Nasdaq $NDAQ listing standards require compensation committee members to be independent directors. Committees often retain outside compensation consultants. Before selecting one, a committee must consider that adviser's independence.

Recovery provisions are part of the package as well. SEC Rule 10D-1, adopted in 2022, requires listed companies to maintain clawback policies. Those policies recover incentive pay that was awarded on the basis of financial results later restated.

Shareholders review the outcome after the fact. A public company describes executive pay in its annual proxy statement, including a section called the Compensation Discussion and Analysis. Under the Dodd-Frank Act, shareholders also cast a nonbinding say-on-pay vote on executive compensation at least once every three years.

The committee negotiates with those disclosures in view. Much of what it agrees to, from salary to severance terms, will later be set out in public filings for investors to read.

For a U.S. public company, naming a new CEO creates a disclosure duty. Item 5.02 of Form 8-K, a filing with the Securities and Exchange Commission, requires the company to report the appointment of a principal executive officer. The filing is due within four business days.

Form 8-K is the current report that public companies file when certain significant events occur. The SEC's rules list the specific events that trigger it, and Item 5.02 is one of them. The annual 10-K and quarterly 10-Q follow a fixed calendar. An 8-K is filed when an event happens.

Item 5.02 covers changes among directors and certain officers. The same item also applies when a principal executive officer retires, resigns or is terminated. A single CEO handover can therefore produce disclosure about both the departing leader and the arriving one.

The appointment section of the item asks for more than a name. The filing states the new officer's position and the date of the appointment. It also covers the person's age and business experience. Any family relationships with directors or executive officers must be disclosed. Certain transactions between the new officer and the company are reported too. A brief description of any material plan, contract or arrangement tied to the appointment is required as well.

The timing rule has some flexibility. A company that announces the appointment through another public channel, such as a press release, may hold the 8-K until the day of that announcement. Filings become public on the SEC's EDGAR database. Investors, analysts and reporters can read them there.

The press release is the version most people see first. Companies usually issue one to name the new chief executive and give a start date. The release describes the leadership change in plain language for employees, customers and the media.

The announcement opens a transition period rather than ending the work. The incoming CEO typically meets with the outgoing CEO to hand over open issues and key relationships. Meetings with senior executives and employees follow. The new leader also meets with investors, who hold a direct stake in how the company is run.
