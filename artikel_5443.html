15 GLP-1 side effects, from most to least likely, including rare vision loss

These GLP-1 side effects of semaglutide and tirzepatide, from common nausea to rare vision loss linked to semaglutide, are ordered from most to least likely

One of the ways GLP-1 drugs work is also behind many of their most common downsides. Semaglutide, sold as Ozempic, Wegovy and Rybelsus, and tirzepatide, sold as Mounjaro and Zepbound, slow the rate at which the stomach empties. Food lingers longer, people feel full sooner, and they tend to eat less. That same slowdown helps explain why the digestive tract registers most of the complaints.

These medicines imitate glucagon-like peptide-1, a hormone the gut releases after eating. Tirzepatide also acts on a second gut hormone, known as GIP. Both drugs were first approved to treat type 2 diabetes. Wegovy and Zepbound are the versions approved for chronic weight management.

The 15 GLP-1 side effects below are a selection, not every reaction these drugs can cause. They are ordered from most to least likely, based on the broad pattern in clinical trials of semaglutide and tirzepatide. Exact frequencies differ by drug and dose. They also differ between people treated for type 2 diabetes and people treated for obesity. For that reason, the order reflects tiers of likelihood rather than precise percentages.

Digestive effects sit at the top. Nausea, diarrhea, vomiting and constipation are the most frequently reported, followed by abdominal pain. Many of these peak in the first weeks of treatment or right after a dose increase. Prescribers raise doses in steps to give the body time to adjust.

The middle of the list covers effects reported less often, such as headache, fatigue and dizziness. It also includes indigestion, gas and bloating, reactions where an injection is given, and hair thinning linked to rapid weight loss.

The final stretch covers less common but more serious problems that appear as warnings on the drug labels. Gallstones, gallbladder inflammation and pancreatitis belong in this group. Each one has warning signs that call for prompt medical care.

Vision loss comes last because it is the rarest item here. The condition, non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy, or NAION, causes sudden vision loss through reduced blood flow to the optic nerve. In June 2025, the European Medicines Agency's safety committee classed it as a very rare side effect of semaglutide. That conclusion concerned semaglutide specifically. Any sudden change in vision still needs urgent medical attention.

Nausea is the side effect people report most often on GLP-1 drugs such as semaglutide and tirzepatide. It is usually worst in the first weeks of treatment and after each dose increase, then tends to fade as the body adjusts.

The queasiness has a physical basis. GLP-1 medications delay the movement of food out of the stomach and into the small intestine. A meal sits in the stomach for longer than usual. That can leave it feeling heavy or unsettled, particularly after a large or rich meal.

The drugs also act on the brain. GLP-1 receptors are found in brainstem regions involved in appetite and nausea. Activity there is thought to help curb hunger. It may also contribute to the sick feeling some patients describe.

For most people, the nausea is mild to moderate and temporary. The prescribing information for the semaglutide and tirzepatide brands describes digestive reactions in those terms. Even so, gastrointestinal effects were the most common reason trial participants stopped taking these drugs.

Gradual dose escalation is the main tool doctors use to keep nausea manageable. Each brand's label sets out a schedule that begins well below the maintenance dose. The amount rises in fixed steps, with a gap of weeks between increases. The same approach applies to Rybelsus, the tablet form of semaglutide. The Ozempic label states that its starting dose is meant to begin treatment and is not effective for blood sugar control. Its role is to give the digestive system time to adapt.

If a step is hard to tolerate, a prescriber can hold a patient at the current dose for longer. The Wegovy label allows dose escalation to be delayed when a dose is not tolerated. Doctors can also decide not to move a patient up to the highest available dose.

Eating habits matter too. Common clinical advice includes eating smaller meals, eating slowly and stopping at the first sign of fullness. Fried, greasy and very fatty foods are frequent triggers. Fat slows digestion on its own. Combined with the drug's effect, it can leave a meal sitting even longer.

Nausea that worsens instead of easing deserves a call to a doctor. The same applies when it comes with repeated vomiting. Persistent symptoms can signal dehydration or another problem that needs medical attention.

Diarrhea ranks among the most frequent digestive side effects of GLP-1 drugs such as semaglutide and tirzepatide. It is usually mild to moderate. It tends to cluster around dose escalation, the weeks when a patient moves up to a higher dose.

Each new dose level gives the gut something fresh to adjust to. Loose stools that faded at one dose can return for a short spell after the next increase. The effect is reported with the weekly injections and with Rybelsus, the oral semaglutide tablet.

In most cases the problem eases as treatment continues. Episodes that persist or worsen are a different matter. They are worth reporting to the prescriber rather than waiting out.

The main concern with severe diarrhea is dehydration. Repeated watery stools carry water out of the body, along with electrolytes such as sodium and potassium. Plain water replaces the fluid but not those salts. An oral rehydration solution replaces both.

Significant fluid loss reduces blood flow to the kidneys. The prescribing information for semaglutide and tirzepatide warns of acute kidney injury in patients who become dehydrated from severe gastrointestinal reactions. Some of those cases have required hemodialysis.

The risk weighs most heavily on people who already have reduced kidney function. The labeling advises monitoring kidney function in patients with kidney impairment who report severe digestive reactions.

Early signs of fluid loss include darker urine, urinating less often, a dry mouth and unusual thirst. These signs indicate that fluid intake is not keeping pace with what the gut is losing.

Over-the-counter antidiarrheal medicines are widely sold. A prescriber or pharmacist can advise whether one is appropriate alongside a GLP-1 drug and other medications a patient takes.

Prescribers have options when diarrhea does not ease. They may keep a patient at the current dose for longer before the next increase. They may also step back to a lower dose. The dosing schedules in the labels allow for that flexibility, and the decision rests with the clinician.

Vomiting is one of the more common digestive side effects of GLP-1 drugs, though it is reported less often than nausea. It usually arrives alongside nausea. The bigger concern is often not the episode itself but the fluid the body fails to hold onto afterward.

Clinical trials of semaglutide, sold as Ozempic and Wegovy, and tirzepatide, sold as Mounjaro and Zepbound, place vomiting among the most frequently reported reactions. Rybelsus, the oral semaglutide tablet, carries a similar digestive profile.

Episodes tend to cluster in the first weeks of treatment and in the days after a dose increase. Semaglutide and tirzepatide are both raised in steps, typically every four weeks, until a patient reaches a maintenance dose.

Persistent vomiting creates a specific dehydration problem. A person who cannot keep water down loses fluid faster than they can replace it, even when they keep trying to drink. Repeated vomiting also drains electrolytes such as potassium and sodium. The body relies on those minerals for normal muscle and heart function.

That fluid loss can reach the kidneys. The prescribing information for semaglutide and tirzepatide warns that acute kidney injury has been reported in patients who became dehydrated from nausea, vomiting or diarrhea. Some of those reports involved people with no known kidney disease.

Several signs suggest vomiting has moved beyond a nuisance. They include being unable to keep any liquids down, passing very little or very dark urine, and a persistently dry mouth. Those symptoms call for prompt contact with a clinician. A prescriber may delay the next dose increase, lower the dose or pause the medication.

Vomiting paired with severe abdominal pain that spreads to the back is a different situation. That combination can signal pancreatitis, and it needs urgent medical care.

Day-to-day management usually centers on eating habits. Common advice includes smaller meals, stopping at the first sense of fullness and cutting back on fatty or fried foods. Fatty meals leave the stomach more slowly. Small sips of clear fluids between episodes are easier to tolerate than large drinks taken at once.

In clinical trials, digestive reactions such as vomiting were the most common reason participants stopped treatment because of side effects. For most people who continue, episodes become less frequent once they settle on a steady maintenance dose.

Constipation is one of the most frequently reported digestive effects of GLP-1 drugs, alongside nausea, diarrhea and vomiting. It is listed as a common adverse reaction in the prescribing information for semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy, Rybelsus) and tirzepatide (Mounjaro, Zepbound).

The link runs through the gut's pace. These medicines slow how quickly food leaves the stomach, and they also slow movement further along the digestive tract. When stool moves more slowly through the colon, the body draws more water out of it. The result is harder, drier stool that is more difficult to pass.

Eating less adds to the problem. GLP-1 drugs reduce appetite. Many people respond by eating smaller meals. Smaller meals usually mean less fiber and less overall bulk reaching the bowel. Some people also drink less when they feel full, which leaves less fluid to soften stool.

Constipation is generally defined as having fewer than three bowel movements a week. Other signs include straining, hard or lumpy stools and a feeling that the bowel has not fully emptied. A clear change from a person's usual pattern can also be a sign. On a GLP-1 drug, constipation can occur alongside bloating or mild cramping.

Standard management advice focuses on fluid and fiber. Drinking water through the day helps keep stool soft. Fiber-rich foods such as vegetables, beans, whole grains and fruit add bulk. Fiber tends to work best when fluid intake rises with it. Adding fiber gradually can limit extra gas. Regular physical activity, including walking, also supports normal bowel movement.

Over-the-counter options exist, including osmotic laxatives and stool softeners. Anyone considering them should check with a prescriber or pharmacist first, especially if they take other medicines.

Constipation is usually manageable, but some symptoms need prompt attention. Severe or worsening abdominal pain, a swollen belly, vomiting or an inability to pass gas or stool can signal an intestinal blockage. Ileus is a condition in which the intestines stop moving their contents along. The U.S. prescribing information for Ozempic lists ileus among reactions reported after the drug reached the market.

Abdominal pain is one of the more frequently reported GLP-1 side effects, and most episodes are mild and pass on their own. Severe pain that does not ease, especially pain that spreads to the back, is different. It calls for prompt medical attention.

The prescribing information for semaglutide, sold as Ozempic, Wegovy and Rybelsus, lists abdominal pain among its common adverse reactions. The labels for tirzepatide, sold as Mounjaro and Zepbound, list it as well.

The everyday version tends to feel like cramping, aching or a sense of pressure in the stomach area. It is most likely in the first weeks of treatment or soon after a dose increase. For many patients, it fades once the body settles into the new dose.

Meal size plays a role. These drugs keep food in the stomach longer. A large or high-fat meal can sit heavily as a result. Eating smaller portions and stopping at the first sign of fullness is standard guidance for managing digestive discomfort on these medications.

Noting when the pain starts can also help. A simple record of dose days, dose changes and symptoms gives a prescriber a clearer picture. It can show whether the pain follows a pattern or is getting worse over time.

The warning signs look different from routine discomfort. Pain that is severe, constant or sharp deserves attention. So does pain that moves from the abdomen into the back, or that comes with repeated vomiting. Fever, or yellowing of the skin or the whites of the eyes, is another reason to seek care.

Those symptoms can point to pancreatitis or gallbladder disease. Both are labeled warnings for GLP-1 drugs. Both are far less common than ordinary stomach upset. The semaglutide medication guides tell patients to stop the drug and call a health care provider right away if severe stomach pain will not go away.

The practical line is persistence and severity. Mild cramping that comes and goes after a dose change is a known pattern. Intense pain that lingers, spreads or arrives with other symptoms is a reason to stop and get checked rather than wait it out.

Headache is a reported side effect of GLP-1 drugs such as semaglutide and tirzepatide. It shows up less often than nausea, diarrhea and the other digestive complaints that dominate reports from people taking these medications.

Why GLP-1 medications are linked to headaches has not been firmly established. Several features of treatment could play a part, but none has been confirmed as the cause. A person with frequent headaches on these drugs may need to rule out more than one possible trigger.

Eating less is one possible contributor. These drugs reduce appetite, and many people end up skipping meals or eating much smaller portions than before. Going long stretches without food is a familiar headache trigger for some people, whether or not they take any medication.

Fluid intake is another candidate. People who feel full or queasy often drink less along with eating less. Mild dehydration is a common cause of headache in general. It can build gradually when drinking feels unappealing.

Blood sugar is a separate consideration for people with type 2 diabetes. GLP-1 drugs on their own carry a low risk of pushing blood sugar too low. That risk rises when they are combined with insulin or with sulfonylureas such as glipizide. Low blood sugar can bring on a headache along with shakiness, sweating, confusion and a racing heartbeat. A headache that arrives with those symptoms calls for a glucose check.

General headache-care habits apply here. Eating small, regular meals instead of skipping them can help. So can sipping water through the day. Keeping a note of when headaches occur relative to each dose can help a prescriber spot a pattern.

Anyone considering an over-the-counter pain reliever should check with a pharmacist or prescriber first. That matters most for people who take other medications or have kidney problems. Drugs such as ibuprofen can strain the kidneys.

Some headaches need prompt medical care regardless of the drug. A sudden, severe headache that peaks within about a minute is an emergency. So is a headache with a stiff neck, fever, weakness on one side of the body, slurred speech or confusion. Those signs should not be put down to a routine side effect or waited out.

A headache that keeps returning, or that worsens after each dose increase, is worth raising with the prescriber. Slowing the escalation schedule or staying at the current dose for longer are options a doctor can weigh.

Fatigue is a commonly reported side effect in clinical trials of semaglutide and tirzepatide. People describe it as tiredness or low energy, and no single cause has been established for it.

Fatigue has been reported in trials of these drugs for both type 2 diabetes and weight management. It is reported less often than the main digestive complaints.

Lower calorie intake is one possible contributor. Reduced appetite on these drugs often means eating far less than before treatment. A body running on fewer calories can feel sluggish. That connection has not been confirmed, and it is not the only possible explanation.

Other side effects can overlap with tiredness. Nausea, vomiting or diarrhea can disrupt sleep. They can also make it harder to eat and drink normally. A person dealing with those symptoms may feel drained even if the drug is not the direct cause of the fatigue.

Diet quality becomes more important when appetite drops. Smaller meals leave less room for protein, fruit, vegetables and whole grains. A doctor or registered dietitian can help plan meals that still cover basic nutritional needs on a reduced intake.

Blood sugar is a separate issue for people with type 2 diabetes. The prescribing information for semaglutide and tirzepatide warns that the risk of low blood sugar rises when they are combined with insulin or a sulfonylurea. Tiredness paired with shakiness, sweating, confusion or a fast heartbeat can signal low blood sugar. A glucose meter reading can confirm it.

Fatigue that does not lift, or that gets worse over time, should be raised with a doctor. Tiredness has many causes unrelated to medication, including anemia, thyroid problems, poor sleep and depression. Blood tests can help rule some of these out.

If side effects make daily life difficult, doctors can slow the pace of dose increases. That adjustment can apply to tiredness as well as to digestive symptoms.

Indigestion, known clinically as dyspepsia, is a recognized upper-digestive side effect of semaglutide. The prescribing labels for Ozempic and Wegovy both list it among the drug's common adverse reactions.

Dyspepsia describes discomfort centered in the upper abdomen, just below the breastbone. People often report a burning sensation, a heavy or overly full feeling after eating, or fullness after only a few bites. It differs from nausea, which is the urge to vomit. It also differs from cramping lower in the abdomen.

Ozempic and Wegovy share the same active ingredient, and Novo Nordisk makes both. Wegovy, the weight-management version, goes to a higher maximum dose than Ozempic. Indigestion appears on the side effect lists for both drugs. That means it can affect people treated for type 2 diabetes as well as people treated for obesity.

The issue is not unique to semaglutide. The labels for tirzepatide, sold as Mounjaro and Zepbound, also list dyspepsia as a common reaction.

Indigestion on these medications can overlap with acid reflux. Gastroesophageal reflux disease, in which stomach acid backs up into the esophagus, also appears among the gastrointestinal reactions in the semaglutide labels. Heartburn behind the breastbone, a sour taste or regurgitation point toward reflux rather than simple indigestion.

Meal habits are the usual first step in managing it. Smaller portions, slower eating and stopping at the first sign of fullness reduce the load on the stomach. Fried, greasy and very rich foods tend to aggravate symptoms. Staying upright for a while after meals and avoiding late-night eating can limit reflux.

Persistent indigestion should be raised with the prescriber. A prescriber may adjust the dosing schedule or check for other causes. Chest pain that spreads to the arm, jaw or back should not be assumed to be indigestion. The same goes for chest discomfort that comes with shortness of breath. Those symptoms need emergency care because they can signal a heart problem.

Dizziness is a less frequent GLP-1 side effect than the digestive complaints, but it appears at a modest rate in trials of semaglutide and tirzepatide. It usually feels like lightheadedness, often on standing up, rather than a spinning sensation.

No single mechanism has been established. Two possible contributors stand out. One is a shortfall of fluid in the body. The other is a drop in blood pressure that can accompany weight loss.

Fluid intake often falls on these drugs. People who feel full sooner tend to eat and drink less. A meaningful share of daily water normally comes from food. A body running low on fluid has less blood volume in circulation. That can leave the brain briefly short of blood flow when a person stands.

Blood pressure is the second possible contributor. Weight loss tends to lower blood pressure, and GLP-1 drugs have been associated with modest reductions in clinical trials. For someone already taking blood pressure medication, the combined effect may push readings lower than intended. Doctors sometimes reduce or stop those medications as weight comes down.

Blood sugar can matter for people with type 2 diabetes. A GLP-1 drug used on its own carries a low risk of hypoglycemia. The risk rises when it is combined with insulin or a sulfonylurea such as glipizide. Lightheadedness, shakiness and sweating can signal a low reading. A glucose meter can confirm it.

A few practical steps address the likely triggers. Rising slowly from a bed or chair gives circulation time to adjust. Drinking fluids steadily through the day helps when appetite is low. Home blood pressure checks can show whether a medication change needs discussing with a prescriber.

Some dizziness is not routine. Fainting, a racing or irregular heartbeat, chest pain, confusion or dizziness that does not let up warrants prompt medical attention. Older adults face a higher risk of falls. Even mild unsteadiness in an older person is worth raising with the prescribing doctor.

Bloating, belching and gas are common digestive side effects of GLP-1 drugs. The prescribing information for semaglutide products such as Ozempic and Wegovy lists abdominal distension, eructation and flatulence among their common adverse reactions.

Those three clinical terms describe familiar complaints. Abdominal distension is the swollen, tight feeling of an overfull belly. Eructation is the medical word for belching. Flatulence is gas passed from the lower bowel.

The three complaints often appear together. GLP-1 drugs slow the rate at which the stomach empties into the small intestine. A meal that stays in the stomach longer can leave a person feeling full and stretched for hours after eating.

Belching usually comes from swallowed air. Eating quickly adds air to the stomach. So do carbonated drinks, chewing gum and drinking through a straw. Some people on these medications describe belches with a rotten-egg smell, often called sulfur burps. That odor comes from hydrogen sulfide, a gas produced when sulfur-containing compounds break down in the gut.

Lower-bowel gas has a different source. Bacteria in the colon ferment carbohydrates that the small intestine does not fully absorb. That fermentation releases gas. Beans, lentils, onions, broccoli and cabbage are common contributors. Sugar alcohols such as sorbitol, found in many sugar-free sweets and gums, can have the same effect.

Practical steps focus on how and what a person eats. Smaller meals put less volume into a stomach that is already emptying slowly. Eating slowly and stopping at the first sign of fullness can limit both pressure and swallowed air. Clinicians commonly advise people on these drugs to cut back on fizzy drinks and on fried or very fatty meals. A short food log can help pinpoint which meals trigger the worst episodes.

Like most digestive effects of this drug class, gas and bloating are usually mild. They tend to be more noticeable early in treatment or after a dose increase.

Ordinary bloating is different from a belly that becomes hard, swollen and painful. Severe pain, repeated vomiting or being unable to pass gas or stool needs prompt medical care. Those signs can point to a bowel blockage. The semaglutide labels list ileus, a condition in which the intestine stops moving its contents along normally, among reactions reported after approval.

Injection-site reactions are a generally minor GLP-1 side effect that shows up as redness, itching or swelling where the dose goes in. They affect the injectable forms of these drugs, not the oral semaglutide tablet sold as Rybelsus.

Most GLP-1 medications are given as a shot under the skin. Ozempic and Wegovy, both semaglutide, are injected once a week. So are Mounjaro and Zepbound, the tirzepatide brands. Older drugs in the class, such as liraglutide, sold as Victoza and Saxenda, are injected once a day. A daily schedule means more needle sticks over the same period.

The shot goes into the fatty layer under the skin rather than into muscle. The prescribing instructions name three areas for it: the abdomen, the thigh and the upper arm. A reaction typically stays small and local. It can look like a pink patch, a raised bump, mild tenderness or a minor bruise around the puncture point.

Rotating the injection site is the main step the drug labels describe. The instructions tell patients to change the spot with each dose. That can mean switching between body areas. It can also mean moving to a different spot within the same area.

People who also take insulin get a specific instruction. The Ozempic label says the two drugs should be given as separate injections and never mixed. They may go into the same body region, but the injections should not sit right next to each other.

Rybelsus is a daily tablet approved for type 2 diabetes. Its label carries separate instructions to take it on an empty stomach with a small amount of water.

A local skin reaction is different from an allergic reaction to the drug. Serious hypersensitivity is a labeled warning for GLP-1 medications. Swelling of the face, lips, tongue or throat calls for emergency care. So do trouble breathing, a very fast heartbeat or a widespread rash. Redness that keeps spreading, feels hot or produces pus after an injection also needs a doctor's attention. Those changes can signal an infection rather than a routine reaction.

Hair thinning is a recognized side effect of GLP-1 treatment and appears in the label for Wegovy, Novo Nordisk's semaglutide drug for weight management. It is generally linked to the speed of weight loss rather than to a direct effect of the drug on hair follicles.

The pattern resembles telogen effluvium, a common form of temporary hair shedding. At any moment, most hairs on the scalp are in a growth phase. A physical shock to the body can push a larger share of follicles into a resting phase at the same time. Those hairs then fall out together weeks later.

Rapid weight loss is one of the known triggers. Others include major surgery, childbirth, high fever and severe illness. People who lose large amounts of weight after bariatric surgery often report the same kind of shedding. That overlap suggests the weight loss itself is the shared factor.

The delay can make the connection hard to spot. Shedding from telogen effluvium typically shows up two to three months after the trigger. A person may notice more hair in the shower or on a brush well after the first weeks on semaglutide or tirzepatide.

The thinning is usually spread across the whole scalp rather than concentrated in patches. Hair often looks less dense, particularly along the part. The condition does not scar the follicles. In most cases, shedding slows and regrowth begins within several months once the trigger settles.

Diet can play a part. GLP-1 drugs reduce appetite, and eating much less can lower intake of protein, iron and other nutrients that hair needs to grow. Low iron and low protein intake are both associated with hair shedding. A doctor can order blood tests to check iron stores, thyroid function and other possible causes.

Patchy bald spots, scalp redness or shedding that continues for many months point to something other than weight-loss shedding. Those signs call for a separate medical evaluation. Any decision to change a dose or stop treatment belongs with the prescribing doctor.

Gallstones and gallbladder inflammation, known as cholecystitis, are labeled warnings for GLP-1 drugs such as semaglutide and tirzepatide. They occur less often than the digestive complaints that dominate trial reports. When they do occur, they can require hospital care or surgery.

The gallbladder is a small, pear-shaped organ that sits beneath the liver. It stores bile, a digestive fluid the liver makes to help break down fats. After a meal, the gallbladder contracts and pushes bile into the small intestine.

Gallstones form when substances in bile harden into solid deposits. Most are made largely of cholesterol. They can be as small as a grain of sand or as large as a golf ball. Many people carry gallstones without symptoms and never learn they are there.

Rapid weight loss is a known risk factor for gallstones. When the body sheds weight quickly, the liver releases extra cholesterol into bile. The gallbladder may also empty less often, which lets bile sit and thicken. The same pattern is well documented after bariatric surgery, another source of fast weight loss. Weight loss on GLP-1 drugs may raise gallstone risk through this route. Whether the drugs also act on the gallbladder directly is less settled.

Baseline risk also varies from person to person. Women develop gallstones more often than men, and the risk rises with age.

Cholecystitis usually develops when a gallstone blocks the duct that drains the gallbladder. Bile backs up, and the organ becomes inflamed. Without treatment, the inflammation can lead to infection. In severe cases, the gallbladder wall can tear.

The prescribing information for these drugs tells patients to watch for specific symptoms. They include upper abdominal pain, fever, clay-colored stools and yellowing of the skin or eyes. Gallbladder pain often centers on the upper right side of the abdomen. It can spread to the right shoulder or the back and may last for several hours.

Doctors typically confirm gallstones with an abdominal ultrasound. For people with repeated attacks or cholecystitis, the usual treatment is surgical removal of the gallbladder, called a cholecystectomy. Most of these operations are done laparoscopically through small incisions. People can still digest food without a gallbladder. Bile then flows directly from the liver into the small intestine.

Pancreatitis, an inflammation of the pancreas, is an uncommon but serious labeled warning for GLP-1 drugs. Its main warning sign is severe, persistent abdominal pain that may spread to the back, with or without vomiting.

The pancreas sits behind the stomach. It makes digestive enzymes that break down food. It also makes hormones such as insulin that help control blood sugar. In pancreatitis, those enzymes become active inside the organ and irritate its own tissue. The result is swelling and pain, and in severe cases lasting damage to the pancreas.

The prescribing information for semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy, Rybelsus) and tirzepatide (Mounjaro, Zepbound) carries a pancreatitis warning. The labels tell clinicians to watch for signs of the condition. They also say to stop the drug if pancreatitis is suspected.

Whether GLP-1 drugs directly cause pancreatitis is still under study. The warning reflects cases reported in people taking these medications. It is not proof that the drug triggered each case.

Pancreatitis pain differs from the everyday stomach upset many people notice when they start a GLP-1 drug. It is usually intense and steady rather than mild and passing. It often centers in the upper abdomen. It may get worse after eating. Some people also develop a fever, a rapid pulse or a swollen, tender belly.

Doctors usually confirm the condition with a blood test that measures pancreatic enzymes such as lipase and amylase. Imaging, such as a CT scan or an ultrasound, can show inflammation and help rule out other causes. Acute pancreatitis typically needs hospital care. Treatment includes intravenous fluids, pain control and a break from eating while the pancreas recovers.

Other factors raise pancreatitis risk on their own. Gallstones and heavy alcohol use are the two leading causes of acute pancreatitis. Very high triglyceride levels are another. Several of the GLP-1 labels note that the drugs have not been studied in people with a past history of pancreatitis. That history is worth raising with a prescriber before treatment begins.

Anyone taking a GLP-1 drug who develops severe abdominal pain that does not go away should seek prompt medical care.

Non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy, or NAION, is a sudden loss of vision caused by reduced blood flow to the optic nerve. In June 2025, the European Medicines Agency's safety committee concluded that it is a very rare side effect of semaglutide, affecting up to 1 in 10,000 people.

That committee is the Pharmacovigilance Risk Assessment Committee, known as PRAC. It reviews safety signals for medicines sold in the European Union. Its conclusion covers semaglutide, the active ingredient in Ozempic, Wegovy and Rybelsus. It does not establish the same risk for tirzepatide or other GLP-1 drugs.

Very rare is the lowest defined frequency band in European medicine labeling. It sits below the common, uncommon and rare categories. That placement is why NAION closes this list.

The optic nerve carries visual signals from the eye to the brain. In NAION, the front of that nerve, where it joins the back of the eye, loses part of its blood supply. The starved nerve fibers stop passing signals properly.

The condition usually strikes one eye. The loss is typically painless and is often noticed on waking. People may see blurring, dimming or a dark shadow. That shadow often covers the upper or lower half of the visual field.

The term non-arteritic separates it from arteritic optic neuropathy. That form is caused by giant cell arteritis, an inflammation of blood vessels. Doctors use an eye examination and blood tests to tell the two apart.

Vision lost to NAION often does not fully return. No treatment has been proven to restore it reliably. In some people, the second eye is affected later.

NAION also occurs in people who take no medication at all. Recognized risk factors include diabetes, high blood pressure, obstructive sleep apnea and a small, crowded optic disc. Type 2 diabetes is the condition Ozempic and Rybelsus are prescribed to treat.

A sudden drop in vision while taking a GLP-1 drug is a reason to seek emergency eye care. An eye specialist can examine the optic nerve directly. Patients should tell the examining doctor which medication they take and when they started it.
