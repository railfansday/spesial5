Sergio Navarro’s style and the key individuals: Milan Futuro tactical analysis

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Milan Futuro decided to go in a new direction this summer by appointing Sergio Navarro, but is it working so far?

Milan launched the Futuro project ahead of the 2024-25 season. with the aim of creating a pathway for academy players to break into the first team. The project was designed to bridge the physical and technical gap between the Primavera and senior football.

Things didn’t go well in year one, as Daniele Bonera didn’t see out the season as the head coach. He recently stated he didn’t think the squad was ready for the level, and ultimately Massimo Oddo was unable to save the team from relegation to Serie D, the fourth tier of Italian football.

With promotion back to Serie C the objective, this summer the Rossoneri turned to Sergio Navarro, a coach with good pedigree in Spain. The Futuro now appear to have a clearer structure, a defined playing identity and the right guidance, as Rohit Rajeev has outlined.

Under Barquero, Milan Futuro operate in a 4-2-3-1 formation. Asanji leads the line, with Ossola playing as the number 10 and Vos and Guernier occupying the wide positions.

Plazotta and Pandolfi form the double pivot, while Cisse and Pagliei partner at centre-back, with Perera and Colombo completing the back four.

Milan Futuro favour a possession-oriented approach, circulating the ball patiently at a measured tempo while waiting for gaps to emerge in the opposition’s defensive structure. They also use passing triangles effectively to create short passing options and play through the opposition’s high press.

In possession, Milan Futuro adopt a 3-1-6 structure, with Barquero showing little hesitation in committing players forward to create numerical overloads in the opposition half.

Asanji positions himself just behind the opposition’s defensive line, keeping the centre-backs occupied and preventing them from stepping forward freely. Meanwhile, Pandolfi and Plazotta maintain parallel positions in the double pivot, providing a stable base for the attacking structure.

In the opposition half, Milan Futuro keep the distances between players very short, allowing them to combine through quick, one-touch passes.

This also helps them create ball-sided overloads, with two or three players surrounding the Milan player in possession to provide close passing options. Once they enter the opposition half, the tempo increases as Milan look to become more direct and progress towards goal.

Similar to Amorim’s approach at Sporting, Milan Futuro form vertical corridors with three players positioned one behind another. This structure facilitates quick passing combinations while also creating movement to manipulate the opposition.

When Milan players drop to form these corridors or create overloads, they often drag defenders with them, opening spaces elsewhere to exploit.

Milan Futuro look to press the opposition high up the pitch, adopting a 4-4-2 shape with a man-oriented pressing scheme. This allows them to put pressure on the opposition’s build-up and restrict the time and space available to play out from the back.

Milan Futuro also set up pressing traps by deliberately leaving the opposition’s wide player unmarked. The pass towards the wide player acts as the pressing trigger, prompting Milan’s players to jump out of their positions and aggressively close down the receiver, while cutting off the available passing options.

Milan Futuro maintain an extremely narrow defensive shape, prioritising the protection of central spaces and forcing the opposition towards the flanks.

Once the opposition attempts to progress through the wide areas, the press shifts across to create numerical overloads around the ball, while Guernier and Colombo provide cover and prevent the opposition from switching play to the opposite flank.

Perhaps the most important aspect of Barquero’s approach is his willingness to build the system around his players’ strengths and allow them the freedom to express themselves.

Rather than focusing solely on tactical adaptability, he appears equally committed to their individual development and helping them grow as footballers. Here are a few examples.

One of Milan Futuro’s methods of chance creation is to play direct passes into Asanji. His ability to position himself between the ball and the opposition defender, using his physicality to shield possession and hold up play, is crucial to this approach.

Rather than asking him to adapt to the role of a more technically oriented, ball-playing striker, Barquero has built a mechanism that makes the most of his existing strengths.

Perhaps the clearest demonstration of Barquero’s coaching ability is his utilisation of Vos and Guernier. Guernier is a natural winger/forward, while Vos is more accustomed to playing as an attacking midfielder.

Within the 3-1-6 structure, Vos often inverts from the left flank into an inside-left role, allowing Perera to provide the width.

At times, he is also given the freedom to interchange positions with Plazotta, allowing him to drop into midfield or advance further up the pitch. This positional freedom helps him develop his midfield qualities and realise his potential.

Guernier, meanwhile, tends to maintain his width, stretching the opposition’s defensive line and creating space for Vos and the other attackers to exploit.

Barquero also gives Guernier the freedom to take on his direct opponent, even when he is outnumbered, allowing him to use his dribbling ability without being restricted by the team’s structure.

Ossola, who is particularly effective at receiving between the lines and turning with the ball, is allowed to operate higher up the pitch, closer to the opposition’s defensive line.

Idrissi, on the other hand, prefers to receive possession deeper, where he can dictate play with his passing range or carry the ball forward through dribbling.
