Amazon loses 40 Leo satellites in ULA Vulcan launch failure — a new setback for its Starlink rival

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

by Todd Bishop on Nov 15, 2026 at 1:41 pmNovember 18, 2026 at 10:12 am

Update, Wednesday Nov. 18: ULA said Wednesday that preliminary findings indicate the first stage’s low thrust came from an inadequate fuel supply to its BE-4 engines, not the engines’ own performance. Read our latest story.

Update, Tuesday Nov. 17: ULA said Tuesday that early data suggests Vulcan’s first stage underperformed just after liftoff. Read our follow-up story for details. This story has been updated with ULA’s preliminary findings.

A United Launch Alliance Vulcan rocket failed to reach orbit Sunday, losing 40 satellites for Amazon Leo, the Seattle company’s planned challenger to SpaceX’s Starlink internet service.

It was ULA’s first total mission failure since the Boeing–Lockheed Martin joint venture was formed in 2006, Spaceflight Now reported. The flight was Vulcan’s first in nine months, and the first of 38 Vulcan launches that Amazon has purchased.

ULA said Sunday that it didn’t yet have specific data on what went wrong, and that it would work with Amazon and others to find the cause and return to flight as quickly as possible.

Amazon Leo said in a post on X that “there is risk with any launch, and that’s especially true for inaugural missions like LV-01.” It added, “ULA has been a trusted partner for Amazon, and we believe they’ll learn from this mission and deliver on their long-term plans for Vulcan.”

The rocket lifted off from Cape Canaveral Space Force Station at 12:40 p.m. ET (9:40 a.m. PT), after two days of delays and a countdown hold with 20 seconds left on the clock to resolve a technical issue. It isn’t known whether that issue played any role.

The rocket’s six solid rocket boosters, made by Northrop Grumman and the source of problems on two earlier Vulcan flights, appeared to perform normally and burned out less than two minutes after liftoff, according to Space.com and Spaceflight Now.

The first stage, powered by two BE-4 engines from Jeff Bezos’ Kent, Wash.-based Blue Origin, fired from liftoff before separating from the Centaur upper stage, which carries the payload the rest of the way to orbit.

ULA said Tuesday that early data suggests the first stage underperformed just after liftoff, leaving the rocket too low and too slow to reach orbit. The Centaur, powered by two RL10 engines made by L3Harris, appears to have started normally and couldn’t make up for the shortfall. ULA appears to have lost telemetry and the mission about 10 minutes into the flight.

Spaceflight Now reported Sunday that the first stage appeared to burn longer than planned, and that a telemetry-driven animation showed the Centaur making unusual orientation adjustments early in its burn. ULA hasn’t said what caused the shortfall.

Impact on Amazon Leo: Before Sunday, Amazon had launched 396 satellites for its Redmond-based satellite internet network, formerly known as Project Kuiper, on 14 missions since April 2025. Its last successful launch was in July, when ULA flew the last of eight Leo missions on its Atlas 5, the older rocket that Vulcan is replacing.

The larger Vulcan carried 40 satellites on Sunday, compared with 29 on the last Atlas 5 launch, and is slated to carry more than 1,500 of Amazon’s first-generation satellites, more than any other rocket in the program.

Melissa Wuerl, Amazon Leo’s director of launch systems, called it the program’s “primary workhorse over the next several years” in a preview of the mission. Amazon had said the second Vulcan mission could fly within weeks of the first. That flight now has to wait for ULA’s investigation.

Amazon also has 24 launches reserved on Blue Origin’s New Glenn, which has been grounded since a May ground-test explosion, and has booked others on Arianespace’s Ariane 6 and SpaceX’s Falcon 9.

In June, the Federal Communications Commission gave Amazon permission to keep deploying satellites even if it missed its July 30 deadline to put 1,616 into service. Satellites that weren’t operational by then temporarily lose priority in key spectrum bands.

Amazon still has until July 30, 2029, to deploy and operate all 3,232 first-generation satellites. Amazon has said it plans an initial rollout of commercial internet service this year.

Vulcan’s track record: Sunday’s mission was the fifth for Vulcan, which debuted in January 2024, and the first with six solid boosters. Boosters suffered nozzle failures on the second flight in October 2024 and on a national security mission in February. Both reached orbit, but the Space Force paused Vulcan national security launches after February.

ULA flew Sunday’s mission with an improved nozzle design. The rocket’s BE-4 engines had also been retrofitted to fix the oxygen valve problem behind the New Glenn explosion in May, ULA CEO Mark Peller said before the flight, CBS News reported.

ULA is also under new leadership. Longtime CEO Tory Bruno left last December for Blue Origin, and Peller, who led Vulcan’s development, was named CEO in August.

Amazon’s next scheduled Leo launch is an Ariane 6 mission carrying 36 satellites, targeted for Nov. 23 at the earliest.
