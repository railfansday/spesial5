Methane emissions stayed stubbornly high despite a federal push to cut them

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

Satellite data show U.S. oil and gas methane emissions stayed nearly flat from 2019 to 2024, even as production grew and federal policies shifted.

Congress created the first federal methane fee in 2022 to encourage oil and gas companies to reduce emissions.

Satellite measurements now show that methane emissions from the sector remained near 11 million tons (10 million metric tons) a year from 2019 to 2024, with a slight increase in the final year.

The findings reveal no detectable decline following the law’s passage, though much of its enforcement framework had yet to take effect.

Methane emissions per unit of oil and gas produced fell by about 11% over the six years.

The researchers linked that decline mainly to new pipelines, better equipment, and earlier leak rules.

Ao Chen, a doctoral student at Johns Hopkins University, led the work with Scot Miller, an associate professor of environmental health and engineering there.

Colleagues at the University of Texas at Austin and McGill University took part.

Methane is nearly 30 times as potent as carbon dioxide at trapping heat, though it lasts a shorter time in the air. That makes it the second most important greenhouse gas to cut, Miller said.

Two instruments in orbit, Europe’s TROPOMI and Japan’s GOSAT, measure how much of it sits in the air below them.

The team fed roughly 2,000 of those readings a day over the lower 48 states into a model of how wind moves gas around. Then they ran it backward to work out where the methane came from.

The result is a map of emissions in boxes about 35 miles (55 kilometers) on a side, one for every day from 2019 to 2024.

Deciding how much of each box’s methane came from oil and gas rather than cattle or wetlands relies on existing inventories.

The team’s eight model runs show that choice can shift the oil and gas total by up to 20%.

The satellite totals ran about 17% above the EPA’s own inventory from 2019 to 2022. Earlier aerial and satellite studies have found the same gap, and some found it far wider.

Oil and gas methane across the lower 48 stayed between 10.3 million and 11.5 million tons (9.3 to 10.4 million metric tons) every year of the study.

It fell by about 640,000 tons (580,000 metric tons) from 2019 to 2023, then rose by about 230,000 tons (210,000 metric tons) in 2024.

Production, meanwhile, grew 8.5% over the same six years, so the methane released per barrel of oil and gas fell by 11.1%, or about 2.2% a year.

Most of that drop came between 2020 and 2022, before any new federal rule took hold.

“The good news is that emissions have not markedly increased, as many scientists feared would happen after an enormous increase in natural gas production in the decades following the hydraulic fracturing boom,” he said.

“The bad news is that oil and gas production is still a large percentage of U.S. methane emissions, and we do not find any evidence that a flurry of federal legislation had an impact on emissions.”

The Inflation Reduction Act created the Waste Emissions Charge, a fee on methane from large oil and gas facilities that was to begin in 2024.

It also set aside $1.36 billion to help companies find and fix leaks. The EPA finalized its own methane rules for new and existing wells in 2024.

Much of that framework had yet to take effect during the study.

The fee’s details stayed unsettled, and the rules for existing facilities gave states until 2026 to file their plans. Then Congress voided the fee rule in early 2025.

The EPA had projected the two measures together would cut 2024 emissions by about 420,000 tons (380,000 metric tons). The satellites saw an increase instead.

“We don’t see a detectable decrease in emissions after the Inflation Reduction Act,” Chen said.

“Of course, there’s no way to test the counterfactual. It’s possible that without the Inflation Reduction Act, methane emissions would be much higher.”

The biggest drop came from the biggest emitter.

Texas oil and gas methane fell 14%, from about 3.9 million tons (3.5 million metric tons) in 2019 to 3.3 million tons (3.0 million metric tons) in 2024. The state has no climate target.

The Gulf Coast Express line opened in late 2019, and the Whistler and Matterhorn Express lines followed, carrying gas from the Permian Basin to the coast and to Houston.

Pipeline access can allow producers to sell gas that might otherwise be flared or vented. Rising exports of liquefied natural gas made that gas worth more.

States with climate targets, among them California, Colorado, and New Mexico, cut oil and gas methane at rates that overlapped with states that have none.

Their inventories were more accurate, though. The satellite estimate was 3.6 times Texas’s official count and 5.8 times Oklahoma’s, while New Mexico’s matched.

California has pledged to cut its total methane 40% below 2013 levels by 2030.

That means cutting roughly 560,000 tons (510,000 metric tons) in four years, against about 130,000 tons (120,000 metric tons) over the past ten.

Colorado’s case is murkier. Its oil and gas methane swings by more than 30% from one year to the next, wider than the 26% cut its 2025 target demands.

That variability makes progress toward the target difficult to assess.

The study cannot establish what emissions would have been without the federal measures.

Continued satellite monitoring will help researchers track whether future policies and industry changes produce sustained reductions.

The full study was published in the journal Communications Earth & Environment.
