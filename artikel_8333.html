After an Accident Left Her Paralyzed, Brittany Chadbourne Found a Way to Keep Climbing

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

“I'm the giant asterisk at the end of ‘climbing is dangerous.'" —Brittany Chadbourne (Photo: Maya Silver)

For some climbers, campusing—climbing with only your upper body—is a training strategy. For Utah climber Brittany Chadbourne, it’s her only choice.

I met Chadbourne in early October at Momentum Millcreek, one of the gyms she regularly visits in the Salt Lake Valley. She pulled onto a V3 boulder problem angled at least 45 degrees, working her way to the top and back down in about a minute. Then she eased herself into her wheelchair, hardly out of breath.

Bouldering carries particular risks for Chadbourne. Because she can’t control how she lands, a fall could cause additional injuries, including damage to the rods in her spine. These days, she saves harder climbing for ropes and sticks to boulder problems she knows she can safely descend. Afterward, I belayed the 28-year-old on a 5.11c. Her powerful shoulders, arms, and back carried her up the route without the use of her legs or lower abs.

Four years ago, a routesetting accident left Chadbourne paralyzed from the belly button down, so she had to find a new way to practice the sport she loves. “I’ve always wanted to be in Climbing Magazine,” Chadbourne told me. “But I thought it would be for something way cooler than a V2 boulder. I’m still proud that I’m continuing with it and being positive, or at least as positive as I can be.”

Chadbourne discovered climbing while in school at Weber State University in Ogden, Utah, and really got into it in 2020. But before she started climbing, while growing up in northern Utah, she was a gymnast who had heard plenty of stories about gymnasts breaking their necks and becoming paralyzed.

“It was something that I always feared and tried to avoid,” Chadbourne says of paralysis.

On November 22, 2022, that fear became reality.

When Chadbourne began working as a routesetter in September of that year at The Front in Ogden, routesetters used a pull-through system of static lines, an alpine butterfly knot, and quickdraw anchors to access the wall. Chadbourne says the setup made her nervous from the start. She worried the knot could slip through the quickdraw carabiners. And the system differed from the one she’d used while routesetting at Weber State University.

“I was pretty new to all of it. So I was just like, ‘If this is what you guys have been using, obviously it’s safe. I’m sure it’s fine.’ Obviously, it wasn’t fine.”

Not long after she started, Chadbourne and The Front have stated that another routesetter reattached the wrong side of the rope when they were lowering and fell from the wall. According to both The Front and Chadbourne, the climber wasn’t seriously injured. After the accident, The Front retired the system and hosted trainings to get all routesetters up to speed on the new standards. Eddie Morillas, the gym’s director of routesetting, told Climbing via email that an updated fall-protection plan took effect on October 18, 2022, removing the alpine butterfly setup.

Under the new standard, Morillas explained that routesetters “are only permitted to gain initial access to anchors and remove working lines while on belay or connected to an autobelay unit, where applicable.” He added that Chadbourne attended two training sessions on the new standard before her accident.

According to Chadbourne, the head routesetter that day instructed her to use the alpine butterfly setup. The Front confirmed this, stating in an email to Climbing: “Our understanding is that the routesetter in charge let Brittany use the alpine butterfly system, which went against The Front’s fall protection plan and the training every setter on the team had completed.”

The precise cause of Chadbourne’s fall remains uncertain. Morillas told Climbing that The Front’s investigation found her knots were tied correctly, but that she pulled the rope in the wrong direction, preventing the knot from cinching around the anchor. According to The Front, the knot temporarily caught in the anchor before slipping through as Chadbourne moved up the rope.

Chadbourne says she followed the head routesetter’s instructions when setting up her static line correctly and had a coworker double-check it. Near the top of the wall, as she slid her ascender upward, she felt the rope go slack. She believes the knot slipped through the quickdraws, just as she’d feared.

“I immediately knew that I was falling,” she says. “So I just closed my eyes and fell 30 feet.”

When Chadbourne hit the ground, her left leg took most of the impact. She shattered her tibia and fibula, and suffered a burst fracture of her T12 vertebra and compression fractures at her C7, T5, and T7 vertebrae. The fall left her paralyzed from the belly button down.

Chadbourne says she receives workers’ compensation benefits that cover her medical expenses and equipment, including wheelchairs. She has chosen not to pursue a lawsuit, saying she was already overwhelmed by the aftermath of the accident and didn’t believe legal action would change what had happened.

Initially, Chadbourne felt embarrassed that her accident happened in a gym rather than outdoors. She’d spent years caving, exploring abandoned mines, and climbing outside without serious injury, only to suffer a life-altering fall inside a gym.

But that embarrassment didn’t diminish her desire to climb.

“I pretty much knew immediately that I would go back into climbing,” Chadbourne says. “Even as I was being loaded onto the helicopter to go to the hospital.” She didn’t know a lot about paraclimbing, but she figured there must be people out there climbing without their legs. “Campusing is a thing, so I’m sure somebody’s doing it,” she recalls thinking.

After two months in the hospital and another month adjusting to her new normal at home, Chadbourne’s doctor cleared her to climb. But her first time back on belay was “pretty rough.” She made it about 10 feet off the ground, partially hoisted up by her belayer. At first, she couldn’t climb high enough to experience the fear she’d felt during her fall. But as she grew stronger and began climbing higher, Chadbourne admits some of the fear came back.

Chadbourne still has days when she cries about her injury, sometimes even while climbing.

“Day to day, my life is just as hard now as it was four years ago [when I was first injured],” she adds. “I’m just doing more.” And she is. She’s traveling more, training more, and trying things she once thought were out of reach, including becoming an ambitious competitive climber.

Before the accident, Chadbourne loved technical slab climbing. Now, without the use of her legs or lower abdomen, she competes in the AL1 paraclimbing classification, also known as seated climbing.

In 2024, just two years after her injury, Chadbourne made Team USA. She earned bronze in the women’s AL1 competition at the 2025 Paraclimbing National Championships and silver in 2026.

But she found herself in a small competitive field. With so few women in the AL1 category, Chadbourne often faced just one or two other athletes. She and fellow paraclimber Carlie Cook advocated for mixed-gender AL1 competition, which would allow women and men to compete against each other. In March 2025, the International Federation of Sport Climbing released updated paraclimbing guidelines permitting mixed-gender competition in the category.

Competing against a larger field has made the sport more exciting for Chadbourne. “If it was just women, it would be like three or four of us in the entire world,” she says. “So to be able to compete against 20-plus people is really exciting. And it makes me want to beat the men!”

Now, Chadbourne has her sights set on making a World Cup final one day. She’s also working to bring more athletes with paraplegia, especially women, into the sport. One day, she hopes there will be enough female competitors to support a separate women’s category.

But there’s another stage Chadbourne hopes to compete on someday: the Paralympics.

“When I had heard rumors that [paraclimbing] was going to be in the LA Games, I got really excited,” Chadbourne recalls. But like many paraclimbing categories, AL1 was excluded from climbing’s Paralympic debut at the Los Angeles 2028 Games.

Chadbourne hopes that will change for the 2032 Paralympics in Australia. In the meantime, she’s also training in Nordic skiing and biathlon, with ambitions of making Team USA in both sports.

For Chadbourne, staying busy is one way of coping with the difficult days. “The busier you are, your brain just forgets to be sad,” she says.

“Continual motion builds on itself,” she adds. “I don’t really have any other choice but to just keep on going.”

Chadbourne’s momentum has carried into her work with other climbers. Between competitions and training, she teaches climbing and continues to set routes at Weber State University.

There, she’s become an advocate for safe routesetting and climbing practices, encouraging climbers to ask questions about systems before trusting them.

“I’m the giant asterisk at the end of ‘climbing is dangerous,’” Chadbourne says about teaching college students. “I want to prepare them that this is a possibility, but then I prepare them to avoid it in as many ways as they can.”

The most important lesson Chadbourne underscores is to always speak up if you don’t understand how something works. No matter their experience level, Chadbourne wants all climbers to feel comfortable raising concerns and asking questions.

“I want them to feel like they have a voice.”
