7 Ultrarunning Lessons Des Linden Learned from Her 100-Mile Debut

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Andy Cochrane and Nick Danielson were on the ground outside of Fountain Hills, Arizona, on October 31 to document Des Linden’s 100-mile debut at the Javelina Jundred. They bore witness to her exceptional first 60 miles, during which she took the lead in under-course-record pace, all the way through to her gritting out the remaining 40 miles to a ninth-place finish. They spoke with Linden after the race to find out what she learned—and why she’s already angling to come back.

Despite Des Linden returning to Javelina Jundred “jeadquarters” five minutes ahead of schedule and in first place, I was worried. I had followed her for the final half mile, camera rolling as her leg visibly seized up, likely from cramping. She had run 60 miles at course record pace, but still had forty to go—a long way without a fully functioning lower half. Linden sat on a chair and talked through the situation with her crew. A shoe switch, a lot more electrolytes, and roll the dice. Before I get into how the rest of her race played out, let me back up.

Being a freelance filmmaker feels a bit like being a mercenary: One moment you have no plans for the rest of the year, and the next, you’re hopping in a rental van and rallying across the Southwest during a historic October storm to get to the next job. This is exactly what my business partner Nick and I did after getting the call to document Des Linden’s first 100-mile race at the Javelina Jundred on October 31.

After landing in Salt Lake City, we picked up a camper van from Roadsurfer, threw our bags in the back, and headed south on I-15. A quick stop in Moab for a 50K race of our own was followed by four days evading floods and trying to figure out which desert roads were impassable after the recent downpours—which turned out to be pretty good foretelling for the race itself.

On the three previous occasions we’ve attended this event, it has been a delightful closing chapter to the season; Javelina has served as a sort of reward for making it to the end of our race coverage for the year, where the burden of logistics is removed from the job of filming. Instead of chasing athletes across the dirt roads of the Sierra Nevada at the Western States 100 or around a heavily glaciated massif at UTMB, the athletes at Javelina run in circles back to you. Five circles, to be exact. Adding to the enjoyment factor, Javelina is typically a giant Halloween party with racers and spectators dressed in costume under the warm desert sun.

However, this year was a little different. Deluge after deluge in the 24 hours leading up to the race saturated the race course. The sky relented just before the gun went off, but by the time the race had ended, over two more inches of precipitation had fallen. Not exactly what you expect for an event in the arid Sonoran Desert outside Phoenix, Arizona, but that’s why we love ultrarunning.

We did our best to keep the cameras dry and our bodies warm as we followed Linden on her third ultra—well, fourth, if you count the road 50K world record. Despite a stunning road running resume, Linden was quick to point out that she’s still a newbie at trails and ultras but is excited to keep learning. Javelina’s first three laps were nearly perfect. But soon after her body shut down it was a struggle to make it to the finish line. At one point she had to stop for almost an hour, sitting and stretching until her legs finally relaxed and let her move again. And move again she did: Linden finished her first 100-miler as the ninth woman in 16:58:01, about three hours off winner Heather Jackson. We sat down with her the day after the race to learn her biggest lessons from the process.

Linden’s biggest takeaway from Javelina is to treat 100-milers, no matter the course, weather, or circumstances, with a ton of humility. Because they ask so much of your mind and body and are a completely unique style of racing, you can’t race a 100-miler like any other distance. “Just like when I started marathons,” Linden said, “I learned that if you’re cavalier with how you race it, you’ll pay the price at some point.”

Linden was patient on her first 20-mile lap, sitting a few minutes back from the leaders. Feeling good, she picked up the pace on the next two laps, picking off all of the ladies in front of her to take the lead around the halfway mark. By the time she made it to the end of the third loop at mile 60, she had a six-minute gap on second—but her legs were starting to cramp.

Survival quickly became the goal of lap four and the best way to do that was by listening to her pacer, Magda Boulet. Thankfully, there is no one on planet Earth better suited for the job. Boulet, a fellow U.S. Olympian in the marathon, is also a Western States winner and president of GU Energy Labs. She helped coax Linden forward, made sure she kept fueling, and stuck with her through all the ups and downs.

One of Linden’s biggest challenges in the transition from road to trails and marathons to ultras is how much she needs to consume during a race—and how hard it is to catch up if she falls behind. At Javelina, Linden took most of her calories and carbs with fluids, drinking three half-liter (500 mL) bottles each lap. This fueling plan was developed for the typical heat of Javelina, but still worked well, as daytime temps almost hit 80 degrees Fahrenheit with 100% humidity.

Unfortunately, it wasn’t enough to keep her lower body from cramping around mile 60, which forced her to stop and stretch many times on the fourth lap. That setback resulted in her giving up the lead and ultimately the chance to win the race. “The fueling game has changed the sport,” Linden said. “You see it on the roads, but especially here. It’s something I will keep working on, probably forever.”

Assuming Javelina would be hot and dry like just about every other year, Linden packed for those conditions, putting her focus on cooling strategies and nutrition for the heat. She expected hardpack trails and excruciatingly hot sun, but the weather gods had other plans. Heavy rain required Linden and her crew to scramble to find key pieces of gear, including a raincoat. And because lap four took much longer than expected, Linden ran the last couple miles of it after the sun set, sans headlamp.

Next time she’ll prepare for worst-case scenarios, but will also understand when to pull the plug. “You need to know when to be tough and when it’s enough,” Linden said. “If I had to do another lap, I would have been on the edge of hypothermia and the trails were so, so slippery. There is nothing wrong with saving it for another day, but also great value in staying tough.”

Pacing strategy is different at every ultra, but there are some broad themes that apply to the majority of runners and racers, especially for someone with Linden’s ability to close in the back half if she’s feeling good. Surprisingly, this is similar to how she used to strategize for marathons. The first third should feel easy, the middle third is about keeping the pace steady, and the final third is when the racing begins. “The first three or even three-and-a-half laps are transportation,”  Linden said, “the final lap or two is about racing.”

When things got hard for Linden on the final two laps, she leaned into her crew to get to the finish. After a lap with Boulet, Linden picked up her husband Ryan as a pacer. He’s one of the most positive and energetic people you’ll ever meet, and his positivity and drive—paired with a little patience—helped get her through heavy rain and strong winds in the last 20 miles.

Her main support at Javelina HQ was Charlie Lawrence, the 100K world record holder and good friend. “A crew that knows you well, knows the sport well, and knows the emotional ups and downs of 100 miles is a real difference maker,” Linden said. “I was excited to be around everyone all week, so it was good energy going into the race, which is key.”

While Linden isn’t sure what the future holds or what her race calendar will look like next year, she walked away from the race with a surprising feeling. “I 100% want to come back to Javelina, it feels like unfinished business,” Linden said. “I didn’t think it would be this clear, but I know I can do so much better here.”
