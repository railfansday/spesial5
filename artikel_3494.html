Why Prince William Is Reluctant to Become Head of the Commonwealth

Tanggal: 2026-11-11

Following King Charles’s successful nine-day tour to the Caribbean, the future of the Commonwealth has become a much debated subject. At last week’s Commonwealth Heads of Government Meeting (CHOGM) in Antigua and Barbuda, Charles, who was designated Head of the Commonwealth, a role he assumed after his late mother Queen Elizabeth, told assembled leaders he found the voluntary union of 56 nations to be a “source of enormous encouragement and pride.”

However, Buckingham Palace has made it clear this weekend that Charles will only serve as monarch in the Caribbean realms “for as long as they wish” and that the monarch is open to change. According to The Times, Charles is “fully open” to those realms removing him as head of state.

While Charles was in the Caribbean, there was ongoing debate about slave trade reparations, which the monarch addressed in his speech to CHOGM. Describing it as a “monstrous injustice” he expressed his “profound personal sorrow” but fell short of making a formal apology for Britain’s role in the slave trade. (Since King Charles is a ceremonial head of state, he is unable to apologize on behalf of Britain unless the government sanctions it.)

Amid calls for reparations, there are questions about the future of the Commonwealth and who its next head should be. In conversation with Vanity Fair, constitutional expert Ed Owens, author of After Elizabeth: Can the Monarchy Save Itself?, discusses what’s in store for the future.

This interview has been edited and condensed for clarity.

Vanity Fair: Prince William has made it clear that he does not necessarily expect to follow his father as head of the Commonwealth, saying that the decision belongs to the Commonwealth itself.

Ed Owens: Prince William hinted after his difficult 2022 tour of Belize, the Bahamas, and Jamaica that he wasn’t focused on following his father as head of the Commonwealth. A couple of weeks ago The Times of London confirmed that the prince’s position was unchanged on this issue. Crucially, the position of head of the Commonwealth is not hereditary. King Charles got the role because his mother and predecessor, Queen Elizabeth II, actively lobbied Commonwealth heads of state to ensure that the role would pass to him when he became monarch, following her death. But it will not automatically transfer to William when Charles dies.

What is the Commonwealth and how significant is that statement?

The Commonwealth of Nations (as it’s officially known) is a voluntary association of 56 independent member states. Most of these member states were once colonies in the British empire. Decolonization began after World War II and most newly independent states—though no longer part of the empire—became part of the new Commonwealth. This new international body represented change but also continuity, enabling Britain to maintain a global (but mostly symbolic) presence at a time when its real geopolitical power was rapidly in retreat. The Commonwealth also provided a stage for the monarchy to continue to exercise an important international role. George VI was the last emperor and first Head of the Commonwealth. The latter role was invented by the British government to replace the former and allowed the monarch to maintain a semblance of global prestige while helping to facilitate the process of decolonization.

Although most member states have historical ties to Britain, any country can apply to join the Commonwealth—exemplified by recent members like Rwanda, Mozambique, Gabon, and Togo. Members commit to shared principles outlined in the Commonwealth charter, including democracy, human rights, the rule of law, and sustainable development. In reality, though, many member states have failed to live up to these principles, and the Commonwealth has, in recent years, felt morally directionless.

William’s reluctance to take on the mantle of Head of the Commonwealth isn’t that surprising: He and his advisers want to future-proof his reign. One way to do this is to identify potential problems in order to avoid them. The Commonwealth is one such potential problem, and William seems to have calculated that the headship of this organization likely represents more trouble than it is worth.

Does it represent a fundamental shift in the monarchy’s attitude toward the organization, or simply an acknowledgement that the old imperial relationship is no longer sustainable?

We have to remember that 15 members of the Commonwealth (including the UK) are also “realms”—i.e. their head of state is King Charles. I think it’s clear even to the current king that his position as monarch of the 14 realms beyond Britain is unsustainable. Eventually all 14 nations, and perhaps the UK too, will opt to become republics, cutting their ties with the crown. Those nations most likely to become republics soonest—owing to the fact that they have surging republican movements—are the Caribbean realms, two of which (the Bahamas and Antigua and Barbuda) King Charles visited during his recent tour to the Caribbean. Indeed, we can even interpret the tour as the king and his courtiers preparing the ground for this transition to republics. This is because, though former realms will no longer have as their head of state the incumbent monarch, they will almost certainly remain members of the wider Commonwealth.

What King Charles was trying to do with his latest tour was to inject energy and meaning into the idea of the Commonwealth, more than attempt to reinvigorate the personal bonds that connect him to his peoples in the Caribbean. In loudly acknowledging the injustices of the slave trade in which Britain was, for a long time, complicit, while also reminding his hosts that Britain was one of the first major powers to abolish and oppose slavery—the king has sought to address and ease concerns in ways that might enable the Commonwealth to function more cohesively in the future, irrespective of whether the UK’s monarch remains in post as head of the Commonwealth.

When Charles eventually leaves the throne, could the Commonwealth decide that it no longer needs a British monarch as its figurehead? What might replace that role? Will the Commonwealth continue?

I absolutely think [the] Commonwealth nations will look to replace the British monarch with a new figurehead. My sense is that many nations would already prefer to have a different kind of figurehead, for example a head who belongs to one of the 56 member states and is elected by member states for a set term. Alternatively, there could be some kind of “rolling” headship, whereby every member nation is invited to elect an official who will take a 12-month turn as head of the Commonwealth.

Whether or not the Commonwealth will continue is more difficult to say. Certainly one of the reasons it has survived until now is because of the energy and engagement of two British monarchs—Charles and Elizabeth II—who have viewed it as a potential force for good in the world. Without its connection to the British monarchy, it may lose what is left of its momentum. However, its survival also depends on wider geopolitical shifts: If individual governments within the Commonwealth view it as [a] useful arena for bringing together states who could benefit from closer association—be that economic, political, or military association—then it may continue long into the future.

There is an increasingly difficult conversation about Britain’s colonial past, slavery, and reparations. Charles has expressed personal sorrow over the slave trade, but the question of a formal apology remains politically and constitutionally complicated. How much freedom does the king actually have to address these issues, and could William take a different approach?

The king has very little freedom to apologize unless his government specifically sanctions and encourages such an apology. As the recent Caribbean tour made clear, I think Charles would prefer to strike a more nuanced line, reminding audiences that, despite the UK’s colonial past and its involvement in the horrors of slavery, Britain was one of the first major powers to abolish slavery and that it played an important role in bringing to an end the maritime trade in human chattel. Whether William takes a different approach will depend entirely on the UK government’s approach to formal apologies and reparations. My sense is that no government—at least not now or in the near-to-medium-term future—will agree to financial reparations, because historians, philosophers, and politicians are increasingly pushing back on such a solution, questioning why UK taxpayers (already under huge strain) should fork out huge sums for the wrongs committed by some of their ancestors more than two centuries ago.

What would Queen Elizabeth II make of this? She saw the Commonwealth as her father’s legacy and her second family.

I think she would probably be relatively relaxed, recognizing that times change and institutions have to evolve. Let’s not forget that as queen she oversaw many key alterations in the way both the monarchy and Commonwealth functioned through her long reign, as was required by shifts in politics, economics, culture, and social norms. While the Commonwealth was of emotional importance to her—not least for the way it connected her back to her father and predecessors—by the end of her reign it was already a shadow of what it once had been and she would have known that it was likely to continue on a trajectory of decline. William has decided that given the challenges and changes of the mid-20th century the monarchy and royal family’s future is likely to be stronger without the post-imperial baggage of the Commonwealth. I think his grandmother would have respected the way he is putting the family firm first in this instance.
