Voyager 1 had a data outage when it hit one light day. It still does.

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

While Voyager 1 pushed past the one light-day distance milestone, the 49-year-old probe actually hadn't sent any science or engineering information home for days.

The trouble started Nov. 14, and it hasn't let up. Voyager 1 still beams a steady radio signal to Earth, which tells engineers the spacecraft is alive and its antenna still points at Earth. But the signal carries no information about the probe's health or what its instruments are measuring, and engineers don't yet know why.

Voyager 1 crossed the milestone at 5:16 a.m. ET Nov. 18, about 16.1 billion miles away, according to NASA. No other human-made object has traveled so far. Because the probe's radio signal takes a full day to cross space, the team didn't get word until 5:16 a.m. ET Nov. 19, when a ground antenna near Madrid picked it up.

NASA marked the achievement Thursday with an hourlong celebration at its Jet Propulsion Laboratory in Southern California that may have overshadowed the problem. Panelists shared memories of the mission, the agency played a video from fans around the world, and astronaut Jessica Watkins aboard the International Space Station provided a recorded tribute. The host covered the data outage in two sentences before opening the floor to questions. Officials delved into the spacecraft's current condition only after a reporter asked how the spacecraft was doing.

"We have an anomaly that is not something unseen before," said Suzanne Dodd, Voyager's project manager. "We do have a very strong carrier signal. We currently don't have a frame sync, so we're not getting the science or engineering data currently."

The carrier signal is the basic radio wave a spacecraft sends out — the same kind of wave a radio station uses to carry music. Voyager 1 normally loads its data onto that wave in packaged blocks, called frames. Antennas on Earth have to find where each block starts before anyone can read it, and that step is called the frame sync. In other words, right now, engineers can tell the station is on the air, but all they get is dead air.

All is not lost. The signal at least shows that the computer keeping Voyager 1 aimed at Earth is doing its job, and that the probe's antenna and transmitter still work. The trouble more likely sits in a different onboard computer, the one that collects readings from the instruments and other systems and packages them for the trip home.

But, clearly, fixing it won't be fast. Every command engineers send takes two days to produce an answer, the reality that comes with achieving the light-day milestone. NASA warned that finding the cause could take weeks or longer.

The team says it has seen something like this before. In 2023, Voyager 1 also sent a carrier signal with no data. Engineers traced that problem to one of its onboard computers, and NASA said that experience is guiding their troubleshooting efforts. Engineers are also checking whether the outage connects to an episode in October, when the mission lost all contact with Voyager 1 for about 48 hours before the spacecraft reconnected on its own.

If the team feared the outage might be permanent, it didn't show at Thursday's event. Panelists spent most of the hour smiling and reminiscing. When the problem came up, they treated it as routine.

"The anomalies have become normal for us," said Kareem Badaruddin, Voyager's mission manager, who later added: "An engineer with a problem is like a dog with a bone. So we are very busy and very energized to solve these problems."

Age makes everything harder. The original commands were sent on punch cards, Badaruddin said, and the team now has to make modern equipment talk to that 1970s hardware. Power keeps shrinking, too. Both Voyager 1 and its twin, Voyager 2, run on generators that turn heat from decaying plutonium into electricity, and each probe loses about four watts a year. To save energy, engineers have shut off instruments over the years. Voyager 1 now runs just two: a magnetometer, which measures magnetic fields, and a plasma wave instrument, which picks up waves in the thin, electrically charged gas of interstellar space.

This summer, engineers tried a risky move and successfully turned off three devices on Voyager 1 while replacing them with lower-power alternatives that still keep its fuel from freezing. The team expects the savings to keep the probe's instruments running until at least the 50th launch anniversary next summer.

Dodd compared the spacecraft to an aging human who takes longer to bounce back.

"All of us old people have our aches and pains, and you know sometimes it takes longer to stretch out your muscles and remember things," Dodd said. "I'm confident that it won't be too long, and it will send us data back."

Voyager 2 remains healthy with three instruments working. NASA has said it expects to lose both spacecraft eventually to problems engineers can't fix. But until then, the team will keep searching for workarounds like this summer's power swap.

"We pulled a rabbit out of a hat," Badaruddin said. "If we can find some more rabbits, we'll keep doing this."
