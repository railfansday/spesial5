The Unapologetic Ferocity of Activism

In the summer of 1981, my family was returning from a meal in Chinatown. I was wedged between my two older sisters in the back seat of my mother’s mint-green Cadillac when my eldest sister, Maria, jutted her chin toward the window.

“What a shame,” she said. “They’re all gonna die.”

Along the West Side Highway in the West Village, throngs of gorgeous, sinewy men posed in the late-afternoon light.

On July 3, 1981, the New York Times had published its first story about the disease that would become known as AIDS. Most of the country hadn’t heard of it. President Ronald Reagan wouldn't publicly mention AIDS until September 1985, and by then thousands of Americans had already died.

I was 13 years old, and I didn’t yet have a word for what I was. Certainly, what those men represented was an abomination to my parents. But I understood, in the wordless way that children understand the things that frighten them most, that the virus killing those men might one day kill me too.

A decade later, in September 1991, Larry Kramer stood up at an ACT UP meeting in New York and screamed that we were in the middle of a plague. By the end of that year, the epidemic had claimed more than 133,000 American lives. The 2012 documentary How to Survive a Plague captures the moment, and 35 years on, I can still hear Kramer’s banshee cry.

Then came 1996 and HAART (Highly Active Antiretroviral Therapy), which combined multiple antiretroviral drugs from different classes into a single regimen. For the first time, an HIV diagnosis was no longer a death sentence. In the years that followed, people living with HIV who maintained an undetectable viral load could expect a near-normal lifespan.

By the time I began working directly with HIV activists in 2015, HIV was considered a chronic, treatable condition. I assumed the dread I had carried since childhood belonged to another era and that young gay men today didn't live with it as I had.

Then I sat around a table with some of the leading HIV activists to discuss a new clinical trial. I came in expecting to collect insights I would turn into a tactical plan. Instead, the discussion turned into a shouting match. "The problem isn't the medicine," one activist said, slamming his hand on the table. "It's how much it costs."

Everyone turned to me, the pharmaceutical representative in the room. Weren't we on the same side?

It took me longer than I’d like to admit to understand what I had walked into. Many of those activists had been fighting for their lives for their entire adult lives. They had buried partners and friends, watched their government turn a blind eye, and learned that politeness got people killed.

On December 1, we observe World AIDS Day to raise awareness, support people living with HIV, and remember those we lost. For me, it’s a day to remember those activists, whom I came to admire as much as I once feared them. Around that conference table, I learned what ACT UP had been shouting since 1987: Silence = Death.

Their ferocity was never the problem. It’s the reason many people are still here.
