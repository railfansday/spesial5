Who does James Bond work for? MI6, the Secret Intelligence Service

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

MI6 vs. MI5 explained in 10 points, from James Bond's fictional employer to the headquarters, legal powers and ministerial oversight of each British agency

James Bond, the spy Ian Fleming created, is written as an officer of a real government agency. That agency is MI6, formally the Secret Intelligence Service, often shortened to SIS. Fleming's novels usually call Bond's employer simply the Secret Service. The films settled on MI6. The 00 section and its license to kill belong to fiction, not to the real service.

Much of the confusion comes from a second agency with almost the same shorthand name. MI5, formally the Security Service, works inside the U.K. Its remit covers counter-espionage, counter-terrorism and protective security at home. MI6 looks outward and gathers foreign intelligence. The two numbers sit one digit apart, but the jobs behind them point in opposite directions.

That divide between foreign and domestic work sits at the center of the MI6 vs. MI5 question. It also means a fictional MI6 officer chasing villains through London is working on another agency's ground. Inside the U.K., that territory would properly fall to MI5 or the police.

The two agencies share a single origin. Both trace back to the Secret Service Bureau, founded in 1909. The bureau split into home and foreign sections. The numbered names came from War Office military intelligence designations. For most of their history, neither service rested on statute. MI5 gained a statutory basis through the Security Service Act 1989. MI6 followed with the Intelligence Services Act 1994. That law also brought public government acknowledgment of the service.

The differences run through the structure of each organization. MI5 reports to the Home Secretary. MI6 reports to the Foreign Secretary. The two work from separate London headquarters: MI6 at Vauxhall Cross and MI5 at Thames House on Millbank. MI6's chief is known as "C." Fleming modeled M on that tradition. MI5's head holds the title Director General. Their powers differ too. MI5 officers cannot make arrests. Police carry out arrests in MI5 cases.

The 10 slides that follow take one angle each. They cover the agencies' shared beginnings, the laws that govern them, ministerial oversight, their buildings and their chiefs. They also explain the limits on MI5's powers and how the Bond films have put MI6 on screen.

James Bond is written as an officer of MI6, the agency formally called the Secret Intelligence Service, or SIS. MI6 gathers foreign intelligence for Britain. Bond's fictional career belongs to that outward-facing remit.

Bond is not a real person. He is a character created by Ian Fleming, and no actual officer stands behind the name. The organization Fleming placed him in is real.

The novels and the films label that organization differently. In Fleming's books, Bond's employer is usually called the Secret Service. The films use MI6.

Those labels point to a single agency. The Secret Service of the novels, the MI6 of the films and the Secret Intelligence Service of formal usage all describe the same foreign-facing body.

MI5 is a different organization. Its formal name is the Security Service. It handles domestic security rather than foreign intelligence. Bond is not written as an MI5 officer.

Some of Bond's best-known trappings have no real-world equivalent. His 007 designation places him in the 00 section, and that section is fiction.

The same goes for the license to kill. MI6 officers do not hold a literal license to kill. The phrase belongs to the stories, not to the real service.

The short answer to who James Bond works for is MI6. The fuller answer is the Secret Intelligence Service, the formal name of the agency that Fleming's Secret Service stands in for.

MI5, formally known as the Security Service, protects the U.K. from threats at home. Its remit covers three areas: counter-espionage, counter-terrorism and protective security within the U.K.

The two names describe one organization. Security Service is the official title. MI5 is the widely used shorthand.

Counter-espionage means detecting and disrupting spying. For MI5, the target is foreign intelligence activity aimed at the U.K. and carried out on British soil. The task is defensive. The goal is to stop secrets from being stolen at home, not to collect secrets overseas.

Counter-terrorism is the second strand of the remit. Here the concern is terrorist violence planned or carried out inside the U.K. MI5's role is to identify those threats and investigate them.

Protective security is the third strand. It covers measures that make people, information and sites harder to attack or compromise. Counter-espionage and counter-terrorism deal with threats that already exist. Protective security aims to reduce exposure before a threat takes shape.

All three functions point inward. None of them involves gathering intelligence abroad. That domestic focus is what separates the Security Service's work from foreign intelligence gathering.

The line between Britain's two best-known intelligence agencies is drawn by geography. MI6 gathers intelligence abroad, while MI5 protects security inside the U.K.

MI6, formally the Secret Intelligence Service, faces outward. Its business is information about other countries and about threats that take shape beyond Britain's borders.

MI5, formally the Security Service, faces inward. Its work is defensive, and it is carried out at home rather than overseas.

The gap is one of purpose as well as place. Foreign intelligence means finding out what is happening in other parts of the world. Domestic security means guarding the country against threats that reach it.

The two shorthand names make the agencies sound like numbered branches of one organization. They are separate bodies with separate jobs. Each has its own legal basis, its own chief and its own minister.

Location usually settles which of the two agencies is responsible. Intelligence gathered in other countries belongs to MI6. Protecting the U.K. from threats at home belongs to MI5.

MI5 and MI6 share a single ancestor: the Secret Service Bureau, founded in 1909. The bureau split into a home section and a foreign section. Those two halves grew into the agencies known today as MI5 and MI6.

The home section dealt with matters inside the country. The foreign section looked outward. That division gave each future agency its basic direction long before either carried its modern name.

The home section is the root of MI5, now formally the Security Service. The foreign section is the root of MI6, now formally the Secret Intelligence Service. The modern split between domestic security and foreign intelligence follows the same line the bureau drew.

The shorthand names came from the War Office. MI5 and MI6 began as military intelligence designations within that department. The "MI" in each name stands for military intelligence. The number identified the section.

The formal titles leave that heritage behind. Neither "Security Service" nor "Secret Intelligence Service" contains the word "military." The popular names still carry the old War Office label.

The phrase "Secret Service" also lives on in fiction. Ian Fleming's novels usually use it for James Bond's employer.

MI5 was placed on a statutory footing by the Security Service Act 1989. MI6 gained the same footing through the Intelligence Services Act 1994, which also brought the British government's public acknowledgment of the service.

A statutory basis means an act of Parliament sets out an agency's existence and role in law. Before 1989, no act of Parliament did that for MI5. Before 1994, none did it for MI6.

Both services descend from the Secret Service Bureau of 1909. Measured from that date, MI5 operated for 80 years before a statute defined it. MI6 operated for 85 years before the same happened.

The two laws arrived five years apart. The domestic Security Service was written into law first. The foreign-facing Secret Intelligence Service followed.

Each agency has its own statute rather than a shared one. The Security Service Act 1989 is MI5's law. The Intelligence Services Act 1994 is MI6's. The separate statutes match the split between a service that works at home and one that gathers intelligence abroad.

The 1994 act did two jobs for MI6. It wrote the service into law. It also marked the point at which the government publicly acknowledged that MI6 existed. Until then, the state the service worked for gave no official public confirmation of it.

That second change separates the two laws. The 1989 act gave MI5 a legal footing. The 1994 act gave MI6 both a legal footing and official public recognition. For 85 years, the real agency behind the fictional James Bond had no official public existence.

MI5 and MI6 answer to different cabinet ministers. The Security Service, known as MI5, reports to the Home Secretary. The Secret Intelligence Service, known as MI6, reports to the Foreign Secretary.

Each reporting line matches the agency's remit. MI5's work on counter-espionage, counter-terrorism and protective security takes place within the U.K. Those are domestic matters. The Home Secretary is the cabinet minister responsible for home affairs.

MI6 gathers foreign intelligence. Its work concerns other countries rather than threats inside the U.K. That outward focus lines up with the Foreign Secretary's brief. The Foreign Secretary handles the U.K.'s dealings with the rest of the world.

The split between the two ministers mirrors the split between the two services. The ministers' job titles offer a quick way to tell the agencies apart. Home points to MI5. Foreign points to MI6.

The same structure shows where James Bond would sit if his job were real. Bond is a fictional character, written as an officer of MI6. A real officer in that role would work for a service that answers to the Foreign Secretary, not the Home Secretary.

MI6, formally the Secret Intelligence Service, has its headquarters at Vauxhall Cross in London. MI5, formally the Security Service, is based at Thames House on Millbank, also in London.

Each address belongs to a different job. Vauxhall Cross houses the service that gathers foreign intelligence. Thames House houses the service responsible for security inside the U.K.

One agency works abroad and the other works at home. Even so, both main bases sit in the British capital. The split between the two services is a matter of remit, not of where their headquarters stand.

The buildings also offer a quick way to tell the services apart. A reference to Vauxhall Cross points to MI6. A reference to Thames House or Millbank points to MI5.

The chief of MI6 is known as "C," a tradition that began with Mansfield Smith-Cumming. MI5 is led by a Director General. The two services give their heads different titles.

Smith-Cumming signed documents in green ink. The single letter "C" did not stay with him. It passed to the post itself, and whoever runs the Secret Intelligence Service is known by it.

The letter works as a title rather than a name. It identifies the office, not the individual who holds it. A reader who sees "C" in a reference to British intelligence is looking at a job title, not a person's name.

MI5 uses a more conventional label. Its head is the Director General, a title that describes the job in ordinary administrative terms. Coverage of the Security Service's leadership uses that title rather than a code letter. The Director General heads the agency responsible for counter-espionage, counter-terrorism and protective security within the U.K.

Ian Fleming drew on the "C" tradition when he created "M," Bond's superior in the novels. M is a fictional character. The single-letter title, however, borrows from real practice at MI6. It gives Bond's boss the same initial-only identity that the real chief of the foreign intelligence service carries.

Fleming's novels usually call Bond's employer the Secret Service. The films use the name MI6. In both versions, M heads the fictional counterpart of the real agency led by C.

The borrowing runs in one direction. Fleming modeled M on C, not on the Director General of MI5. That fits the fiction as a whole, in which Bond serves the foreign-facing service rather than the domestic one.

The titles also offer a quick way to tell the agencies apart. A reference to "C" points to MI6, the Secret Intelligence Service, which gathers foreign intelligence. A reference to the Director General points to MI5, the Security Service.

MI5 officers do not have the power to arrest anyone. When a Security Service case reaches the point where a suspect must be detained, police officers make the arrest.

That limit applies across MI5's work. An officer on a counter-terrorism or counter-espionage case cannot personally take a suspect into custody, however far the investigation has gone.

The result is a case shared between two bodies. MI5 works on the threat itself. Police handle the arrest that follows. The detention happens under police authority, not the agency's own.

This separates the real MI5 from the image of an intelligence officer who both uncovers a plot and seizes the people behind it. In MI5's case, those are two different jobs. Two different organizations hold them.

It also marks a clear boundary around what the Security Service is. MI5 is an intelligence and security agency, not a police force. Its officers investigate threats. The power to arrest stays with the police.

The James Bond films place their hero inside MI6, the Secret Intelligence Service. GoldenEye (1995) and later films in the series show the agency's real headquarters at Vauxhall Cross in London.

The building on screen is not an invented office. Vauxhall Cross is where the real MI6 is based.

GoldenEye was released the year after the Intelligence Services Act 1994. That law placed MI6 on a statutory basis. It also brought the government's public acknowledgment of the service.

Judi Dench played M, the head of the service in the films. The character's title traces back to Ian Fleming, who modeled M on the tradition of "C." C is the title used by the real chief of MI6. That tradition began with Mansfield Smith-Cumming, who signed documents in green ink. On screen, the chief is M. In reality, the chief is C.

The films use the name MI6 for Bond's employer. Fleming's novels usually called it the Secret Service.

Parts of the screen job have no real counterpart. The 00 section is fiction. No MI6 officer holds a real license to kill.

Geography marks another gap between the films and reality. MI6 gathers foreign intelligence and reports to the Foreign Secretary. When a film sends Bond after a threat inside the U.K., that work would properly fall to MI5 or the police.

MI5, formally the Security Service, covers that domestic ground. Its remit is counter-espionage, counter-terrorism and protective security within the U.K. It reports to the Home Secretary rather than the Foreign Secretary. Its headquarters is Thames House on Millbank, London. Its head holds the title Director General.
