So you got a Microsoft Voluntary Exit Offer? Here’s what to do next

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

by Nikesh Parekh on Nov 19, 2026 at 12:41 pmNovember 19, 2026 at 12:41 pm

Nikesh (Niki) Parekh is a former Microsoft GM who is now CEO of Seattle-based AI talent recruiting startup Provn. This is the fourth post in his series on “Life after Big Tech,” including the Seattle startup guide, laid off at 50 and VC funding is changing dramatically.

Tens of thousands of Microsoft employees have a big decision to make in the next few weeks: take the company’s offer to leave, or stay and take their chances.

Microsoft chief people officer Amy Coleman announced the initiative in a memo on Oct. 26: a Voluntary Exit Offer (VEO) for about 60,000 employees at level 70 and below, which includes general managers and some vice presidents, in the U.S., Canada, the U.K. and India. That’s roughly 27% of Microsoft’s 223,000 people. The company expects 3,000 or fewer of them to accept.

Microsoft says the terms match its standard severance: base pay tied to level and tenure, continued stock vesting, a period of health coverage and career transition help. The severance package varies greatly based on the seniority or level of the employee and the number of years of service. Employees have until early December to decide and would leave by the end of the year.

The offer follows the Voluntary Retirement Program (VRP) that Microsoft extended to about 8,750 U.S. employees in April. About 30% of them, or roughly 2,600 people, took that offer.

All of this comes as Microsoft cuts jobs and changes the kinds of roles it hires for, while pouring money into AI infrastructure and data centers. At the same time, AI is reshaping the broader workforce, flattening management layers and changing what employers look for in new hires.

The worry I hear most is whether more layoffs are waiting on the other side. So the real choice is whether you leave on your own terms or let the company decide for you.

I hear two questions from Microsoft employees thinking about the VEO: How easily can I get another job, and how hard is it to start a company right now?

Harder than you think, and it depends a lot on who you are. We are in the “low hire, low fire” economy.

There is MASSIVE demand for AI-native builders — product managers, data scientists and engineers — as companies adapt products to deal with AI. Companies are also in the process of adopting AI.

One of the fastest-growing tech roles is the “Forward Deployed Engineer,” who implements AI solutions at customers. Security is also job #1 as models and agents get smarter and better at hacking legacy systems.

But it is very hard to be seen by recruiters and hiring managers right now. Candidates are using AI to customize their resumes to each job and using AI agents to apply, sending out 100 to 200 job applications per day. Recruiters are inundated with resumes and job applications, many of which are fake or inappropriate for the role.

It is critical to leverage your network in the job search, which can be hard if you have spent your entire career within one company.

“It has never been easier to build a product, and it has rarely been harder to fund a company.”

Hiring managers have changed what they look for, too. AI fluency is becoming table stakes. The “process” of how you build/sell/market with AI is more important than what you build. Candidates are expected to show prototypes and proof of how they use AI tools every day.

The jobs themselves are changing. A friend told me last month that he wanted an engineering manager role. There are fewer manager roles in the market now.

AI has driven a reduction in management layers and a general flattening of organizations. The engineering team at a small AI company today is a CTO and a group of strong individual contributors. It is normal for managers to have 10 to 30 direct reports now, versus five to 10 in the pre-AI era.

Product management, program management and design teams are shrinking, as well, as AI automates certain jobs.

Hiring managers may view spending your whole career at Microsoft as a yellow flag. Microsoft is an iconic but unusual company. Almost no one else sells to and services customers the way Microsoft does, with an account executive inside nearly every large enterprise in the world.

The skills that get you promoted in Redmond don’t always carry over to a 500- to 5,000-person software company.

It has never been easier to build a product, and it has rarely been harder to fund a company.

Software is cheap to build now. A product leader and an engineer using AI tools can ship in weeks what used to take a team a year.

This cuts both ways for startups. Every good idea attracts a dozen competitors within months, and the big platforms will copy anything that works.

The true strength of a startup today is the ability to grow revenue quickly and establish leadership in a beachhead market. The sales, marketing, product-led growth and distribution strategies are becoming the most critical parts of the business.

The bar for venture funding is extremely high. Jason Lemkin of SaaStr recently shared data from ICONIQ showing that top “AI forward” startups under $10 million in annual recurring revenue are growing 900% a year at the median.

One venture investor told me they advise their portfolio companies that if they aren’t on track for a $10 million run rate within two years, they won’t raise another round. Last week I spoke with an investor who owns part of a business with $4 million in revenue, growing 100% a year and losing $1 million, and it can’t raise.

1) Think VERY big, raise a lot and grow fast enough to justify it. Build an incredible team pursuing an ambitious idea. Raise a large round based on the promise of becoming the next Anthropic, OpenAI or legal AI startup Harvey. Many of the most interesting companies are not software or AI-related, but are “deep tech” companies in defense, robotics, energy and biotech.

2) Raise a moderate amount of money from angels, family offices and nontraditional funds to build a business that has the ability to be profitable on one round of financing. You can control your destiny by building a company that doesn’t rely on the venture capital funding market.

3) Bootstrap and build a business that you own. Fund it yourself, grow it with revenue and get to profitability.

The question that most people are NOT asking is: What do I really want?

Before December, block out an afternoon and think about the last time you were happy at work.

There are many frameworks for figuring out what you want in your career and life in general. I’m a fan of the Four Elements exercise. Write down three times in your career when you felt energized and effective. From those, work out what gives you energy, where you have the most impact, what culture you do best in and what your constraints are: money, kids, aging parents and how many more years you want to work.

On LinkedIn, I have seen examples of Microsoft employees taking the VEO to start their own businesses in property management, buy car washes, take time to recharge, spend time with family and travel.

Other employees are starting the job search in earnest but not taking the VEO. Many others are just getting back to work and doubling down on Microsoft.

Whichever way you go, start now. Start by aligning on your priorities and what you want from your career. If you stay, use the next year to build your skills with AI and to grow a network outside Redmond. If you leave to build something, decide early which of the three funding paths you’re on, because that choice shapes everything that follows.

The people I’ve watched land well after Big Tech had one thing in common: they knew what they wanted before they walked out the door, whether that was a new role, a company of their own or a year with their family.
