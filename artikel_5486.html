10 lawmakers who set records for tenure and age in Congress

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Free daily briefing on global business news.

A selection of the longest-serving members of Congress and its oldest record holders, from John Dingell's more than 59 years in the House to Strom Thurmond's Senate exit at 100

John Dingell spent more than 59 years in the House of Representatives. Strom Thurmond was still a senator at age 100. Those two careers sit at the far edges of two different records. Together they mark the outer limits of tenure and age on Capitol Hill.

Length of service and age in office are separate measures. Dingell, a Michigan Democrat, holds the record for the longest service in Congress. He entered the House in December 1955 and left in January 2015. Robert Byrd of West Virginia holds the Senate record, with more than 51 years in that chamber. Thurmond, who represented South Carolina, holds a record of a different kind. He was the oldest person ever to serve in the Senate. Ralph Hall of Texas holds the same distinction in the House. He was 91 when he lost his 2014 primary.

Long tenure matters in Congress because seniority shapes power. Committee chairmanships have traditionally gone to members with the most years on a panel. Jamie Whitten of Mississippi, who served in the House for about 53 years, chaired the House Appropriations Committee. That committee writes the bills that fund the federal government. In the Senate, the post of president pro tempore customarily goes to the longest-serving senator in the majority party. Byrd held that office. So did Daniel Inouye of Hawaii, a Medal of Honor recipient who was president pro tempore when he died in 2012.

Dianne Feinstein of California holds a narrower age distinction. She was the oldest sitting senator when she died in office in September 2023 at 90. She had served in the Senate for about 30 years.

The 10 lawmakers below are a selection of the longest-serving members of Congress and its oldest record holders, not a ranking or a complete list. Some hold records outright. Others are included for the length or shape of their careers. Carl Hayden represented Arizona for more than 56 years in the House and Senate, starting in 1912, the year Arizona became a state. Ted Kennedy held a Massachusetts Senate seat for nearly 47 years. Chuck Grassley of Iowa served in the House from 1975 to 1981 before entering the Senate in January 1981. Each slide covers one career: how it began, how it ended and the record or role that defined it.

John Dingell holds the record for the longest service in the history of Congress. The Michigan Democrat served in the House from December 1955 to January 2015, a span of more than 59 years.

His path to Washington began with a family loss. His father, John Dingell Sr., also served in the House and died in office. The younger Dingell won the seat after his father's death. The seat that carried him to the record had first belonged to his father.

House members face voters every two years. Senators serve six-year terms. Dingell's record rested on winning reelection again and again rather than holding a few long terms. Each of those House races could have ended his career.

The record for congressional service counts total time in Congress, including years in both chambers. Dingell spent his entire congressional career in the House. That sets him apart from Robert Byrd, the West Virginia Democrat whose total he overtook. Byrd's total combined his years in the House with his longer run in the Senate. Dingell surpassed that combined figure while serving in one chamber alone.

Dingell passed Byrd's total in 2013. He stayed in the House after setting the new mark. Each term he served after that point extended a record no other member of Congress had reached.

His tenure also carried him past the record for service in the House alone. Jamie Whitten of Mississippi had held that distinction until Dingell surpassed him. That left Dingell holding two marks at once: the longest service in the House and the longest service in Congress overall.

Dingell left the House in January 2015. He died in February 2019.

Robert Byrd of West Virginia holds the record for the longest service in the history of the U.S. Senate. The Democrat took his Senate seat in January 1959 and held it for more than 51 years, until his death on June 28, 2010, at age 92.

Byrd's congressional career started in the other chamber. He won a seat in the House of Representatives and served there from 1953 to 1959. He represented West Virginia in both chambers. He moved directly to the Senate in 1959, the year his House tenure ended.

Those six House years count toward a second kind of record: total time in Congress across both chambers. The longest-serving senator and the longest-serving member of Congress are separate titles. One lawmaker does not always hold both.

For decades, the benchmark for combined congressional service belonged to Carl Hayden of Arizona, who sat in the House and then the Senate. Byrd's House and Senate years together eventually passed Hayden's total, giving the West Virginian the combined record as well.

That combined mark did not last. John Dingell, a Michigan Democrat who served only in the House, passed Byrd's total in 2013. Dingell's record rests on House service alone. It did not affect Byrd's Senate record. The two records measure different things.

Byrd also served as president pro tempore of the Senate. Article I, Section 3 of the Constitution creates the office. The president pro tempore presides over the chamber when the vice president is absent. The officeholder is third in the line of presidential succession, after the vice president and the speaker of the House. The Presidential Succession Act of 1947 sets that order.

Byrd's Senate career did not end with a retirement or an election defeat. He was still a sitting senator when he died. His Senate record therefore covers one continuous tenure that began in 1959 and ended in 2010.

Strom Thurmond of South Carolina is the oldest person ever to serve in the U.S. Senate. He retired from the chamber in January 2003 at age 100.

Thurmond was born on December 5, 1902. He was born in the first years of the 20th century, and his Senate service continued into the 21st.

He took an unusual route into the Senate. He first won the seat in 1954 through a write-in campaign. A write-in candidate's name does not appear on the printed ballot. Voters who want to back that candidate must write the name in themselves. Thurmond won the seat that way.

His first stint in the chamber was brief. Thurmond resigned his seat in 1956. He then ran for the same seat in a special election. A special election fills a vacancy outside the regular election calendar. Thurmond won that race and returned to the Senate.

After that return, Thurmond held the South Carolina seat for the rest of his career. His tenure ran through the second half of the 20th century and ended with his retirement in January 2003.

Thurmond's record measures age in office, not length of service. It rests on how old he was while he held the seat, not on how many years he spent in the chamber. That makes it a separate distinction from the tenure records held by other members of Congress.

Thurmond died in June 2003, the same year he left the Senate.

Jamie Whitten, a Mississippi Democrat, held the record for the longest service in the history of the House of Representatives until John Dingell surpassed him. Whitten entered the House in 1941 and left in January 1995. His tenure lasted about 53 years.

His House career began in the same year the U.S. entered World War II. It ended in January 1995, when the 104th Congress convened. That Congress gave Republicans a House majority for the first time since the 1950s. Whitten spent the entire span in a single chamber.

Franklin D. Roosevelt was in the White House when Whitten arrived. Bill Clinton held the office when he left.

Every House seat goes before voters every two years. Holding the seat that long meant winning his Mississippi district again and again over more than half a century.

Whitten chaired the House Appropriations Committee. The panel writes the annual spending bills that keep federal agencies and programs running. Its power traces to Article I, Section 9 of the Constitution. That clause bars any money from being drawn from the Treasury except through appropriations made by law.

The House traditionally handed committee gavels to the members with the longest continuous service on each panel. Whitten's decades on Appropriations put him at the front of that line.

Whitten's mark counted years in the lower chamber alone. Some lawmakers built longer careers by serving in both the House and the Senate. Carl Hayden of Arizona sat in the House from 1912 to 1927 and in the Senate from 1927 to 1969. His combined service exceeded Whitten's House tenure. Robert Byrd also served in both chambers, moving from the House to the Senate in 1959.

Whitten's House record eventually passed to John Dingell, a Michigan Democrat who entered the chamber in December 1955. Dingell moved past Whitten's total while still in office and became the longest-serving House member in history.

Carl Hayden represented Arizona in Congress for more than 56 years, serving first in the House and then in the Senate. The Arizona Democrat held the record for the longest congressional service in U.S. history until Robert Byrd passed him.

Hayden's congressional career began in 1912, the year Arizona became a state. Arizona entered the Union on February 14, 1912, as the 48th state. Hayden became the new state's first member of the House of Representatives.

He served in the House from 1912 until 1927. House members face voters every two years. Holding the seat for that long meant winning one election after another.

In 1927, Hayden moved across the Capitol to the Senate. He won the seat in the 1926 election. He held it until 1969. Senators serve six-year terms. After the switch, he faced Arizona voters far less often than he had as a House member.

His Senate career came after a major change in how senators were chosen. The 17th Amendment, ratified in 1913, gave voters the power to elect senators directly. Before that, the Constitution left the choice to state legislatures. Every Senate term Hayden served fell under the newer system of popular election.

Hayden's exit from Congress came by choice. He did not seek reelection in 1968. He left the Senate when his final term ended in January 1969.

His combined total in both chambers came to more than 56 years. That figure stood as the benchmark for congressional longevity until Byrd's service in the House and Senate went past it.

Hayden's record depended on time in both chambers rather than either one alone. Byrd, who later took the record, followed the same route. He served in the House from 1953 to 1959 before moving to the Senate. Daniel Inouye of Hawaii also started in the House, in 1959, and moved to the Senate in 1963.

Daniel Inouye represented Hawaii in Congress from 1959 until his death on December 17, 2012, first in the House and then in the Senate. A World War II Medal of Honor recipient, he was serving as president pro tempore of the Senate when he died.

Inouye entered the House in 1959, the year Hawaii became the 50th U.S. state. He was the first Japanese American to serve in Congress. He stayed in the House until 1963, when he moved to the Senate.

He held the Senate seat for the rest of his life. Senators serve six-year terms, compared with two-year terms for House members. Inouye was still in office when he died in December 2012.

His military record came before his political career. During World War II, Inouye fought with the 442nd Regimental Combat Team. That U.S. Army unit was made up largely of Japanese American soldiers. He lost his right arm in combat in Italy in April 1945.

The Army first awarded him the Distinguished Service Cross for those actions. In 2000, President Bill Clinton upgraded the award to the Medal of Honor. Inouye was one of 22 Asian American veterans honored at that White House ceremony. The Medal of Honor is the highest U.S. military decoration for valor in combat.

In the Senate, Inouye sat on the select committee that investigated the Watergate scandal in 1973. He chaired the Senate select committee on the Iran-Contra affair in 1987. From 2009 until his death, he chaired the Senate Appropriations Committee, which handles that chamber's spending bills.

The president pro tempore is a Senate office created by the U.S. Constitution. Its holder can preside over the chamber when the vice president is absent. The office ranks third in the presidential line of succession, after the vice president and the speaker of the House. Inouye took the post in 2010, after Robert Byrd died, and held it until his death.

In 2013, President Barack Obama awarded Inouye the Presidential Medal of Freedom posthumously.

Ted Kennedy held a U.S. Senate seat from Massachusetts for nearly 47 years. The Democrat entered the Senate in 1962 and kept the seat until he died on August 25, 2009.

Senators serve six-year terms. Holding one seat for nearly 47 years meant Kennedy had to win reelection from Massachusetts voters repeatedly over the course of his career.

Kennedy's Senate service ended with his death in office. He did not retire from the seat.

He was the brother of John F. Kennedy and Robert F. Kennedy.

Dianne Feinstein was the oldest sitting senator when she died in office on September 29, 2023, at age 90. The California Democrat had served in the Senate for about 30 years.

Feinstein was born on June 22, 1933. She joined the Senate in November 1992.

Her age distinction was relative, not all-time. It compared her with the senators serving alongside her when she died. It did not compare her with every senator in the chamber's history.

She held the seat until her death. Her Senate career did not end through retirement or an election defeat.

Her place among these lawmakers rests on age in office rather than on length of service. Her Senate tenure was shorter than the multi-decade records held by several other members of Congress.

Ralph Hall of Texas is the oldest person ever to serve in the U.S. House of Representatives. Born on May 3, 1923, he lost his 2014 primary at age 91.

Hall's House career began in 1981. He took his seat as a Democrat representing a Texas district.

He did not stay in that party. Later in his House tenure, Hall switched his affiliation and became a Republican. As a result, he served his Texas constituents under two different party labels.

His age record rests on how the Constitution treats House service. Article I sets a minimum age of 25 for representatives but no maximum age. A representative must also have been a U.S. citizen for at least seven years and must live in the state he or she represents. Article I also sets two-year terms. Every member must face voters again at each election to keep the seat.

Congress has no term limits either. In 1995, the Supreme Court ruled in U.S. Term Limits v. Thornton that states cannot impose term limits on their members of Congress. A representative who stays in office into old age has done so by winning one election after another.

Hall's run ended in 2014. A primary is the contest in which a party's voters choose its nominee for the general election. Losing his meant Hall would not appear on the November ballot as his party's candidate for the seat.

He served out the rest of his term. His tenure ended in January 2015, when the new Congress convened and his successor was sworn in. The 20th Amendment sets noon on January 3 as the start of each new Congress, unless lawmakers pass a law choosing a different day.

Hall's mark is a record for age, not for length of service. John Dingell of Michigan holds the record for the longest House tenure. Hall's record also applies to the House alone. The Senate keeps its own separate mark for the oldest person to serve in that chamber.

Chuck Grassley, an Iowa Republican, served in the U.S. House from 1975 to 1981 and entered the Senate in January 1981. His time in Congress spans both chambers.

Grassley was born on September 17, 1933. That is the same year Dianne Feinstein of California was born.

He began representing Iowa in the House of Representatives in 1975. His House tenure ran through 1981.

The switch to the Senate came at the start of that year. Grassley took his Senate seat in January 1981.

His path from the House to the Senate matches the careers of Robert Byrd, Carl Hayden and Daniel Inouye. Each of them also served in both chambers.
