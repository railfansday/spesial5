The professions Americans trust the most — and least

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Doctors, scientists, and teachers earn the most trust in a 31-country survey, while politicians and influencers sit at the bottom of 20 groups.

A patient fills a prescription on a doctor's word. A parent signs a field trip form because a teacher vouched for the outing. A voter hears a campaign pledge and weighs whether to believe it. Each of these moments turns on a quiet judgment about whether the person on the other side deserves credence. Professionals shape the health care people receive, the schooling their children get, the money they save, the laws they live under, and the news they read. Most people cannot check that work for themselves. Few patients can audit a diagnosis, and few savers can inspect a bank's books. Faith in expertise fills the space between what people know and what they must accept, and it steers everyday choices large and small.

Public confidence splinters across these roles. Some occupations command broad goodwill, and others draw open suspicion. The divide runs through single fields and single institutions, where two jobs that share a label can land far apart in public esteem. A broad tag such as "business" or "government" can hide those splits, and anyone reading the popular mood through one catchall word risks missing them. Approval and doubt coexist. A single job can win praise from one slice of the population and scorn from another, and a tally of supporters on its own misses the balance between the two camps. Measuring how far trust outruns distrust, or the reverse, tells a clearer story about where each line of work stands.

Visual Capitalist ranked 20 professions and groups by that balance, using data from the Ipsos Global Trustworthiness Index 2026. Ipsos polled 23,537 adults online across 31 countries, with fieldwork running from June 19 to July 3, 2026. Respondents judged whether each profession or group in their own country was trustworthy or untrustworthy. Net trust, the measure behind the ranking, subtracts the share who chose untrustworthy from the share who chose trustworthy and expresses the result in percentage points. A group that 50% of respondents call trustworthy and 30% call untrustworthy, for example, would earn a net score of +20. The figure captures the margin of trust over distrust. It does not represent the share of people who trust the group. A positive score shows trust outweighing distrust, and a negative score signals the opposite. Each of the 31 countries carries equal weight in the averages, so the results do not form a global estimate scaled to population. The published figures leave the countries unnamed and offer no results for any one of them.

Doctors claim the highest net trust of all 20 groups in the survey, at +43 points. They belong to the health and knowledge category, a cluster that also takes in scientists, teachers, and personal trainers. Among adults polled across 31 countries, no other occupation drew a wider margin of confidence over doubt.

A 91-point gulf separates doctors from politicians, who post -48 at the opposite end of the list. Visual Capitalist calls the distance stark. No two groups stand farther apart, so the doctor-politician pairing marks the full width of public opinion the index captures.

Skepticism toward politicians runs deeper than goodwill toward doctors. Politicians sink 48 points below zero, and doctors climb 43 points above it. The bottom figure outweighs the top one by five points in magnitude, which suggests the survey's harshest verdict carries more force than its warmest endorsement.

Doctors hold a 27-point margin over the armed forces at +16. Every group outside the health and knowledge category, from judges and police to bankers and journalists, falls behind physicians by at least that distance.

Personal trainers, a fellow health and knowledge group, register +10, a full 33 points beneath doctors. A shared category label does not guarantee a shared standing with the public. The health label cannot account for the doctors' edge, because a group in the same field lags them by a wider gap than the one separating doctors from the armed forces.

Scientists hold a net trust score of +42 points, one point behind doctors and second among the 20 groups in the survey. A gap of a single point at the head of the list amounts to a near tie. Adults across the 31 countries rate researchers almost level with the physicians who treat them.

Doctors, scientists, and teachers fill the three highest positions in 27 of the 31 countries surveyed, according to Ipsos. Four countries depart from that order. The trio's grip across so many countries shows the standing of scientists does not hinge on a few outliers in the combined average.

Teachers, the third member of that trio, trail scientists by five points at +37. The one-point margin separating scientists from doctors is a fifth the size of that cushion. The summit of the list plays out as a two-way contest, with teachers a step back.

Scientists stand 90 points above politicians, the group with the lowest score of the 20. Nearly the full spread of opinion in the survey separates the people who conduct research from the people who seek elected office.

Respondents place influencers nearer to politicians and ministers than to the anchors and journalists who share their field.

Politicians finish with a net score of -48 points, the weakest of all 20 groups, after 13% of respondents called them trustworthy against 61% who called them untrustworthy.

Skeptics outnumber believers by close to five to one. Each respondent who vouched for politicians stood alongside about 4.7 who dismissed them. The 61% figure also forms an outright majority, so more than half of everyone surveyed put politicians in the untrustworthy column. Politicians are the only group whose separate trustworthy and untrustworthy shares appear in the published results. Their -48 is the one score on the list readers can break into its two parts.

The two shares account for 74% of respondents. The other 26% landed in neither camp and offered no verdict either way. Those uncommitted respondents form the only pool politicians could win over without changing anyone's mind.

Politics posts the poorest average of the seven categories, at -42.5 across its two groups. Media comes next at -19 for its three roles, leaving the political category 23.5 points behind the second-worst field. Social media influencers account for most of that media deficit, pulling the category below the scores of TV news anchors and journalists, two roles that sit far closer to neutral. Politics has no such buffer. Its other group, government ministers, also draws heavy distrust. Influencers, at -44, still sit four points above politicians and form the politicians' closest neighbor on the list.

Politicians would remain in negative territory if every one of the uncommitted 26% swung to their side. Their trustworthy share would climb to 39%, leaving them 22 points shy of the 61% who distrust them. An even score would require 24% of respondents to cross from distrust to trust, leaving both shares at 37%. Any path to a positive score runs through people who have written politicians off.
