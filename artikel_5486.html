Reno's freeze-free season grew 69.6%, the largest gain of 16 ranked U.S. locations

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Longer allergy seasons can follow longer freeze-free growing seasons — see 10 of 16 U.S. locations ranked by how much theirs grew since the 1970s

Pollen season starts where frost ends. Every night that stays above freezing gives trees, grasses and weeds more time to grow, flower and release pollen. In the places ranked below, those nights now come in longer unbroken runs than they did in the 1970s.

The measure is the freeze-free growing season: the average yearly count of consecutive days with minimum temperatures above 32°F. Climate Crisis 247 ranked 16 U.S. locations by how much that stretch has changed, using a Climate Central analysis. The readings come from station records in the Applied Climate Information System, a NOAA program. The analysis compares the 1970-79 average with the 2015-24 average. Locations are ordered by percent change, not by the number of days added.

The growing season is a stand-in for allergy season, not a direct count of pollen or symptom days. The link runs through plant biology. A year's pollen intensity rises and falls with the length of that year's growing season. Higher carbon dioxide levels can also push plants to make more pollen. Forecasts cited by Climate Crisis 247 put growing seasons in some regions more than a month longer than they were 50 years ago. Those connections tie longer allergy seasons to a warming climate.

This list covers 10 of the 16 ranked locations, presented in the ranking's countdown order from No. 10 to No. 1. They sit in eight climate regions, from the Northeast to the Southwest. Three are in Montana: Bozeman, Helena and Missoula. Their starting points differ widely. In the 1970s, Bozeman averaged 93 freeze-free days a year, while Las Cruces, New Mexico, averaged 196.

Reno, Nevada, holds the No. 1 spot. Its freeze-free season grew 69.6%, from 112 days to 190. That is the largest gain among all 16 ranked locations by a wide margin. Missoula follows at 39.4%, rising from 104 days to 145. Las Cruces now averages 253 days above freezing, the highest 2015-24 figure in this selection.

Bozeman's freeze-free growing season has grown 25.8%. It went from an average of 93 days a year in 1970-79 to 117 days in 2015-24. Local plants now have a longer window to grow and release pollen.

Bozeman sits at No. 10 in the Climate Crisis 247 ranking, which Yahoo News published.

Bozeman added 24 days to its average yearly run of consecutive days with overnight lows above 32°F. Its 1970s baseline of 93 days is the shortest among the 10 locations in this selection. Its recent average of 117 days is also the lowest of the group.

Bozeman falls in the Northern Rockies and Plains climate region. Helena and Missoula carry the same regional label.

Helena's freeze-free growing season averaged 152 days a year in 2015-24, up from 118 days in the 1970s. The 28.8% increase puts the Montana city at No. 9 among the 16 U.S. locations in the Climate Crisis 247 ranking.

That works out to 34 more days a year in an unbroken run without a freeze. For local trees, grasses and weeds, those days add time to grow, flower and release pollen before cold weather returns.

Helena shares the Northern Rockies and Plains climate region with Bozeman and Missoula. Among those three, it started with the longest 1970s baseline. It also has the longest 2015-24 average.

Its percentage gain is larger than Bozeman's 25.8% and smaller than Missoula's. Measured in days added rather than percent, Helena also lands between the two. Bozeman gained fewer days, and Missoula gained more.

Marquette, Michigan, now averages 132 consecutive days a year with daily lows above freezing, up from 106 days in 1970-79. That 24.5% expansion puts the city at No. 8 of the 16 locations ranked.

The gain works out to 26 more freeze-free days a year on average. Trees, grasses and weeds around the city have that added time to grow and shed pollen.

Marquette started from a short base. Among the 10 locations in this selection, only two averaged fewer freeze-free days in the 1970s. Both are in Montana: Bozeman and Missoula.

Marquette's season is still short by this selection's standards. Only Bozeman, Montana, posts a lower 2015-24 average among the 10. Marquette's recent figure of 132 days also falls below the 147-day average that Toledo, Ohio, recorded in 1970-79.

Marquette's 24.5% rise is the smallest percent increase among the 10 locations here. Tupelo, Mississippi, comes closest to it.

Marquette is the only Upper Midwest location in this selection.

Toledo's freeze-free growing season averaged 186 days a year in 2015-24, up from 147 days in 1970-79. That 26.5% increase places the city at No. 7 among the 16 U.S. locations Climate Crisis 247 ranked.

The gap works out to 39 more days a year. That is more than five weeks of additional time with overnight lows above freezing. The recent average now covers roughly half the calendar year.

Toledo is the only city in this selection from the Ohio Valley climate region. Its 1970s baseline of 147 days was already longer than that of any of the three Montana cities on this list.

The figures do not show whether the extra days arrived in spring, in fall or at both ends of the season. They count consecutive days above freezing, not days with pollen in the air.

That timing is relevant to ragweed. Ragweed pollen is the leading cause of allergy symptoms in late summer and early fall. The plant is most common in the Midwest, the region that includes Ohio.

The effects of warming on pollen extend beyond the calendar. Climate change can shift when pollen season starts and ends. It can also change how long the season lasts and how much pollen plants produce. Those changes increase people's exposure to pollen. They also raise the risk of allergy and asthma symptoms.

Tupelo, Mississippi, averaged 237 consecutive days a year with minimum temperatures above freezing in 2015-24, up from 190 days in 1970-79. That 24.7% increase places the city at No. 6 in the source's countdown of 16 ranked U.S. locations.

In raw terms, the change adds up to 47 more frost-free days in an average year. Each of those days pushes back the point at which a freeze cuts off the season for local trees, grasses and weeds.

Tupelo is the only location from the South climate region among the 10 covered here. It also entered the comparison with a long season already in place. Among these 10, only Las Cruces, New Mexico, started the 1970s with a longer average freeze-free stretch. The same order holds in the recent decade. Tupelo's 2015-24 average is the second-longest in this selection, again behind Las Cruces.

That high starting point shapes how the percentage reads. A moderate percent gain on a 190-day base translates into a large number of added days. Only Marquette, Michigan, posted a smaller percent increase among these 10.

Concord, New Hampshire, posted a 36.3% increase, yet it gained 41 days. That is six fewer than Tupelo added. Because the ranking runs on percent change, Concord sits two places above Tupelo despite adding fewer days.

Tupelo's 1970s average is the same figure Reno, Nevada, the No. 1 location in the ranking, reached in 2015-24.

At 237 days, Tupelo's average freeze-free period now spans roughly 65% of the calendar year.

Las Cruces, New Mexico, now averages 253 consecutive days a year with minimum temperatures above 32°F, up from 196 days in the 1970s. That 29.1% increase places the city at No. 5 among the 16 U.S. locations in the ranking.

Measured in days rather than percent, the city's typical freeze-free run grew by 57 days between 1970-79 and 2015-24.

No other city in this selection started from a higher baseline. Its 1970s average already exceeded the 2015-24 average in seven of the other nine cities covered here. Only Tupelo, Mississippi, and Medford, Oregon, now average more than 196 days.

That high starting point shapes the percentage. When a season is already long, each added day makes up a smaller share of the total. The same number of added days would produce a larger percent change in a city with a short 1970s season.

The city sits in the Southwest climate region. It is the only Southwest location among the 10 cities in this selection.

A freeze-free stretch of 253 days leaves most of the calendar open for trees, grasses and weeds to grow and release pollen.

Concord, New Hampshire's freeze-free growing season has expanded 36.3%. It averaged 113 days a year in 1970-79 and 154 days a year in 2015-24.

That change places Concord at No. 4 in Climate Crisis 247's countdown of 16 U.S. locations. It is the only location from the Northeast climate region among the 10 covered here.

Measured in days, Concord now averages 41 more consecutive days a year with minimum temperatures above 32°F than it did in the 1970s. That is nearly six more weeks a year in which local plants can grow and release pollen.

Missoula, Montana, added the same 41 days. Its percent gain was larger, at 39.4%. The difference comes from the starting point. Missoula began with a shorter season, so the same number of added days made up a bigger share of it.

Medford, Oregon, shows the reverse pattern. Its percent change of 36.5% is close to Concord's. Medford started at 156 days and added 57, which is 16 more days than Concord gained. Its longer baseline means those extra days amount to a similar share of the season. The two sit side by side in the countdown, at No. 4 and No. 3.

Reno, Nevada, offers a third comparison. It began the 1970s with almost the same freeze-free season as Concord. Its percent gain, however, was nearly twice as large. Two locations with nearly matching baselines ended up far apart. In this selection, a similar starting length did not produce a similar gain.

Concord's 113-day baseline falls in the lower half of the 10 locations in this selection. Its current average of 154 days sits just above that of Helena, Montana, which now averages 152 days.

Medford, Oregon's freeze-free growing season averaged 213 days a year in 2015-24, up from 156 days in 1970-79. That 36.5% increase puts Medford at No. 3 in the countdown of 16 ranked U.S. locations.

Counted in days, the change comes to 57 more consecutive days a year with overnight lows above freezing. The measure tracks the longest unbroken run of nights that stay above 32°F. In Medford, that run now lasts nearly two months longer than it did in the 1970s.

Medford is the only location in this selection from the Northwest climate region. Its percent gain edges past Concord, New Hampshire's 36.3% by 0.2 percentage points. Only Missoula, Montana, and Reno, Nevada, rank higher in the source's list.

Medford also started from a longer baseline than Concord. Its 1970s average of 156 days was 43 days longer than Concord's 113. Even from that higher starting point, Medford's season grew by more than a third.

The gap between the two cities has widened in absolute terms. In 2015-24, Medford's 213-day average ran 59 days longer than Concord's 154. A similar percentage gain adds more days to a season that was already longer at the start.

Missoula's freeze-free growing season grew from an average of 104 days a year in 1970-79 to 145 days in 2015-24, an increase of 39.4%. That is the second-largest percentage gain among the 16 U.S. locations in the ranking.

That works out to 41 more consecutive days a year with overnight lows above 32°F than in the 1970s. Only Reno, Nevada, posted a larger percentage increase. Missoula trails it by 30.2 percentage points. Medford, Oregon, follows Missoula at No. 3 with 36.5%, starting from a longer 1970s baseline of 156 days. That leaves 2.9 percentage points between the No. 2 and No. 3 spots.

The ranking places Missoula in the Northern Rockies and Plains climate region. It gives the same label to Helena and Bozeman, the other Montana cities in this selection. Missoula's gain is larger than either of theirs. Helena's season rose 28.8%, and Bozeman's rose 25.8%. Missoula's 1970s baseline of 104 days was shorter than Helena's 118 days but longer than Bozeman's. By 2015-24, Missoula's 145-day average still trailed Helena's 152 days but exceeded Bozeman's 117.

Missoula lies in western Montana, in the area that separates the Sapphire range from the Bitterroot range. No other U.S. municipality fully ringed by the Rocky Mountains is larger.

The city's predominant pollen types span trees, grasses and weeds. Tree pollen comes from alder, maple, elm, juniper, poplar, pine, birch, willow, larch and ash. Grasses, sedge and cattail also contribute. Weed sources include ragweed, pigweed, goose-foot and wormwood/mugwort.

Plant growth in Missoula's ecosystem depends on winter snow. Snowmelt waters vegetation in spring. Runoff from that melt then supplies most of the moisture for plants along rivers and on upland ground through summer and early autumn.

The 145-day average measures frost-free time, not pollen. It does not show when Missoula's allergy season begins, how long symptoms last or how high pollen counts climb.

Reno, Nevada's freeze-free growing season expanded 69.6% between 1970-79 and 2015-24, rising from an average of 112 days a year to 190. No other location among the 16 in the ranking posted a larger percent gain.

That works out to 78 more days a year in Reno's average run of consecutive days with lows above 32°F. Those added days give trees, grasses and weeds in the West climate region a longer stretch to grow and release pollen. Reno is the only location from that region in this selection.

Reno started from a modest base. In the 1970s, its 112-day average sat close to those of colder northern locations in the ranking. Concord, New Hampshire, averaged 113 days in that decade, and Helena, Montana, averaged 118.

Those two places have gained less since then. Concord now averages 154 days and Helena 152. Reno's 190-day average runs 36 days longer than Concord's and 38 days longer than Helena's.

Reno has also overtaken Toledo, Ohio. In the 1970s, Toledo's season averaged 147 days, well ahead of Reno's. By 2015-24, Toledo averaged 186 days, a few days short of Reno's 190.

Reno has narrowed the gap with Medford, Oregon, too. In the 1970s, Medford's freeze-free season averaged 156 days, 44 days longer than Reno's. By 2015-24, Medford's 213-day average led Reno's by 23 days.

The largest percent gain is not the same as the longest season. Tupelo, Mississippi, averaged 190 days in the 1970s, the same length as Reno's current average. Tupelo has since climbed to 237 days. A location that starts with a long season needs far more added days to post a large percentage increase.

Summer heat in Reno is uneven. Neighborhoods near the airport run much hotter than the tree-shaded homes close to the Truckee River. Downtown, heavily paved sidewalks around casinos and shopping centers offer little relief.
