Triathlon Gear Our Editors Used and Loved in October 2026

It’s officially the off-season. In Ohio, we’re having the best fall, where no one complains about the weather (rare for Ohioans) and no wind or rain storms have blown through, robbing us of the vibrant change in the leaves. Hiking is a staple way to enjoy the crunchy leaves, swimming has moved fully indoors, and biking has a sort of leisure to it that it doesn’t have when the landscape is green.

As we slow down and explore other outdoor hobbies in the coming months, here’s a look at the gear our editors are reaching for.

This might seem like an unusual choice for a triathlete, but as the race season winds down and we transition from workouts to just-getting-out, I find myself blasting all of my tempo trainers to the back of my closet in favor of this pair of Jeep-wheel-level all-terrain crushers. For those unfamiliar with Norda, the Canadian brand hit the scene in 2021 (bad timing, sorry) with the release of their ultra-premium — read: ultra-expensive — $285 shoe dubbed the 001. Folks had a field day with the high price, but few could deny that the 001 was something different than what was out there already.

Fast forward to today, and Norda is still (crazy) expensive, but they also still offer shoes that are entirely novel in the increasingly crowded trail running shoe world. The 055 was released earlier this summer as a maximal take on the well-loved, but race-focused 005. For me, I love how you can point your foot at literally anything and the thick-but-stable base will eat it up. Better yet, the woven gaiter adds another layer of stability and protection, meaning you don’t really have to think much about where you put your feet, allowing me to, quite simply, zone out and enjoy the view. And that’s what fall “training” is all about, anyway.

Winter is coming, which in Phoenix means two things: socks come out of storage, and they better be day-glo. Because I live and train downtown, where traffic is abundant and drivers are always (always!) distracted, I dress as obnoxiously as possible to make sure they see me. That means neon fabrics, reflective materials, and LED lights on as many points as possible.

Years ago, I interviewed a safety expert who said you especially want these materials on the parts of your body that move he most when you ride or run, as movement is more likely to catch the eye of a driver. These bright socks have reflective yarn woven into the fabric, making my legs easier to spot on rides and runs. They’re comfortable, they stay up, and they stand up to multiple runs and laundry cycles each week.

Do I look like a 90s fever dream in my neon wardrobe? I sure do. But you’ll see me coming from a mile away, and that’s the whole point.

I haven’t had long hair in the 14 years I’ve been a triathlete — until now. I hate the feeling of hair skimming my neck or swishing back and forth as I run, so it has to be all the way up in the ugliest messy bun. Basically, I’m the opposite of Marjolaine Pierré.

I found that the Goody ComforFlex holds my hair well without pulling, no matter the intensity of workouts. And I don’t end up with tons of hair wrapped around them, which leads me to believe they are keeping my hair at least somewhat healthy despite regular chlorine exposure and frequent washes.

They’ve also held up well, so I can use the same one for at least a month before replacing it.

I’m ashamed to admit I’ve been one of *those* triathletes showing up at the local bike shop, frazzled and looking for the nearest mechanic, then frantically explaining how my seat clamp fell down my downtube and lodged itself somewhere seemingly impossible to reach. Yes, this happened to me at Ironman Chattanooga last year. As someone who prides myself on fixing my own bike issues, this was and still is my worst nightmare.

But I know I’m not alone. Between packing and trying to fit in final workouts ahead of travel, we triathletes can make some poor choices. Add to it that we’re limited in what tools we can bring to fix the problems we create, and it’s a recipe for disaster. Even when we have room and enough weight left to throw them in the bag, we often rush the pack job and toss them in without much organization.

After Chattanooga, I vowed to be more prepared. That’s when a friend showed me this trusty little roll-up tool bag that they use for camping. Small enough that it doesn’t take up all the room, big enough to fit what I need, and cheap enough to buy one for myself and a friend. It was the perfect recommendation.

And the best thing is you don’t have to travel for triathlon to get use out of it. Mine hangs in the garage when I’m home, and I use it every time I work on my bike.

— Travis Mundell, social media strategist
