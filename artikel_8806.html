Want to Ride More at Home? These 9 Stationary Bikes Make Training Easy, Quiet, and More Consistent

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

Find the right at-home ride, whether you want a budget spin bike or a premium smart-bike setup.

Weather and daylight don’t always cooperate with our riding schedules. And when we add work hours, family time, yardwork, and life’s daily grind, finding time to ride can feel impossible. Especially during the dark days of late autumn and winter, indoor riding becomes the only realistic option for many of us.

Luckily, indoor cycling is no longer the boring, arduous task it was only a decade ago. Today’s stationary bikes range from no-frills machines to fully connected setups. Some even feature live and on-demand classes, immersive virtual rides, and easy syncing with popular fitness platforms like Strava.

All of our editors and test team ride or train indoors for at least part of the year, sometimes even during the summer months to escape the heat. We’ve logged countless hours on stationary bikes and standard indoor trainers. In our testing, we’ve wrestled with finicky machines, endured glitchy interfaces and endless updates, and spent plenty of time grinding out virtual miles from our basements, garages, and home gyms; some of our editors have even taught spin classes.

Stationary bikes come in three main styles: Upright, recumbent, and dual-action. Upright models feel most like riding a traditional outdoor bike. Recumbent bikes offer a more reclined position, while dual-action bikes add upper-body work to the pedaling action.

Our testing and reviews focus on upright stationary bikes made by cycling-specific brands and general fitness companies. To differentiate between the two, we call models from fitness companies “exercise bikes” and those from cycling brands “indoor bikes” in this story.

Exercise bikes by fitness-oriented brands often feature a more upright riding position, multi-position handlebars with foam padding, and wide, comfort-style seats. The pedals usually have toe clips for use with running shoes.

Indoor bikes from traditional cycling brands typically mimic the body position, ride feel, and functional layout of outdoor bikes and feature dropbars, brake hoods, adjustable crank length, and narrow saddles. They usually feature high resistance for sprint workouts and virtual racing. These bikes used to be expensive, but prices have dropped over the last three years, making them a great choice for everyday indoor riders (not only hardcore enthusiasts).

Stationary bikes come in three main options: upright, recumbent, and dual-action. The bikes recommended here are upright style, which puts you in a position that mimics how you’d sit on a traditional bicycle.

Recumbent exercise bikes seat you in a slightly reclined position with the pedals out in front of your hips rather than directly below, which can be easier on your back and neck. Dual-action exercise bikes have handlebars that move in and out, so riders incorporate their arms into the workout as they pedal.

Look for models with adjustable saddle height and position, plus adjustable handlebars. More adjustability makes it easier to find a comfortable riding position and get a good workout. Like a regular bicycle, a proper fit is crucial for the best experience. I like my indoor bike’s fit to match the position I ride outdoors.

All bikes feature bar-height adjustments, and some models let you adjust reach. Saddle height and setback adjustment options are also a must-have. They also have graduated markings, making it easier to get back to your preferred position after someone else uses the bike.

A dropbar offers hand positions that mimic road bikes, while a traditional bullhorn bar will be more familiar to those of you who frequent studio classes.

The two most common methods for regulating an indoor bike’s resistance are friction and magnets. Friction-based systems use leather or synthetic pads that press against a flywheel—It’s a simple, effective way to add precise, linear resistance. These resistance systems don’t require outside power to operate.

Magnetic resistance is quieter than friction systems. They use magnets (adjacent to the flywheel) to create an electric current that resists the wheel’s rotation. By increasing the current (strengthening the magnetic force), you have greater resistance. With no contact between the flywheel and magnets, this type of bike may offer a smoother ride than a friction-based one.

Some models use electromagnetic resistance controlled by software from a connected app. This allows platforms like Zwift to replicate hills and wind.

Bluetooth-equipped exercise bikes let you transmit your ride data directly to third-party training apps like Peloton or Zwift for in-ride metrics. Most indoor bikes function similarly; the accessories that set them apart. The most basic models are as simple as it gets, just an ergometer with a weighted flywheel and a pad to set resistance. At the other end of the spectrum are bikes like the Peloton, with elaborate software for recorded and live classes.

Decide how much technology you actually need for your indoor rides. Some cyclists want a feature-rich experience with training plans, classes, and group rides, while others want a stripped-down bike that covers the bare essentials.

Measure your available space before buying. If you will need to move it, also check the model’s weight and whether it has wheels.
