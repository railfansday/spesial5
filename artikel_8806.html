5 Pain-Free Arm-Building Alternatives to Barbell Curls

The barbell biceps curl is arguably a cornerstone of arm day as well as a favorite among gymgoers.

But even with its popularity in the weightroom, there are subtle issues that could come from barbell curl, the main one being a straight bar could be uncomfortable for your wrists.

Unlike dumbbells, which offer more freedom of movement, the barbell doesn’t. That fixed grip might not vibe with your wrists and forearms as the weight gets heavier. With form issues like wrist hyperextension, questionable grip width, and a little too much body English, your wrists may rebel.

This doesn’t mean you should ditch it at the first sign of wrist discomfort. Sometimes, adjusting your grip, cleaning up your form, or checking your ego at the gym door is all that’s needed to start curling without wrist pain.

But sometimes, the barbell and your wrists will not get along.

Next, I’ll break down four common reasons barbell curls strain your wrists and what you can do about it. Then I’ll cover five biceps-building alternatives that let you pump up without the pain.

Here are four common reasons barbell curls bother your wrists and how to fix them.

Unlike dumbbells, a barbell locks both hands in a fixed position. Depending on your forearm anatomy, mobility, and grip width, this position may cause wrist discomfort. A barbell leaves little wiggle room, and your wrists may tell you all about it.

Quick Fix: Adjust your grip width for more comfort, or switch to a reverse grip.

As the load gets heavier or you become more fatigued, wrist hyperextension may happen. Instead of maintaining a wrist and forearm alignment, the bar pulls the hands backward. That adds extra demands on a body part that’s already working hard.

Quick Fix: Think knuckles in line with your forearms, and do a partial wrist curl to get your hands in that position.

There’s a time and place for body English, but if it causes unnecessary discomfort by pulling your wrist out of neutral during the curl, reconsider your strategy.

Quick Fix: Lighten the load, cut the set short when excessive momentum takes over, or use a three-second eccentric to slow things down.

Your forearms stabilize your wrists, and if you’ve already done a lot of grip work, your wrists may be tired before you begin. When your forearms and wrists fatigue, your grip may weaken, your wrist position may change, and your biceps will be left unsatisfied.

Quick Fix: If your grip gives out, do direct biceps work earlier in your workout with lighter loads, different grip positions, or the alternative exercises below.

If the form fixes didn’t work, it’s time for the alternative. A good alternative trains the biceps hard while addressing what made barbell curls uncomfortable in the first place.

Angled grips, neutral grips, and even overhand grips may feel better depending on your wrist and forearm anatomy. For example, an EZ bar reduces the amount of supination required, while hammer curls keep your palms facing each other.

Dumbbells, ropes, and resistance bands give your hands and wrists more freedom of movement than a barbell.

Look for exercises that keep your wrists neutral, let you control the lifting and lowering phases, and maintain biceps tension. Dumbbells, cables, and bands each feel different but may let you control the weight better without sacrificing gains.

The bottom line: A good barbell curl alternative should solve your wrist problem without creating a new one.

With that covered, here are five exercises that let you keep building bigger biceps without being married to the barbell.

Each exercise addresses one or more of the problems we discussed earlier, so choose wisely. Also, keep a mirror handy.

Solves: Wrist discomfort from the barbell’s fixed position.

The EZ-bar curl is the closest alternative to the barbell curl without locking the wrist in place. Its angled handles allow your forearms to sit in a slightly more neutral position, which many lifters find more comfortable than the barbell.

Form Tip: Choose a hand position that feels comfortable, and keep your wrists neutral.

Solves: Discomfort from the palms-up grip.

If you’ve been hammering the palms-up position, it’s time to switch. Using a palms-down grip shifts the exercise’s emphasis, placing greater demand on the brachioradialis and brachialis while the biceps still get the attention they deserve. The reverse curl provides a different stimulus and gives you another grip option if wrist pain is an issue.

Form Tip: Keep your elbows close to your sides, curl without body English, and lower with control.

Solves: The difficulty of keeping your wrists aligned during barbell curls.

The rope-cable hammer curl combines the wrist-friendly benefits of the dumbbell hammer curl with a touch of cable magic. This variation gives your wrists and hands more wiggle room, letting you adjust your hand spacing throughout the curl. The cable also provides constant resistance throughout the range of motion, enhancing biceps development.

Form Tip: Attach a rope to a low cable pulley and stand tall with your palms facing each other. Curl the rope toward your shoulders, then let your hands separate at lockout.

Solves: Wrist discomfort from a barbell’s fixed grip.

If barbell curls hurt your wrists, the answer may be to lighten the load and switch resistance types. That’s where the looped resistance band curl earns its place. Unlike a barbell, which provides the same external weight throughout the rep, a resistance band’s resistance increases as it stretches.

And don’t mistake bands for beginner-only equipment. Take a challenging set to near muscular failure with good form, and your biceps will thank you.

Form Tip: Grip the sides with your preferred grip, keep your wrists aligned, and curl toward your shoulders. Lower slowly without letting the band snap your hands back.

Solves: Wrist discomfort from limited freedom of movement.

Unlike a barbell, dumbbells let you rotate your forearms as you curl, moving from a neutral grip at the bottom to a palms-up position near lockout. This rotation is more joint-friendly while still engaging the biceps’ two primary functions: elbow flexion and forearm supination.

Form Tip: Start with dumbbells at your sides, palms facing inward. As you curl one dumbbell toward your shoulder, rotate your palm up. Squeeze your biceps at the top, lower under control, and repeat with the opposite arm.

Sets & Reps: 2–4 sets of 12-15 reps per side

Your biceps cannot tell the difference between a barbell, dumbbell, resistance band, or cable machine. They only care about tension. If that tension comes from a barbell and your wrists are annoyed, why keep hammering it when you have options?

If the form fixes above didn’t work, these five alternatives will help you train for gains without the pain.
