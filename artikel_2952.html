​Should You Trust AI? You Need To Understand The Work First

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Patrick Dajos - Founding Team at Hyperbound.

“Vibe coding” has brought new attention to an already controversial topic: Should you trust AI with your work? I have seen both terrible and exceptionally good results produced with AI.

In my experience, what separates the two often comes down to the person directing the agent: their understanding of the work, the technology and the limitations of the approach they have chosen.

Responsible AI use requires two kinds of competence. You need to understand what the agent produces and why it works. You also need enough technical knowledge to recognize when its approach is failing and identify a better one. Both matter when you are accountable for the result

I treat my AI agent like a coworker. I explain the assignment, provide context and review the work. Effective delegation depends on knowing which responsibilities it can handle, what information it needs and where I will need to intervene.

With software, reviewing the visible result is insufficient. A feature can behave correctly in a demonstration while mishandling permissions, losing data under certain conditions or introducing a dependency the team cannot maintain.

Before approving a change, I should be able to explain what was generated, how it works and why the approach is appropriate. That includes understanding how it fits the existing system and which assumptions its correctness depends on.

An agent’s explanation can help me investigate those questions. It cannot substitute for checking the implementation. If I accept its account without comparing it with the code and observed behavior, I am trusting another generated answer.

A practical review should establish what changed, what was tested, which failure cases were considered and what remains unverified. The reviewer then needs to check the consequential claims. I need enough understanding to defend the decision and help resolve a problem if it fails.

Technical understanding also determines how effectively you can direct an agent before there is anything to approve. I encountered this when I asked Codex to create motion graphics for marketing. It generated images using an AI image model and animated them in After Effects. It completed the assignment, but the result was underwhelming. It looked like the “AI slop” I wanted to avoid.

Having worked with computer graphics, I knew there was another way: I could show AI examples of what I wanted and ask it to write the instructions that tell the computer what to draw as the animation plays. Those instructions are called shaders. Writing code is one of AI’s strengths, and once I gave it that job, it produced an animation we could actually use.​

That experience illustrates why knowing how to ask for something is only part of using AI well. You need to understand the available methods, recognize their limitations and identify which approach gives the agent a better chance of meeting the requirement. Without that knowledge, you may keep requesting improvements within a method that is poorly suited to the task.

The same judgment applies in software development. When an implementation repeatedly fails, you need to assess whether the problem lies in the requirements, the available context or the design itself. Each diagnosis calls for a different intervention.

This places a responsibility on the user to recognize their own limitations. If I cannot assess an approach or explain its output, I need to address that gap before relying on the result.

AI can be exceptionally useful for doing exactly that. It can help research an unfamiliar subject, compare methods and explain a mechanism through successive examples. I can question an explanation, ask what would make an approach fail and work through the parts I do not understand.

The test is whether I can then reason about the decision myself. Can I explain its tradeoffs? Can I recognize a situation in which another approach would be better? Important claims still need verification against reliable sources.

Nobody needs to master every specialty, but consequential technical work needs someone qualified to assess it. When my understanding is insufficient, I need to learn more or involve someone with the relevant expertise.

For technology leaders, these competencies should shape how AI is introduced into a team. Faster output has value when people can choose an appropriate method, evaluate the implementation and support the finished work.

AI can perform a substantial share of the execution. The person approving it remains responsible for knowing what they are accepting and why it deserves their confidence. If the work goes out under my name, I need to be able to stand behind it.​
