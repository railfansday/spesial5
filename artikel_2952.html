A Personal AI Assistant For Every One Of Us? Good Idea Or Not?

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Michal Rahamim is Co-Founder & COO of Liberty Pixel, leading AI innovation, operations and technology strategy across its gaming portfolio.

​As someone born in the 1980s, I have to say that the world never stops messing with my head.

I was born into a world with no mobile phones, no home internet and, in the worst-case scenario, you went downstairs and yelled to your neighbor to come play with you. In the best-case scenario, you called their house, their parents answered and they let you speak to them.

I lived through the BBS revolution, the internet, then the smartphone, and just when I finally got used to having a TikTok account, I now have to start preparing for the fact that, at some point, I’m going to have a personal AI assistant. Whether it’s Grok Bot, Claude Duet or Meta’s Muse, new tools are popping up like mushrooms after the rain—and let me remind you, we’re in 2026.​

The agentic internet is growing fast, and I believe that around 2030 to 2035, your agent will simply talk to the agent of whatever website you’re visiting and take care of everything by itself based on very simple instructions from you.

This is already starting to happen today, but it will become far more common. And then comes the question: Is this even something you would want?

I’m not going to review all the new tools here. I’m more interested in the psychological and philosophical point behind all of this.

The advantages are obvious: less effort filtering emails and doing repetitive things that consume precious time from your life.

The disadvantages are also obvious: giving an agent enormous control over your life and your apps—okay, we aren’t at Skynet yet—and losing some control over the noise and activity in your own life, or more accurately, the weakening of the thinking muscle.

We already got used to asking our favorite chatbot to write letters to the tax authorities or customer support for us. Now it’s going to do those things on its own.

Ray Kurzweil defined the technological singularity as the point at which the pace of technological progress becomes so fast and exponential that we can no longer predict or fully understand the future, and the boundaries of progress can no longer really be mapped. His timeline estimated that human-level artificial intelligence, or AGI, would arrive around 2029, with the singularity itself arriving around 2045. According to him, that doesn’t leave us much time before we reach a stage where the human brain and technology—AI, nanotechnology and other systems—begin to merge, blurring the line between biological and synthetic.

More realistic estimates suggest that somewhere around 2035, the internet as we know it—browsers, manual searches and isolated apps—will disappear. It will be replaced by an economy of autonomous agents.

Every person, company and government system will have deeply capable AI agents that act, negotiate and make independent decisions 24/7 without human involvement. In other words, they’ll think for you.

Today, we think about reading the news, reading opinion columns—like this one, for example—playing games or enjoying a funny Reel. But once we give agents the power to do everything for us, will we feel that we have more time to enjoy those things, or will we become so passive that we’ll no longer even need to search the internet for the games we love and feel excited about when we find them? Instead, the games will be spoon-fed directly into our mouths—or, to be more accurate, directly into our brains—to the point where the experience of enjoyment itself may look completely different.

You probably think I’m exaggerating. But Kurzweil, that same futurist, argued that toward the end of the 2030s, the internet will become biological and nanobots—tiny robots the size of blood cells—will enter our bloodstream and navigate their way to the brain.

They’ll connect our cortex directly to the cloud. In other words, you want to watch a Reel? You just think about it.

The agentic internet becomes an “internet of thoughts.”​ If you want to know a language or understand quantum physics, you won’t need to search for it online. The AI directly inside your brain will provide the answer instantly, to the point where you may no longer know whether a thought came from your biological side or your digital side.

Admit it. It sounds like an episode of Black Mirror.

But it doesn’t stop there. Futurists argue that by around 2045—only about 20 years from now—humanoid robots will be working around us everywhere, and their prices will eventually become something like buying a pink vest on Temu.​

Then, robots will begin assembling other robots themselves.

Molecular robotics will eventually add the ability to change the structure of matter. This would mean that we could “manufacture” physical objects, clothing or even food directly from software, potentially solving global resource scarcity.

And then, as we approach 2045—the year of the singularity—the digital, non-biological part of our intelligence will become far more dominant than the biological part. Humans will be able to change their physical appearance at will, live inside virtual realities that feel completely real and perhaps even perform “mind uploading” to the cloud, effectively becoming digitally immortal.

Well, at this point, it’s a brave new world.​

Personally, I’m thinking twice before letting the new personal AI assistant take over my life just yet. Because what will I have left to enjoy if I no longer get the challenge of replying to—and arguing with—the customer service department of the website where I ordered my latest luxury bag?
