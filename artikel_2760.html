Will Arch Manning, Dante Moore and other QB prospect questions influence NFL trade deadline?

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

As Tuesday's NFL trade deadline draws closer, front offices at all 32 teams are asking themselves: How much do we value our coming draft picks?

And how does that value compare to our assessment of this year's trade candidates?

The question circulates every year. But with a 2027 NFL Draft class that was strongly heralded in the spring, the calculus feels different.

"Last year, the '27 draft was looming over the '26 draft far more than usual," said a league source with a long history of evaluating the NFL Draft and its quarterback landscape, speaking on condition of anonymity in order to speak freely. "And it has not been a stellar year."

In particular, the first half of the 2026 college football season has not raised the NFL's excitement level about the quarterback class that awaits. Fair or not, that buzz tends to influence the perception of the entire class.

So while talent evaluations between now and April could still shift, it's worth asking: Will a shift in perception of the coming quarterback class influence the NFL trade deadline next week?

Texas QB Arch Manning has cast doubt on his NFL draft potential with his inconsistent play for the Longhorns this season.

In April, teams were wary of trading away 2027 picks due to the buzz around the class, which stemmed largely from buzz around the crop of potential first-round quarterbacks. If the hype has dimmed, are those picks more for sale?

Like most trends in the NFL, the philosophy will differ from team to team. But conversations with league and team voices acknowledge that some draft-eligible players are losing their shine, and some teams could parlay that belief into feeling emboldened at the deadline to acquire players at positions they may not feel the draft is guaranteed to deliver.

Information on draft prospects is incomplete by early November. But one high-ranking AFC executive said they could already get a general sense of the deeper and shallower areas of the draft even if individual evaluations remain unfinished.

"You have all the information, you have general medical history and then you have early background and you can kind of form [opinions]," one high-ranking AFC executive told Yahoo Sports. "You can get close enough to where it's like, 'OK, it's deep. We can solve that in the draft.' Or you're blending with the draft from free agency where it's like [a position] is weaker than other years based on our information [so] it probably makes sense to make this move because between now and the draft, we probably can't solve it the way we want to solve it if we don't do this.

"You're trying to use all that information to piece together the plan."

What information will teams across the league be piecing together? Here's what Yahoo Sports is hearing.

Conversations with executives and evaluators across six different teams offered a mosaic of opinions.

Evaluators from two AFC teams and a high-ranking executive from an NFC team each confirmed they do not view the 2027 NFL Draft as highly now as they did this summer. Each said that reflected their current evaluations of the quarterback class.

"I was part of [the hype], too," one AFC evaluator said. "I got caught up, probably told a bunch of people how this is going to be one of the best draft classes in a long time and it's turning out [not] to be. … There's some good players and there's some really good players at the top, like [Ohio State receiver] Jeremiah Smith. There's a few other players that are really, really talented, but they have a lot of baggage. …

"But this draft isn't turning out to be as deep and as loaded as I anticipated and hoped."

The evaluator from the other AFC team said they believe that 75-80% of draft classes are similarly talented, but perceptions of elite players at premium positions — quarterback, receiver, edge rusher and offensive tackle — disproportionately shift perceptions of each draft. This year, the evaluator saw a dip in receiver, edge and quarterback certainty.

"If that's the case, it's worth buying a player that you can get control on rather than waiting and guessing at the draft," an NFC executive said. "I do think that's part of the Texans' thesis with Marvin Harrison."

Quarterback questions from evaluators rage: Is Mississippi's 6-foot Trinidad Chambliss big enough for the league? Will Miami's Darian Mensah have faced tough enough defenses to predict his pro success accurately? Are college defensive coordinators overwhelmingly cracking the code on college quarterbacks more effectively?

The two biggest question marks at quarterback this season stem from Texas quarterback Arch Manning and Oregon quarterback Dante Moore. Manning has proven inconsistent through eight games, including in pivotal moments. Moore's season ended Sept. 26 after he suffered a concussion. Questions on each player weigh their readiness for the NFL and their interest in declaring pro this spring rather than exploring another college year.

Evaluators are split on whether a transfer would help Manning find a better offensive fit. One camp believes that would be the fresh start he needs, while the other believes his family pedigree and résumé will already net him first-round consideration so having just one year to adjust to a new system alongside new teammates could end up hurting his stock more than helping it. Moore, meanwhile, could help his stock if he shows a cleaner bill of health and gets more than his current limited sample size of 19 college starts, evaluators said.

Several quarterbacks are still expected to be drafted in the first round, including the season's biggest riser to this point in Oklahoma State's Drew Mestemaker. One AFC evaluator said Mestemaker, Notre Dame's CJ Carr and Mensah each would have earned higher evaluations than 2026 first overall pick Fernando Mendoza. Chambliss and Missouri's Austin Simmons intrigue evaluators as well.

Even with mixed reviews on premium positions, including quarterback, a high-ranking AFC executive and a high-ranking NFC executive agreed the draft has more consensus high-end talent than the 2026 class.

"I would say this [year] is maybe a little deeper than last year's in the top 15-20 picks in other positions than quarterback," the high-ranking NFC executive said. "But then after that, it seems like it's a little leaner, and the drafts have been getting a little leaner."

The high-ranking AFC executive credited last year's offensive tackle class and a handful of elite players, like Giants linebacker Arvell Reese. The executive wondered: Are quarterbacks shifting the perception too much?

"The quarterback class, I think people just keep overcorrecting," the executive said. "I think they overcorrected high and now they're overcorrecting all these guys can't play, they'll all stay in school.

"A lot of the sentiment on the draft is driven by what people think about the QBs. But I think even if you take them out, there's some really, really high-end dudes in this draft."

Evaluators and executives were split on how much the coming draft truly influences teams' trade deadline strategies.

Some evaluators and executives emphasized building a roster is a holistic process that constantly requires a multiyear vision integrating the trade deadline, free agency and draft.

Others believed trade-deadline acquisitions, and their often steep costs, prioritize the current season's outlook. A league source considered teams in three categories.

"There's a team that they're not going to get in the playoffs and so they're doing the best they can to build a winning culture and they're basically playing for next year," the source said of the first group. "Then you have a group of teams that they can get in the playoffs, but they can't win in the playoffs. …

"Then you get teams [that], hey, get in the playoffs and anything can happen."

Expect teams like the Buffalo Bills, Kansas City Chiefs and Chicago Bears (Jeffery Simmons already, but perhaps more?) to buy, capitalizing on their already-high ceilings.

Expect teams like the Minnesota Vikings, Pittsburgh Steelers and Seattle Seahawks (like the Bears, they've already started) to assess whether they can narrow the gap on rosters that simultaneously hold glaring weaknesses and playoff-caliber strengths.

And then expect teams like the Indianapolis Colts (in progress), Tennessee Titans (in progress, via Simmons trade) and perhaps even the Las Vegas Raiders to recognize that accumulating assets for next year and beyond is more valuable.

That trifecta should guide teams making bigger swings, the league source said. A high-ranking AFC executive believed there was more nuance involved.

"Honestly, every team in each segment has an argument to make a trade," the executive said. "It's just the level."

Players with potential to meaningfully impact a team beyond 2026 could attract suitors from different competitive stages. Expensive players at depreciating positions (looking at you, Jonathan Taylor) make less sense for teams that aren't contending this season.

Whether teams have a scarcity mindset around draft picks could unfold similarly: Teams like the Titans, New York Jets and Los Angeles Chargers would have little reason to send off their first-round pick as they struggle to win games. But sending a mid-round pick for a player on an expiring deal? Those decisions are more record- and draft class-agnostic, some believe.

"I don't think people care when they trade a third or a fourth," an AFC talent evaluator said. "They look at those as pretty stable resources year over year.

"But I think the teams maybe that are at the top of the draft would maybe consider it because you can kind of anticipate those spots by what's going to be available."

Arguably the most important caveat in any of these decisions: accurately assessing a team's place in its competitive and resource-allocation lifecycle. Trades bust most when teams overestimate their readiness, as the Colts did last year when trading two first-round picks for cornerback Sauce Gardner and the Atlanta Falcons traded back into the first round of the 2025 NFL Draft for edge rusher James Pearce Jr. In contrast, a Bears team that won two of three games even without Caleb Williams this year gave counterparts more confidence in its logic to send two first-rounders for defensive tackle Jeffery Simmons.

"The teams that do the best job on these trades [are] really understanding what phase you're in and stacking it," the AFC talent evaluator said. "Seattle has a loaded roster and one elite guard really helped them. The Jets have tons of draft capital so acquiring picks makes sense. When you cherry-pick and only do one here and there, I don't think you really get the value or help your franchise long term.

"It's really just a constant balance and a constant push and pull of trying to identify where your team is and what you're going to be in a year or two years because that's where it becomes really valuable."
