How Kigali’s Developer Communities Transformed My Journey

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

Posted on Nov 14 • Originally published at gentil.quevvy.com

Before becoming involved in Kigali’s developer communities, most of my learning happened alone. I spent a significant amount of time working on personal projects, reading documentation, watching tutorials, solving errors, and experimenting with different technologies.

Independent learning helped me build important technical foundations. It taught me how to search for solutions, remain patient when facing problems, and continue working without constant supervision. However, learning alone also had limitations.

I could write code and build projects, but I had limited access to people who could review my ideas, challenge my decisions, introduce me to new opportunities, or help me understand how the professional technology ecosystem worked.

Moving into Kigali’s technology environment introduced me to a different way of learning and growing. I began meeting students, developers, founders, community organizers, and experienced professionals who were also interested in software development, open source, artificial intelligence, entrepreneurship, and digital innovation.

Through communities such as ALX Tech Club UNILAK, OpenSource Kigali, Cursor Community Kigali, and Laravengers Community, I discovered that becoming a better developer required more than spending time in front of a computer.

Technical ability remained essential, but communication, teamwork, leadership, networking, presentation, and community participation were also important parts of professional growth.

ALX Tech Club UNILAK helped me connect with other technology enthusiasts within the campus environment. It created opportunities to meet fellow students who were interested in learning, building projects, exchanging knowledge, and developing practical skills.

The community of doers within the club encouraged action. Instead of only discussing ideas, members were motivated to experiment, build, collaborate, and share what they were learning.

Working and interacting with campus colleagues gave me a stronger sense of belonging. It showed me that I was not the only person trying to build a future through technology.

The club also helped make technology feel more accessible to students who were still beginning their journeys. People could learn from one another, ask questions, discuss challenges, and discover opportunities that they might not have found alone.

OpenSource Kigali introduced me to the culture of open-source software and collaborative development. Open source is not only about accessing code without paying for it. It is also about transparency, shared learning, contribution, documentation, community maintenance, and collective improvement.

Through this environment, I became more interested in how developers can learn from existing projects, inspect real codebases, identify problems, report issues, improve documentation, and contribute solutions.

Open-source communities also helped me understand that meaningful participation does not always begin with a large code contribution. A person can start by asking a useful question, improving an explanation, reporting a reproducible bug, testing a feature, or helping another participant.

This perspective influenced the way I approach repositories and reusable projects, particularly while thinking about the future architecture of Quevvy AI.

Cursor Community Kigali exposed me to developers interested in modern development workflows and artificial-intelligence-assisted programming. It created an environment where people could explore how AI tools are changing the process of building software.

The community became especially important during the Cursor Hackathon in Kigali. The event gave me an opportunity to move beyond personal experiments and build a project under real time pressure.

During the hackathon, I worked with NGIRUWONSANGA VEDASTE on CIR — Citizen Issue Report. CIR was designed as a civic platform through which people could report issues affecting their communities, including damaged roads, broken streetlights, and sanitation problems.

The project aimed to make reports easier to submit and track while improving communication between citizens and responsible authorities.

Building CIR during a hackathon taught me that producing a working solution within a limited period requires strong prioritization. A team cannot implement every possible feature. It must identify the central problem, build the most important workflow, divide responsibilities, test the result, and prepare a clear presentation.

Although the final result did not fully meet all our expectations, the experience remained valuable. It gave me practical lessons about teamwork, project scope, time management, prototyping, product presentation, and preparation.

Laravengers Community connected with an important part of my technical identity: Laravel development. Laravel is one of the main technologies I use to build web applications and backend systems.

Being around people who work with similar technologies makes learning more practical. Discussions can move beyond general programming and focus on real development challenges, including application architecture, databases, authentication, deployment, security, performance, and maintainability.

A specialized community also helps developers discover better practices and avoid remaining limited to the methods they learned when they first started.

One of the most important changes these communities brought to my journey was the transition from isolated development to collaborative development.

When working alone, I could make every technical and product decision without explaining it to anyone. In a team or community, I had to communicate my ideas clearly, listen to other perspectives, accept criticism, and adjust my approach.

This was sometimes uncomfortable, but it was necessary. A developer may understand a solution internally but still struggle to communicate it to teammates, users, judges, or potential partners.

Community participation helped me practise expressing ideas in a clearer and more structured way.

Meetups gave me opportunities to interact with people who had more experience in software development, open source, entrepreneurship, and technology leadership.

These connections helped me understand that senior professionals are not valuable only because they know more technical concepts. Their experience also allows them to identify risks, ask better questions, simplify difficult decisions, and recognize problems that less-experienced developers may overlook.

A short conversation with an experienced person can sometimes reveal a mistake that would otherwise require several weeks of experimentation to discover.

These interactions helped me become more open to feedback and more intentional about the people from whom I learn.

Confidence in technology does not come only from completing courses. It also grows when a person presents an idea, participates in a discussion, contributes to a team, demonstrates a project, or helps another developer solve a problem.

Every meetup and community activity gave me another opportunity to practise these skills. I gradually became more comfortable discussing my work, explaining Quevvy, asking questions, and connecting with people I had not met before.

This confidence was not created in a single event. It developed through repeated participation.

Kigali’s technology communities also influenced the development of Quevvy Platform. Conversations with developers, creators, students, and professionals helped me examine the platform from different perspectives.

As a founder working alone on an idea, it is easy to become attached to personal assumptions. Community interactions create opportunities to hear questions and reactions that expose unclear ideas, unnecessary features, or missing functionality.

The communities also strengthened my interest in reusable projects, artificial-intelligence-assisted development, open-source tools, collaboration, and platforms that help people create digital solutions more easily.

Joining a community does not automatically make someone a better developer. Attending events without practising, building, or following up rarely creates meaningful progress.

The value of a community depends partly on what a member contributes and how they use the knowledge, relationships, and opportunities they receive.

I still needed to return to my computer, read documentation, write code, correct mistakes, and continue building my projects. Community participation gave that work more direction, context, and visibility, but it did not replace discipline.

My journey within Kigali’s developer ecosystem is still developing. I continue to learn, meet new people, improve my communication, and understand how to contribute more meaningfully.

These communities have shown me that technology is not built by isolated individuals alone. Behind every successful project are people exchanging ideas, reviewing work, sharing opportunities, documenting knowledge, organizing events, and supporting one another.

Kigali’s developer communities did not simply give me contacts. They helped me become more collaborative, more confident, and more aware of the professional and human dimensions of technology.

They transformed my journey from a mostly individual learning process into a shared experience of building, contributing, and growing with others.
