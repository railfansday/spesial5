This is happening because of the design of self-driving cars. There is no system in the world that is immune to bugs, hardware or software. But software systems are especially vulnerable to bugs, because they're so much more complex than a purely hardware solution. There's just a whole lot more things that can go wrong.

These cars autonomously drive based on 1) past recorded trips, 2) cameras' ability to see the road, 3) lidar/radar's ability to pick up things on & near the road, 4) software that analyses camera and lidar/radar images, 5) models trained on behaviors of how cars drive in traffic and how pedestrians move, 6) heuristic rules of what to do/not do in specific situations, 7) predictive models about the potential future actions of things on & near the road, etc, etc. It's a whole lot of complex shit. Keep in mind that all of this complex interaction is happening without any input from the state. When a road gets closed, that information isn't being fed to Waymo officially. They have to *guess* these changes to what is otherwise a more-or-less predictable roadway.

So really, it's a wonder there haven't been a lot more issues. And there are solutions to prevent these problems which don't involve all this uncertainty. But we haven't invested in them, because they're expensive. We are just hoping this private company will invest billions of their own dollars into their own solution and praying nobody gets hurt. If we really want a long-term, sustainable, public autonomous transportation network, we're going to have to invest in it ourselves. And it'll actually be more reliable.

The systems I describe include automated guided vehicles (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automated_guided_vehicle), which have actually been successfully tested on fleets of cars on highways (https://web.archive.org/web/20111021134832/https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/publicroads/97novdec/p97nov32.cfm) over 30 years ago. These systems are still being built and tested (https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/engineering/smart-highway/) today, yet nobody is willing to pay for it, despite it being an obvious public good that could improve the lives of millions.

Another simple solution for the safety of pedestrians is protected roadways (that is: you either fence them off and provide raised walkways/paths, or partially bury the roadway so vehicles can't leave the road and the raised walkway is easier to build, or you tunnel under the road). It would obviously save lives, prevent many accidents, and provide faster transit for pedestrians (no waiting to cross). But it would be very expensive, so we don't consider it. Instead we just accept all the casualties and damage, and that resulting expense.
