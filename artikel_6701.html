What’s It Like to Be Tennis’s World Number One? Daniil Medvedev Lets Us Into the Club

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

The ATP Finals—the most important men’s tournament on the tennis calendar outside of the Grand Slams—kicked off on Sunday in Torino. Founded in 1970 as a season-ending competition pitting the highest-ranked players against each other, the current iteration features the world’s top eight men—Alexander Zverev, Carlos Alcaraz, Ben Shelton, Daniil Medvedev, Flavio Cobolli, Arthur Fils, Frances Tiafoe, and Rafael Jodar—facing each other first in a round-robin group format and then in elimination stages. (It’s similar in format to the women’s WTA Finals, which concluded Sunday in Indian Wells, with Mirra Andreeva taking home the big trophy.)

Ranking, of course, is everything—it’s what gets you invited to the finals—and the summa achievement of the world’s top ranking is the subject of a new book, ATP No. 1 Club: Icons of Men’s Tennis, just out from Phaidon. It features iconic photos of each number-one player since 1973—the men’s game has seen only 30, including Alcaraz, Zverev, and Medvedev—along with text by the late award-winning tennis writer Mark Hodgkinson.

We sat down with one of those iconic players—Medvedev, one of the five current or former number-ones still competing—to ask him, you know, what it’s like to do something better than anyone else in the world.

Vogue: Do you remember, from your earliest years playing tennis, who was number one at the time?

Daniil Medvedev: I started playing tennis when I was six, in 2002, but I was not yet a tennis fan, let’s say. By the time I started to understand tennis a bit more, Roger [Federer] was there already, and I think for most of my childhood it was Roger at number one, and then Rafa, who from time to time took the top spot. And then came Novak—who, as we know now, took the leading spot for a longer period of time. One time, years later, I made a game of it—I think I named, not all of them, probably 27 of them.

Did you have a tennis hero when you were a kid?

So I have to be honest here: I’m still probably a bit like this, but when I was young, whoever was winning too much—doesn’t matter which sport—I stopped liking them. I’m like, Okay, he’s only winning. So at one point I was supporting anyone against Roger—I don’t say it in a bad way, it’s just how I am. At one moment it was Barcelona with [their former coach] Pep Guardiola and they were beating everyone, and I was like, Okay—give us something new.

But it changed from tournament to tournament: I supported Marat [Safin in 2005] when he won the Australian Open. This was incredible—we, as kids in Russia, were waking up early in the morning to watch his matches. Andy Roddick—I loved his serve; sometimes I was copying it. Novak as well, when he just came in, before he started beating everyone and I was cheering for the underdog. I never had one hero.

When you were dreaming kid dreams, was it about winning Wimbledon or another tournament? Was being the world number one ever part of that?

I never was thinking about number one or a Grand Slam. Of course, when I grew up and started playing better tennis, those kind of goals came into place. But when I was really young, I remember hitting against the wall, and because the wall never misses, I was thinking that I’m playing against Roger, Rafa, Novak. I was trying to beat them, but of course you cannot beat the wall. Those were fun memories—and I think, probably, in the back of my mind, I knew that if I’m playing them, it means that I’m somewhere in the top rankings.

My dream was to become a great tennis player, and I feel like I achieved it. I’m happy about it: Every day you go to practice, you work hard, you fight for the matches, and you don’t think about it. But when I talk to someone like you and put myself back there, I’m like, I actually achieved my dream. It’s just that the dream constantly changes, because you want more.

Did you feel different when you first became the ATP number one player in 2022?

Yes and no. Maybe there’s a tiny bit more pressure and attention paid to you, but it’s also just such a nice feeling. I mean, you are the best in the whole world, and everyone else is just trying. And it was not easy, but I made it. [Medvedev became the first player outside the so-called Big Four—Djokovic, Federer, Nadal, and Murray—to hold the top ranking in 18 years.]

Did that put a kind of target on you that hadn’t been there before?

For sure, especially in the world we live in now, that happens. Whenever you lose: “Oh, he’s not good enough to be world number one.” But I didn’t care, because I’m like, “I’m number one,” and it felt so good. People would ask me after a loss, “Maybe the ranking put more pressure on you? Maybe that’s why you didn’t perform.” I’d just say, “Maybe yes,” but I didn’t care. It was such a good feeling. Anyone who has been there, I think, will tell you the same: It’s a nice feeling. I want to try to be there again, of course—it’s going to be super tough, especially with Carlos and Jannik [Sinner] winning so much.

Did people—either inside or outside of tennis—treat you differently when you were number one? Did things come to you more easily?

For sure, when I was the world number one—I was on a big stretch of winning the 2021 US Open, playing some Masters finals, winning some Masters—I got some, whatever you call it, invitations. I remember going to Los Angeles before Indian Wells after I became world number one, and I went to some restaurants and I would ask for the bill and they’re like, “Yes—someone took care of it.” So stuff like this—when you are world number one, people talk a bit more about you; you’re on TV more. These are things which come with, let’s call it fame, which also can have a downside: Some people do this kind of thing just for pleasure, but maybe someone else pays and thinks that you’re going to sit and talk to him for 30 minutes. And I don’t want to be obliged to do something because someone did something for me—that’s just how I live. Especially if I’m rushing somewhere, you know: I’m okay to pay, man. As I say, there are some upsides, some downsides. If you get invited to a football match and you meet some players, or a baseball match: fun.

Ah, game. Okay. I didn’t know this. In any case: I just enjoy life. I’m number one, I enjoy life. I’m number 10, I enjoy life.
