50 Percent of People In Relationships Would Take a Trip Together to Solve Their Problems

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

A “baecation” often entails a romantic getaway, passionate activities, and a lot of vacation sex. But what if traveling with your significant other meant a lot of difficult conversations, a heated back-and-forth, and working to fix your relationship. I know: this sounds nothing like the dreamy R&R you pictured—but for some couples, it’s exactly what they planned for.

According to Booking.com’s 2027 Travel Predictions, 58 percent of U.S. travelers said they would consider taking a trip to resolve conflicts or arguments.

Now, traveling itself already can cause real strain and arguments on relationships on its own. In the same data set, more than one-third of respondents said they found out they were incompatible with their companion after a trip. Nearly three in 10 people have considered ending a relationship or friendship over a trip, while more than one in five have actually cut a vacation short because of a bad travel companion.

But if you’re already having relationship issues with your partner, is a trip a good idea? People still seem to think so. About 81 percent said that taking part in at least one vacation activity with another person helps them have honest or difficult conversations.

So should you be planning a trip the next time you and your partner start arguing?

A change of scenery might be exactly what you and your partner need to see eye-to-eye again.

“Getting away from work, chores, kids, in-laws, or house and childcare stuff can lower defenses,” couples therapist Justena Kavanagh tells Cosmopolitan.

She explains that when you’re home, you’re essentially having every argument on the same “battleground.”

“Somewhere new often brings back the honeymoon period, when you could really see each other without life’s stresses in the way,” she says. And if both you and your partner have busy lives and packed schedules, a vacation may really be the only time you can focus on the issues in your relationship without distraction.

Plus, vacations encourage you to relax—which can go a long when in an argument. “When we’re rested, we’re more regulated, and when we’re regulated, we can listen instead of defending,” Kavanagh says.

She explains that it’s important to establish why exactly you’re going on the vacation in the first place. Are you getting away to talk about an issue? Or simple to get away from it and reconnect? Agreeing on the purpose for the trip before you start packing your bikini will help you and your partner get on the same page—so you don’t end up just arguing more later.

“Trips suit conversations about connection, like feeling distant, intimacy, or where the relationship is going,” she says. Other things like finances or in-law conflicts may need a follow-up at home.

If you decide that you’re going to talk your issues out, remember that you are still on vacation. That doesn’t mean everything has to be wonderful, of course. But Kavanagh encourages couples to treat the trip like a summit. This means talking early in the day (after a good night’s rest) and working on things while taking breaks.

The trip should essentially be your opportunity to discuss what’s currently happening in the relationship, where you want the relationship to go, and how to shift priorities to get there.

Sure, a beautiful view and hotel sex will definitely lighten the mood and probably even make you more agreeable—but it isn’t a magical bandaid for your relationship issues.

“If couples don’t have relational skills at home, they won’t miraculously have them on vacation,” Kavanagh warns.

So if you’re going to go on a vacation to work on things with your partner, you’re going to want to put a plan into place. The best method? Talking about the pattern behind the argument, rather than hyperfocusing on the latest incident.

And even if you do come to a resolution on vacation, make sure you’re setting a plan in place for the positive energy to flow into your everyday lives.

“Be realistic about home, where you’re more stressed and old patterns return fast,” Kavanagh says. “Plan follow-up support, a regular check-in, and what you’ll do when things go off track, because they will.”

Okay, so not all of us can afford to book a flight every time we get into an argument with our partner—but you don’t have to. Out of all the activities that people do on vacation, respondents said that it was mostly hikes or walking that was the most effective at getting them to open up and have the difficult conversations.

Lucky for you, taking a hike is totally free—and can do wonders for whatever issue you may be facing with your partner.

Kavanagh explains that walking and hiking take pressure off of an argument.

“Face to face can feel like a confrontation; side by side, you’re facing the same direction and don’t have to hold eye contact in the hardest moments,” she explains. This is something we see in psychotherapy: think about someone lying on a couch, looking at the ceiling. This helps physiologically and emotionally. Plus the actual physical activity helps, too.

“Walking also discharges stress and is a natural form of bilateral stimulation, the left-right rhythm used in therapies like EMDR,” she says.

When your partner is irritating you, it’s likely that the last thing you want to do is travel with them. But if you’re determined to move past whatever may be causing a rift, that might be exactly what you need.

Honeymoons and other happy occasions shouldn’t be the only time we travel with our partners with the purpose of growing closer, reconnecting, and strengthening the relationship. Sometimes, even in hardship, a little getaway could be the jumpstart your relationship needs to get back on track. Just remember that after the vacation ends, you do have to go back to reality.

“The good feelings are real, but the test is whether they last back home, where life is harder,” Kavanagh says.
