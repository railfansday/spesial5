“Do You Like It? Would You Wear It? Would You Buy It?”—An Oral History of Marc by Marc Jacobs

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

In the 2000s, Marc Jacobs was the high-low king of fashion. A decade after he propelled grunge onto the runway with his landmark collection for Perry Ellis, he was the first creative director at Louis Vuitton, and LVMH owned a majority share of his business. Meanwhile, he was selling $2 condoms in matchbooks alongside wares in every category: rarefied lace-trimmed cocktail dresses for the well-heeled, shrunken denim military jackets for downtown girls, and of course, those ubiquitously irreverent tchotchkes on Bleecker Street for everyone in between. Ineffable cool across all price points was the name of the game, especially with the launch of Marc by Marc Jacobs in 2001, which loomed large over the booming contemporary market of the era. It was so Kirsten Dunst. It was so Alexa Chung. And at the same time it was so me circa my first trip to NYC with a five-dollar lipstick pen and not much else in a branded shopping bag.

Behind Marc by Marc was a small team encouraged to follow its instincts, with creative direction by legendary stylist and photographer Venetia Scott, who had been consulting on the main line since 1997. Together, they trawled vintage shops and film costume warehouses for inspiration. But, perhaps most of all, there was Robert Duffy, the shrewd fashion executive who was Jacobs’ co-founder and longtime consigliere, tasked with turning the designer’s whimsical runway impulses into dollars and cents. They were the Saint Laurent and Pierre Bergé of a new generation, and together they built a company that was both creative and commercially viable. Introducing Marc by Marc Jacobs was a perfect example of their dualities coming together to produce harmonious results.

And, oh, the stores. Jacobs’s shops helped transform a once sleepy stretch of the West Village into a destination, with six outposts within walking distance of each other. Among them was the Marc by Marc Jacobs accessories store, the pièce de résistance for tourists and broke fashion kids alike. Whether it was your first trip to New York or your tenth, you lined up on Bleecker. Tote bags came stamped with the most iconic run-on sentence in fashion history: Jacobs by Marc Jacobs for Marc by Marc Jacobs in Collaboration with Marc Jacobs for Marc by Marc Jacobs. With a simple tongue-twister, Jacobs let us all in on the joke.

Marc by Marc Jacobs was folded into Marc Jacobs in 2015, but it lives on as a moment of accessible cool that can’t be replicated. For those who worked on it, its magic was in the freedom they were given to create. Twenty-five years after it was first launched, we spoke with members of its creative team about the vintage hunts, the last-minute fittings, and how they were able to recreate the downtown cool around them for the runway.

In a serendipitous turn of events, Vogue learned that there are plans underway by to revive the Marc by Marc Jacobs label, sometime next year. While details are currently under wraps, this is the place for updates on the beloved brand that had an outsize imprint on fashion in the early aughts.

Venetia Scott began working with Marc Jacobs on the main collection in 1997. By the time Marc by Marc was taking shape, she had become a central creative collaborator and operated as the creative director on a day-to-day basis with the team.

Venetia Scott, Creative Director (2001-2015)Joe McKenna had been [styling] the Marc Jacobs runway. He suggested that I would be good to take it over, and Nian [Fish] from KCD also thought so. I had just had my daughter about three weeks before, and had gone down to Cornwall. And then my agent rang and said, “Will you go and meet Marc Jacobs and Robert Duffy?” They ended up coming to London, and then that was it. I started that September. When I was assembling my team, I brought Alister Mackie to style men’s. Frederic Tremblay and Aude Tabet were already on the team. Fred was focused on the army aesthetic and denim. Susanne Deeken came in to do summer dresses. Irene Chung did the shoes.

We needed accessories for the show. I knew Katie Hillier from London, and she had been doing necklaces and stuff for Luella’s shows. So I got her to come out to make stuff, strings and beads. Eventually we’d put a small run in the Marc by Marc shop, and then later, it grew into bags.

Alister Mackie, Menswear Consultant (2003-2014)I started on the Marc Jacobs main line around 2001, when they showed men and women together. In spring 2003, we started putting men into the Marc by Marc show. Venetia would set the narrative. At the first meeting, she would have spoken to Marc, and this story of who these kids were would be laid out by Venetia. We would then have to go off and find a boy that was relevant to that. This was really driven by character, which made it unusual, and it made it a lot of fun.

Venetia ScottThere was not really a strategy behind a lot of it. It sort of evolved as it went along. [Co-founder] Robert Duffy was very much like, “Let Marc do what he needs to do, and he will do it.” We never hit our deadlines. I remember Marc saying, “It’s great, we do what we want to do, and it seems like people like it.” I think we were lucky to be able to work in that vacuum without a lot of people dictating what the needs were of the line.

Frederic Tremblay, Senior Design Director (1999-2008)LVMH was seeing the contemporary market brewing. Our price point was a fraction of the main collection, but we took the essence of what Marc was. There was this big opportunity for department stores, who were also telling us, “We’ll give you tons of square footage because this is what we’re seeing as the next thing, but there’s nobody there.” Co-op was happening at Barneys, so we were one of the first big brands.

Backstage at the spring 2006 Marc by Marc Jacobs show.

Venetia ScottMarc was the author of the collection, and so we would have conversations, decide where we were going with it, and then I would work doing the research on fabrics or prints. And then I would go to Paris or to New York, and take him the research that I had done. Marc by Marc was a little group of us, and it was much more like a collaboration.

Camille Bidault Waddington, stylist (2000-2014)Venetia was styling the Marc Jacobs show and was working on the Marc by Marc collection through the season. I was coming for the last two weeks to style the show with her and the team. I was doing looks, but she had the final word. Sometimes it was a fight, but a gentle one. Marc was obviously coming to see us, also to have a breather in the middle of the sophisticated [main collection] prep. We were the kids.

Frederic TremblayMarc would review the collection ahead of the show and suggest pieces we were missing. It was always a great little thing that maybe when we were in the moment, we weren’t necessarily thinking about it, but he would come out and be like, "You know, you need this now." And we were looking at it, we were like, “Yep!”

Senior design director Frederic Tremblay and Venetia Scott trying on clothes during a research trip in London.

Venetia ScottWe’d go to L.A. for the Rose Bowl Flea and the costume houses, or we’d go to Paris, Antwerp… We’d choose a different place every season. Our thing was always, as a group, “Do you like it? Would you wear it? Would you buy it?”

Camille Bidault-WaddingtonAt the beginning, we were customizing stuff to accessorize the looks, and invented crazy bits and bobs with Katie Hillier, or used things from markets and punk shops. It was very, very free.

Frederic TremblayWe amassed these amazing vintage pieces, and then we had a model coming in to try on pretty much everything. We started doing a bit of a look with the vintage, and that was our starting point. Those amazing costume rental places are truly magical. You’ve got to see Home Depot-size warehouses where you have to go on those industrial ladders, and there’s three, four rows of clothes, rows and rows. I think we just let our gut tell us where to go in the aisles.

Aude Tabet, Design Director and Director of Product Design, Little Marc Jacobs and Marc by Marc Jacobs Swimwear (1999-2015)We reworked prints, color, and artwork based on the vintage pieces we’d found, either internally or using a print studio. I still go by the Marc by Marc color palette we developed. It’s ingrained.

Backstage at the fall 2005 Marc by Marc Jacobs fashion show.

Venetia ScottIn the beginning, it wasn’t an idea to do a cheaper line. It was, let’s make some things that we all want. Marc’s always liked that high and low mishmash of things. Marc and Sofia Coppola were good friends, and other people around him would do this good mix of wearing the bouclé jacket with a silk shirt, with a pair of jeans, with a pair of flats. So that was natural.

What was great was building this gang of cool girls. One might be in a jumpsuit, one might be in jeans and a T-shirt, one girl’s in a summer dress, but they all look like the same gang.

Camille Bidault-WaddingtonThe point was to represent what cool girls would wear. Models at the time had supercool style, and we were inspired by them, from Kirsten Owen to Angela Lindvall and Tasha Tilberg. They were super-stylish, and we wanted to capture the New York streets too—the musicians and the It girls.

Alister MackieSometimes it’s quite hard to fit the men into a women’s story, but it was a fun challenge to make it believable that these girls and boys were together. Fall 2007 (above) was these sorts of winter college kids who were also really grungy. It was very much that character that we kept going back to.

Camille Bidault-WaddingtonSince I’m French, sometimes I thought we were a bit much, but I was then going downstairs with Alister Mackie, who was styling the boys, and actually noticed people in New York were pretty daring. So that was the kick to go back up and add a hat here or a triple sock there.

Alister Mackie[Marc Jacobs HQ on] Spring Street was like the kingdom, and everyone was always there. There was a whole floor of ladies sewing, like a factory, so loud and intense. The whole thing was vibrating. Military coats were getting made up to the last minute, all this food arriving, and all these shoes in the corridors.

Irene Chung, who did the shoes, would fly from Italy with batches. Then she’d fly back and get more because Marc wanted to see all the options, and everything had to be just so perfect. It was this very theatrical experience to be part of.

Aude TabetOur first show was for spring 2001, but our first fall show (above) was my favorite: Dark denim, the coolest girls, slightly English styling, and very Bohemian. It felt almost like the American version of Katharine Hamnett [the English designer known for her mix of tailoring and a vintage-inspired look that was revolutionary at the time].

Camille Bidault-WaddingtonThe music was very experimental, indie, and daring. Frédéric Sanchez was coming up with upcoming bands or weird choirs or film soundtracks. And we were blasting them. Some journalists got annoyed, but it was very fun.

Marc by Marc Jacobs’ legacy lives on in some of its most ubiquitous products, like the Moonboots and the Souris mouse flats, first launched in 2002 and soon a cult favorite that would live on for decades and have recently found a new life with a new wave of whimsy enthusiasts.

Hillary Duff seen wearing the Marc by Marc Jacobs signature mouse slipper in 2008.

Venetia ScottThe original mouse shoe is a pointy one, which was done for the Marc Jacobs collection around 1998 with a [bow] that potentially looked like whiskers. And so Marc called it the mouse shoe. The more literal mousey thing with whiskers is what it later became.

Tote bags emblazoned with “Jacobs by Marc Jacobs for Marc by Marc Jacobs in Collaboration with Marc Jacobs for Marc by Marc Jacobs” became another signifier of Jacobs’s tongue-in-cheek approach to the brand.

Frederic TremblayThat was Marc realizing the repetition of his name and the number of lines was getting a little silly. It was just something, kind of, funny, you know? Marc has that ironic thing. He just came up with that, him and Robert.

Amid the designer denim boom of the 2000s bolstered by the likes of Diesel, True Religion, and 7 For All Mankind, Marc by Marc denim launched alongside the first collection for spring 2001. The following February, Vogue reported that its creased jeans were flying off retailers’ shelves.

Aude TabetWe did a creased pair of jeans for our denim launch. The jeans were creased as if they had been folded always on the side, like this forever. That was what we called our “San Francisco SF” wash. It was a mid-pale blue with a gray cast, developed in L.A. And we did all those drummer boy jackets with it.

The main collection and Marc by Marc shared a photographic and visual language, shaped by photographer Juergen Teller and Scott, and later Mackie.

Venetia ScottRight at the beginning, when I worked on Marc Jacobs, Juergen and I were together. Juergen would come over and stay in New York. The conversation came up that there wasn’t any budget, but “maybe when you go back to London, you could photograph Kim Gordon?” And “Venetia, you could take a dress, and you could go to the Shepherd’s Bush Empire, where she’s performing, and she’ll put the dress on,” and “Juergen, you take some pictures of her up on stage or backstage in the dress.” It was just a conversation that didn’t ever really go through lots of tiers of people to be able to get any permission. We just did it.

Early on, there wasn’t any money for those ads. It was just something that we wanted to do. And then it carried on like that, really, as a conversation of who it could be, who’d be a good person this time. Sometimes Juergen would come up with an idea [or see a new actress], like, “Who is she? Oh, I think the girl who’s just been in that film.” And it would be Samantha Morton. Or Dakota Fanning, who I’d just seen in a film when she was tiny, and thought she was the most amazing thing. “Maybe we could shoot her?” We’d just say, “Yes, let’s do that.”

Often Juergen would go off and do the Marc Jacobs campaigns on his own. And then Marc by Marc, we usually used who we thought had been the best girls in the show, or who seemed the most right for it. It’d be maybe three or four of those girls, and they’d often do the shoots. It was more models that had been used on the runway.

Alister MackieSometimes I went with Juergen on trips, and they were phenomenal. We were going to Milan, to Venice, to upstate New York, and we went on these very, very lo-fi trips with him and the models.

In 2010, the New York Times called Jacobs’ West Village retail footprint a monopoly. In other cities, from L.A. to Lisbon, the stores felt like anchors of cool, devoted to a high-low mix that epitomized fashion at the time. Under Duffy’s leadership, Jacobs’s world opened up to the masses via the accessible “junk” operation: an ever-changing assortment of novelties for people who couldn’t afford the clothes.

Aude TabetI moved to the West Village in ’92. The Mercer Street store was the nucleus—this crazy, beautiful, minimal location in that former garage. You would just want to be there, sit in a corner, and people-watch. Before Marc was there, Bleecker was very sleepy. There were a couple of antique stores. Then Marc went there and ended up opening a few stores, and that changed all of Bleecker. Every place where Marc went and opened up retail, everyone followed.

Frederic TremblayA lot of the ideas for the trinkets came from Fiorucci [the popular 1970s brand and store known for carrying an eclectic mix of high-end clothes alongside novelty trinkets and small gifts and where Jacobs worked as a teenager]. Marc would often talk to us about that reference. He would go to the Fiorucci store by Bloomingdale’s, and they had a lot of that stuff too—products that were satellite to the main brand of clothing or accessories, those little things that anyone could afford.

Alister MackieWhat we made was really for the show, but Robert and the merchandising team made all these products, which were really fun and successful, and then they put them together in the stores. Somehow, one elevated the other, and one made the other more fun. The people who worked in the shop represented Marc as much as we did, or the people in the office and the models. Everybody had the same kind of positivity and welcoming spirit.

Camille Bidault-WaddingtonIt was very, very cool to sit on the bench at Balthazar and see so many people walking by, wearing Marc by Marc. It was very cool because it was a collection on its own, not a variation of the other line. It was deep, not just a commercial version.

Venetia ScottYou’ve got your little haul of a tote, and this and that and the other, all for under 50 bucks. It was a fun idea, a bit like Biba in the ’70s, where you could get a T-shirt for very little money or buy a poster to stick up in your room at school. But I think the cheap products that were Marc by Marc, in the long run, probably didn’t do us any good. What we were doing was something very different. We were doing boots that you could sit on the pavement, kick about, and have fun in. They’d get better and better. It wasn’t meant to be a cheap version of something expensive. It was meant to be what it was.

Alister MackieMarc threw these huge parties, and it was very early on for that kind of branding, having these great parties and everybody wanting to be part of it. Chloë Sevigny and all these fabulous women were around—all that was part of the magic of the brand.

Marc’s parties were the talk of the town, and he took it to the extreme. In Venice, he was a pigeon. And then the Arabian Nights, he was camel toe. He took it to this other kind of level of surrealism.

Those parties were also part of the myth of the brand, because everybody was going. It wasn’t exclusive. Everyone in town wanted to be there. That was part of the expansionist generosity of Robert and Marc at that time.

By 2013, the contemporary market that Marc by Marc Jacobs had helped define was crowded with competitors, including Alexander Wang, 3.1 Phillip Lim, and Rag & Bone. That year, as Katie Hillier and Luella Bartley were appointed to lead its next chapter, Duffy acknowledged that the line had lost some momentum. “The competition has become too great,” he told WWD. “We rested on our laurels, and it hurt us.”

Venetia ScottWhen it got really big, then we were asked to do things like, “Oh, somebody’s got, you know, this brand’s got some [legging trousers called] treggings.” And we’d all think, “Well, we don’t want to do treggings.” And I think that was, for me, that was sort of maybe where it lost its way a little bit, and the identity was not as clear as it had been in the beginning. Before, when we were just doing what we wanted to do, that seemed to sell well.

Frederic TremblayAround 2006, 2007, Topshop became a thing. Zara and H&M started to get more momentum. They took a lot of our market. We started feeling like, okay, we really need to offer something different. We weren’t the only ones in contemporary to feel that. We couldn’t really compete.

Venetia ScottOther brands could do that army jacket in a second, and have it out in the shop at the same time that we would. So I think all that contributed to it probably not having the sales that it had once had. I think it was time for a refresh. Katie and Luella came in, and I continued with them to consult on the process. They set up a London studio, and we all worked together on the show and the styling.

In May 2013, Katie Hillier was named creative director of Marc by Marc Jacobs, with Luella Bartley joining as women’s ready-to-wear design director. Their debut for fall 2014 brought an edgier, London-inspired vision to the brand, but it didn’t last long. In March 2015, it was announced that Marc by Marc Jacobs would be absorbed into Marc Jacobs, bringing contemporary and luxury products together under one name. “We believed that fashion could exist at lots of different prices,” Jacobs told WWD at the time.

Beginning with spring 2016, the consolidated label incorporated lower-priced pieces alongside its runway collection, a strategy Jacobs later acknowledged “didn’t really work.” Given the extensive retail footprint of the Marc by Marc Jacobs line, rather than the main line, much of its exposure was lost to customers, leaving space for other brands to replace it.

Venetia ScottThe [pieces] have a character that you don’t find if you go to a high street store. They still are around, what is it, 20 years later? They still look great.

They had all these extra details that you discovered as you wore them. You’d have a piping on the inside of the cuff, which was some little candy cane stripe. They were quite honest clothes, I think.

Aude TabetI wear those pieces from time to time, and I love the emotional connection. I love how disruptive it was. In a good way, positive, disruptive.

Frederic TremblayI’ve been doing mentoring at SCAD, and there are students who will have picked something in a vintage store that’s Marc by Marc. They kind of know what it is, but they don’t really have the whole story about it. They don’t know about the real impact it had when it was made. So it’s always fun when one of the students wears one of those pieces, and I can tell them a little bit about it.
