Lightweight packaging has a surprising recycling problem

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

Lightweight packaging may be more likely to end up in the trash because its flimsy feel affects how people judge its remaining value.

People often say they recycle, but their actual choices can depend on how an empty package feels.

A new study suggests that lightweight, crinkly bottles are more likely to land in the trash than sturdier ones.

Lyndsay Loomer and Sarah Whitley from the University of Georgia investigated this pattern across five studies.

The researchers found that packaging density is a powerful and largely unexamined driver of recycling behavior.

Lightweight packaging, the kind increasingly common as companies trim material costs under sustainability mandates, is significantly more likely to end up in a landfill than its heavier counterpart.

In some cases, it was 92 percent more likely to be trashed.

Lead author Lyndsay Loomer came to this question from the inside. Before earning her doctorate at Georgia’s Terry College of Business, she worked in brand marketing for a beverage company in Texas.

She watched her firm introduce lightweight, thin-walled bottles to reduce plastic use in the late 2010s.

Consumers bought them without complaint. But she kept thinking about what happened afterward.

“As I became more familiar with sustainability literature, I thought if the package feels low quality and like trash – what if they’re more likely to throw it away?” Loomer said.

“Then we’re kind of shooting ourselves in the foot, right?”

“It’s related to this idea of waste aversion. We don’t want to get rid of things that we feel have more useful life or value. And we infer a lot of that through the material properties – the weight or feel – of whatever item it is.”

The team tested this intuition across five studies combining field observation and controlled experiments.

The pilot study capitalized on a real-world packaging change: Coca-Cola’s Dasani brand transitioning to a lighter bottle in spring 2022, reducing plastic density by over 40 percent.

Researchers distributed both bottle types at a campus speaker event and audited the disposal bins afterward. The low-density bottles landed in the trash at a measurably higher rate.

Zero high-density bottles were trashed; 4.76 percent of the lightweight ones were.

The pilot was small, so the team went further. They audited every trash and recycling bin on the first floor of Georgia’s business school complex over a full week.

They cataloged 172 recyclable plastic containers and weighed each one.

Containers found in recycling bins had an average density 60 percent higher than those found in the trash.

Each additional gram per fluid ounce of density increased a container’s odds of being recycled by nearly tenfold.

“When you look at disposal behavior, it’s not enough to just have people indicate how they will dispose of an item,” Whitley said.

“You have to see if they’re actually going to do it. Everyone says they’re going to recycle because there’s a lot of social desirability bias there. We had to have evidence of people doing it.”

That evidence held across materials. Subsequent experiments replicated the density effect with paper.

In each case, the pattern was the same: lighter means more disposable, in both senses of the word.

The mechanism the researchers identified runs through two distinct judgments people make almost instantaneously at the moment of disposal.

The first is a value assessment: flimsy packaging reads as lower quality and therefore lower value.

The second is emotional – what researchers call waste aversion, the discomfort of throwing something away that still seems useful.

Heavier packaging triggers more of that discomfort than lightweight packaging.

Participants in the controlled experiments weren’t told to consider weight, suggesting that packaging density can influence disposal without explicit prompts.

The more practically useful finding may be what happened when the researchers tried to interrupt this process.

In Study 4, they added a simple printed message to a low-density paper envelope: “You may be done with this envelope, but it still has value.”

That single line increased the likelihood of recycling the envelope by 52 percent.

“Material lightweighting seems like a good idea on the surface because people don’t really care about it so much from a buying perspective, it lowers costs, and it’s sustainable,” Whitley said.

“But manufacturers need to think one step further.”

The implication for companies is pointed. Lightweighting reduces material use and transportation emissions on the production side.

Lower recycling rates could offset some of those benefits. The findings highlight why companies need to consider disposal behavior alongside material use and transportation.

The researchers describe this as a form of unintentional greenwashing: sustainable at the factory, unsustainable at the bin.

“If we’re not examining the downstream consumer implications of manufacturing changes, we’re not going to move forward in terms of some of these sustainability goals,” Loomer said.

The findings suggest that recycling behavior depends on more than people’s intentions. The physical properties of packaging can also influence which bin they choose.

Recycling rates in the U.S. have stagnated for years despite widespread awareness campaigns and strong public support for recycling.

The solution may not require heavier packaging.

A brief message about an item’s remaining value could help companies reduce material use while encouraging consumers to recycle.

The study is published in the Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science.
