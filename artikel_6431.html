Sunflowers reveal a surprising secret about how plants sense light

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

New research reveals why sunflowers sometimes grow toward weaker light instead of brighter sources, challenging assumptions about plant behavior.

Plants grow toward light, so it seems natural that they would always bend toward whatever shines brightest.

Young sunflowers don’t always follow that rule. When two equally strong lamps shone on them from opposite sides, the seedlings grew toward a third, weaker lamp instead.

The researchers concluded that plants don’t simply grow wherever the most light is.

Different sides of a sunflower stem respond to the light they receive. Those signals combine to guide growth, allowing two equally strong lights from opposite directions to effectively cancel each other out.

Yasmine Meroz, a professor at Tel Aviv University, carried out the study with Ahron Kempinski, Amir Porat, and Mathieu Rivière. All four are part of the university’s Center for Physics and Chemistry of Living Systems.

“What is fascinating is that plants can integrate complex information about their environment without a brain or central processing system,” Meroz said.

“The computation emerges from the way light is sensed and processed locally across the stem.”

The team worked only with sunflower seedlings about a week old, grown and tested indoors under blue lamps. How full-grown plants handle light coming from many directions outdoors hasn’t been tested.

In many animals, sense organs such as skin collect signals and a brain sorts them out. A plant has no brain to do that job.

Instead, the light-sensing proteins that steer its bending are found mainly in the outermost layer of cells on the stem. Each patch of the stem’s surface responds to the light that lands on it.

Some light also passes straight through. Using a 3D-printed sleeve to hold seedling stems in front of a light meter, the team ran 31 measurements.

On average, about 10% of the light hitting a stem got through, giving the sensors on the far side a weak signal pointing the opposite way. A stem bends by growing faster on one side than the other.

The team’s model adds up the light signals from every side to work out which way the stem will grow.

Young sunflowers famously track the sun as it moves across the sky.

The sunflowers in this study were much younger, grown at 77°F (25°C) and picked when they stood 0.4 to 1.2 inches (1 to 3 cm) tall.

The team painted two fluorescent dots near each seedling’s tip under dim green light, so the plants wouldn’t start reacting to light too early.

Strips of blue LEDs then lit five seedlings at a time while the plants were photographed from the side for at least 21 hours.

The angle between the two dots showed how far each tip had bent. After about 16 hours, the seedlings settled at a steady angle.

With one lamp, brighter light made the seedlings bend further. But across a tenfold range of brightness, the bend didn’t keep pace.

The team’s measurements fit a pattern in which a light twice as bright produces a signal well short of twice as strong.

Senses in many species work this way, responding to how much a signal changes compared with where it started.

The researchers compare this response to human hearing, where a 10-decibel increase is typically perceived as roughly twice as loud.

Compression of this kind lets a plant respond across a wide range of light, from shade under other leaves to direct sunlight, the researchers wrote.

Next, the team placed seedlings between two lamps facing each other at different strengths.

The seedlings bent toward the brighter lamp, and a bigger gap in brightness meant a bigger bend, though again not in a straight-line way.

The team’s model, using the compression measured with one lamp, predicted where those seedlings would settle. It matched the measurements well, with one small miss the team put down to slightly uneven lighting.

By the model’s logic, two lamps of equal strength on opposite sides should cancel each other out.

The two-lamp runs already suggested as much, because nearly matched lamps barely moved the seedlings off vertical.

So the team added a third lamp at a right angle to the first two. The two strong lamps were set almost exactly equal, and the third was less than half as bright as either one.

The team filmed 34 seedlings from above for 21 hours, and a computer program trained to recognize leaves tracked where each plant grew.

On average, the seedlings grew almost straight toward the weak lamp, less than 1 degree off, though individual plants varied widely around that average.

The researchers wrote that this result isn’t intuitive for a plant that needs light to make food. In their view, the finding shows that plants don’t grow to collect as much light as possible.

The same math predicts something stranger. With two lamps at a right angle, one brighter than the other, the most light comes from a direction between them, closer to the brighter lamp.

But in the team’s model, the plant compresses each lamp’s signal before adding them up, which changes how strong the two signals are compared with each other.

So the direction the plant favors ends up a little off from the direction of the most light.

The researchers call this a kind of optical illusion, though it doesn’t mean the plant sees anything.

In their calculations, the gap is at most about 6 degrees, and it disappears when the two lamps are equally bright or when only one is on.

“Our work shows how the physical organization of a sensory system can determine how an organism perceives and responds to its environment,” Meroz said.

The team couldn’t measure the illusion in its living seedlings.

Growing sunflower stems trace circling motions, and in the final three hours of the three-lamp test, a seedling’s growth direction typically varied by about 17 degrees.

A 6-degree shift is too small to see against that much motion. The team’s analysis found that the circling wouldn’t average the distortion away.

Meroz’s lab in the university’s School of Plant Sciences and Food Security has studied the same circling in crowded sunflowers before. Here, that motion limited what the experiment could detect.

The model also missed one detail of the movement. Real seedlings bent past their final angle and then eased back, and the simulated ones didn’t.

The team suggested that uneven growth along the stem, or the stem sagging under its own weight, could explain why.

Bacteria and some other single cells also sense their surroundings across their whole surface, with no brain in charge.

“This principle may extend far beyond plants, to cells, bacteria, and other living systems that rely on distributed sensing,” Meroz said.

For now, the optical illusion remains a mathematical prediction. Confirming it will require measurements precise enough to distinguish the subtle effect from the seedlings’ natural circling movements.

The full study was published in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.
