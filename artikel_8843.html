‘I always run on feeling’: jennifer latour builds imaginary creatures from flowers

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

the artist draws on her background in special effects makeup to create imaginary botanical creatures in her photographic series.

Vancouver-based artist and photographer Jennifer Latour assembles flowers, stems, and other botanical fragments into unfamiliar sculptural forms in her ongoing series Bound Species and Shapeshifters. Drawing on her background in special effects makeup for film and television, she approaches each composition as a character, giving individual plants new shapes, proportions, and personalities.

‘The overall composition has to speak to me, and I often see the pieces alive in my head. I visualize how it would speak and walk if it could. To me, each piece has their own distinct personality,’ she tells designboom.

Her interest in creating fictional forms goes back to childhood, when she was fascinated by moving shapes and objects. ‘I had hanging mobiles as a child and I remember the feeling of being captivated by moving shapes in daylight and seeing things morphing,’ the artist recalls.

Each composition is physically constructed and photographed, with its eventual appearance dependent on the behavior of the materials. Sometimes the flowers remain stable long enough for Latour to revisit and adjust a piece. More often, their gradual wilting determines when the work is complete. ‘It’s instinctual – I always run on feeling,’ she says, explaining that the sculpture sometimes ‘almost decides on its own.’

Born in Seven Islands, Quebec, and now based in Vancouver, Jennifer Latour began experimenting with photography in 2006. Her practice took a new direction during the first COVID-19 lockdown in 2020, when she turned to still life and started experimenting with flowers. Bound Species grew from the idea of treating botanical elements as extensions of the body. By joining different plants, petals, stems, and buds, she began constructing unfamiliar organisms that suggest limbs, bodies, and other anatomical features.

The creative possibilities of this process are what continue to draw her in. ‘There’s something endless about it all and very exciting! I love the imagination I tap into when I start a new piece,’ Jennifer Latour tells us. For Latour, the compositions need to follow an internal logic of color and form, even when their final appearance belongs to an imagined world. She describes this as having ‘the vision that it could possibly exist somewhere on another planet.’

Although the artist many times begins with a rough idea or drawing, the materials available at the time play a significant role in determining the final composition. Seasonal flowers introduce an element of unpredictability, requiring her to respond to their individual shapes and textures. ‘When I get to a florist or garden center, I’m at the mercy of the season and the flora that stands out to me that’s available,’ she explains. From there, she begins joining and balancing the different elements, moving them around until the composition feels resolved. The process can take several hours, but the flowers’ short lifespan places a limit on how long she can work. When creating outdoors, wind can introduce another complication. ‘I’ve had countless pieces ready for the photo and just as I grab my camera everything falls apart!’ the artist shares. ‘Patience and determination play a huge part in my practice!’

Jennifer Latour’s background in special effects makeup has shaped the way she approaches her botanical work. Having spent years creating prosthetics and fictional creatures for film and television, she is accustomed to building characters through the combination of individual sculptural elements. The same thinking informs her approach to the plants, which she assembles into figures with their own imagined identities. ‘Creating characters through my sculptures is directly connected to how I approach a character in film,’ she highlights. She often visualizes the finished pieces as if they were alive, imagining how they might move or communicate. This character-driven perspective is particularly evident in Shapeshifters, where the botanical compositions take on more pronounced creature-like forms.

Her visual references span sculpture, photography, cinema, and animation. She cites Alexander Calder’s mobiles and Isamu Noguchi’s wood sculptures, alongside the photography of Tim Walker and the films of Wes Anderson and Tim Burton. Childhood memories also remain part of her visual vocabulary, including a pop-up edition of ‘The Wind in the Willows’ that she received as a child and became obsessed with. ‘Many more but the impact film, music and art have on me is an endless list,’ Jennifer Latour adds.

While each sculpture has its own character, the two series also consider the relationships between different forms of life. Latour sees the botanical figures as part of a wider reflection on how organisms are connected through both their similarities and differences. She hopes viewers will use the unfamiliar forms as a starting point for their own imagined worlds. ‘I hope they tap into their own imagination and can envision a world where the sculptures could exist,’ she says. ‘Everything is interconnected and we are all of us flora, creatures, humans, etc., both deeply bound together by our similarities and our differences.’
