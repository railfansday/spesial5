Quantum computers are getting too powerful to check. Scientists have a solution

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

As quantum computers grow more powerful, researchers face a troubling question: How can they know whether the results are correct when conventional computers can no longer verify them? Scientists have now developed a promising new method that could help overcome this challenge by measuring the accuracy of quantum simulations and identifying how much uncertainty their predictions contain.

The technique uses experimental measurements to reconstruct how a quantum system behaves, then calculates how uncertainties in that reconstruction affect the results. Researchers successfully demonstrated the approach using a quantum simulator with up to 51 qubits in Innsbruck, Austria.

Quantum systems are expected to become capable of solving increasingly complex problems that traditional computers struggle with or cannot handle at all. However, this progress creates a verification problem. When a simulation is small enough, scientists can compare its results with calculations performed on a conventional computer. As quantum systems grow beyond those limits, that direct comparison becomes impractical, making alternative ways to assess their reliability essential.

A New Way to Measure Quantum Computing Accuracy

The breakthrough comes from an international research team led by Tristan Kraft at the Technical University of Munich and Peter Zoller at the University of Innsbruck and the Institute for Quantum Optics and Quantum Information of the Austrian Academy of Sciences, working alongside Barbara Kraus of the Technical University of Munich. The researchers developed a method for experimentally determining how a quantum simulator operates and translating uncertainties in its behavior into measurable limits on the accuracy of its results.

Quantum simulators are specialized physical systems designed to reproduce the behavior of other quantum systems. They are especially useful for investigating situations involving large numbers of interacting particles, where the calculations required can quickly overwhelm even powerful conventional computers.

But quantum experiments are never entirely free from errors. "But no real experiment is perfect," says Tristan Kraft. "Interactions may turn out differently than expected, the system is influenced by its environment, and measurements are also subject to uncertainties."

Rather than assuming a quantum simulator behaves exactly as intended, the new approach examines experimental data to determine what is actually happening inside the system. This allows researchers to identify important interactions and account for disturbances such as fluctuations and noise.

"From this data, we determine the relevant interactions as well as key influences from fluctuations and noise. We then calculate how the uncertainties in this model affect the simulation results," explains Tristan Kraft. "The quantum simulator thus provides not just a single value, but a result with error margins that quantify its accuracy."

Successfully Tested on a 51 Qubit Quantum Simulator

To evaluate the method, the researchers began with a relatively small quantum system containing ten trapped ions. Because conventional computers can still calculate the behavior of a system of this size, the team could test its reconstructed models and calculated error limits against independent measurements.

After this initial validation, the researchers expanded their experiments to a chain of 51 trapped ions. The larger demonstration, carried out by a team led by Manoj Joshi and Christian Roos using an ion trap quantum simulator, showed that the technique could also work with substantially more complex quantum systems.

This represents an important step toward establishing how trustworthy quantum simulation results are, particularly as quantum devices advance into territory where direct verification with traditional computing methods becomes increasingly difficult.

Extending the Breakthrough to More Complex Quantum Systems

The next challenge is to bring the technique to two-dimensional quantum simulators. These systems can model more complicated interactions among particles, making them valuable for exploring quantum physics. At the same time, their growing complexity makes their results particularly difficult to confirm using conventional calculations.

"This is particularly important because classical calculations for such systems become significantly more difficult as the number of particles increases," explains quantum computing pioneer Peter Zoller. "This also makes independent verification of the results increasingly complex, making the question of experimentally determined error limits all the more important."

The researchers are now adapting their method for the newest generation of two-dimensional quantum simulators, which offer improved precision and can investigate systems containing larger numbers of particles.

Looking further ahead, the approach could help scientists establish a more meaningful way to measure quantum advantage, the ability of quantum systems to outperform conventional computers. Instead of comparing machines based only on their processing speed or the size of the problems they can handle, researchers could also examine how accurately each system produces its answers.

"After all, when a classical computer and a quantum simulator tackle the same problem, it's not just a matter of which one delivers a result faster. What's also crucial is which one can solve the problem with a smaller, verifiable margin of error," says Peter Zoller.

This shift could change how progress in quantum computing is evaluated. Future breakthroughs may be judged not simply by how many particles a simulator can model or how quickly it performs calculations, but by its ability to solve increasingly difficult problems with demonstrably reliable results.

The findings were published in Physical Review X. The research received support from the Austrian Science Fund (FWF), the German Ministry of Research, Technology and Space, the European Union, BMW, and other funding organizations.
