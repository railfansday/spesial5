Locusts may help scientists detect hard-to-treat lung infections

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

In lab tests, locusts could smell the difference between bacterial biofilms and free-floating bacteria, a finding that could inform future breath tests.

Bacteria become much harder to kill once they attach to a surface and build a slimy protective layer.

These colonies are called biofilms. No test can currently detect them in a patient’s lungs without an invasive procedure.

A locust’s sense of smell might help one day.

In lab tests, researchers used signals from locust brains to tell biofilms apart from free-floating bacteria of the same species by smell.

The clearest signal appeared about a second after the odor reached the locusts’ antennae.

Debajit Saha and Jonathan Hardy were the senior authors of the study. Both are associate professors at Michigan State University (MSU). Michael Parnas was the first author.

Saha works in the College of Engineering. Hardy is based in the Department of Microbiology, Genetics and Immunology. Both researchers also belong to MSU’s Institute for Quantitative Health Science and Engineering.

Only bacterial cultures grown in flasks were used. Breath samples and patients were not tested.

Saha noted that before the experiments, it was not clear that an odor alone could show how bacteria were growing.

“Seeing such a high level of discrimination was therefore quite surprising,” Saha said.

A biofilm forms when bacteria settle on a solid surface. A sticky coating is then built around the whole colony.

Inside that coating, bacteria can be up to 1,000 times more resistant to antibiotics than free-floating bacteria. The authors gave that figure in the paper.

Some bacteria deep in the colony have a slower metabolism. Those cells can survive treatment, and the biofilm is rebuilt afterward.

Biofilms account for about 80% of bacterial infections in people, according to earlier research cited in the paper. Wounds, implants and the lungs can all be affected.

An infection is usually identified by growing a sample in a laboratory. That process takes a few days. Faster tests exist, but they cannot separate a biofilm from free-floating bacteria.

Bacteria also release chemicals into the air as they grow. Together, those airborne chemicals form an odor. An odor can be sampled from outside the body.

Locusts smell with their antennae. More than 100,000 odor-sensing nerve cells are found on each antenna.

The signals from those cells all pass through about 800 output neurons. These neurons are located in one small smell-processing region of the brain.

For the experiment, the brain was surgically exposed in six living locusts. Each insect was held still, and an electrode was placed in that region.

The team then blew air from five flasks across the antennae. Four flasks contained bacteria. The fifth contained only the broth in which the bacteria had been grown.

Two species were used, Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Staphylococcus aureus. Each species was grown in two ways, as a biofilm and as a free-floating culture.

Each odor was delivered for four seconds and repeated five times, with one minute between deliveries. The activity of 32 neurons was isolated from the recordings.

The locusts had not been trained on these odors. Insect brains have been used this way by Saha’s lab before. In earlier work, recordings were taken from locusts and honeybees exposed to the odors of human cancer cells.

Trained dogs can also detect some diseases by smell. However, a test based on an animal’s behavior gives only a yes or a no, the authors wrote.

A computer program compared the firing pattern from each trial with the patterns from the other trials. The closest match was then selected.

When all five odors were included, 22 of 25 trials were matched to the correct flask. That is an accuracy of 88%. Guessing would be right only 20% of the time.

Both biofilms were identified in every trial.

One of the five free-floating Staphylococcus trials was mistaken for the Staphylococcus biofilm. The plain broth was also mistaken for a Staphylococcus odor twice.

In this analysis, every biofilm was caught by the program. One free-floating sample was wrongly flagged as a biofilm, however.

The researchers reported accuracy of up to 96% when individual biofilm and free-floating odors were classified.

The largest differences in firing patterns were seen between the two species. Biofilm and free-floating cultures of the same species produced more similar patterns. Even so, they were still separated by the recordings.

Saha said bacteria release a different mix of airborne chemicals when their growth state changes. These subtle changes can be picked up by the insects.

Before the odor arrived, the program performed no better than guessing. Accuracy rose quickly once the odor was delivered.

The team then tested how little brain activity was needed. A half-second slice of the recording was enough for 91% accuracy. That slice was centered about one second after the odor arrived.

A quarter-second slice from the same moment gave 82% accuracy. A laboratory culture, by comparison, takes days.

The 32 neurons represent about 4% of the roughly 800 output neurons in the smell-processing region. The biofilm analysis was also repeated on random subsets of the 32.

Performance improved as more neurons were included.

Only one laboratory strain of each species was used. Human breath is more complicated. It also carries chemicals from food, medications and the surrounding air.

Several other limits were listed by the authors. The number of bacteria could not be kept exactly equal in the biofilm and free-floating flasks. That difference alone could change an odor.

All 32 neurons were pooled from six insects. The study therefore does not show a single locust doing the job.

The recordings stayed strong for about four hours after surgery. How long a locust lasts as a sensor depends on its health and the surgeon’s skill, the authors wrote.

Drug-resistant strains such as MRSA were not tested either. The team expects those strains to be harder to distinguish from ordinary Staphylococcus.

No one has yet tested whether a locust brain can pick a biofilm’s odor out of human breath.

Breath studies are the next step, the authors wrote. Hardy said the team is interested in testing breath samples from children with cystic fibrosis. Persistent lung infections that involve biofilms can develop in these children.

Pseudomonas is a major cause of lung infections in people with the disease, according to the Cystic Fibrosis Foundation.

Custom electrodes are also being developed so that enough data can be collected from a single locust.

In the long term, the researchers want to build a portable sensor. It would connect a living locust’s smell system to small electronics and artificial intelligence.

More bacterial species and samples from patients will be needed in future work. The results will also have to be compared with the laboratory methods already used in hospitals.

The full study was published in the journal Communications Biology.
