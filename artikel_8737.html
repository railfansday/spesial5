Otso’s Waheela R Is a Gravel Race Bike Built Around Rider Choice

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Otso might not be a name you see as often as Specialized or Trek, but you’ve likely come across its sister company, Wolf Tooth Components. The Minnesota-based bike brand was founded in 2016 by the engineers behind Wolf Tooth, and the two companies share a home and a practical approach to bike design. Otso’s small lineup includes gravel, mountain, and fat bikes, offered with a level of custom options that puts most big brands to shame.

The Waheela R sits next to the Waheela C in Otso’s lineup as the racier bike with a more focused mission. Otso gave it a longer front end, a slacker head tube angle, aero-shaped tubing, and internal storage, all to build a bike that can be raced hard over rough terrain.

That longer, slacker geometry is the part I noticed most while riding. The Waheela R felt composed at speed and gave me confidence on steep, loose descents. But it never felt like a handful when I slowed down for a tight turn. I also appreciated the choices Otso gives riders when setting up the bike, from cable routing to the parts that come on a complete build.

There are some compromises, particularly for riders who need one of the two smaller frame sizes. But after spending time on the Waheela R, I came away thinking that Otso got a lot of things right.

There’s nothing particularly flashy about many of the Waheela R’s frame details. It has a threaded BSA bottom bracket, a round 27.2 mm seatpost, an external seatpost collar, and a UDH derailleur hanger. In a world where it sometimes feels like every new bike comes with a proprietary part you’ll eventually have to track down, I find that extremely refreshing.

Perhaps the biggest example of Otso giving riders a choice is the cable routing. If you want a completely clean front end, you can route the lines through the handlebar, stem, and headset. If you’d rather keep them out of the headset entirely, there are ports at the head tube. Or you can do what I did and take the middle ground, running the lines under the stem and into the headset.

As far as I know, this is the only production bike that lets you choose between fully internal and external headset cable routing right out of the box. While brands might point to the small amount of extra weight added to the frame to accommodate this feature, I still think it can be worthwhile on more bikes, particularly ones aimed at regular riders rather than world tour pros.

The down-tube storage is another detail I like. Its opening sits above the bottle cage, so you can get into the compartment without removing your bottle. More importantly, the door isn’t also responsible for holding the weight of the cage and bottle in place. The compartment feels well sealed and has enough room for a flat kit and an emergency jacket.

There are plenty of useful mounts, including space for three bottles, a top-tube bag, and a partial frame bag. Otso also includes fender mounts, though you won’t find cargo mounts on the fork legs. For a bike designed primarily around gravel racing, I think that’s a sensible decision.

The frame is 1x-only and clears a round chainring up to 50 teeth. Rear tire clearance is 50 mm on medium through extra-large frames, but drops to 45 mm on the extra-small and small. That smaller-size limit of just 45 mm is the one frame detail that feels like a big miss for Otso.

In general, while more tire clearance is good, I’m okay being in the minority of tech writers and saying that for most people 50 mm is plenty. At the same time, 45mm-frame clearance isn't enough in 2026, and I would probably advise riders considering the two smaller frame sizes to look at other options because of that one detail.

The Waheela R has a longer front end and a slacker head tube angle than many gravel race bikes. A medium has a 405 mm reach and a 70.5-degree head angle. For comparison, a 54cm ENVE MOG has a 389 mm reach and a 71.25-degree head angle, while a 54cm Specialized Crux 5 has a 388 mm reach and a 71-degree head angle.

The Otso also has a longer wheelbase: 1,058 mm on a medium, compared to 1,027 mm for the 54 cm MOG and 1,026 mm for the 54 cm Crux. The BMC URS is closer to the Otso’s approach, with a 410 mm reach, 69.5-degree head angle, and 1,070 mm wheelbase on a medium.

One thing to keep in mind is that frame sizes don’t always line up between brands. The medium Waheela R is closer in stack and reach to a 56cm Crux 5 than to a 54 cm. Stem length, handlebar reach, and spacers all affect the final fit, so it’s worth looking closely at the geometry chart before ordering.

I spent a lot of time riding the Waheela R on all kinds of terrain, from smooth gravel roads and flowy singletrack to steep, loose doubletrack. If I could reasonably tackle something on a gravel bike, I rode the Otso on it. And just about everywhere I took it, the Waheela R was very enjoyable to ride.

The longer front end and shorter stem gave the bike a stable, confident feel, particularly when I was moving quickly over rough ground. On steep, loose descents, I felt comfortable relaxing my grip on the bars and letting the front end roll and stabilize through rocks and ruts. The Waheela R never felt nervous or like it needed my constant attention to navigate every trail imperfection.

What impressed me was that the Waheela R didn’t feel sluggish when I slowed down. It was still easy to steer through tighter turns on singletrack, and I never found myself fighting the bike to get it pointed where I wanted to go. I probably wouldn’t choose it for a cyclocross race, but that’s also not what Otso designed it for.

The bike was just as capable going uphill. On steep, loose climbs, I felt like the longer front end gave me room to move forward and keep the front wheel planted without giving up too much rear traction. It’s a subtle thing, but it helped me stay seated and keep moving when the grade got very steep.

The Waheela R also has the stiffness and responsive feel I expect from a gravel race bike. It felt eager to accelerate when I pushed on the pedals, and I never found the ride particularly harsh. That said, I’d still rank the Specialized Crux 5 ahead of the Otso for comfort. The Waheela R strikes a good balance for a race bike, but it isn’t the smoothest gravel bike I’ve ridden.

The Waheela R is very competitively priced, especially given how much freedom Otso gives you to customize the build. The frameset price is $2,950, but it’s the complete bike prices that really shine. A complete Waheela R with mechanical Shimano GRX 600 starts at $4,250. A SRAM Force XPLR AXS build starts at $5,450.

Of course, you can spend more if you want to. Otso offers upgrades including ENVE wheels and cockpit components, along with a range of Wolf Tooth parts and anodized colors. But what I appreciate most about the configurator is the ability to choose things like stem length, handlebar width, and seatpost offset when you order the bike.

That can save you the expense and frustration of buying a complete bike, only to immediately replace parts to get the fit right. Depending on the build, you can also choose your wheels, tires, gearing, and cable routing. Some options add to the price, but you get to decide where you want to spend or save your money.

It’s the kind of flexibility I wish more brands offered. Whether you want a relatively straightforward GRX build or a Force-equipped bike with ENVE wheels, Otso lets you put together the bike you actually want from the start.

The Waheela R is a fun, capable gravel race bike, but what sets it apart for me is how much choice Otso gives riders. You can route the cables through the handlebar and headset, keep them entirely out of the headset, or choose something in between. Add in a threaded bottom bracket and a standard round seatpost, and you get a bike that will be easy to live with for years to come.

That flexibility extends to the build options. Otso lets you choose your stem length, handlebar width, seatpost offset, wheels, and other parts when you order. The pricing is good, too. Even a Force AXS build decked out with ENVE wheels and components can come in under $10,000.

The biggest miss is tire clearance on the two smallest frame sizes. While medium through extra-large frames fit tires up to 50 mm, the extra-small and small are limited to 45 mm at the rear. For riders who want more tire volume for rough courses or the option to run wider rubber in the future, that will be a deal breaker.
