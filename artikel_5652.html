The Proof Is In The Lettuce: Perovskite Makes Things Grow Better

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Last year’s sharp U-turn in energy policy came with catastrophic consequences for farmers who were counting on federal assistance for solar power on their land. Still, the pace of innovation in the solar industry never stopped, and farmers may ultimately benefit whenever the policy winds blow in a more positive direction. In one particularly interesting development, the US startup UbiQD has assembled more evidence that its “UbiGro” perovskite-enhanced glass can help hothouse farmers improve their yields while saving water, too.

For those of you new to the topic, perovskite refers to a new class of relatively inexpensive, easy-to-fabricate solar materials, characterized by a crystalline structure based on the naturally occurring mineral perovskite (see lots more background here).

Raw perovskite is too unstable to function as a solar material on its own, but it can be combined with more durable materials in a solar cell, or in a solar module. Silicon-perovskite combinations are already emerging in the market, for example.

UbiQD developed its UbiGro glass to incorporate sub-microscopic particles of perovskite. Particles of that size are known as “artificial atoms” or quantum dots, as capably explained by scientists at Los Alamos National Laboratory:

Quantum dots are ultra-small bits of semiconductor matter that can be synthesized with nearly atomic precision via modern methods of colloidal chemistry. Their emission color can be tuned by simply varying their dimensions. Color tunability is combined with high emission efficiencies approaching 100%.

As Los Alamos indicates, quantum dots initially appeared in the LED field of the lighting industry before moving into solar energy applications as a means of ramping up the efficiency of solar cells. By 2016 researchers were experimenting with quantum dots in a perovskite solar cell. The pioneering startup Blue Dot was among those pursuing the perovskite connection.

Activity began to accelerate in 2025 when UbiQD acquired BlueDot. In the meantime, UbiQD had previously hooked up with the US thin film solar manufacturer First Solar to develop quantum dots for solar panels. That project also ramped up in 2025 when UbiQD closed on a $20 million round of Series B funding. According to UbiQD, its enhancements could double the solar conversion efficiency of First Solar’s bifacial solar panels.

In 2025, UbiQD also announced the findings from a study of its UbiGro greenhouse glass. The glass is treated with quantum dots as a means of fine-tuning sunlight to improve crop yields. The study was published by researchers at the University of California – Davis in the journal Materials Today Sustainability under the title, “Spectrally selective quantum dot laminated glass for photonic modulation and climate regulation in greenhouses.”

— Fresh biomass increased by 37.8%. Plants were nearly 40% heavier, indicating significantly more edible yield.

— Leaf area was increased by 38.0%. Larger leaf area means higher photosynthetic surface.

— Root length increased by 38.0%. Deeper roots improve water and nutrient uptake, resilience, and shelf life.

— Light-use efficiency was increased by 41.0%. The plants used sunlight more effectively, with more biomass per photon.

— Nutrient concentrations (N, P, K, Mg, Zn, Cu) were significantly elevated. The crops were more nutritious, with higher levels of essential nutrients.

— Spectral red:blue ratio increased by 61.0%, with no significant PAR losses. More red light was delivered without sacrificing total usable light levels.

“The findings highlight a promising pathway toward climate-smart greenhouse envelopes that integrate photonics with sustainable food and energy strategies,” the research team concluded.

In a series of followup trials last Spring, UC-Davis focused on the impact of UbiGro on water consumption by greenhouse-grown lettuce. The results were similarly encouraging.

“Water use efficiency improved by approximately 9%. Lettuce grown under QD glass produced 101 grams of fresh biomass per liter of water compared 93 grams per liter under conventional glass,” UbiQD noted.

“Unlike technologies that require additional energy inputs or changes to irrigation infrastructure, UbiGro glass works passively by optimizing the sunlight already entering the greenhouse while optimizing water use efficiency,” UbiD emphasized in a press statement.

UbiQD’s VP of Sales and Marketing, Eric Moody, also chipped in his two cents. “It’s an important new area of validation for UbiGro as we continue exploring how our technology can help growers get more from the resources they already use,”said Moody.

Want more details? Check out the study, “Crop Water Use and Evapotranspiration Estimation in Deep Water Culture Hydroponic System under Quantum Dot Glazed Greenhouse,” presented by UC-Davis Ph.D. candidate Mala Parvin at the 2026 American Society of Agricultural and Biological Engineers Annual International Meeting in Indianapolis last July.

First Solar has also been making some moves of its own. The iconic US firm is of particular interest for its focus on CdTe (cadmium-telluride) solar film instead of the silicon formula commonly used around the world. Here in the US, the Department of Energy has been promoting CdTe since the early 1990’s as a domestically sourced alternative to silicon, and it has become particularly popular in utility-scale applications.

First Solar has been embroiled in patent disputes of late, but the dust appeared to be settling last month when the company announced a non-exclusive patent licensing agreement with the US firm CubicPV Inc.

Among other, undisclosed terms of the agreement, First Solar will have access to CubicPV patents covering perovskite solar technology in the US. “The license further clears the path for First Solar to continue its development of photovoltaic (PV) devices built with a perovskite semiconductor for the US utility-scale, commercial and industrial, and residential markets, and applies to sales and distribution of those products domestically,” First Solar explains.

Somewhat ironically, if US President Donald Trump were to lend his full support to the domestic solar industry, First Solar would be a feather in his cap. First Solar bills itself as the largest producer of thin film solar in the world, and the largest solar manufacturer of any kind in the Western Hemisphere.

First Solar is also among the solar stakeholders contributing to the President’s oft-stated ambition of bringing more manufacturing back to the US during his time in office. “The Company’s investments include a new perovskite development line at its Perrysburg, Ohio, campus that produces small form-factor modules featuring a perovskite semiconductor, allowing First Solar to meet key internal milestones in its perovskite development program, including initial-stage efficiency, stability, and manufacturability objectives,” First Solar notes.

That’s just for starters. By the middle of next year, First Solar expects to have a pilot line up and running at a facility in Ohio, which will apply the technology to its existing Series 6 solar modules.

In addition, last month First Solar announced the start of Phase I of operations at its sixth facility in the US. Located in Gaffney, South Carolina, the new factory represents another step in First Solar’s onshoring plan for Series 6 production.

“The second phase is forecast to be completed by mid-2027, at which time the approximately $330 million facility is expected to double its annual nameplate capacity to 3.4 gigawatts (GW) and produce the Company’s CuRe technology, while supporting nearly 700 jobs. Approximately 500 of those jobs have already been filled,” First Solar explains.

For the record, the Gaffney facility expands First Solar’s existing footprint in South Carolina. The company also operates in Alabama, Louisiana, and Ohio. All together, First Solar anticipates to have a total of 17 gigawatts’ worth of domestic solar module manufacturing under its belt by the end of this year, which is just around the corner.
