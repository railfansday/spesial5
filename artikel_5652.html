New Obesity Definition Flags More Adults at Dementia Risk

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

— Dementia risk shifts as obesity definition expands

A new definition of obesity identified more adults with an increased risk of dementia compared with defining obesity by body mass index (BMI) alone, pooled data from two cohorts showed.

Using the European Association for the Study of Obesity (EASO) definition, more than 80% of adults classified as overweight were reclassified as having obesity, according to Rui Zhou, PhD, of Guangdong Pharmaceutical University in Guangzhou, China, and co-authors.

Compared with people of normal weight without comorbidities, those with EASO-defined obesity had a significantly higher risk of dementia (pooled HR 1.93, 95% CI 1.50-2.48), Zhou and colleagues reported in Neurology.

Those with EASO-defined overweight, on the other hand, had a lower risk of dementia than those with normal weight (pooled HR 0.61, 95% CI 0.45-0.81).

The EASO framework incorporates BMI, body fat distribution (waist-to-height ratio), and medical, functional, or psychological comorbidities.

"These findings underscore the inadequacy of using BMI alone to define obesity," Zhou said in a statement. "The results support the integration of body fat distribution and other health conditions into classifying obesity."

Obesity is linked with dementia, with midlife obesity having an especially high risk. Evidence from both animal and human studies suggests that obesity negatively affects the brain through multiple mechanisms, including hypothalamic dysregulation and metabolic, inflammatory, and vascular disruptions that target the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex.

The BMI definition of obesity has drawn criticism in recent years, leading to alternative frameworks from groups like EASO and the Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology global commission.

The implications of the new obesity definitions for dementia risk have been unclear, Zhou and co-authors noted. To help clarify this, they compared the prevalence of obesity under the EASO framework versus traditional BMI criteria and examined how obesity was associated with incident dementia and all-cause mortality.

The study used data from the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA; 6,542 people) and the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS; 12,402 people).

The researchers defined dementia using algorithmic criteria or self-reported physician diagnoses, and determined all-cause mortality from death registries, medical records, or interviews with relatives. They included 17,617 participants in the dementia analysis and 18,197 in the mortality analysis.

The mean baseline age in the ELSA group was 65.9 years and 54.3% were women. In CHARLS, the mean baseline age was 58.2 and 53.2% were women. The median follow-up was 11.5 years in ELSA and 8.2 years in CHARLS.

Under the EASO framework, 85.8% of ELSA participants and 80.2% of CHARLS participants who were previously classified as overweight by BMI shifted to the obesity category.

Overall, 37.7% of ELSA participants and 24.9% of CHARLS participants had obesity using the EASO definition. The most common comorbidities in the newly defined obesity group were high blood pressure (42.6%) and diabetes (35.1%).

Compared with people with normal weight and no comorbidities, people with EASO-defined obesity had higher dementia risk in both cohorts. They had higher mortality risk only in the CHARLS cohort, but the pooled mortality estimate was significant (pooled HR 1.30, 95% CI 1.01-1.68).

EASO-defined overweight was associated with lower dementia risk in both cohorts and with lower mortality (pooled HR 0.32, 95% CI 0.13-0.81).

The so-called "obesity paradox" seen in earlier studies may stem from failures to account for obesity-related comorbidities or to exclude unhealthy individuals from comparison groups, the researchers suggested.

When normal weight without morbidity was the reference in this study, both EASO-defined and BMI-defined obesity were consistently associated with increased dementia risk, they noted. "However, when all normal weight individuals (including those with comorbidities) were used as the reference, these harmful associations were masked," Zhou and colleagues pointed out.

Obesity was measured only at baseline, and changes over time were not captured, the researchers acknowledged. Despite adjusting for multiple variables, residual confounding cannot be excluded.
