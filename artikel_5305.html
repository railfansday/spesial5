Yum China CEO Joey Wat: What China’s consumer market can teach global business

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Joey Wat is the CEO of Yum China, the largest restaurant company in China by system sales. The company operates around 20,000 stores across six brands in China, including KFC, Pizza Hut and Lavazza.

In 1987, KFC opened its first restaurant in Beijing, marking the debut of China’s first Western quick-service restaurant brand. Nearly four decades later, KFC China has expanded to more than 14,000 stores in a market that has transformed around it.

KFC has changed alongside it. In addition to the Original Recipe Chicken and Zinger, our menu offers energy bowls and sparkling coffee – many of which cannot be found in KFC menus outside China. More broadly, we have adapted everything from marketing to store formats as consumer preferences, technology and competition have evolved.

For any global brand, keeping pace with such change requires tremendous effort. I believe one reason KFC has been able to keep adapting is the strength of our local teams. They have the know-how and autonomy to respond to the market with agility. The brand may be global, but decisions are made close to the consumer.

KFC is one of the six restaurant brands operated by Yum China, which together span 20,000 stores across the country. Our experience, including 10 years as a company listed on the New York Stock Exchange, has reinforced three lessons for global brands seeking to win in this market. While shaped in China, I believe they extend far beyond it.

One fundamental reality about China is that it is not a single market, but many. Consumer preferences, consumption occasions and competitive landscapes vary across regions and city tiers. A single playbook is not enough to win, which is why we tailor store formats and menus for select lower-tier cities. Yet highly tailored solutions can be costly, making a dual focus on innovation and efficiency essential.

Yum China’s 20,000-store portfolio gives us a strong platform. Two years ago, we began carving out space within existing KFC stores for side-by-side modules such as KCOFFEE cafés to tap into fast-growing coffee demand. Since then, KCOFFEE cafes have expanded to over 4,000 locations, driving incremental sales and profit without incurring the investment of a new store. We have also extended this approach to KFC’s KPRO and Pizza Hut’s Burger Bar to capture the emerging light meal and the large burger market respectively.

Consumers look for exciting new products and great value. We meet those needs by launching 500-600 new or upgraded products a year across our brands. To make this both efficient and sustainable, we draw inspiration from hero products to launch innovations such as the Intriguing Oreo Original Recipe Chicken Burger and Pizza Dough Burgers. As we drive transaction growth and tap into smaller-order occasions, we have been able to sustain the margins. Doing so requires transformation across nearly every part of the business, from supply chain and operations to technology.

This is why innovation and efficiency can reinforce each other: innovative solutions drive efficiency, and efficiency in turn creates room for further innovations.

In fast-moving markets, there is constant pressure to focus on visible results. But resilience in challenging times requires investment in capabilities whose value may not be immediately apparent.

I often think of bamboo, common across southern China, including my hometown in Fujian Province. Its strength lies underground, in roots that grow deep and wide, forming a complex, mutually reinforcing web. When green shoots finally emerge, they can grow with astonishing speed and become a forest that can weather storms.

A business follows a similar pattern. The capabilities that make scale possible are often invisible for years. At Yum China, we have invested patiently in a root system: supply chain, digital and membership ecosystems, food innovation and organizational strength.

These capabilities allow new concepts to draw on systems that have been built and strengthened over decades, rather than starting from scratch.

Bamboo teaches us patience and discipline. Invest in the foundation, and trust that scale will follow. It also reminds us that success is interconnected. Like the roots of a forest, strong businesses reinforce one another.

When I was 15, I worked part-time as a restaurant food runner. It taught me the dignity of hard work and the importance of frontline employees.

That experience shaped a belief I still hold today: employees come first, followed by customers and shareholders. This may sound like sacrilege to some, particularly for a public company, but it is essential to creating sustainable long-term value. Take care of employees, who shape customer experiences. Customers enable business performance. Business performance ultimately drives shareholder returns.

Frontline employees often see changes in consumer sentiment and behavior early. They spot problems before they appear on dashboards. Having the right strategy is important, but without the right people to execute it, it cannot succeed. Put simply, execution is growth.

This people-first mindset matters even more in the age of AI. AI can help forecast demand and optimize staffing. But these systems are only effective when they are built on the knowledge and judgment of experienced operators.

As agentic AI begins to reshape how companies operate, one of the most undervalued assets in any organization may be its experienced operators – the “old masters” who understand the business deeply and can guide the integration of new technology into real workflows. Technology alone does not create advantage. People who know how to apply it do.

The same lesson applies to global companies seeking to sustain success in China. Many strong local companies are founder-led. They move with speed, focus and entrepreneurial conviction. To compete effectively, multinational companies need local teams with good judgment and enough autonomy to respond just as quickly.

China’s lesson for U.S. business leaders is clear: brand heritage can open doors and global scale can create powerful advantages. But both become liabilities if they breed complacency or slow decision-making.

The real test is not whether a company can succeed in a market once. It is whether it can keep adapting as consumers and technology evolve. It is no longer enough for leaders to see where the future is headed. The best leaders build organizations capable of riding on the opportunities and challenges.
