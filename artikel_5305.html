Anthropic is betting $900 billion that customers won't switch to cheaper AI

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Telecom firms went bankrupt in 2002 when the price of moving data collapsed. Anthropic’s shareholders could lose money the same way if AI gets cheaper

Michael Nagle / Bloomberg via Getty Images

Stu Clott, an operations manager in San Diego who writes software on the side, used to do his coding with Claude, the AI model made by Anthropic, until he switched to DeepSeek, a model from a Chinese company. An hour of coding cost Clott about $10 on Claude and less than 50 cents on DeepSeek, he told Rest of World, so he now pays less than a twentieth as much for the work. "I laugh every time I go see [the costs]," Clott said. "The output quality, to be honest, I can't tell the difference."

Anthropic has reason to worry about customers like Clott, because the company has already agreed to keep paying for computing power for years to come. Anthropic has signed up to pay about $900 billion for computing power and data centers between now and 2045, according to its prospectus, and many of the contracts, which the company calls "substantial fixed obligations," require it to pay whether or not it uses the computing power it reserved. At the pace Anthropic brought in money in the first nine months of this year, about $44 billion a year, the $900 billion equals about 20 years of revenue. The prospectus warns that customers may decide the most advanced models "do not justify the additional cost" and "migrate toward smaller, cheaper, or open-source alternatives," the move Clott already made. Anthropic's pitch to IPO investors assumes Claude's customers will keep choosing Claude. Its shares opened at $111.50 when it went public last week, about a third above the $83 IPO price, before falling below that price this week.

In the late 1990s, telecom companies borrowed heavily to lay fiber-optic cable for internet traffic they expected, and many went bankrupt when traffic grew far more slowly than they had planned. JPMorgan $JPM Chase warned last week that the more than $5 trillion it expects the world to spend on data centers, AI equipment, and power over the next five years could turn into a "repeat of the telecom and fiber build-out experiences," DatacenterDynamics reported. Like the telecom companies of the late 1990s, Anthropic has agreed to pay for years of computing power whether or not customers pay to use it.

Telecom carriers in the late 1990s spent as though internet traffic would keep growing at its fastest pace ever. Publicly traded U.S. phone and fiber companies spent $47 billion on networks and equipment in 1995 and $121 billion in 2000, more than two and a half times as much, according to a 2004 paper from the Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco. The carriers competed to lay cable into the biggest cities. Chicago and Minneapolis were each served by about five long-haul carriers in 1990 and by about 20 apiece by 2004.

The fast growth the carriers planned for had lasted just two years. Internet traffic grew about 100-fold during 1995 and 1996, when millions of people signed up for home internet accounts and started using web browsers, Andrew Odlyzko and K.G. Coffman, researchers at AT&T $T Labs, found. In 1997, traffic growth slowed to about a doubling each year. Many in the industry kept believing traffic doubled every three months, and the carriers built their networks to match. Michael Powell, then chairman of the Federal Communications Commission, testified before the Senate Commerce Committee in July 2002 that carriers had built networks that, by some estimates, could carry 500 times as much data as before, expecting demand to double every 100 days.

Internet traffic kept doubling every year, but at that rate it would take about nine years to fill networks with 500 times the room they used to have. "The problem in the story line is, they did not come," Powell said. In late 2002, the consulting firm TeleGeography estimated that only about one in 10 long-haul fibers in Europe and North America carried any signal. The carriers cut prices to sign up whatever customers they could find. The monthly price of a high-speed data line between London and New York fell from about $208,000 in 1999 to about $4,400 in 2003, so the cost of one month in 1999 would have covered nearly four years in 2003, according to a 2010 conference paper by the researchers Alan Mauldin and David Ross.

The carriers' revenue fell while their debts didn't shrink, and the biggest of them went bankrupt within two years of the spending peak in 2000. Global Crossing had put $15 billion into 100,000 miles of cable connecting 27 countries. Global Crossing filed for bankruptcy in January 2002 with $12.4 billion in debt, brought down by falling prices and more room on its cables than it could sell, and it also faced investigations into its accounting. WorldCom filed in July 2002, in what was then the largest bankruptcy in U.S. history, and its CEO, Bernie Ebbers, was later sentenced to 25 years in prison for an $11 billion accounting fraud.

Internet traffic eventually put more of the cable to use, but the original carriers didn't collect the money, because many lost the networks in bankruptcy for a fraction of what the cable cost to build. Singapore Technologies Telemedia paid $250 million in 2003 for 61.5% of Global Crossing as it left bankruptcy, the Associated Press reported. The deal valued the whole company at about $406 million, less than 3 cents for every dollar Global Crossing had put into its cable. In 2011, Level 3 Communications agreed to buy Global Crossing for about $2 billion in stock, about five times the $406 million ST Telemedia's deal had implied, and to take on $1.1 billion of its debt besides. Level 3 and its rivals were still competing so hard on price that the rates they charged to carry data kept falling, Erik Kreifeldt, a senior analyst at TeleGeography, told the AP in 2011. As late as 2016, the science writer Jeff Hecht wrote, Europe and North America still had enough unused fiber to handle years of further growth.

Many businesses now shop for AI the way telecom customers bought space on fiber lines once prices had collapsed, picking whichever model is cheapest and still good enough. In the first half of 2025, Chinese models handled about one in 20 of the tokens U.S. companies ran through OpenRouter, a service that connects developers to a range of AI models through one account. From early February through early July, Chinese models handled more than three in 10 every week, and as many as nearly half, CNBC reported. "Price is doing the work here," Harpreet Arora, who leads work on tools for AI agents at Vercel, a web-hosting company for developers, told CNBC. "When a task doesn't need the best model, teams are beginning to route it to the cheapest one that's good enough, and the recent wave of models coming out of China is winning that trade."

Ruben Garcia Jr., a Dallas developer who builds websites and phone apps for businesses, runs AI agents to handle his clients' requests on their own. He pays $500 a month for Claude and ChatGPT, which he uses only for the hardest planning and review work, and $200 a month for three Chinese models, Minimax, Moonshot's Kimi, and Xiaomi's MiMo, he told Rest of World. The Chinese models handle 90% of his work, from coding to voice recognition, for less than half of what he spends on the American ones. "If the Chinese models come out and they are frontier and cheaper, I'm going that direction," Garcia said.

Falling prices haven't cut what businesses spend on AI, because they keep using more of it. Customers of Vercel's AI Gateway, which lets developers reach many companies' models through one connection, paid about half as much in September for each token, the small chunk of text AI companies bill by, as they had in December 2025, yet they used about 24 times as many tokens and spent about 13 times as much, according to Vercel's figures. Open-weight models, which any developer can download and run, carried about seven of every 10 tokens on the gateway in September but collected less than a quarter of the money, because each of their tokens costs far less. Anthropic still collects more on the gateway than any other company, and its top model, Claude Opus 5.5, took 11% of September's spending, more than twice as much as OpenAI's best.

Anthropic's computing deals are part of a wave of data center spending far bigger than the spending on fiber-optic cable that sank the telecom carriers. Forecasters expect Amazon $AMZN, Meta $META, Oracle $ORCL, Microsoft $MSFT, and Google $GOOGL to spend about 3% of U.S. GDP a year on data centers from 2027 through 2029, two and a half times the share telecom companies invested at their peak in 2000, Torsten Slok, chief economist at Apollo Global Management, wrote in August. Forecasters also expect data center spending to climb by close to a percentage point of GDP a year from 2025 through 2027, more than five times as fast as telecom investment ever grew. When telecom spending collapsed in 2001, the drop helped cause a U.S. recession, the mildest since World War II. "A cycle that builds at 0.85 percentage points a year can unwind at a similar pace, and that, rather than the buildout itself, is the macro risk if AI demand disappoints," Slok wrote.

Anthropic has told investors what would happen to the company if customers used less AI. Most of Anthropic's revenue rises and falls with how much customers use its products, and much of the money doesn't come from long-term contracts, the company warns in its prospectus, while the deals it signed for computing power generally last several years. Many of Anthropic's contracts let customers use less or cancel with little or no notice, and nobody is obligated to renew, according to an earlier draft of the filing. If Anthropic brings in less than it owes for computing, the company says, it "may be unable to satisfy these obligations." Under Anthropic's loan agreement, a default on some of its business contracts could let lenders demand nearly all of the company's debt back at once, and Anthropic warns that a chain of defaults could set off "a cascading series of adverse consequences," including creditors moving against it together and the loss of data centers it depends on.

After the telecom carriers went bankrupt, people paid far less to move data, and Level 3 picked up Global Crossing's cable for a fraction of what it cost to lay. Anthropic's new shareholders own a company that has agreed to pay for computing power through 2045, the position the telecom companies' investors held when the price of moving data collapsed.
