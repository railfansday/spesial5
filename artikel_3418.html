‘Grand Theft Auto VI’ Made Nearly $4 Billion Doing What Movies Can’t

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

“Grand Theft Auto VI” took less than 24 hours to generate $3.79 billion in global sales. That’s roughly 15 percent of the year’s worldwide box office — in under a day.

But Hollywood’s biggest concern about games might not be what else consumers are spending their money on. It could be just how many days, weeks, or even months people end up playing “GTA VI” instead of streaming movies or TV on other platforms.

Even before Rockstar’s record-breaking launch on November 19, entertainment news outlets like TheWrap were asking whether the game studio’s historic release would seriously disrupt the attention economy. Several analysts echoed that concern in their own industry assessments, warning investors that the sprawling open-world adventure could monopolize living-room airspace until well after the holidays. There’s a sort of delicious irony in realizing a game named after a felony has Hollywood panicking about stolen screen time. Personally speaking, as a former video game critic who now mostly covers film and television, I’m fascinated by the competition for both audience engagement and consumer interest.

Still, that particular side of “GTA VI” discourse drives me up the wall. Games, movies, TV shows, and social media feeds do indeed battle for our attention, and the business of entertainment is ultimately all about influencing how strangers use their free time.

Rather, the issue lies in the assumption that one hour spent with one type of story is automatically equivalent to another hour spent with an entirely different medium — and that comparing those numbers tells us a thing about what either is really worth.

Last Thursday, I was at my favorite bar, catching up with a friend visiting from Seattle, when she got up to greet someone else. Scrolling on my phone, I opened a DM from my old coworker that said he was… already playing “GTA VI”? That couldn’t be!

Confused, I quickly searched “GTA 6 release date” and discovered I wasn’t just a few hours away from entering Vice City — but that I was already a full day behind the rest of the gaming world. Yes, Rockstar had spent more than a decade preparing its masterpiece, and I had somehow missed the actual drop like a tricky freeway exit.

I really should’ve known better, too. I’d preordered “GTA VI” and downloaded it on my PlayStation days earlier, but even $79.99 deep, I was caught off guard by the sudden arrival of Lucia and Jason, who, even welcome, had seriously complicated my night.

Never one to miss a groundbreaking moment in a fake place, I ran home to my apartment, determined to make up for lost time using whatever post-bar energy I had. (As it turns out, that wasn’t much.)

There’s something weirdly prudish about Hollywood’s anxiety over just how much time “GTA VI” fans might spend playing it — as though immersive and interactive narratives were an indulgence taking hours away from more legitimate entertainment. Games have been a major market and creative stage for decades, and still, the conversation around Rockstar’s new blockbuster brings to mind the sort of worried parents who would’ve been afraid of TV in the ’90s.

Those pearl-clutching concerns also miss a practical distinction. Playing a video game demands a different kind of physical and mental engagement than watching a movie or TV show. Admittedly, the first few minutes of “GTA VI” make that distinction remarkably easy to forget — opening on Lucia in prison for a sequence so beautifully lit and photographed that interacting with inmates feels at once totally real and like Rockstar showing off.

And yet, even in the “cinematic” wonder of that moment, rushing home left me in no state to steer a virtual boat. My first real “GTA VI” mission involved navigating choppy waters to pick up (and/or drop off, I honestly cannot remember) bricks of cocaine, but unfortunately, bro, I could not operate heavy machinery.

What felt like hours of circling checkpoints was probably closer to 15 minutes. Still, the technical wizardry bobbing across my screen clashed with my stomach contents, and I was desperate to stop. So, I turned off my PS5 and streamed “Knocked Up,” instead.

I woke up to the sound of Katherine Heigl crowning and went to bed at 1 a.m., truly grateful that Judd Apatow’s 2007 comedy had freed me from the obligation to captain anything. But that night, sober as a judge and eager to get behind the wheel again, I remembered what the decade-plus of “GTA VI” hype was all about.

My first in-game police pursuit was astounding in its intricacy, with Leonida (i.e., Rockstar’s Florida) providing a jaw-dropping menagerie of local characters, each living out a personal drama or comedy of errors against a backdrop of boundless scenery.

Weaving through traffic and cutting down alleys, I realized I wanted the full “Fast and Furious” feeling early and often. You know, tightly choreographed fever dreams complete with stunt drivers, cinematographers, and someone other than me to call cut. But here, all I had was myself: a shitty chauffeur, a worse debut director, and a leading man who kept mistakenly jumping onto the roof of her car before getting in.

To be fair, “GTA VI” offers a truly impressive range of camera options when you decide to properly educate yourself on the system’s customizable controls. But as a woman who couldn’t keep her wheels on the road, let alone capture Jason’s stubble from a flattering angle, I was practically begging for a union rep — and a body double. Worse still, I was getting more and more worried about my girl, Lucia.

Rockstar had sold me on Jason as a good boyfriend, and I wanted to prioritize that interpretation in my playthrough. But the longer I waited for Lucia to get out of prison, the more convinced I became that she might never be coming back. Had I missed a mission? Decapitated the wrong innocent bystander? Whatever had gone wrong in my game, uncertainty powered Jason’s decline.

I robbed a woman after she refused my offer to help with her broken-down car. I took surprising pleasure in dispatching adversarial gang members. After a seemingly infinite number of crashes, I cared less about playing the romantic lead than about combining my growing enthusiasm for scuba diving with organized crime.

In a movie, that sort of moral deterioration might suggest a carefully constructed character arc. In “GTA VI,” it mostly reflected my own impatience. Rockstar had given me the raw materials for a compelling love story, but the longer I waited for Lucia, the more willfully criminal my version of Jason became. By Friday night, I was having a good time — but I was also getting critically hardened, and increasingly convinced I’d done something wrong to derail the romance I craved.

Around 3 a.m. on Saturday, I was throwing up into my toilet with the word “WASTED” practically hovering above my head in enormous white letters. My plans to spend the weekend robbing old ladies and getting gradually pissier with the absent Lucia had been derailed by a profoundly gross bout of what seemed to be food poisoning.

Yeah, it was a real bummer, dog. But unable to muster the constitution for a car ride, scuba diving, or the next step in my budding drug empire, I settled for fixing up Jason’s rickety house and lifting weights on his back deck. Nauseous, I even briefly considered finishing Lucia’s bedroom, but gave up after getting stuck picking just one color from a trio of offscreen paint samples. (I didn’t know her well enough to know which one she’d like, and fighting a fever, that felt like an argument in the making.)

There was something genuinely lovely about occupying Rockstar’s beautifully realized environments without any particular objective in mind. Even under the weather, I understood why I’d seen so many people on social media comparing “GTA VI” to “slow cinema,” a style of filmmaking that finds meaning in naturalistic observation and drawn-out routines.

Its more intimate moments sometimes reminded me of Sean Baker’s “The Florida Project,” too, with its colorful vision of the Sunshine State smashing together the faded appeal of amusement parks and painful hallmarks of everyday desperation.

That’s a lot to contend with when you’re sick, though, so I turned on “The Hunting Wives” (another gloriously lurid piece of modern American satire!) and, while scrolling through social media, I was struck by a contrast.

Eight episodes of “The Hunting Wives” could simply wash over me. To get the same experience from “GTA VI,” I’d have to hunt through Vice City and its vast collection of in-universe media myself. Both had plenty to say about contemporary America. Only one required me to do much of anything to hear it.

So I was genuinely surprised when I began encountering livestreams on TikTok of people playing through later stages of the “GTA VI” story, including a scooter-stealing mission I’d just discovered but hadn’t tried yet. Then I caught a glimpse of someone playing as… Lucia?!

I scrolled away immediately. Having spent Friday convinced I’d sabotaged the romance through my own choices, I was relieved to see I was still on track, but simultaneously annoyed that someone else’s playthrough had spoiled part of my plot.

Still, I couldn’t stop laughing at the online debate over whether “GTA VI” was too woke, or whether Rockstar had successfully tricked millions of players into simulating healthy relationships. I’d bought the game partly because I wanted to participate in a massive cultural moment, and here I was, too sick to play but still in the thick of things — watching it unfold through Netflix, TikTok, and strangers whose versions of Vice City were already far ahead of mine.

It was exciting, irritating, occasionally informative, and even without my controller, I found myself making decisions about how I wanted to play later. (One person, for example, painted Lucia’s bedroom peach, a choice I disliked so much that I made both a mental and literal note to go with green in the end.)

By Sunday night, I was finally feeling well enough to enjoy the entertainment I’d been trying to consume all weekend. Lucia had come home in my playthrough, and exploring their relationship together made “GTA VI” click in a way no amount of aimless driving could.

At a lavish yacht party, interacting with other guests became an opportunity to figure out what sort of couple these two actually were. Were Jason and Lucia the kind of boyfriend and girlfriend who did drugs together? Would romantic jealousy be an issue, or were we cool with [wink] that?

Suddenly, I wasn’t waiting for Rockstar to tell me what their connection meant. I was actively deciding, and having thoroughly mastered the mechanics of walking around without looking like a complete lunatic by then, I was putting some swag into it.

A particularly sexy jet-skiing sequence made an impression, with Jason holding onto Lucia as she drove instead of the other way around. It was a sweet little reversal of gender norms, and the kind of affectionate character detail I’d been waiting to discover. Even better, it was happening in a game that seemed to understand exactly how much I wanted to drive its love story.

Then came the Carly Rae Jepsen car chase.

Glossy and expensive, our borrowed vehicle reflected the neon lights of Vice City like a scene from “Blade Runner.” With 2015 chart-topper “Run Away with Me” blasting from the speakers, Lucia and Jason were living out the spectacular criminal romance I’d been hoping for. Skyscrapers towered around our blossoming love, and I was riding high…

… Until I smashed us into a cement divider and Jason’s head exploded.

As the game reset and made my sudden brush with vehicular homicide a thing of the past, I found myself laughing like gunfire alone in my apartment. I was suddenly orchestrating a crime of continuity no movie could ever get away with — and it was one of my favorite “GTA VI” moments yet.

Great movies and TV shows often succeed by creating characters who sincerely feel like real people. Rockstar’s new release is exceptionally good at that. But an equally extraordinary connection between art and its audience can emerge from acknowledging that the people experiencing your story have their own needs, limitations, and personalities, too.

That’s the fundamental difference between this extraordinary simulator and a fantastic film. At its best, cinema invites you to inhabit a vision so singular it could only belong to someone else. A great video game gives you the tools to make someone else’s vision singularly your own. That takes a degree of commitment that doesn’t always accommodate the realities of being a person, like, oh say, racing to the bathroom with your digestive system in active, contaminant-born revolt.

Even so, when I think about the hours I spent with “GTA VI” this weekend, I’m not particularly interested in how much other “content” I could have been watching instead. No filmmaker could have anticipated the precise emotional satisfaction I’d get from that magenta-hued highway catastrophe, and, purely from a practical standpoint, no self-respecting artist should have to.

Rockstar built Vice City over the span of a decade, but that spectacularly stupid scene in all its accidental skull-smashing glory was mine — proof there’s a world of difference between commanding someone’s attention and handing them the keys.
