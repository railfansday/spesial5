Quantum physics can prove where you are

Davide Bacco is in the Department of Physics and Astronomy, University of Florence, Florence 50019, Italy.

Where you are can matter as much as who you are. Banks, power grids and satellite-navigation systems all depend on knowing where a request or a signal comes from, yet claims about location are notoriously easy to fake. Spoofing and jamming attacks on satellite navigation, for example, are on the rise. Writing in Nature, Kavuri et al.1 report an experiment in which the position of a distant party is certified using the peculiar correlations between quantum particles, known as entanglement. Remarkably, the scheme’s security does not rely on trust of any quantum hardware, only on measurement statistics and the fact that nothing travels faster than light.

The simplest way to confirm a device’s location is through timing (Fig. 1a). Two verifiers, stationed some distance apart, each send a random ‘challenge’ — a single bit of information. This process is timed so that both bits arrive at the claimed location simultaneously. The party being checked, called the prover, must combine the two bits through an agreed mathematical operation and send back the answer. Because signals cannot outrun light, a prompt reply shows that the prover is near the expected spot.

Figure 1 | Position verification. a, To check the location of a party called the prover, two verifiers each send a random bit (a challenge), which are timed to reach the location simultaneously. The prover combines the bits and returns the answer. Because no signal travels faster than light, a prompt reply shows that the prover is at the location. b, However, if two adversaries, one near each verifier, copy a challenge and pass it to each other, they can answer in time even though nobody is at the claimed location. c, In Kavuri and colleagues’ scheme1, one verifier generates two entangled photons, keeping one and sending the other to the prover. Both verifiers then transmit classical challenges. The prover uses the challenges to determine how to measure its photon and returns the result, while the first verifier measures the other photon. If the prover’s result arrives in time and the two measurements pass a statistical check (a Bell test), the transmitted photon must have been measured at the claimed location.

Unfortunately, that scheme is easily fooled (Fig. 1b). Two colluding adversaries, positioned near each verifier, can simply intercept the incoming challenges, copy them and then share them. Each adversary can then reply correctly in time. In fact, it has been proved that no purely classical protocol can securely certify the location of a remote party2.

Quantum physics offers a solution to this problem. Unlike ordinary bits, quantum states cannot be copied, so the cheaters’ trick fails if the prover’s task involves quantum particles. Quantum position-verification protocols were first proposed more than a decade ago3,4 and experiments have since begun to implement them5,6. But their deployment across real-world infrastructure introduces a practical bottleneck. Conventional quantum schemes assume that any hardware is fully trusted and perfectly characterized. In practice, however, physical implementation flaws or unverified components can generate weaknesses that attackers can exploit7.

Kavuri and colleagues sidestepped this problem by using an experiment known as a Bell test. This is an experimental procedure that determines whether correlations between pairs of entangled particles are stronger than any prearranged strategy could produce.

In the authors’ protocol, one verifier generates a pair of entangled photons, keeping one particle and sending the other to the prover (Fig. 1c). Both verifiers then transmit synchronized classical challenges. When both challenges are received, the prover combines them to determine how to measure its received photon and immediately returns the result of that measurement. Simultaneously, the verifier holding the other half of the photon pair measures its own particle to complete the Bell test.

If the prover’s result arrives inside the time window imposed by the speed of light and the two measurements exhibit genuine Bell correlations, the prover must be inside the target region. Distant colluders cannot cheat: they cannot access both challenges in time, nor can they clone the single transmitted photon to share the task. Furthermore, because the Bell test itself certifies the correlations, no trust is needed in the photon sources or detectors — the scheme is device independent.

Kavuri and colleagues demonstrated their protocol at the National Institute of Standards and Technology in Boulder, Colorado, with the two verifiers 195 metres apart. Each position check used about two minutes of data — 30 million rounds of the protocol — and was designed so that a cheater would pass with a probability below 1 in 1019. These position checks are the first to have device-independent security and the certified region was 4.5 times smaller in volume than could be guaranteed by any classical scheme with the same signalling delays. Moreover, unlike classical schemes, this protocol strictly excludes the verifiers’ own locations from the target zone, which means that it would be possible to shut down spoofing attacks made by colluders stationed right next to them.

The most compelling feature of this work is that it solves a problem that is provably beyond the reach of classical physics by merging two landmark achievements: loophole-free Bell tests8,9 and device-independent cryptography.

The authors’ statistical security analysis is also commendable for its rigour: security parameters were fixed before data was collected; the data set remained blinded until processing was finalized; and no events were discarded. Moreover, because entangled photons can be distributed in advance of the challenge signals, transmission losses do not compromise security and can be tolerated if the successful arrival of each photon is confirmed by an additional signal. This makes the protocol well suited to future quantum networks.

Important caveats remain. Crucially, no quantum scheme can withstand adversaries that share unlimited entanglement: given enough quantum resources, impostors can always spoof the prover’s location. Entanglement is thus a double-edged sword — protecting the honest user but potentially arming the cheaters.

As a result, security depends entirely on how much entanglement the attackers have, and the bar is currently low. Demonstrations such as this one are secure only if adversaries share almost no entanglement — just two weakly entangled pairs would suffice to break it. Using longer challenges, made of a larger number of random bits, strengthens security, but only gradually. Whether a wide gap can be opened between honest and dishonest resources remains an open question10.

Practical hurdles also remain. Currently, hardware latency limits the size of the certifiable region to roughly that of a large building, with each verification taking a few minutes. However, the authors argue that upgrading to faster components could shrink the target area to a single room and slash the verification time to roughly a second.

Kavuri and colleagues show that location can be anchored directly in fundamental physical laws rather than in trusted hardware or pre-shared keys. Alongside established technologies such as quantum key distribution and quantum random-number generation, position verification could become a cornerstone application for emerging quantum networks. It could enable a bank, for instance, to guarantee that a high-value transaction genuinely originates from inside one of its secure branches.
