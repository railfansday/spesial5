The Nintendo 64 was era-defining for kart racers. Mario Kart 64, like Mario 64 before it, helped to ease the world-famous plumber into the 3D era, and it was rivaled heavily, maybe even surpassed, by Diddy Kong Racing. I even remember having a great time with Mickey's Speedway USA, Lego Racers, and Micro Machines.

PlayStation, of course, tried to get in on the action with Crash Team Racing, but really, only one series has stood the test of time: Mario Kart. It's one of the best-selling franchises in the history of gaming, and Mario Kart 8 Deluxe is Nintendo's second-best-selling game of all time.

As more and more games begin to make their way to browsers, unfortunately, with the help of Claude and vibecoding, one player has taken things back to Mario Kart's first 3D outing, creating a web browser version of Mario Kart 64, designed exclusively for online racing.

Mario Kart 64 Online is an online-only version of Mario Kart 64, playable completely through your web browser. It was designed by Oliver Sauter and is based on the SpaghettiKart engine decompilation.

It features all 16 tracks from the main game, including fan favorites like Bowser's Castle, Yoshi Valley, and Kalimari Desert, alongside all four Battle Mode courses.

It's very well streamlined, with a new race starting every 30 seconds, and it works with a mouse, keyboard, and controllers. It has a leaderboard and even features camera and microphone support. It's not likely to be around for long, though.

Nintendo is on the warpath again, targeting fan-ported PC titles. Just yesterday, we reported on how the company had DMCA'd four popular fan ports, including Skyward Sword, Super Mario Galaxy, Mario Tennis Fever, and Mario Kart: Double Dash. Because this Mario Kart 64 port distributes an unlicensed copy of the game, it's a prime target for the litigious company.

Vibecoded multiplayer ports are all the rage within gaming at the moment. While they rely heavily on AI for creation, titles like Need for Speed: Underground 2, Halo: Combat Evolved, and SoulCalibur 2 are currently all playable in your browser. It may not be the case for much longer, though.

Claude has now been programmed to make things a lot harder for players to slopcode these games, and it has also been told to ignore any prompt containing the word "Pokemon". Clearly, as they should be, these AI companies are scared of game developers, and hopefully we soon move back to the older method of decompilation, with players coming together to decode binary 0s and 1s.
