Zelda Producer Eiji Aonuma Talks About The Benefits Of "Long-Term-Tenured Staff" At Nintendo

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

"It contributes to some of the sense of continuity"

One morsel from Nintendo's latest Ask the Developer interview revealed how the team behind the Zelda: Ocarina of Time remake got the artist "who had created illustrations for the N64 version" to draw a new map for the Switch 2 release.

It's not every day developers reviving older games can directly consult original team members, but this is just one of many examples at Nintendo. Zelda producer, Eiji Aonuma, has now elaborated on the benefits of Nintendo's "long-term tenured staff members", explaining how it provides "balance" and the "sense of continuity" when crafting projects like the remake.

Here's what he had to say during a chat with GameSpot:

Eiji Aonuma: "I think you see a real benefit to having some very long-term-tenured staff members around who have shared the same experiences over time because every time you get to the next step, it’s easier to discuss how to proceed, or if you’re going to do something new. When you have new staff coming in, of course you want to make sure that you’re bringing everyone up with a lot of discussion, explaining how to do things, why you’re doing things certain ways, as well as the language that you’re using, but that takes some more time as well. So you need a bit of a balance. You don’t want your new staff doing things by rote or without understanding the reasons, you have to invest there. And so you do see the Zelda team change over time, but there is a continuity of core staff, certainly at least from one project to the next, so that we have that base that we can build from and bring up new staff within it. And this is something that I think Nintendo has done for a very long time, even since Miyamoto originally worked on projects like this. And it contributes to some of the sense of continuity that you do feel in games in the Zelda franchise, for example."

As the source notes, Nintendo's "unique culture" of retaining employees "for years" is often quite the contrast to other companies and studios that arguably don't show long-serving employees enough respect, and may not always consider their extensive experience and history as a positive influence moving forward.

In some cases, the dismissal of veteran staff at certain game companies has resulted in younger and less experienced teams navigating the development of remakes, remasters and sequels without any assistance or advice, as there are no long-term staff members left at the company to consult (or direct contact lines). So it's great that Nintendo seems to have found the right balance.
