The High-Rise Bottleneck: Why EV Charging in Indian Apartments Is an Engineering Puzzle

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

Most Western clean-energy reporting takes a very specific picture of home charging for granted. You pull into a private driveway, plug into an attached garage wall-box, and pull cheap power while you sleep.

That reality does not exist for most urban drivers in India.

Data from Kazam and the Alliance for an Energy Efficient Economy (AEEE) shows that roughly 70% to 75% of urban households in major Indian metros live in multi-family apartments and cooperative housing societies. Parking means an open ground level slot or a space two levels down in a concrete basement. When someone here buys an electric car, their biggest hurdle isn’t battery degradation or highway chargers. It’s their own building.

As an electrical engineer in India, I watch this play out every week. Someone buys an EV, assumes they can hire a local wireman to pull power from their flat’s meter to their parking bay, and runs straight into transformer limits, cable physics, and nervous managing committees.

Here is what is actually going on beneath the floorboards.

Indian housing societies buy electricity under a bulk “sanctioned load” from their local utility (DISCOM). The utility sizes the local transformer using an electrical diversity factor the simple assumption that not all 100 flats will run their air conditioners, geysers, and induction stoves at the exact same moment.

A domestic geyser pulls 2 kW for ten minutes and shuts off. A 7.4 kW AC wall-box pulls 32 amps continuously for six to eight hours. When several residents plug in around 8:00 PM, that heavy, unbroken draw lands on the transformer right as domestic air conditioning and kitchen loads hit their evening peak.

Here is where the grid cracks: just five or six cars charging at 7.4 kW pull 37 kW to 44 kW. That wipes out the 30 kW buffer completely. If eight cars plug in at once (59.2 kW), the transformer goes straight into thermal overload.

When that happens, the main incomer breaker trips plunging common areas and elevators into darkness or the utility slaps the society with massive Maximum Demand penalties on their next power bill. Getting a utility to upgrade a transformer or approve higher sanctioned load requires significant capital, transformer yard space, and months of red tape.

In a detached house, wiring an EV charger means running 5 or 10 metres of cable from your distribution board to the garage.

The courts are starting to step in. In Amit Dholakia v. The State of Maharashtra (2025), the Bombay High Court pushed state authorities to issue clear guidelines preventing arbitrary NOC rejections. More recently, in early 2026, the Supreme Court of India issued notice on a Public Interest Litigation challenging arbitrary charger bans across states. While the legal momentum is clearly moving toward protecting a resident’s right to charge, taking a court ruling to a heated Sunday morning society meeting rarely makes for a quick installation.

Treating an electric car like an extra microwave where everyone pulls their own line from their domestic meter does not scale. Societies have to build shared electrical infrastructure.

India’s EV transition is moving fast on two and three wheels. But passenger electric cars won’t reach mass adoption until high-rises solve the overnight charging puzzle.

The vehicles are ready, and buyers want them. But until building codes, dynamic load balancing, and society committees catch up with automotive engineering, the hardest part of driving electric in India will simply be charging at home.
