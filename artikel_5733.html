Medicare for All Has Capitol Hill Abuzz, but It's Unlikely to Pass Soon, Experts Say

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

— Instead, gradual additions like lowering the eligibility age are seen as a better bet

The idea of expanding Medicare to cover the entire population -- otherwise known as "Medicare for All" (MFA) or "single-payer" -- has been a popular catch phrase among the incoming Democratic members of Congress.

"In the richest, most powerful country in the world, you should not have to suffer for your healthcare," Sen.-elect Abdul El-Sayed, MD, DPhil (D-Mich.), an MFA supporter, told a roomful of nurses last month, The Guardian reported.

"Medicare for All is at the top of my list," Rep.-elect Peggy Flanagan (D-Minn.) told the Minnesota Women's Press last month.

But is that a realistic possibility, especially when Democrats -- although they gained large numbers of seats in the midterms -- don't have enough votes for a veto-proof Senate majority? Probably not at this point, Katie Keith, JD, MPH, director of the Center for Health Policy and the Law at Georgetown University, in Washington, said in a phone interview. "There is a lot of talk ... that there's a need to improve traditional Medicare before you get to open it up and transition to a single-payer system," she said.

This is best illustrated by Sen. Bernie Sanders (I-Vt.) -- a long-time champion of single-payer -- who recently came out with a plan to get to MFA, but incrementally. "It doesn't seem to be an abandonment of MFA itself but that's what Sanders came out with -- lowering the Medicare eligibility age; focusing on [adding] dental, vision, and hearing coverage; and covering kids," Keith said. "These are all significant changes."

An incremental approach also appeared in a memo sent in September to House Majority Leader Hakeem Jeffries (D-N.Y.) -- widely expected to be elected Speaker of the House in the incoming Congress. The memo from the Democrats' Cost-of-Living Healthcare Working Group called for capping Medicare beneficiaries' annual out-of-pocket costs at $5,000 as well as adding dental, vision, and hearing coverage -- all part of the group's "First 100 Hours" plan. Then, during the first year in office, the proposal calls for Democrats to "commit to achieving universal coverage" through "comprehensive examination of various single-payer and public option models."

But aspirations alone won't determine the fate of incoming Democrats' legislation, Keith added. "Who knows what committees these new members will be on? Sure they'll be committed to their campaign pledges, but where will they each be in the Senate or the House to push on policy positions of their choice? If El-Sayed is on the [Health, Education, Labor, and Pensions Committee], that gives him a platform, but he might have other priorities. On the House side, there will be a change in leadership at the Energy and Commerce Health Subcommittee -- what new members will be joining that? It will be interesting to see who lands where and what their priorities are."

Mark Weller, JD, shareholder at the Polsinelli law firm in Washington, doubts that MFA will make it across the finish line any time soon. "The most ambitious healthcare proposals have little chance of becoming law," he said in an email. "In the Senate, Democrats will have 56 [votes], but it's not 60, and it's certainly not the 67 needed to override a veto. President Trump still has the veto pen, and Republicans can still block things in the Senate."

In addition, "there are competing factions in the Democratic caucus," he said. "The challenge is for Democrats to deliver on the affordability mandate. The alternative is spending the next 2 years explaining why divided government prevented them from doing so. If voters feel the economy is not working for them, hearings on Medicare for All aren't going to make their life better. The trick will be to find some opportunities for bipartisan progress."

MFA also has plenty of influential detractors inside and outside of Congress. "MFA is grabbing lots of attention while keeping candidates from discussing much more important issues like our $40 trillion public debt, how to reduce our $2 trillion-per-year federal budget deficits, solvency of Social Security and Medicare, child and elderly poverty, and climate change," Wendell Primus, PhD, visiting fellow at the Brookings Institution's Center on Health Policy, wrote prior to Election Day in an unpublished opinion article, which he shared with MedPage Today. "Let me explain why it should never be enacted."

First of all, compared to the Affordable Care Act (ACA), "MFA is an even more mammoth undertaking because the 10-year cost of $32 trillion is impossible to finance," wrote Primus, who, as the senior health adviser to former House Speaker Nancy Pelosi (D-Calif.), was the lead House staffer for getting the ACA passed in 2010. "While the health plan is generous and would provide more coverage than most health plans today, there will be many employers and individual voters who would consider themselves losers when they compare their tax bill to their out-of-pocket health costs. Those who lose because of MFA will stand in the way of enactment."

"In addition, many key stakeholders will balk at the initiatives to lower healthcare costs that will necessarily result," he added. "Specifically, it will be extremely difficult to get hospitals, doctors, insurance companies, and all other care providers to be convinced that MFA would be better than the status quo. And the fear of the unknown will also be a powerful force against enactment."
