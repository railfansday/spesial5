Setting the Record Straight On Senator Cornyn And The N.D. Tex. Seat (Updated)

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

At the outset of President Trump's second term, there were a number of vacant district court seats throughout Texas. A reasonable person might have predicted that given two Republican senators, those seats would be filled quickly, but no such luck. The first nomination--Nicholas Ganjei for S.D.Tex--was not made until November 2025. Yes, while Missouri quickly filled all the seats, Texas lagged behind. Two more nominations in the Western District were made in January 2026--Andrew Davis for the Austin seat and Chis Wolfe for the Waco seat. It's worth noting that both Ganjei and Davis served as Chief Counsel for Senator Cruz. And the story of the Waco seat is enough for another post. In April 2026, two more nominations were made for S.D. Tex. seats in the Valley: John Marck and Arthur Jones.

Up to this point, none of the vacancies in the Northern District of Texas were tapped. Finally, in May 2026, Kasdin Mitchell was nominated to one of the seats in N.D. Tex. At that time, Angela Colmenero was tapped for another seat in Houston. Four more months would lapse. In September, Richard Bennett was nominated for a seat in Houston and Courtney Coker was nominated for a seat in Dallas.

But there were still two open seats. The critical vacancy was the Western District of Texas seat in Austin, which had remained open for some time. Virtually all of the litigation against the state government is filed in Austin. And, for reasons that continue to elude me, David Ezra, a senior judge from Hawaii, brings the Aloha Spirit to muck up Texas jurisprudence.

Shortly after the midterm election, President Trump nominated Justice James Sullivan of the Texas Supreme Court for the Austin seat. Around the same time, Trump nominated Elliot Gaiser for the remaining N.D. Tex. in Dallas. Ultimately, Senator Cornyn would block Gaiser's nomination, and urged the White House to nominate Steve Fahey. That's where thing stand now.

Many people, even those in Texas, cannot make sense of this situation. Why on earth did it take so long to fill all of these seats? Why did nominations for the final two seats only come after the election? Why did Trump pick Gaiser if Cornyn had objections?

Let me try to set the record straight on a few matters here.

Traditionally, Texas District court nominees have gone through something called the Federal Judicial Evaluation Committee (FJEC). Senators Cornyn and Cruz have used this structure to allow local attorneys (now about 30) to make recommendations on potential judicial candidates. The committee will screen applications, conduct interview, and recommend finalists to the Senators. Generally, if both senators sign off on a finalist, they recommend the name to the White House. At that point, the White House will conduct an interview, and if things work out, a nomination is made. I hedge slightly, because sometimes the process goes in a different order. It can happen that the White House really likes a particular candidate, and speaks to them in parallel, or even before the FJEC process goes through. But FJEC remains a central cog in any district court nomination.

For more than a year, there has been something of a standoff concerning the N.D. Tex. seat. There were two relevant finalists from FJEC. The first was Michael Berry, a Marine Veteran, longtime attorney with the First Liberty Institute, and--perhaps most importantly--former Chief Counsel to Senator Cruz. The second was Steve Fahey, a lawyer I did not know. He was an assistant United States Attorney until 2021, and now has a solo private practice. I'm told one of his most significant case was seeking a writ of coram nobis to protect an illegal alien from deportation. (The petition was denied.)

Senator Cornyn blocked Berry. Or to be more precise, Cornyn's Chief Counsel, Franci Becker, blocked Berry. The exact reason is not entirely clear. I've heard some rumors that Becker blames Berry for blocking another district court nominee, but that never happened. Senator Cruz supported that nominee. The White House found problems in that nom's record that would have made confirmation impossible. Ultimately, she withdrew from the search, and took a lucrative in-house position. Another explanation is that Becker, and Cornyn, opposed Berry because he was too conservative. Whatever the reasons, Berry had deep ties to Texas. The White House had interviewed Berry, and was prepared to make the nomination, but Cornyn kept saying no. The Washington Examiner reports on this story.

What about Fahey? I'm told that Cruz never actually blocked Fahey, but the White House had reservations about the nominee--especially his work defending illegal aliens. Ultimately, the decision was made to just let the seat stay open and it would be filled once Cornyn is out of office. The Austin seat was vital, as it involves so much state-wide litigation, but the Dallas division seats are interchangeable. This is not Amarillo or Wichita Falls. Indeed, even if Paxton were to lose the election, the Senate was likely to stay in Republican control, and a deal could be made with Senator Talarico for the Dallas seat. Alas, the election day bloodbath changed things.

The Sullivan nomination for the Austin seat was made quite quickly, even though it had been in the works for a while. Sullivan had already gone through the background check, as well as the FJEC approval. But what about Elliot Gaiser? It is true that Gaiser did not go through FJEC, but he had something very important: a recent FBI background check and Senate confirmation. He could (in theory) be slotted into the final confirmation hearing planned for November 18.

Despite my best efforts, Senator Cornyn blocked Gaiser's nomination. Cornyn (or at least someone on his staff) put out this social media post to explain his actions:

If President Trump wanted to fill a judicial vacancy in Dallas, why did he wait until after the midterms to nominate someone? The seat has been vacant since 2025. Then, without any consultation, he nominates a non-Texan with little connection to the Lone Star State, with no trial court experience, and who has not been appropriately vetted for a lifetime job. The Senate has a constitutional responsibility to provide "advise and consent," which is impossible without consultation.

Still, I would like to work with the President to fill the seat while we still have a Senate majority. I have forwarded an outstanding candidate who was highly rated by Senator Cruz's and my bipartisan Federal Judicial Evaluation Committee, and who has now been interviewed by White House Counsel. All that is needed is an FBI background check with they stand ready to do on a timely basis.

Back to you, Mr. President. Time is of the essence.

This post is misleading. Why did Trump wait until after the midterms to nominate someone? The short answer is that Cornyn blocked the White House's and Cruz's preferred candidate, and the White House did not want to pick Cornyn's candidate. That's how things work. Senators can withhold consent by not returning a blue slip. But they can't force the White House to make a nomination they don't wish to make. The White House then tried a list-ditch effort to get someone into the seat before the new Congress concluded. I'm sure the administration was hoping that Senator Cornyn would realize that a filled seat is better than an empty seat, but he refused, and blocked Gaiser. Moreover, Cornyn refused to even meet with Gaiser, so he is no position to complain about a "lack of consultation." FJEC could have performed a review of Gaiser on short notice, but it was never convened.

Cornyn says Fahey was "was highly rated by Senator Cruz's and my bipartisan Federal Judicial Evaluation Committee." But that does not mean Senator Cruz himself approved of Fahey. Indeed, Senator Cruz can still withhold a blue slip of Fahey. That comeuppance would be just, after Cornyn blocked both Berry and Gaiser unreasonably. Cornyn has earned no favors at this point--especially after cursing at Cruz on the Senate floor and calling him a "fragile thing" on social media. After the events of the past few weeks, I think James Talarico would be a more trustworthy partner on nominees than Cornyn. Remember, Cornyn aided and abetted the spiking of Lee Rudofsky's nomination to the Eighth Circuit. Soon enough, Judge Ada Brown's NDTX seat will become open--either through resignation or impeachment. I can see a deal being made with Cruz gets one of the Dallas seats and Talarico gets the other. All Cornyn could do that point is grouse.

The bigger problem is that there is no time left for a hearing. The November 18 hearing was the end of the show. Cornyn's stunt is now putting pressure on Senator Grassley to break even more China by holding a December nomination hearing. There is no reason that Grassley should give Cornyn this courtesy--especially after the recent stunts Cornyn has pulled.

This post is based on my best understanding of some complex deliberations over the course of nearly two years. If you spot any errors, please send me an email.

Update: Tuesday evening, the press secretary from Senator Cornyn's post reached out and said "It's inaccurate to say that Franci Becker is responsible for blocking Mike Berry's nomination." She asked me to post a statement, which I will do:

Attributable to a Cornyn spokesperson, Franci Becker did not block Mike Berry's nomination, as staff in Senate offices do not have the authority to make decisions about a nominee, and the claims in this story were not corroborated by Sen. Cornyn's office.

Of course, the ultimate decision to sign a blue slip belongs to the Senator. In any office, a staffer cannot take a final action. So in a hyper-technical sense, no staffer is capable of blocking any nominee. But Chief Counsels routinely make decisions about candidates that their senators defer to.

What the statement does not say is far more important than what it actually says. There is no denial along the lines of, "Franci Becker played no role in Mike Berry not receiving a blue slip." I also posed a few other questions about Becker's involvement with the Dallas seat, that the press secretary did not answer.

I stand by what I wrote, which is something I have heard from multiple sources in the Senate over the course of nearly a year. Staffers from other Senator's offices thanked me for this post. Two federal judges who are familiar with the situation thanked me for writing this. People I trust and who are familiar with the situation reviewed it and found no errors.
