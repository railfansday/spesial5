New FAA Rule For Planes Lining Up On Runways May Cause Delays

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) has just added some new restrictions to how air traffic controllers can clear aircraft to “line up and wait” on the runway, in anticipation of takeoff clearance. This is intended to add an extra safety buffer, but to quote an airline pilot on an ATC frequency, “that’s probably going to cause some delays, that’s for sure.”

As of today (November 24, 2026), the FAA has introduced a new rule regarding how aircraft can be cleared onto runways at airports. Air traffic controllers want to utilize runways as efficiently as possible for both takeoffs and landings, and that often involves having planes line up on the runway and wait, in anticipation of takeoff clearance.

To give an example, sometimes a plane will be cleared for takeoff, and then a bit of separation is required until the next plane can depart. In those situations, it’s common for the air traffic controller to clear that next plane to “line up and wait.”

The idea is that the plane then has clearance to enter the runway, and then await further takeoff clearance. After all, it might take 30 seconds or so for a plane to taxi from the hold short point of the runway, to getting into position on the runway.

Under the new FAA rules, air traffic controllers will only be allowed to clear pilots to “line up and wait” in situations where there’s a dedicated supervisor or controller-in-charge who can’t perform other duties while these operations are underway. In the past this was a recommendation, but now this is a rule.

Given constant air traffic control staffing shortages, this could actually impact operations a good percentage of the time. Here’s how the reason for this policy change is described:

The core purpose of this change is to elevate the safety standard for Line Up and Wait (LUAW) operations by removing ambiguity in staffing requirements. By upgrading the guidance from “should” (a recommendation) to “must” (a mandatory requirement), the FAA ensures that the personnel providing watch supervision (OS/CIC) are fully dedicated to overseeing the safety of the operation without the distraction of simultaneously managing other operational tasks.

I can appreciate the concept behind this, which is to improve safety. After all, we have air traffic controllers who are spread too thin, and all too often, they get a bit distracted due to the volume of things they have to deal with. That has definitely been a contributing factor to many accidents and incidents over the years.

I do find the timing of this being introduced to be interesting, as it comes during one of the busiest travel periods of the year. I don’t think this will actually lead to any major issues, and for that matter, safety shouldn’t be compromised just because flights are busy.

If you’re an observant passenger who is always looking out the window and is on Flightradar24, you may notice this policy in practice, for situations where you feel like the plane should already be lining up on the runway, but isn’t.

I can’t imagine this policy change will lead to any major delays on a widespread basis, but then again, given how at capacity many airports are, even a little change can make a big difference. Given how short staffed air traffic control often is, this could have some implications.

I don’t think this will meaningfully slow things down on a national level, but for airports that make the most of every second to get planes in and out, it could definitely have implications.
