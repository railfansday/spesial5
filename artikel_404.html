Which MacBook to Buy (2026): Here’s Which Laptop Is Right for You

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

MacBooks went to some adventurous new places this year. The MacBook Neo pushes the boundaries of how cheap and low-powered a MacBook could go, while the OLED MacBook Pro takes it in the opposite direction. While the midyear price hike put a damper on all the new releases, prices won’t be going down anytime soon.

Having tested every new MacBook you can buy, I can guide you to the right laptop for your budget and performance needs.

For more recommendations, check out the rest of our computer buying guides, like Best Laptops, Best Mac Desktops, Best Cheap Laptops, Best iPads, and Best MacBook Accessories.

Updated November 2026: I’ve added notes from my testing of the M6 MacBook Pro and OLED MacBook Pros, along with additional buying advice given the new prices and rumors about future updates.

Although the MacBook Neo is more affordable, and the M5 MacBook Pro offers a better display with the far more powerful M5 Pro and Max chips, the MacBook Air remains the model I recommend for most people. The 2026 M5 refresh isn’t particularly exciting, but if your livelihood depends on sitting in front of a MacBook all day, it’s the best option.

The starting price for the 13-inch model is now $1,299, up $200 from before. Notably, for much of last year, you could buy this laptop for as little as $750. That’s a painful reality, but it doesn’t change my recommendation that this is the MacBook model most people should buy.

Although the exterior of the MacBook Air hasn’t changed since 2021, Apple has slowly evolved the laptop into something that feels airy and near-weightless. Most users need not worry about having too many tabs or applications open, eating up all that memory. You won’t be sitting around waiting for a file to download, because the SSD is blazing fast. With support for up to two 5K external monitors, the MacBook Air can function as a proper workstation when paired with a docking station. The 12-megapixel, 1080p webcam is plenty good for video calls.

What matters most is that the M5 can do far more than most people will ever ask of their MacBook Air. Apple has boosted the GPU so much over the past generations that the M5 is actually on par with the M3 Pro. You’ll probably spend most of your day in browser tabs, Slack, Zoom, office applications, and spreadsheets, but the MacBook Air can just as easily handle Photoshop or Lightroom. You can even dabble in video editing or some light gaming if you want. If you were a professional video editor, you’d likely be bothered by the render times. But if it’s more of a hobby or side project, the M5 MacBook Air likely won’t impede your flow. Again, that’s where the MacBook Air really lives up to its do-it-all reputation.

As always, there are two sizes, measured diagonally: 13.6-inch and 15.3-inch. Aside from the $200 price gap, the screen size and speakers are the only notable differences between the two. Apple even scales up the pixel density to make sure the display on the 15-inch model looks just as sharp. The speakers on the 15-inch model sound incredible, second only to the 16-inch MacBook Pro. But choosing between the two sizes really comes down to how you use your laptop. If you have it plugged into an external monitor most days, you may as well get the 13-inch model and save yourself the extra space when you need to carry it around. But if a laptop is your primary machine, having that 15.3-inch screen really makes a difference, especially for more complicated applications or for using Split View.

As you may notice, the M5 MacBook Air now starts with 512 GB of storage. That’s twice what previous models packed and helps to rationalize the price gap between the Neo and the Air. Having 512 GB of storage means you won’t have to worry about filling up your drive right away. This kind of headroom allows the technology to fade into the background—a design philosophy Apple has always championed. If you are a power user, consider adding more memory. Remember, MacBook memory and internal storage aren’t expandable down the road.

After years of rumors, Apple has launched a new MacBook Pro with never-before-seen features—most notably, the tandem PROLED XDR touchscreen. That’s right: this MacBook Pro is the first to ever have a touch-capable display, and you can now interact with it directly with your finger. Many people will be drawn in by the novelty of the feature (even though it’s been available on Windows laptops for almost a decade now), but I am skeptical about how useful it will be for day-to-day tasks. You will still primarily use the keyboard and trackpad, but I can see how the touchscreen would help in certain situations. Apple has made some optimizations to make the interface more touch-friendly, though I think there’s still room for improvement.

But don’t get me wrong: This MacBook Pro’s screen is the real deal. I’ve never seen a screen quite like this. Despite OLED’s reputation for not being bright enough, Apple’s is different. It delivers the insane contrast and clarity you’d expect from OLED, but with a maximum brightness that’s hundreds of nits higher than the previous-gen Mini-LED MacBook Pros.

It has a few tricks up its sleeve too, such as the return of the glow-up Apple logo on the lid and the hole-punch camera to replace the notch. As someone who has never gotten used to that ugly notch, that might be the change I was most thrilled to see. Apple even brought Dynamic Island from the iPhone to the MacBook Pro, using that hole creatively while hiding the camera. All in all, there’s a lot to be impressed by here.

The only real concern is that it still uses the older M5 Pro or M5 Max chip. These are still some of Apple’s most powerful chips, but they have been out for over a year. This is the first time Apple isn’t introducing new chips alongside its new MacBook Pro in the past six years. Rumors indicate that the company may be skipping the M6 Pro and M6 Max altogether. That’s made stranger by the fact that Apple is keeping the previous-gen models in the lineup—another uncommon move.

There’s a $400 difference between the 2025 M5 Pro and the 2025 PROLED M5 Pro, and for this pro-level demographic of buyers, it’s a relatively small price to pay for all the upgrades. I appreciate that Apple kept the older models around for those who want the extra performance without the fancy, more expensive display. On the other hand, if Apple wasn’t currently selling them, third-party retailers might have dropped the price more while they were still in stock.

The MacBook Neo (7/10, WIRED Recommends) came out earlier this year, and it had a lot to prove. That’s especially true since it debuted at just $599, an unheard-of price point for Apple laptops—much less than any other model, even before recent price hikes took effect. And even though the MacBook Neo now starts at $699 after those price increases, it remains an aggressively affordable option. To keep pricing low, Apple had to make the following compromises:

That’s a big list, and the memory limitation is the most significant drawback. Unlike on the MacBook Air, you can find the limits of the Neo fairly easily—and not on purpose. If you’re coming from a 16 GB MacBook Air, for example, you likely developed a habit of keeping idle applications or tabs open. On the MacBook Neo, you’ll need to be a bit more aware of what’s open to avoid slowdowns. You’ll also want to know which ports you’re using, since only the rear USB-C port supports an external display. The other USB-C port is only USB 2.0, rated at a max of 480 megabits per second. That’s definitely not the one you want to use for file transfers. If you don’t have the Touch ID model, you may even need to watch who you’re sitting next to in a coffee shop when entering passwords. We haven’t had to worry about these things on laptops for many years.

In exchange, Apple delivers an incredible front-of-screen experience on the MacBook Neo, never before seen on a laptop of this price. The display is sharper and brighter than anything else you can buy for $699, while the keyboard and trackpad are both excellent. Build quality is every bit as strong as any other Apple product, and at 0.50 inches thick, it’s still quite portable.

On top of all that, the Neo has three really good features over the Air. There’s a wider range of color options—and a lot of people will gravitate toward the Neo for that alone. My favorite change is the lack of a camera notch. While the bezels are slightly thicker, I’d still prefer a uniform screen without an ugly notch blocking part of my view. Perhaps most meaningfully, the Neo is reportedly the most repairable MacBook in the lineup, swapping out adhesives for screws on components such as the battery and speakers. This is great for classrooms and businesses hoping to keep fleets of these up to date without contributing as much to the mounting e-waste problem.

For most of the time, you’ll enjoy using the MacBook Neo. As a parent, this should be the go-to device for high school and college students. It also works equally well as a secondary laptop away from your workstation or as a small travel laptop.

The newer PROLED MacBook Pros are flashier, but they use the same M5 Pro or M5 Max chips as the previous-gen models. And interestingly, Apple still sells these older ones. So, while these 2025 models don’t have touchscreen OLED displays, they’re every bit as powerful. You aren’t missing out on any performance, and if the rumors hold, these will be the top-of-the-line MacBook Pro chips for a while.

Of course, these MacBook Pros are still very expensive, though. Like its PROLED sibling, the standard M5 Pro and M5 Max MacBook Pro (8/10, WIRED Recommends) aren’t for the average person. The price alone should clue you in, but if you are the target audience and willing to pay well over $4,000 for a powerful laptop, the M5 Max MacBook Pro is unmatched in its capabilities. As always, the “Max” is primarily about considerably more GPU performance. Along with the option of two extra CPU cores, you can get up to 40 GPU cores, each with a built-in AI accelerator.

The M5 Max’s power applies to three use cases: creative applications, on-device AI processing, and gaming. Whether it’s 3D modeling, video editing, or some other resource-heavy application, the M5 Max MacBook Pro will tear through it. But AI performance also takes a significant leap. I tested a 17-billion-parameter Llama-2 model, which ran at conversational speed locally (the speed an AI model runs on a device), and it was 31 percent faster than running the same model on the M3 Max MacBook Pro.

Then there’s gaming. Nobody is going to buy the MacBook Pro for its gaming prowess alone, but it’s there when you want it. For the first time ever, you can hit over 60 frames per second in Cyberpunk 2077 on the Ultra preset with no upscaling. That’s an RTX 5070 Ti-level of laptop gaming performance. And unlike laptops that actually have that GPU, the MacBook Pro gets incredible battery life. Lastly, creative professionals will appreciate the faster storage speeds than previous generations.

The Mini-LED display is as gorgeous as ever, even if it pales in comparison to the new OLED models. You get a solid selection of ports, including three Thunderbolt 5 ports that offer ample external display support. It’s at the top of its class in nearly every aspect. Beyond CPU/GPU performance, you can choose between two screen sizes that perform similarly.

If this machine is overkill, you can drop down to the M5 Pro, which starts at $2,499 but is a power champ in its own right. Its multi-core and GPU performance are also stronger than the new M6 (see below).

The MacBook Pro is a slight misnomer because there are really three variants. I’ve talked about the PROLED and M5 Pro/M5 Max versions above. The third, the base model, is the new M6 MacBook Pro, and despite using the latest chip, it’s the cheapest of the Pro options. Unfortunately, it’s also the hardest to recommend. That has less to do with the laptop itself and more to do with its price and how it fits into the lineup.

Like its M5 predecessor, it remains a beautiful, well-made laptop with all the high-end features you’d expect from a MacBook Pro. That includes the excellent speakers and very bright Mini-LED display. But it costs $1,999. Keep in mind that the M5 Pro MacBook Pro is $2,499, and you can still buy the M5 model for $1,799 or the 15-inch M5 MacBook Air for $1,499—hence the “awkward middle child” designation.

To be fair, you get a terabyte of storage and 16 GB of RAM, while the M5 MacBook Pro started with 512 GB of storage. Apple also kept the starting price the same—that is, the price after Apple increased pricing across the board (the base MacBook Pro used to start at $1,699), but let’s not go there.

The M6 chip is certainly faster than the M5, but it’s less of a big leap forward than in previous generations. You’re looking at about a 15 percent improvement in CPU performance. I think the bigger disappointment is only a 9 percent improvement in graphics. That’s been an area where previous generational chip leaps have been more significant.

If you are considering the M6 MacBook Pro, I strongly suggest you look at the 15-inch M5 MacBook Air. It starts at a much lower price for the base configuration, but you can bump up the specs and still stay below $2,000. Yes, the MacBook Air is a different machine, but it has the performance that would impress many MacBook Pro users.

Currently, only the 14-inch MacBook Pro gets the latest M6 chip. Rumors suggest Apple will skip the M6 Pro and M6 Max in this generation, saving the chip update for the MacBook Pro until the M7 Pro and M7 Max.

Apple’s processors are scattered across different MacBook models, and you can find older models online from third-party retailers, either new or refurbished. If you do stumble upon older chips, you might be wondering how they compare to newer options. Here, get a breakdown of the differences to consider.

M6: Apple has already released its latest M6 line of chips, though the rollout has been slower than normal. As of this year, it’s only available in the base 14-inch MacBook Pro, iMac, and Mac Mini. I expect to see this arrive on devices like the iPad Pro and MacBook Air eventually, but the rumors about the rest of the M6 lineup are perplexing. Reports indicate Apple will skip the M6 Pro and M6 Max entirely and jump straight to the M7 Pro and M7 Max, perhaps in late 2027. That makes sense given what Apple just launched with the OLED MacBook Pro, which uses the same M5 Pro and M5 Max chips as the previous generation.

M5: Apple launched the M5 chips in late 2025 with the 14-inch MacBook Pro, iPad Pro, and Vision Pro. The base M5 offers up to a 10-core CPU and 10-core GPU, though a lower-tier 9-core CPU is still available on the iPad Pro. The M5 is about 10 to 15 percent faster in CPU performance than the M4 and takes a significant step up in GPU, AI workloads, and even storage speed. The M5 is now also available on the MacBook Air.

M5 Pro: These more powerful M5 chips are currently available in the 14-inch and 16-inch MacBook Pros. The M5 Pro comes with a 15-core CPU and a 16-core GPU, with an option to upgrade to an 18-core CPU and a 20-core GPU. As with previous generations, the Pro chip gets all the same benefits as those in the M5 architecture but adds more cores and improved memory bandwidth. The biggest advantage is in GPU and on-device AI performance. This time around, however, both the M5 Pro and M5 Max also debuted the “Fusion Architecture,” a new version of UltraFusion (previously used only on the Ultra chips) that combines two dies across a superfast interconnect.

M5 Max: The M5 Max is the M5 Pro—only with more GPU power. You can get up to 40 GPU cores, putting the MacBook Pro on par with high-end gaming laptops in graphics performance. With neural accelerators built into each GPU core, you’ve got one of the most powerful AI laptops on the market. It comes in either the MacBook Pro or the Mac Studio.

M5 Ultra: Like the other “Ultra” chips, the M5 Ultra is available only in the 2026 Mac Studio. It’s by far Apple’s most powerful chip, but you can’t configure it in a MacBook.

M4: The M4 launched in 2024. It has a 10-core CPU and a 10-core GPU. Apple claims the M4 delivers 1.8 times faster CPU performance and 2.2 times faster GPU performance than the M1. Meanwhile, the neural engine is more than three times faster than the original and twice as fast as the M3. It also starts with 16 GB of unified memory, which helps to power Apple Intelligence (the company’s suite of artificial intelligence features) more smoothly. It’s available on the 14-inch MacBook Pro (2024), iMac (2024), and MacBook Air (13-inch and 15-inch, 2025).

M4 Pro: The M4 Pro has a 14-core CPU (which Apple claims is up to 1.9 times faster than the M1 Pro) and up to a 20-core GPU, with up to 64 GB of unified memory. Built on a second-generation 3-nanometer process, it also supports enhanced GPU features like mesh shading and ray tracing—the latter of which is now twice as fast as on M3 chips. You’ll find it on the 2024 MacBook Pro (14-inch and 16-inch) and Mac Mini (2024).

M4 Max: This chip has a 16-core CPU and up to a 40-core GPU with support for up to 128 GB of unified memory. Apple says the CPU is up to 2.2 times faster than the M1 Max, while the GPU is up to 1.9 times faster. Like the M4 Pro, it supports mesh shading and ray tracing. The M4 Max is available as an option on the 2024 14-inch and 16-inch MacBook Pro, and you can also get it as an option in the current Mac Studio.

M3: The M3 was available on the 14-inch MacBook Pro (late 2023), 13-inch MacBook Air (2024), 15-inch MacBook Air (2024), and 24-inch iMac (2023). It packs an 8-core CPU and up to a 10-core GPU, with up to 24 GB of unified memory. Compared with the M1, Apple claimed CPU performance was up to 35 percent faster and GPU performance up to 65 percent faster. The company said the CPU and GPU are both 20 percent faster than the M2. As with the M1 and M2, it was great for basic tasks like word processing, sending emails, using spreadsheets, and light gaming. The 13-inch and 15-inch MacBook Air also supported two external displays (one up to 6K at 60 Hz and another up to 5K at 60 Hz).

M3 Pro: With a 12-core CPU and an 18-core GPU, Apple claimed the M3 Pro’s GPU was only up to 10 percent faster than the M2 Pro, making it a marginal upgrade from its predecessor. Compared with the M1 Pro, however, the M2 Pro was up to 40 percent faster in GPU performance and 20 percent faster in CPU performance. It was available on the 14-inch and 16-inch MacBook Pro from 2023. It was the ideal in-between for those who needed a chip more powerful than the M3 but didn’t need the full power of the M3 Max.

M3 Max: The next step up from the M2 Max. It had a 16-core CPU, 40-core GPU, and up to 128 GB of unified memory. According to Apple, the CPU performance was up to 80 percent faster than the M1 Max and up to 50 percent faster than the M2 Max. As for GPU performance, it was said to be up to 50 percent faster than the M1 Max and 20 percent faster than the M2 Max. The M3 Max was available on the 14-inch and 16-inch MacBook Pro (late 2023).

M3 Ultra: Apple introduced the M3 lineup in 2023, but only announced the M3 Ultra in 2025. It was even better than the latest M4 Max and M5 at the time. It had up to 32 CPU cores (24 performance cores) and up to 80 GPU cores. Apple claimed it was up to 2.5 times faster than the M1 Ultra. It also came with 96 GB of unified memory, upgradable to 512 GB, and up to 16 TB of SSD storage. This chip was available only on the 2025 Mac Studio.

M2: The M2 was an entry-level chip like the M1, with slightly more processing power. It packed an 8-core CPU and up to a 10-core GPU (two more GPU cores than its predecessor), and supported up to 24 GB of unified memory. Apple said the second-generation chip has an 18 percent faster CPU and a GPU that’s 35 percent more powerful than its earlier version. The M2 was great for daily tasks like word processing and web browsing, but tasks such as editing multiple streams of 4K footage and 3D rendering should be reserved for Pro or Max chips. It was available in the MacBook Air (13-inch, 2022), MacBook Air (15-inch, 2022), and MacBook Pro (13-inch, 2022).

M2 Pro: The M2 Pro had up to 12 cores in the CPU and up to a 19-core GPU, with up to 32 GB of unified memory. Apple claimed performance was up to 20 percent faster than with the 10-core M1 Pro and graphics were 30 percent faster. We recommended this chip for intermediate video and photo editors. It was a marginal upgrade compared with the M1 Pro, but it was the best option for those who wanted a more future-proof processor. You could find it in the MacBook Pro (14-inch and 16-inch) from early 2023 and the Mac Mini (2023).

M2 Max: The M2 Max packs up to a 12-core CPU and up to a 38-core GPU (with support for up to 96 GB of unified memory). According to Apple, graphics are 30 percent faster than on the M1 Max. The M2 Max is an excellent choice for those who work with graphics-intensive content, including graphic design, 3D modeling, and heavy-duty video footage. But as with the M2 Pro, it’s an incremental upgrade if you’re coming from an M1 Max. It’s available in the MacBook Pro (14-inch and 16-inch) released in early 2023 and the Mac Studio (2023).

M2 Ultra: The successor to the M1 Ultra, the M2 Ultra was available in the second-generation Mac Studio and the Mac Pro (2023). Composed of two M2 Max chips, using Apple’s UltraFusion technology, the M2 Ultra had a 24-core CPU and a GPU configurable with 60 or 76 cores. Apple claimed the CPU delivered up to 20 percent faster performance and that the GPU was up to 30 percent faster than the M1 Ultra. This was the chip to get if you were working with extremely heavy-duty content that you believed the M1 Ultra, M2 Pro, or M2 Max simply couldn’t handle. You’d have known if you needed a chip this robust.

M1: Introduced in 2020, the M1 was Apple's first custom silicon designed for the Mac. The M1 had an 8-core CPU and up to an 8-core GPU. Originally, you could get up to 16 GB of unified memory (RAM) for an extra cost, but now you can only purchase the 8-GB model. It was much faster than any previous Intel-powered MacBook Pro and the practical choice for most people. It packed more than enough processing power for day-to-day tasks and even light gaming. It could also handle more intense jobs, like photo editing. Long after its initial release, Apple continued to sell the M1 MacBook Air through Walmart for just $599, a killer price for this laptop. The MacBook Neo has since replaced it, using the A18 Pro chip from the iPhone 16 Pro models.

M1 Pro: The next step up was the M1 Pro. It had up to 10 CPU cores and up to a 16-core GPU, with up to 32 GB of unified memory. Apple said the performance and graphics were both twice as fast as on the M1. We found it to be considerably more capable than the base chip, making it ideal for anyone who works heavily on MacBooks for music production or photo and video editing. Only the 2021 MacBook Pro (14-inch and 16-inch) used this chip.

M1 Max: Like the M1 Pro, the M1 Max had a 10-core CPU but a heftier 32-core GPU (with support for up to 64 GB of unified memory). Apple said it was four times faster than the M1 for graphics. As testing showed, this chip was extremely powerful and handled every heavy-duty task with ease. It was the go-to choice if you needed a computer that could handle multiple streams of 8K or 4K video footage, 3D rendering, or app development and demos. You probably already knew whether you needed this much power. It was available in the MacBook Pro (14-inch and 16-inch) from 2021.

M1 Ultra: The M1 Ultra was the most powerful of the M1 series. It’s two M1 Max chips connected with a technology called UltraFusion. It packs a 20-core CPU, 64-core GPU (configurable up to 128 GB of unified memory), and a 32-core neural engine—complete with seven times more transistors than the base M1. Even when newer models were available, the M1 Ultra remained powerful and a solid option for anyone who needs a heavy-duty processor for intense visuals and graphics work. It was available only on the first-generation Mac Studio.

Take Advantage of Apple’s Education Discounts

Apple always offers small discounts on hardware for students and teachers. To access the deals, purchase through Apple’s Education Store, choose the product you want (you should see the discounted pricing), and place the order as you normally would. You don’t need to show proof that you’re a student at the time of purchase, but be honest, as Apple can ask for verification later. It’s worth noting that the discounts are not as good as what you’ll find on Amazon and sometimes at other retailers like Walmart, so be sure to check prices.

You have to be very careful when shopping for older MacBooks. Don’t get me wrong: Since the introduction of Apple Silicon in 2020, almost every MacBook Air and MacBook Pro has been a really good laptop. Never buy a MacBook that predates 2020, regardless of how cheap it is. Trust me.

But even buying an older Apple Silicon model requires a discerning eye. Most older generations of MacBooks are sold only refurbished or secondhand, which adds risk. Meanwhile, prices on refurbished models fluctuate frequently, so you’ll need to watch prices closely to get the best deal. Apple’s own refurbished store prices are often higher than buying new models from third-party retailers like Amazon or Best Buy, but you do get some protection as they come with an Apple warranty. However, third-party retailers are always less risky than buying used on a secondhand market like Facebook Marketplace or eBay.

With that in mind, here are the older MacBooks you might see for sale, and whether I’d recommend buying.

MacBook Pro (M5): Apple doesn’t sell this one anymore, but some retailers are selling it at a discount for $1,799. That’s for 16 GB of RAM and a terabyte of storage. Since it’s nearly identical to the newer M6 MacBook Pro, except for a small performance bump, it’s a really good deal right now. I’d still say most people are better off getting a 15-inch MacBook Air because it’s cheaper and thinner, but if you want the better Mini-LED display, the M5 MacBook Pro is a solid choice right now.

MacBook Air (M4): The previous-generation MacBook Air is a fantastic laptop, but it’s no longer being sold new. However, you can now buy it refurbished for under $1,000. This configuration only comes with 256 GB of storage, though, and I don’t think it’s cheap enough to be worth it compared to the M5 model.

MacBook Air (M3): This one isn’t being sold new anymore, and the refurbished models I could find weren’t any cheaper than the M4 MacBook Air. Just stick with the M4.

MacBook Air (M2): With the launch of the MacBook Neo, there’s really no reason to buy an older MacBook Air with only 8 GB of RAM—especially at this price.

MacBook Air (M1): Thanks to the Neo, refurbished M1 MacBook Air models have sunk to pretty dramatic lows. You can currently buy a refurbished M1 for around $400. While the M1 MacBook Air gets you a backlit keyboard, the new MacBook Neo is certainly the more handsome and portable of the two. Still, if you need something fairly limited and are willing to risk buying refurbished, I can see why an Apple Silicon MacBook for less than $400 has its appeal.

MacBook Pro (14-inch, M4): This is no longer being sold new, but you can buy the refurbished model for $1,499. Earlier this year, you could have bought the M5 model for less than this. Sigh. Even still—definitely not worth it.

MacBook Pro (M3 Pro or M3 Max): Given how low the prices have dropped, these refurbished models may be worth considering, particularly for people who want a bit more performance than they’d get from a MacBook Air.

At this point, I do not recommend buying refurbished MacBook Pros from the M2 or M1 generation. I haven’t seen deals strong enough to justify buying something that old.
