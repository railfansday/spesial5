Geologists discovered a unique kind of landslide after a frozen Canadian hillside slid and blocked a valley

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

A permafrost hillside above the Redstone River, and a slope near the Johnson River, slid off their thawing base, creating a new type of landslide.

Above the Redstone River in Canada’s Northwest Territories, a forested permafrost hillside broke away and slid into the valley. Another slope did the same in the Johnson River valley, within a year of it.

Both sit in the central Mackenzie Valley, and the slides came down in 2017 and 2018. The paper puts the ground they moved at about 4.5 million and 18 million cubic meters (5.9 and 23.5 million cubic yards).

Their debris blocked the drainage below. The lakes that formed behind it are about 0.7 and 2.2 miles (1.1 and 3.5 kilometers) long.

The slopes did not thaw at the surface and slump, which is the usual pattern in permafrost country. They let go at the bottom of the frozen ground, 60 to 66 feet (18 to 20 meters) down. The whole frozen layer went downhill in blocks, many with plants still on them.

Geologist Joseph Young of the University of Alberta and seven co-authors named them permafrost detachment slides, and the paper describes such failures as a distinct process. Their simple heat model explains only part of how they start.

The Redstone River slide is in the foothills of the Mackenzie Mountains. The slope runs at about 16 degrees, at 1,640 to 2,300 feet (500 to 700 meters) above sea level. The ground is clay-rich glacial till.

The hillside had been moving for several years on a deep plane. Then it failed. It left a scar about 980 feet by 0.75 mile (300 meters by 1.2 kilometers). The debris tongue is about as long.

The team flew drones over the site in 2019 and again in 2021. Checked against older satellite elevation data, the surveys put the moved ground at about 6.5 million cubic yards (5.0 million cubic meters) by 2021, a little above the 4.5 million cubic meters the paper gives for the failure itself.

One field photo in the study shows a helicopter parked on the debris. Four people stand on a molard, a mound of frozen ground that rode down the slope in one piece.

The Johnson River slide is on flat country by comparison. The local slope is about 7 degrees. The ground is ice-rich lake mud from the retreat of the Laurentide Ice Sheet.

For more than ten years before it broke, Landsat pictures caught it creeping 33 to 98 feet (10 to 30 meters) a year. Ponds on the surface moved downhill from picture to picture. The creep started after a forest fire in 1995 and got faster right up to the collapse.

When it went, the paper puts the moved material at about 18 million cubic meters (23.5 million cubic yards). The scar is some 1,970 feet by 2 miles (600 meters by 3.2 kilometers).

Mudflows kept coming out of the scar afterward, and by 2021 the total stood at about 24.3 million cubic yards (18.6 million cubic meters).

The authors wrote in their conclusions that the two slides are “amongst the largest contemporary permafrost failures observed in northwestern Canada.”

Field crews worked at both sites from 2019 to 2021. At Redstone, the flat surface the slide rode on was still exposed in the upper scar. It lay about 66 feet (20 meters) under the old ground level.

They also ran electrical resistivity lines with an ABEM Terrameter, using electrodes every 16 or 33 feet (5 or 10 meters). Frozen and thawed soil carry current differently. The lines showed about 66 feet (20 meters) of permafrost beside the scar and none at all inside it.

At Johnson River, mud had covered the old sliding surface. The crew found massive ice at the base of the permafrost instead, about 59 feet (18 meters) below the scarp.

Samples, cut with an ice ax or drilled out, went back frozen to the university’s Permafrost Archives Laboratory in Edmonton.

Isotopes in that basal ice point to the early Holocene, the period just after the last ice age. Ice wedges on each side of the Redstone headscarp gave modern values. They had formed recently from water running into open cracks.

So the break was at the base of the permafrost at both sites. To the authors, the frozen layer weakened from below before it let go. They describe it as a process that had not been recognized before.

Big fires burned across both study areas in 1995. In a 2022 paper, the same group counted 156 deep-seated permafrost landslides in the valley that began between 2004 and 2020. More than 150 of them failed about two decades after a fire.

In July 2020 the team set up two temperature boreholes, 4.9 feet (1.5 meters) deep. One is in the old burn and one in unburned forest beside it.

From September 2020 to August 2022, the forest averaged 30.6°F (-0.8°C) near the surface and the burn 32.7°F (0.4°C), according to the ground temperature records.

They then ran a heat model called FreeThaw1D. A fire was simulated as a 2.7°F (1.5°C) jump in surface temperature that faded over 75 years. Ice near the base of the permafrost, about 66 feet (20 meters) down, began to thin only decades later.

That matches the lag seen in the valley. In Siberia, after the fire seasons of 2019 and 2020, scientists warned that Arctic warming will increase permafrost megafires.

Near the Mackenzie River, permafrost is only 66 to 130 feet (20 to 40 meters) thick. It sits above 30.2°F (-1°C), and it warmed by about 0.5°F (0.3°C) per decade from 1980 to 2020.

The authors expect more of these slides where permafrost is that warm and thin, with floods from debris-dammed lakes among the risks.

One of the largest thaw slumps on record, on the Peel Plateau, took six decades to open a scar of 5.4 million cubic yards (4.1 million cubic meters). The Redstone slide moved a similar amount in a rapid failure.

Thaw slumps, where an icy wall melts back a little every summer, are the better-known way that thawing Arctic hillsides collapse.

“Permafrost slopes are now failing in new and unprecedented ways,” the authors wrote in their plain-language summary.

The heat model leaves out background climate warming, local site conditions and water draining down tension cracks. The authors say that water is likely a stronger driver of deep thaw than heat moving slowly through the soil.

Only two slides were studied on the ground. The team calls for more thermal and geotechnical work. The hundreds of similar landslides it mapped have not been checked one by one.

Resistivity data from both sites are in the Canadian Permafrost Electrical Resistivity Survey database. Drone flythrough videos of the two slides are on Zenodo, an open research archive.

Two co-authors work at the Northwest Territories Geological Survey, and PermafrostNET, a Canadian permafrost research network, helped fund the work.

The full study was published in the journal Geophysical Research Letters.
