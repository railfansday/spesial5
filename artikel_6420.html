Vaccinating moms before they give birth saves their babies from dangerous infections

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Offspring whose mothers got a Klebsiella vaccine survived a lethal infection, and the antibodies in her milk lasted six weeks.

Newborns arrive with almost no immune defenses of their own. A bacterium called Klebsiella pneumoniae kills thousands of them every year in low- and middle-income countries. It resists a growing list of antibiotics, and no Klebsiella vaccine exists.

So researchers vaccinated the mothers instead, in mice. Pups from unvaccinated mothers almost all died, whereas 80 percent of those from vaccinated mothers survived.

The protection came from antibodies the mothers passed on through the placenta and their milk. It was still working six weeks after birth, by which point the pups’ antibody levels had fallen low. The work is in mice.

David A. Rosen led the study at WashU Medicine, where he is an associate professor of pediatrics and of molecular microbiology.

He worked with Drew Schwartz, whose lab studies the microbiome in newborn sepsis. Paeton Wantuch, a staff scientist in the Rosen lab was the paper’s first author. Rosen said a vaccine for pregnant women is several steps away.

Sepsis is the body’s extreme reaction to an infection that has reached the blood. In low- and middle-income countries, Klebsiella pneumoniae causes more of it in newborns than any other pathogen.

In one survey of seven such countries, 85 percent of the bacteria collected from sick newborns carried genes that disable common antibiotics.

Antibiotics are failing worldwide, and Klebsiella strains that resist a further class of them now infect about three in every 1,000 babies in neonatal intensive care. Up to a quarter of those infants die.

A 2023 modeling study in PLOS Medicine estimated that a maternal Klebsiella vaccine could prevent 80,000 to 400,000 newborn deaths a year.

The vaccine targets the sugar coat that wraps each Klebsiella cell. Two coat types, called K1 and K2, were each linked to a harmless carrier protein, which teaches the immune system to recognize the sugar.

Omniose, a St. Louis startup, built both vaccines, and four of the paper’s authors hold a financial stake in the company.

Female mice received a first dose on the day they were paired with males. Some got one booster two weeks later, and others got boosters at one week and two weeks. A third group received the carrier protein alone.

By day 21, the two-dose and three-dose mothers had made similar amounts of antibody against the sugar coat. That matters for people, the authors wrote, because follow-up visits in pregnancy are hard to guarantee.

Pups born to vaccinated mothers had the antibody against the sugar coat in their blood from birth, and it was still detectable at six weeks of age.

Antibodies reach a baby before birth through the placenta and afterward through milk, so the team collected milk from nursing mothers about eight days after the pups were born.

The team used a human breast pump modified to fit a mouse. The milk held the same antibody the mothers had in their blood, and none of the antibody type that dominates human breast milk.

The authors flagged that difference as one reason mouse results won’t translate directly.

At two weeks old, each pup was injected with enough Klebsiella to kill it. The K1 strain killed every pup whose mother had received the carrier protein alone. More than 80 percent of the pups whose mothers got the K1 vaccine lived, whether she had two doses or three.

Fewer than 35 percent of control pups survived K2, compared with more than 80 percent of pups from K2-vaccinated mothers. The vaccine made the difference, and the number of doses did not. Groups ran from 11 to 30 pups.

Pups from the same mothers were weaned at three weeks and infected at six weeks, alongside female mice that had never been vaccinated. Every pup from a K1-vaccinated mother survived, compared with 40 to 60 percent of the unvaccinated mice.

For K2, pups of three-dose mothers all survived, while the two-dose group’s 70 percent survival was close enough to the unvaccinated mice’s 30 percent that chance can’t be ruled out.

In an interview with Earth.com, Rosen said the last result was the one he hadn’t expected.

“I was somewhat surprised that the vaccines we tested in our model worked even after the pups were six weeks old,” he said.

“The pups had very low detectable antibodies against Klebsiella by this timepoint, but they were still largely immune to Klebsiella infection.”

The authors listed the gaps themselves. Most newborn sepsis strikes in the first week of life, and these pups were infected at two weeks.

The two strains came from human patients but not from newborns, and K1 in particular is rare in newborn sepsis.

The researchers injected bacteria directly into the abdomen. This skipped the gut, the typical starting point for most real infections.

And every pup was born at full term. Many human cases are premature babies, and the authors wrote that a mother’s antibodies may not reach a preterm infant in time.

Rosen told Earth.com the next steps are choosing the best mix of targets, then testing safety in people who are not pregnant, then in pregnant women.

A large final trial would come last, and he said it would need governments across Africa and Asia and funders such as the Gates Foundation.

He added that the risk of any one pregnant woman’s baby getting sick from Klebsiella is still relatively low. “This means that a phase 3 study would require a very large population to determine if a vaccine really works,” he said.

Rosen said Acinetobacter and E. coli also sicken and kill many babies, and vaccines against them could one day be combined into a single shot given during pregnancy.

For now, the team is testing updated vaccines built to cover more of the drug-resistant Klebsiella strains found around the world.

Whether a mother’s antibodies can protect a baby in its first week, when most of these infections happen, is the question the mouse model hasn’t tested yet.

The full study was published in the journal Science Advances.
