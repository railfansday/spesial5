Power stations (i.e., solar generators) have become increasingly popular because they are easy to run, cost less over time than gas generators, and offer eco-friendly, emission-free operation, especially when paired with a solar panel. Some are small and compact, ideal for off-grid adventures, while larger ones are able to back up critical home circuits during a blackout.

Unlike fuel-based generators, solar generators can’t create electricity in real time. They’re giant battery packs, not true generators, and need to be recharged using alternating-current (AC) power or solar panels, the latter of which often takes many hours in ideally sunny conditions.

Despite those distinctions compared to portable generators, there’s nothing more convenient than a power station for charging or running everything you need for day-to-day life—phones and laptops, lights, and even a small appliance or two. Plus, it’s nice to have a device that doesn’t produce carbon emissions and replenishes itself freely from the sun’s rays. Whether you’re seeking a highly portable option or need one that’s heavy-duty enough for extended use, these are the best solar generators we’ve tested and researched to date.

To pick the best solar generators, we tested some of these power stations for charging capacity, ease of use, weight, and different use cases. Our picks were reviewed by Popular Mechanics editor Brad Ford, who provided input for the larger-capacity units. Other models on this list were reviewed by test editor David Dritsas or freelance writer Maggie Slepian.

Our primary concerns were portability and convenience. Ease of use was also important—was the display easy to read? Did the screen readouts make sense? Bonus points if we could tell how much power is available at a glance. In the past few years, these criteria have become easier to meet. There are now plenty of compact, affordable units that have better charging capabilities than before.

The station offers recharging through AC power (including a 240-volt turbo option), but it’s serious about solar recharging, too. There are two ports for connecting solar panels up to 1,200 watts each, which is uncommon in a station of this size and price point. Plus, even higher input is possible by pairing with a Bluetti SolarX 4K voltage regulator, which is designed for connection to larger solar arrays.

During our tests, we discovered that the Apex 300 uses two inverters as opposed to one, giving you two banks of 120-volt, 1,920-watt output (two outlets in each bank). This enabled us to easily run two high-powered appliances at the same time, including a space heater and a toaster, with no issues at all.

At 84 pounds, it’s quite heavy to lug around, so this station makes more sense as something you keep in the home to provide backup power during an emergency or as something you could potentially build into a larger system later that can tap into solar recharging, too.

Power stations use watts and watt-hours to express size and capacity. Watts (size) refer to how much power it produces. For example, if you need to run a 900-watt microwave, you’ll need a station that outputs at least that much power (ideally more, since factory specs are never exact).

Meanwhile, watt-hours (capacity) give you an idea of how long devices can be powered continuously, though how much time depends on the amount of power being drawn—watt-hours don’t dictate a set time frame. Higher-powered devices will drain the battery a lot faster than lower-powered ones.

That may seem confusing, but a simple equation gives you an approximate idea of how it works: total watt-hours (of the generator) ÷ watts (of the device) = hours the power station runs.

For example, if you had a power station with a 2,048-watt-hour capacity and wanted to run a 750-watt space heater consistently at its highest setting, it would probably run for around 2.5 hours, so long as nothing else is plugged in. But for a refrigerator, whose power draw fluctuates between 10 and 300 watts (depending on how often the compressor turns on), you might be able to run it for nearly 24 hours.

Keep in mind that manufacturer specs are taken from perfect conditions; in the real world, you’ll likely see slightly less capacity from your power station. If you’re not sure how much power the different devices in your home draw, sometimes it’s written on a label, or you can use a device like a wattmeter.

Just as a power station has specifications on output size and capacity, solar panels are rated by how many watts they produce for a device after they convert the sun’s rays into electrical current (e.g., 100 watts, 200 watts, etc.). Because real-world conditions are never perfect, it’s not uncommon for panels to only output 50 to 75 percent (or even lower) of their total potential.

Your panels must also be within the acceptable input range of the power station. For example, connecting a 400-watt solar-panel array to a power station that only accepts 110 watts of solar input can cause an overload and damage the station.

While weight and size don’t directly correlate to the technicalities of power banks, they impact the convenience of each model. Many camping and overlanding setups have limited space, so the more compact models likely have a higher appeal as a result.

That said, most units weigh at least 10 pounds, and the weight and size increase quickly with greater capacities. Before purchasing, consider where you plan to store the power bank and the space in your vehicle or camper where it will live while you’re on the road. If you plan to use this power bank solely as a home backup option, portability will matter less, but consider how much effort it will take to move it around within your home or in your outdoor space.

I am a low-maintenance camper, and most of my background is in lightweight backcountry travel. That means I prioritize space-saving and ease-of-use, so my go-to power banks have between 250 and 600 watt-hours, and weigh less than 15 pounds.

This is perfect for a weekend trip, with the on-the-go (car or solar) charging capabilities to put my mind at ease for longer trips. Mostly, I’m charging small items, so one charge lasts a long time. However, plenty of people in the van-life and remote-work world are powering their RVs and vans, and relying on multiple laptop charges. For that, I’d recommend one of the larger units.

A solar-powered generator charges and runs almost anything that requires power—it just depends on the capacity. These generators can charge power tools in the shop, laptops for remote work, and personal devices like Bluetooth headphones and speakers.

A solar-powered generator with a higher power capacity can even power household appliances in the event of a power outage. And the fact that these are solar-compatible means you aren’t reliant on the electrical grid to recharge them.

Every unit on our list has multiple charging options, including wall (AC outlet), car (12-volt), and solar. Each input method charges your unit at a different speed, and solar also depends on the conditions. AC power is the fastest, followed by 12-volt, and then by solar (though a larger solar array could potentially be faster than 12-volt).

I try to charge up my power banks whenever I can, so I’m never caught off guard trying to leave on a trip with a dying power bank. If this happens (which it has), I’ll charge it in my vehicle’s 12-volt port while I’m driving, though this is often slower than solar or a wall unit.

Solar power is the most sustainable and typically fastest way to power these—just be sure you have enough wattage in the solar panels to make it effective. I recommend keeping the generator stored with at least an 80 percent charge, and unplugging it once it’s fully charged—keeping it plugged in longer than necessary degrades the battery.

Most units will have a simple readout display showing the remaining battery charge, and it’s a good practice not to allow your battery pack to get below 20 percent.
