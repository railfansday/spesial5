Why 2 Pinterest vets and a neurosurgeon are building a Reddit for doctors

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Medicine’s complexity and ever-evolving nature means doctors will always have more to learn and discuss—but in the digital age, there’s also a question of where those conversations can best take place.

Online, healthcare workers’ discourse is distributed across various social media platforms, forums, and group chats. Meanwhile, offline, medical professionals are “hyper-specialized,” with limited opportunities to work with colleagues from other disciplines, according to Dr. Rohan Ramakrishna, chief of neurosurgery at New York-Presbyterian Brooklyn Methodist Hospital.

As a neurosurgeon who specializes in brain tumors, he said, “the doctors I interact with on a regular basis are radiation oncologists and neuro-oncologists, and that’s it.” Even hospital doctors’ lounges are disappearing, he added, removing another opportunity for doctors to socialize and discuss medicine.

So Ramakrishna teamed up with Vikram Bhaskaran and Arun Ranganathan—two Pinterest alums who previously led engineering teams related to creators, content, and partners—to cofound an app that could foster that sense of community. 

Initially, the trio envisioned Roon as a place for doctors to provide patients with information; but in the app’s early days, Bhaskaran said, they found that physicians were especially interested in seeing what their own peers had to say—so they decided to rework the platform to facilitate those interactions.

Now, a year after its launch, the platform is adding features that bring Pinterest’s focus on sharing to the thousands of physicians on the platform to share everything from treatment advice to conference photos. As social media apps shift and online misinformation proliferates, the founders are hoping Roon will help physicians separate the signal from the noise.

Where doctors gather online

In some ways, Roon’s concept echoes that of #MedTwitter, which became a widely used, informal hub for medical experts and others to exchange information, ideas, and opinions, creating “a completely open-source environment that democratizes medical information,” as two physicians wrote in 2021. The hashtag still exists, and some continue to use it, but Twitter’s transition into X—and the accompanying changes—scattered some of  #MedTwitter to other platforms.

But medical science is always evolving, Ramakrishna said, as are politics and policy changes, and doctors lack a “home” for making sense of it all. Roon is “a place where you have net new knowledge creation, because people are trying to unpack what it all means, and understand it together.” 

To help shape Roon’s knowledge-creation network, the company enlisted more than 400 “founding physicians” from a range of specialties and institutions, who the company said “are owners with equity in Roon.” 

“A year ago, we had kind of a thesis: that physicians should have a place online where they can collaborate, connect, and that was safe and private,” said Bhaskaran, and it’s “been really heartening” to see how physicians are now using Roon. 

Creating community for a core audience

Roon joins a growing number of communications platforms designed for clinicians. Doximity, a networking service that offers an AI scribe and other tools, says more than 85% of U.S. physicians are registered members. There’s also Sermo, Figure 1, Mednet, Doquity—which is used by clinicians across much of Asia—and other new ventures like Marit Health, where medical professionals can share salary data. 

Each of them varies in form and function. Roon, which has the benefit of being designed by Pinterest veterans, is built to look and feel like mainstream social media sites. On it, users discuss new research, policy changes, and health topics that emerge in the news cycle, Ramakrishna said, and ask for advice on patient care. For example, he said, a family medicine doctor who posed a question related to reproductive health and the pituitary gland got replies from peers in family medicine, OB-GYN, high-risk maternal-fetal medicine, neurosurgery and neurology. 

There’s also an ability to form private groups—for example, Ramakrishna said, there’s one for chief medical officers, and another for residential program directors—and a new, Pinterest-board-inspired “Collections” feature that lets users group related content and media together. 

Selective, intentionally

Roon also differs from most medical communications apps in that, for the time being, it’s exclusive to physicians—M.D.s and D.O.s—who are licensed in the U.S.

Bhaskaran says the criteria mean that Roon can function as a place to share expertise built in the field—something that augments published literature in an important way. 

“There’s so much of medicine that’s outside of the published literature, that’s not accessible via chatbot or via AI: that’s in people’s heads,” he says. 

Roon currently has tens of thousands of users, Bhaskaran says, noting the goal is to reach 250,000 by the end of next year. That would constitute almost a quarter of the 1.08 million physicians that were licensed in the U.S. as of 2024. 

Part of the challenge of growing is the fragmentation of physicians across various social media apps. Some physicians also say they value the diverse perspectives offered by other healthcare workers, like nurses and pharmacists, and by those in other countries. 

They do plan to invite certain non-physician specialties, like infectious disease scientists, Bhaskaran added, but they still have work to do to build up the community of physicians, which remains their first goal. They also plan to expand Roon internationally, “but again, very thoughtfully, because verification is just a little bit of a hard problem once you leave the United States.” In the meantime, the company has a waitlist for medical professionals who’d like to join.

Human amid AI

Roon currently employs AI for tasks like filling out physicians’ profiles, routing users’ questions to specialists, and generating summaries of content. Its team is also working on new AI-powered functions to keep users up-to-date, like an audio digest feature that summarizes medical news and related Roon posts.

Then, there’s the training question: a database of medical information based on real-life expertise would be valuable for training LLMs, and physicians on X have expressed concern about it being used that way. A company spokesperson said Roon “currently has no plans to sell its data,” though the company may use it to refine its own models, and hasn’t made a “definitive commitment ruling out all future uses involving third-party LLMs.” 

But in the face of AI’s all-consuming nature, “our thesis is actually [that] the human network will become more valuable over time, especially as most of the world goes, ‘hey, we’re unclear who wrote this content,’ or ‘everything you see on the internet is AI slop,'” Bhaskaran says. ”We’re taking kind of an old school bet—which is, there’ll be value for community, and that’ll transcend any specific technology.”
