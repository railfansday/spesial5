The midterm elections revealed an AI backlash. Here’s what it means for CEOs

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

Hello and welcome to Modern CEO! I’m Stephanie Mehta, CEO and chief content officer of Mansueto Ventures. Each week this newsletter explores inclusive approaches to leadership drawn from conversations with executives and entrepreneurs, and from the pages of Inc. and Fast Company. If you received this newsletter from a friend, you can sign up to get it yourself every Monday morning. 

I was in Silicon Valley for part of last week, where many investors, founders, and executives seem to shrug off the midterm elections, in which voter distrust of big tech and AI safety played a role in the blue wave. Their conclusion? Data centers—those massive, resource-heavy computing facilities—simply have a “PR problem.” They maintain that the tech sector needs to launch a charm offensive to remind communities of all the good that will come from these massive computing facilities, such as jobs and support for small businesses. Heck, the tech giants might even send some employees to volunteer at local schools!  

A growing concern 

This interpretation of the election results is naively narrow and largely misses the point. “Data centers are the foil,” says Jim O’Leary, CEO of the global advisory firm Penta. “They’re the face of the AI backlash, but the issue is bigger.”

Indeed, a Pew Research Center report published in August found that 52% of Americans said they are more concerned than excited about the use of artificial intelligence in daily life, up from 37% in 2021. Sentiment analysis from Penta found that negative press coverage of AI safety rose 233% from May to October of this year versus from November 2025 to April 2026.  

All that translates into lawmakers focusing their attention on AI and the companies that are building and deploying the technology. Nearly half (46%) of policymakers interviewed by Penta in the weeks prior to the midterm elections said safety and responsibility were their top AI concerns—and government interest in AI is only going to grow in the run-up to 2028. “I think AI will be the defining issue of the general election,” O’Leary says. 

Beyond data centers  

Some of the scenarios O’Leary is contemplating make the data center backlash seem almost quaint. Over the next 24 months, he foresees one or more of the following AI-related crises: a safety incident that could involve loss of life, an AI market correction or outright bubble burst, or a cyberattack larger than those we’ve recently seen.  

If all three scenarios materialize, “it will create an environment that is extraordinarily complicated,” O’Leary says. “A significant portion of the population will push for an AI slowdown. And business leaders will need to ask, ‘How do we continue to drive AI transformation across our business, which we know we have to do?’” 

Even business leaders who have a strong conviction to expand AI usage in their companies will bump up against stakeholders such as employees and community leaders who may want to pump the brakes. Leaders will need to engage in conversations with all of these groups to understand objections and, where possible, work together to overcome them.  

After two years of courting the White House, big businesses will need to build bridges with an emboldened legislative branch. Says O’Leary: “Corporations are now going to have to start prioritizing congressional engagement in a way they didn’t over the past 24 months.”
