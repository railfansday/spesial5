What’s happening to crypto startup valuations?

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Last month, Axios reported, citing multiple sources, that Visa was in late-stage talks to acquire Fireblocks for about $2.5 billion. That's less than a third of the $8 billion valuation Fireblocks reached in January 2022. Fireblocks denied the report, telling The Block, "This is news to us, and not true at present time."

Regardless of the denial, the reported price brings up the question of how much private crypto companies are worth now, particularly those that raised capital during the 2021-22 boom.

Sources said the reported Fireblocks price is not far from what they have been seeing in secondary markets, whether or not Visa ends up buying the company.

"On our network, 2021-22 infrastructure supply has been quoted 60% to 70% under the last round for over a year," said Omar-Shakeeb Zahir, co-founder and CEO of SecondLane, a crypto secondary market platform. "A $2.5 billion figure against $8 billion sits right in that range."

A number of crypto companies haven't raised new funding since 2021 or 2022, so their last reported valuations are outdated. But shares sold by investors and employees in secondary markets are now going for much lower prices.

"A 2021 round is worth about 40 cents on the dollar today," Zahir said.

Jonas Thiele, founder and CEO of crypto secondary market platform OFFX, also said shares of well-known 2021-22 unicorns are regularly offered at discounts of 50% to 70%, "and for some of the biggest names of that cycle it's closer to 90%."

Karl-Martin Ahrend, co-founder and managing partner at crypto investment banking and M&A advisory firm Areta, estimated discounts of 50% to 80% across secondary markets and M&A. Thomas Klocanas, managing partner at Strobe Ventures, said secondary prices for late-stage companies from 2021 are generally 40% to 70% below their last rounds, "often with no clearing bid at all."

Data from secondary markets is helpful because many companies haven't had a new funding round to establish a lower valuation. Of the 49 companies in Architect Partners' database that raised at valuations of $1 billion or more in 2021 and 2022, 25 haven't announced another financing, said Adlee Heshmat, senior financial analyst at Architect Partners, a crypto investment bank and M&A advisor.

"Down rounds aren't really announced," Heshmat said, adding: "The repricing tends to show up in secondaries and in M&A, where buyers are pricing off of today's revenue, strategic fit and public company multiples."

Some crypto companies are still getting much higher valuations than in the past, especially in payments, stablecoins, trading and prediction markets. But many companies that raised at the 2021 peak are now trading at large discounts to their last funding rounds.

"It's a two-speed market. Most companies that raised at the 2021-22 peak trade well below those rounds today," Thiele said. "What holds up is anything with real revenue and a regulatory moat."

Heshmat made a similar point, saying, "It's a two-tier market right now. At the very top, valuations are now above the 2021-22 peak." Since last November, Architect Partners has tracked eight funding rounds at valuations of $20 billion or more, involving Ripple, Kraken, Kalshi, Polymarket, Crypto.com and OKX.

Only one company reached that level in 2021 and 2022, Heshmat said. He didn't name the company, but it was likely FTX, which raised at a $32 billion valuation in early 2022 before collapsing that November.

"Below that, late-stage valuations are still about a third under early 2022 averages," Heshmat added.

Payments and stablecoin companies have raised 39% more funding this year than at the same point in 2022, while funding for NFTs, gaming, protocols and developer tools is down around 80%, according to Heshmat.

The sources pointed to revenue as one of the main reasons some companies are doing better than others.

"There isn't one private crypto market anymore. There are two, and they barely talk to each other," Klocanas said. "Businesses with revenue, licenses and real distribution are at or above 2021 levels. The token-first infrastructure of the last cycle is the opposite," he added.

Klocanas said stablecoin payments, onchain credit, regulated trading and tokenization are doing better than other crypto sectors. General-purpose Layer 1 and Layer 2 blockchains, middleware companies with no revenue and consumer apps that depend on token incentives have struggled.

Prediction markets are one area where valuations have risen sharply, with Kalshi and Polymarket among the biggest players.

Ahrend said there are only a few major prediction market companies, and their valuations depend on whether they can stay ahead of competitors. On stablecoins, he said valuations "are justified for the few leaders with real volume, licenses and distribution, not for the long tail."

Klocanas agreed, saying: "The category leaders can grow into those numbers. The fifteenth stablecoin orchestration startup raising at 50x ARR [annual recurring revenue] probably can't."

The sources I spoke with said valuations were so high in 2021 largely because crypto markets were booming at the time.

"The 2021 rounds were priced in a hype phase," OFFX's Thiele said. "Crypto was a young market, trading volumes were at record highs, and investors were willing to pay for growth almost at any price."

Zahir said interest rates were near zero when those rounds took place. Valuation multiples were also high, and much of the revenue came from trading activity and token prices. Investors were expecting that growth to continue.

"Rates rose, multiples came back to earth, and much of that revenue never grew into the valuation," he said.

Lex Sokolin, co-founder and managing partner at Generative Ventures, said, "Multiple compression is at play — from 50x to 10x as growth stalls."

Thiele said many companies are still good businesses, "some much better than in 2021. But the price was set at the top of the cycle, and a company doesn't stay worth its peak valuation just because that was the last round."

Funds are also looking to get cash back from their investments, even if they have to sell shares for less than their last funding round prices. Founders also have to decide whether to wait for better prices or sell at current valuations.

"Funds now need DPI [distributions to paid-in capital], not TVPI [total value to paid-in capital]: cash at half the last round beats a paper 1x that never converts," Ahrend said.

Zahir also said, "LPs now judge on DPI, so GPs will use secondaries to sell parts of their winners and take the discount on the tail."

Some funds and early employees have already started selling their shares at discounts instead of waiting for the market to recover.

"For them, liquidity today is often worth more than a higher mark that may never be realized," Thiele said.

He added that founders must decide "whether to wait for the market to grow back into the old valuation or to accept a realistic price now."

Ahrend advised founders to start looking for buyers early. "Businesses are bought, not sold. Start a process with 18 months of runway, not six."

The sources I spoke to don't expect to see valuations across the crypto market returning to their 2021 highs. Though more exits that return cash to investors, clearer regulations, and revenue that can hold up when crypto prices fall could help some companies recover.

A few major IPOs and acquisitions could also help the market establish more realistic valuations.

"A few clean IPOs and strategic sales at sensible prices would reset the reference points for the whole market, more than any new funding round could," Thiele said.

Regulation is another factor. Stablecoins now have a U.S. law, but the crypto market structure bill stalled in the Senate in September, and with Democrats taking back control of the House and the Senate, the chances of further crypto legislation during President Donald Trump's second term are slim. Despite that, Klocanas said institutions need "predictability more than a single statute."

As for what could help valuations recover, Thiele said companies need revenue "that doesn't depend on token prices or trading volumes," while Sokolin said crypto businesses need to "challenge banks for their core money and lending offerings, or their capital markets venues."

Still, Ahrend expects many of the companies that raised during the 2021 boom to be acquired, recapitalized or shut down.

"Most of the 2021 class won't see peak marks again and will be resolved through M&A, recaps or wind-downs," Ahrend said. "2027 looks like a consolidation year."

Klocanas said returning to 2021 prices shouldn't be the goal. "We don't expect a broad recovery to 2021 levels, and in fact I don't think we should want one," he said. "What's ahead is a market that prices crypto companies like fintech and market infrastructure companies, on revenue, regulatory moat and distribution."
