Single-Family Construction Rebounds in November, but Permits Nosedive

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Construction of new single-family homes rose a bit in October, but permits fell sharply, the U.S. Census Bureau reported Wednesday.

Single-family housing starts rose 4% last month from September, to a seasonally adjusted annual rate of 864,000. On an annual basis, single-family starts were down 3.2% from a year earlier.

The monthly increase comes from upticks in the West and South, while the Northeast saw a larger increase in starts and the Midwest a slight decline. Single-family starts rose 15.7% on the month in Northeast, 4.2% in the South, and 3.4% in the West, but fell 0.8% in the Midwest.

Total privately owned housing starts, including more volatile multifamily units, rose 9.9% from September and 2.8% from October 2025. The month extends the uneven year for housing starts, which after a brief August rebound, then fell sharply in September.

Meanwhile, new building permits in October fell 7.4% month over month and 8.7% year over year to an annualized 1,295,000 units.

At the same time, housing completions increased 6.5% from September but fell 10.4% compared to last year.

Even so, the slowdown of building permits reflects greater pessimism in the housing market for builders. Many of the nation's major homebuilders have reported large year-over-year declines in revenue.

"The challenges facing builders have not subsided, and while there are some glimmers of hope in the housing starts figures, it must be noted that the current pace of single family home starts is still below last year’s actual total," Realtor.com® economist Joel Berner says. "This month’s data is an improvement, but from a very low baseline readout in both September and last October."

This week, the National Association of Home Builders/Wells Fargo Housing Market Index (HMI) plunged to a 14-year low, reflecting the industry's growing pessimism amid high mortgage rates and strained affordability.

Berner says the permits number is the lowest since the early days of the COVID-19 pandemic in May 2020. The pullback is clearest in single-family homes, with their 12.9% year-over-year decline. Multifamily homes, which tend to be more volatile, also fell 5.2% month-over-month.

"Builders are dealing with a litany of obstacles to selling homes, whether it’s rising costs of land, labor, and materials, high financing costs, or weak buyer demand," Berner says. "This month’s data shows them slowing down activity at the top of their funnel, which will result in slower months of starts and completions later on."

With mortgage rates above 7%, housing affordability is a major constraint on buyers, says Selma Hepp, chief economist at Cotality. Hepp pins that on high rates that keep payments elevated, high construction costs, rising insurance premiums, and elevated property taxes—not to mention everyday bills.

"For housing, the key question is not simply whether mortgage rates decline, but whether they fall enough to meaningfully improve purchasing power and bring more buyers and sellers back to the market," Hepp says. "Until then, I expect activity to remain relatively subdued, making the spring homebuying season an increasingly important test of whether the market can finally turn a corner."

The nation's housing supply shortage is complicated by red tape. American Enterprise Institute tracked 10 state bills passed this year and estimates they could potentially lead to 103,000 new homes a year. But people have to afford those homes.

So, Berner says, the slowdown in construction represents a "step in the wrong direction."

He was previously a senior reporter at Bloomberg Law, and before that covered real estate for the Washington Business Journal. Earlier in his career, he spent a decade reporting on business and real estate in Dayton and Columbus, OH. A Cincinnati native, he holds a journalism degree from Ohio University.
