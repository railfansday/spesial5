Before the first cold snap, have your car’s battery tested free at an auto parts store, check tire tread and pressure, top up antifreeze and winter washer fluid, and replace worn wipers. Then pack an emergency kit for the trunk in case you’re stranded anyway.

Cold weather lowers a battery’s power just as the engine needs more of it to start, so a battery that starts the car slowly in October can fail on the first frigid morning.

AutoZone and O’Reilly Auto Parts test batteries free, right in the car. Replacing a weak one now costs only a battery you’d soon need anyway, while waiting until it fails adds a jump-start or tow, plus a late start to work.

Where snow and ice are routine, switch to winter tires, which keep their grip below about 45 degrees, when all-season tires stiffen. Your regular tires don’t wear while they’re stored, so they last more seasons. If you’ll keep the car a few years, put the winter set on its own wheels, so each spring and fall swap skips paying a shop to remount the tires. Where snow is occasional, all-weather tires can stay on all year, and unlike most all-season tires they carry the mountain-and-snowflake symbol for severe snow.

Stock the trunk with an ice scraper, a blanket, a flashlight, jumper cables or a jump starter, kitty litter for traction, water, snacks, and a phone charger. Save a roadside assistance number in your phone, and decide whether a AAA membership is worth it.

Drivers stuck in snow should stay with the car, keep the tailpipe clear so fumes can’t back up inside, and run the engine about 10 minutes an hour for heat, with a window cracked.
