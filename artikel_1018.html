Brighton are rocking the Premier League elite. But can they mount a title tilt?

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

A combination of smart analytics, a young manager and players hand-picked for their particular skills has the Seagulls flying high

They lined up in sky‑blue shirts, white shorts. A combined value of £327m. Enzo Fernández, Ayyoub Bouaddi and Elliot Anderson were starting their first match together in Manchester City’s midfield.

But it was a Brighton midfielder who made the game’s first decisive contribution. Yasin Ayari slotted the ball home. The away crowd at the Etihad Stadium roared, then watched their team defend relentlessly, securing Brighton’s first league victory away to City.

It was 31 October 2026. There are dangers in reading too much into a single result this early in the season. Teams are still finding their rhythm, new signings bedding in. But this result packed some punch.

If spending decided football matches, Brighton & Hove Albion should not be competing with, let alone beating, Manchester City, who sign established stars for astronomical fees and wages. Erling Haaland is paid £525,000 a week.

According to an independent commission, to circumvent the Premier League’s spending limits, which restrict a club’s losses, City artificially inflated their commercial revenues by £830m between 2009 and 2018. Brighton, by contrast, tend to buy potential, scouring the market for undervalued talent, often in less prominent leagues. After developing them and benefiting from their performances, the club sell them for profits.

They paid just £3.5m for Ayari, offering him about £10,000 a week, and paired him in midfield with Chema Andrés and Pascal Gross, signed for a combined fee of less than £25m.

The most remarkable thing about Brighton’s win was that it wasn’t really a shock. In September, they dismantled Arsenal, the Premier League champions and favourites for the title. As they prepare for Sunday’s match at Hull, they are second in the league, sandwiched between Arsenal and City, and have scored more goals than anyone else in the division. How, then, do Brighton excel on the pitch without haemorrhaging money? And is there a ceiling to what they can achieve?

On a crisp late October morning, I drove to Brighton in search of answers. Deep inside the Amex, the club’s stadium, I met Paul Barber, Brighton’s CEO, a cheerful man who speaks with the calmness of someone with nowhere else to be.

“Being the size of club that we are, we had to find a way of being competitive that was probably different to any of the other bigger clubs that we were aiming to compete with,” Barber said, “because their revenues are always going to be higher: bigger stadiums, more established sponsorship models, bigger stars, bigger brands.”

Hours before Brighton beat City, Chelsea had beaten Manchester United. In their respective midfields were Moisés Caicedo and Carlos Baleba, whom Brighton had signed for a combined fee of less than £30m and sold for about £180m. “It’s those trading profits that are effectively the core of the Brighton model,” Barber explained.

Most clubs recruit and develop talent, I pointed out, but few do it better than Brighton. Who, outside Ecuador, had heard of Caicedo before he signed for Brighton in 2021? Two years later, the world’s biggest clubs were competing for his signature. The same can be said of dozens of others, among them Marc Cucurella and Alexis Mac Allister. One diamond exits, another enters. Caicedo replaced Yves Bissouma in central midfield. Baleba replaced Caicedo. The same applies to the dugout. Before joining Brighton, Fabian Hürzeler was coaching St Pauli in Germany; before him, Roberto De Zerbi had been at Shakhtar Donetsk.

Barber grinned. “There’s a myth that we have this secret supply of players,” he said. The explanation is Jamestown Analytics, a sports analytics company founded by the club’s owner, Tony Bloom, as an offshoot of Starlizard, the gambling consultancy he established in 2006. Just as Starlizard uses proprietary modelling to identify mispriced odds in betting markets, Jamestown uses algorithms to identify underdeveloped football talents. “They’re all out there,” Barber said. “The data just enables Brighton to home in on them faster and earlier.”

When I asked for more detail about how the system works, Barber fiddled with his watch. Jamestown tracks a vast number of performance indicators for every player competing in professional football, many of which aren’t captured by other data providers. “Every action that players are taking on a football pitch is being looked at, measured and codified in some way,” he said.

When the club identify a position to strengthen, they draw up a list of attributes for the ideal player. Some are universal to the position: a centre-back generally needs to be tall. Others depend on the specific coach and their style of football.

Later, when I spoke to Hürzeler, the head coach, he explained that the data had to reflect the specific demands of his playing system. “A player needs to have some basic things that fit our style of play, so if a player doesn’t want to have the ball then it’s difficult to play in our team. If a player doesn’t want to press high then it’s difficult to play in our team. These are just two examples.”

One reason Brighton recruited Luka Vuskovic in July 2026 was because Hürzeler wanted a mobile centre-back. Reflecting on the City match, Barber recalled seeing Vuskovic pushing “so far forward that you’d have thought he was a right midfielder”. It was the sort of mobility Hürzeler’s system demanded, and Jamestown had identified.

Before Brighton’s victory over City, I met Mike Cave, the club’s sporting director, at the Lowry hotel in Manchester. A few of the Brighton players wandered around in club tracksuits. Cave wore a suit and tie and his hair was slicked back. He was telling me it was a big day without saying it.

Brighton’s recruitment advantage lies not simply in the quality and quantity of the data at their disposal, he said, but in their ability to interpret it and trust what it tells them. “You could have the best data in the world but if you’re not accustomed to using it, and if you’re not clear about how it informs your decision-making process, then it’s just a lot of data that doesn’t move the dial.”

The power of data is that it strips away emotions and preconceptions. When watching games, Hürzeler said he often identifies a player he thinks is performing well and texts Cave, only to learn that the numbers don’t support his impression. The conversation ends. “The numbers have to be right otherwise we have no chance of getting a player in.”

Conversely, the data identifies players he might not otherwise have considered – such as Diego Gómez, the Paraguayan signed from Inter Miami in 2024. Despite the strength of Gómez’s underlying numbers, the club had a free run at signing him. Cave suggested this reflected a misconception that players in MLS aren’t equipped to play in the Premier League. “What the data has allowed us to do is step away from some of those biases and just be clear on what the evidence tells us, to look at the signal rather than the noise.”

But identifying a player who can perform for Brighton is different from identifying one who will, Cave said. The club’s requirements are so specific that the data typically identifies only around five candidates, Hürzeler says, and fewer still are affordable. Of those remaining, the recruitment team and Hürzeler will speak to anyone with experience of them – players, coaches and sponsors – to learn more about their character and personality, and prepare dossiers. Together, they’ll identify a primary target and make a move. “It’s not like I get a list and then decide which players; it’s something we all decide together,” said Hürzeler.

As Barber explained: “The more we can find out about a player’s character the less risk there is of that player being rejected like a bad organ transplant.” There’s no exhaustive list of desirable characteristics, he added, but “coachability” is key. Brighton’s model hinges on players having the capacity to improve, and the best indication is their drive to do so. “The biggest element of coachability is attitude.”

Hürzeler asks himself: “What are they willing to give for their development? Are they willing to suffer?” He’s convinced this desire is innate. “These are things I can’t control.”

Conversations with people around Gómez painted a picture of “a boy that is clearly trying to get absolutely everything out of his career”, said Cave. But sometimes the qualities Brighton are looking for aren’t so obvious. Whereas the data is objective, says Barber, references are “someone’s opinion”. It’s challenging to know how much weight to give a single person’s negative judgment, particularly when it conflicts with other opinions, without dismissing it altogether. “Sometimes when you want to sign somebody you can subjectively screen out the negativity because you want them, and the data says you need them.”

Beyond character, the team consider a player’s ability to settle into a new country, and whether their partner will, too. Once Brighton make an offer, the player is normally keen to join, in Barber’s experience, because they know it can lead to a move to “potentially bigger things”. The selling club is often receptive because there are rarely any other comparable offers on the table.

But this has been changing, says Barber. Brighton’s success means their interest in a player “alerts other clubs to that talent”, creating competition. Selling clubs, meanwhile, have reason to believe that the player “has the potential to be valued for a lot more”. Increasingly, then, Brighton are having to pay more, making “our ability to trade profitably ever harder”.

Even if a deal can be agreed, it doesn’t always represent good business. As Cave explained: “You’re dealing with human beings, in a complex system and a complex game, so you will never have a 100% strike record.”

Asked what Brighton looks for in recruits, Gordon Greer, who played for the club from 2010 to 2016, was quick to answer: “high potential”. But that potential rarely means they’re ready to slot into the first‑team. Today, as Brighton’s pathway development manager, Greer’s job is to bridge that gap.

To understand how Greer does this, I joined him for coffee at the Lowry. He told me that he begins planning a player’s development while the club are still negotiating his signing. “In the buildup to us signing him, we’ll start to analyse him … what’s his strengths, what’s his weaknesses … where do we think we can help?” Once he’s met the player and understood their ambitions, he turns that assessment into a development plan.

To begin, Greer explained, he asked himself: “What are this player’s limiting factors to play for Brighton?” Sometimes the answer lies off the pitch. Hürzeler requires his players to speak English. It helps embed his playing principles by allowing the players to transmit them to each other. “We want to have a common language, so they can coach each other on the pitch,” he explained. Other times the shortcomings are more directly related to football, such as back‑post defending. A typical one is physicality; Hürzeler’s counter-pressing requires repeated sprints and many recruits would risk injury if they were thrown into the first team. “The game is so fast, and the intensity is crazy,” Jack Hinshelwood, who made his first‑team debut in 2023, has said.

One pathway for development is the under-21s team, Greer says, but the physical demands of the game fall short of those in the Premier League. Hinshelwood, who came through the under-21s, suffered a stress fracture in his foot after a few top-flight games. “You need time to adapt,” he said. In Greer’s view, the level doesn’t challenge most recruits enough for them to learn. “We want to put them in a position where they’re stretched enough that they’re uncomfortable but they’ve got enough success that they’re doing things that they enjoy.”

An alternative is the loan market. Since 2018–19, Brighton have agreed more than 20 loan deals in each campaign. Playing time is an important consideration when arranging a loan, Greer explains, because a player’s value can drop if they’re not playing often. But he also considers the location, manager, formation and quality of the pitch. Position is crucial, too. Sending a striker to a team near the bottom of their league, where they’re likely to be living off scraps, can see their confidence and value drop, but sending a defender into the same environment can accelerate their development with repeated defensive situations. “You’re trying to get to a point where they’re impacting the game.”

A successful loan addresses a player’s limitations. “You’re trying to expose someone to something totally different,” Greer added. In 2017, Ben White – now of Arsenal and England – was loaned to Newport County to improve the “urgency in his defending”.

“The game model was end‑to-end, back-to-front, League Two, where you’re up against it,” Greer added. In 2024, Ibrahim Osman, shortly after signing from Nordsjælland in Denmark, went to Feyenoord, a possession-based team, to develop his technical game. “Physically, he was a beast, but he was quite untidy,” said Greer.

Throughout a loan, each player is continually reassessed. Those ready are brought into Hürzeler’s first team. If they’re not, another spell elsewhere might be arranged. But if their development has stalled for too long, they’ll be encouraged to leave permanently. That was the case with Leo Østigård, a Norway international signed from Molde in 2018. He was a skilled defender but he “wasn’t good enough on the ball”, Greer said. His development slowed down during a loan at Coventry, and then, at Stoke, it became “clear he wasn’t going to be for us”.

Rather than continuing to work on Østigård’s weaknesses, the club sought to maximise his strengths. A loan was arranged at Genoa, a defensive team where Østigård would shine. A year later, he signed for Napoli. “We tried everything but it didn’t work,” Greer said, “so we thought: ‘How do we create as much value as possible?’”

After speaking to Barber, I headed to Brighton’s training base, a short drive away from the Amex, to meet Hürzeler. Academy players milled around outside, while the first team was elsewhere preparing to face Getafe in the Conference League, a competition Brighton are being tipped to win.

I noticed a mural bearing the words: “Challenge the Establishment,” a reference to the so-called big six clubs. A member of staff pointed out that the mural had been Hürzeler’s idea, but the philosophy it exhibits has existed since Bloom took over the club in 2009. Brighton, the member of staff whispered to me, saw themselves as the “antidote” to big-spending clubs such as City.

Before Hürzeler and I sat down in the press conference room, another journalist asked him whether he thought Brighton could win the title. He dismissed the question. His focus was improving the team, he said. He suggested to me, however, that victories such as that over City were the fruits of his work developing the squad and an environment that allows talents to excel. “If you work with players for a longer time, you get used to each other and they get more and more of your principles.”

Hürzeler tells me he relies on older players who embody these principles, such as Lewis Dunk and Pascal Gross. At 33, Hürzeler is younger than both, and became the youngest permanent manager in Premier League history when he was appointed in 2024. When you’re developing raw talents from abroad, while constantly selling and bringing in new players, you need a core group to “build the right culture”. Hinshelwood told me he has experienced this first-hand: “I remember watching their habits every day, how they live their lives, how they’re training, what they’re doing in the gym, how they’re communicating, and I learned so much just by watching them.”

Hürzeler also emphasised the importance of tailoring the support players receive. “You can’t treat a player like Yankuba Minteh who is coming from [the] Gambia the same as a player like Lewis Dunk, who comes from England,” he said. He wants to cultivate an environment in which, as he put it, players, “no matter which age, no matter from where they are coming from, can express themselves”.

To that end, Hürzeler appointed Mike Anthony, who spent 14 years working with New Zealand Rugby, as head of player development and high performance – a new role. One of Anthony’s priorities, he said, has been giving the players more responsibility. Players and the coaches increasingly “lead the programme together”, with players invited to lead discussions.

“They don’t want to just execute things, they want to be valued, they want to be appreciated, they want to be heard by us coaches,” Hürzeler said. “If you don’t give them ownership and responsibility off the pitch, how can you expect them to take responsibility for their own execution on the pitch? It’s not possible.”

A key part of his role, Hürzeler told me, is listening to his players. It helps them perform, and makes him a better coach. He was reminded of this in February 2025, when there were calls for him to lose his job after a poor run that included a 7-0 defeat at Nottingham Forest. James Milner and Danny Welbeck suggested he was overcomplicating his messaging around counter-pressing. Taking their advice on board, he made changes that helped turn things around. “It was definitely one reason why we turned around the results.”

Asked why Brighton have such a strong record for developing talent, Hinshelwood smiled: “You’re allowed to make mistakes, not effort mistakes, or applying yourself badly, but if you make a technical mistake you know you’ve got 10 other players who want you to try things,” he said.

Hürzeler also credited the club’s summer recruitment with this season’s success. Yet Brighton had sold Jan Paul van Hecke to Tottenham and Baleba to United, I pointed out. How could a club expect to improve while selling their best players?

Although he often tries to prevent departures by convincing Bloom not to sell, Hürzeler said he has no final say. “I can’t control whether a player gets a big offer and Tony wants to accept this offer.” But he’s come to see departures as an opportunity to revitalise the squad with new profiles. “When you sign for Brighton, you know that it’s part of the identity, so automatically you have the mindset that you have to deal with these things.”

Being transparent about the club’s model with coaches is a crucial component in their success, according to Barber, but even then it can create tensions. The decision to sell Caicedo was one reason De Zerbi departed, as was the Italian’s insistence on big-name signings rather than trusting the data. It’s no different with supporters, Barber said: “Sometimes when a big player leaves a club and the fans are blindsided by that, it creates this bigger sense of injustice, anger, disappointment.”

Barber also explains that he must go to great lengths to keep costs down. “The difference between what we bring in and what we spend is what we put out on the pitch.” The club’s use of experienced players such as Dunk is ultimately driven by financial constraints. They can’t afford to sign players in their prime, either in transfer fees or wages, so rely on players a little past their prime to counterbalance the inexperience of their younger recruits. “They’re providing that level of experience of a 27-, 28-, 29-year-old, albeit four or five years later, but at a much lower cost.”

Brighton also use data to identify and recruit coaches whose philosophy aligns with the existing squad, minimising the need to overhaul the squad to fit a new style of football. Hürzeler was identified in large part because the numbers showed he could build on the players he inherited. “Revolutions cost a lot of money,” Barber said. “If you can match the coach that you’re bringing into the squad that you’ve got, the cost of change is minimal.”

The final part of the model is knowing when to sell. Barber tries to have a replacement through the door before letting a player go. It becomes trickier to negotiate a fee for a replacement when the selling club knows Brighton have just sold a player for £100m. Barber told me he delayed selling Caicedo until he was confident Baleba could step up. “We felt Carlos had a lot of potential that we could help to realise quite quickly … he’s a quick learner and quite raw, but at the same time you could see rapid improvements in him.”

But other factors matter too, such as whether a player still has room to develop, and therefore increase his value further; the state of the market; and the risk that turning down an offer might jeopardise the chance of a future sale. “If there is a dearth of central midfielders at the moment, maybe now is the time to sell because there will be a significant premium because there are no other options.”

Barber adds that refusing to sell Caicedo to Arsenal in January 2023 was easier because other clubs wanted him in the summer. “Not doing the deal didn’t affect our ability to do the deal with Liverpool and then Chelsea the following summer.”

In their last match before the November international break, Brighton beat Brentford and leapfrogged City. They’d progressed in Europe too: a goal from Malick Yalcouyé, a £6m signing from ​​IFK Göteborg, had beaten Getafe. Outside the ground, fans were dreaming of titles. Barber said he hoped they would not have to wait much longer. “We now need to take the next step and put some silverware on the table.”

But if Brighton are to compete for major trophies, I wondered, will they have to invest more heavily in players, and keep them in their prime? Can the club’s current model allow them to become part of the establishment, rather than simply challenging it?

The club are already adapting, says Barber. The signing of Vuskovic for a club record £46m demonstrates an appetite for talents better equipped to immediately contribute to the first team. “Those players are already at a level of transfer fee that we wouldn’t have contemplated, and 10 years ago we wouldn’t have even considered.” As Jamestown’s algorithm improves, Barber says, he’s hopeful it will become easier for the club to invest these sorts of sums in players, because the risk of getting the assessment wrong will be lower.

But Barber believes player trading is likely to stay as the core of the club’s strategy, partly because they are good at it, but also because they need the revenue. “There’s always going to be this gap [in commercial sales and ticket revenue] so the need for us to trade will always be there.” Even if they did decide to keep players such as Baleba and Van Hecke, would they be able to? As much as a title or two might make it easier to convince top players to stay, Barber said he struggles to envisage a time when they’ll be able to compete with the bigger clubs on wages.

And even if Brighton could one day afford to retain their best players, would it make them less attractive to the next generation of recruits, who might no longer see the club as a stepping stone on the way to the so-called establishment? Could the club become so successful that the prices of their targets rise beyond what they can afford? Might the model self-cannibalise?

Hürzeler tells me he reflects on these same questions. “It’s interesting, this discussion, because these kinds of discussions we also have in the building.” When we spoke on the phone before the Hull match, I brought up Hearts, who have partnered with Jamestown and came close to winning the Scottish title in May for the first time since 1960, challenging the Celtic-Rangers duopoly. I asked whether this gave him the belief that Brighton could go one step further and break the big clubs’ stranglehold.

“Of course, you see this as motivation for what is possible,” he said. He revealed a belief that he can lift a trophy with Brighton, which is why he has extended his contract to 2029. “When I see the team now, they are so into it, they are so committed to it.” He just wouldn’t say when. “Will it be this season? I don’t know. Will it be next season? I don’t know.” Beyond that, he tries not to give it much attention. “These kinds of questions, we can answer them one day.”
