Arthur Conti Was Booked This Year With Roles in ‘Carrie’ and ‘Ebenezer.’ Now He’s Looking to Get “a Little Strange”

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

The 22-year-old has tackled iconic characters in pop culture this year, but he's keeping himself open about what's next.

British actor Arthur Conti is fresh off a whirlwind year. With roles in Mike Flanagan’s Carrie adaptation and the recently released Ebenezer, he’s been busy crafting new interpretations of well-known characters.

“To play an iconic character that’s been played by many people before, and to get to give your own spin on it, is very exciting,” Conti tells The Hollywood Reporter over Zoom from London about what he’s been up to this year.

The 22-year-old actor is back in the U.K. after over a month of press for Carrie and Ebenezer and a stint in Portland, Oregon. “It’s been a bit of a whirlwind, but it’s been very enjoyable,” he says. After a breakout performance in 2024’s Beetlejuice Beetlejuice, 2026 has been the actor’s biggest year to date between the two projects and a role in Jane Schoenbrun’s Teenage Sex and Death at Camp Miasma. With Carrie, Conti stepped into the role of teen bully Billy Nolan, first played on screen by John Travolta in Brian De Palma’s 1976 film. Conti had read Stephen King’s novel but hadn’t seen De Palma’s film, which he stayed away from during filming. Conti’s Billy, while still one of the villains of the story, is different from Travolta’s take on the character. “I was worried that even inadvertently or subconsciously I would copy things about that performance, and I really wanted it to be all my own,” he says.

Ebenezer finds Conti stepping into the shoes of the titular character of Ebenezer Scrooge — well, the younger version of him anyway. The actor heard that Ti West would be making the film, and as a big fan, he sent in a self-tape. Much like with Carrie, he tried not to take too much inspiration from previous versions of the story, but there’s one adaptation that stands out.

“[The Muppets version] is probably the best one. I mean, it’s amazing, that film. Michael Caine gives such a genuinely brilliant, dramatic performance in that film against the Muppets,” he says. “He takes it so seriously, and that is the beauty of that film is that you’d think he was talking to John Gielgud, and he’s talking to Fozzy.”

Despite The Muppets Christmas Carol being his main experience with the story beforehand, he did watch the 1951 film adaptation starring Alastair Sim as Scrooge. “I really liked,” he notes.

Below, the actor digs into his last year in roles, what he’s looking to do next and how he’d spend a perfect day in London.

I’m always curious, as someone who enjoys it — have you seen Carrie: The Musical?

We watched it. We saw a theater production on Granville Island in Vancouver. It was a youth theater production of Carrie: The Musical. [The cast of Carrie] all went to go and see it. Mike [Flanagan] actually put the cast of that production of Carrie in the show. They’re all at the prom. Throughout the show, they’re in the background at the school, which was really, really fun.

How has the year been? You were busy with Carrie and now Ebenezer.

[Carrie] was my first big press tour. It was my first time doing a press junket and all of these things. It feels like the biggest thing that I’ve done so far [with] how much I’ve been promoting it. It was all incredibly exciting. [In Carrie,] I’m playing another character that has several sides to him, after [I did in] Beetlejuice. It’s another deceptively charming, manipulative character, which is interesting that I keep taking. (Laughs).

Something about me, I don’t know. Something untrustworthy. Honestly, it is a treat to play characters like that because you get the chance to do multiple things at once, present a character that has many layers and is complex and isn’t wholly one thing or another. It was a great role. It was a really meaty role to sink my teeth into, and it was an honor to play a character of that literary significance. It was a privilege to be entrusted with that.

As an actor, are you drawn to characters that are different from you, then?

It’s nice to get outside of yourself — that’s part of the fun of it. Growing up, the films I was watching, I was a lot more drawn to flawed or strange or darker characters. I grew up watching Steve Buchemi in Fargo and Philip Seymour Hoffman in Punch-Drunk Love and Talented Mr. Ripley. Those are the kind of characters that I always really loved to watch.

The scope of the projects you had this year has been quite large. Are you drawn to that or looking to change it?

You’re right. They are quite large-scale. I think I’m drawn to things that are a little out there and a little strange. Horror is one of the best genres to explore that area because it emboldens filmmakers to experiment and take risks and be a bit weird and a bit dreamlike. That’s why I gravitate towards horror a lot, and it’s probably what I watch the most of in the cinema.

Is that where you got your love for it? Were you big into horror as a kid?

I think so. It’s not horror, just things that are a little strange. My mom brought me up on Charlie Kaufman films. Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind is my mom’s favorite film, so I watched that quite early. Things that tap into that dreaminess and that weirdness and spookiness — that’s up my alley. But it would be a dream to do a Before Sunrise kind of thing, where it’s just two people talking for an hour and a half. I would love to do something like that next.

Is that what you’re looking to do going forward then?

It could be anything. I really want to leave myself open. I would love to do something a little more scaled down, kind of dialogue-heavy thing. I could never have imagined the caliber of director and the caliber of actor that I’ve been working with. If I can only just continue to do that, I will be very lucky indeed. I’ve gotten to work with some of my absolute heroes, and that’s been wonderful. But I want to keep working in horror as well. Horror’s having a really exciting moment right now, especially young people in horror, with Obsession and Backrooms. I will stay as long as it’ll have me. Horror, fantasy — that’s what I grew up with, and I want to explore that more as well.

I would wake up at 10:30 a.m.-ish. I’m a very late-to-bed, late-to-rise kind of person in my free time. I’d wake up then; I’d get a coffee; I’d go for a walk. I have to do 10,000 steps a day. I’m like a dog. I have to be walked. Then I would go and see a movie. I would go to the cinema. I would probably go to the Prince Charles Cinema, which is my favorite in the world. I don’t know whether I’d see it on my own or with somebody else. I do go on my own to the cinema a lot. That’s one of my favorite ways to watch a film. Then I’d get a nice dinner. I might go shopping a little bit. I might go record shopping, go DVD shopping. Then I would go to the pub maybe, and then I would go to bed at 1 a.m. I’m being very specific with the times.

I’m unfamiliar with Prince Charles Cinema; why is that your favorite theater?

Just the vibe there, and the programming. There’s always something on there that’s worth seeing. I honestly have so much to thank that cinema for because the education in film that it has given me is just insane. I’ve seen so many films there for the first time. It’s one of the few places, in London especially, where you can see old films and cult films and things that don’t get screened very often. You can go and experience them in the theater, and that’s a very valuable thing. They were going to possibly get shut down, but they’ve just won the battle against the landlord or whatever. They’re going to be there for another 25 years. I’m very happy about that.
