What Yoga Can Teach You About Quieting Your Thoughts

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

It’s one thing to practice yoga and to think maybe you’ll try meditation one of these times. It’s another to understand how these practices can support you in everyday life. In his latest book, accessible yoga teacher and YJ contributor Jivana Heyman explains the connection among yoga, meditation, and how you navigate your days. Although books explaining poses and esoteric theories abound, Heyman’s Yoga Meditation: Finding a Path Back to Your Self explains how these apply to any situation through storytelling, relatable examples, and insightful questions you can contemplate or rely on as journal prompts. The following excerpt helps you understand the connection so you can begin to try it in your own life.—Renee Marie Schettler, Editor in Chief, YJ

In order to embrace meditation, I found it incredibly helpful to study yoga philosophy and try to understand why I’m practicing. I realize that many people find philosophy boring, but I always recommend that my students begin meditation with some understanding of the philosophical concepts behind the practice.

The Yoga Sutras of Patanjali is generally considered the primary source for yoga philosophy, although some scholars question how this came to be. There is also a debate among scholars about the date of the text, which may be anywhere from 2200 to 1500 years old. Personally, I think the Sutras have staying power because Patanjali consolidated thousands of years of teachings into a relatively terse collection of 196 incredibly insightful aphorisms.

The reason I love starting our meditation conversation with the Sutras is that it offers much insight into how the mind works, as well as how to use yoga to work with the mind. His main goal is to teach us to quiet our mind so that we can experience the eternal true Self, or spirit.

Yoga is a process of remembering who we really are and what Patanjali does, in a variety of ways, is show us how to do just that. We don’t need to become something else or earn enlightenment. In fact, my favorite thing about the Sutras is that he starts with the positive assumption that we are spirit and the recognition that our spiritual nature has been obscured by the mind’s attachments and desires. In other words, there’s nothing to gain, just a lot to release.

In the very beginning of the Sutras, Patanjali offers what is considered the definition of yoga: “Yogah chitta vritti nirodhah.” (1.2) which is often translated as, “Yoga is the stilling of the changing states of the mind.” 20 It is a surprising statement because he’s saying that quieting the thoughts and emotions in the mind is both the practice of yoga and the state of yoga—union with the true self.

So right off the bat, Patanjali is already talking about the practice of meditation and working with the mind. In the next Sutra, he goes on to say that, “When that is accomplished, the Seer abides in its own true nature.” (1.3) 21 In other words, when the mind quiets down and you find stillness, you experience peace, joy, and bliss—whatever words you’d like to use for the experience of your divinity.

According to Patanjali, the process of quieting the mind begins with concentration, which is at the heart of meditation. He’s open-minded about what we’re concentrating on. Instead, he emphasizes the process of concentration. He explains that you can remove the obstacles to the experience of yoga, “By meditating on anything one chooses that is elevating.” (1.39) 22

Consciously working to quiet the mind is a theme throughout his treatise on yoga. In other words, the entirety of the text is basically about meditation. He goes on to expound on the contents of the mind, the obstacles to practice, how to avoid suffering, and eventually he describes the eight limbs of yoga, which is essentially a detailed meditation program.

There’s a reason Patanjali devotes the Sutras to working with the mind. Because of the nature of the mind, as well as our noisy culture, we seem to have a distorted understanding of ourselves. We have identified with our body and mind, yet these are the parts of us that are always changing. Instead, Patanjali is asking us to identify with the part that is eternal—our unchanging spirit.

It’s also interesting to consider how asana (yoga poses) is considered in the Yoga Sutras of Patanjali. In this text, asana doesn’t simply lead us to meditation, it is meditation. People often recite Patanjali’s definition of, “Asana is a steady, comfortable posture,” (2.46) 34 but they neglect to include the next sutra, which explains how to practice asana. “By lessening the natural tendency for restlessness and by meditating on the infinite, posture is mastered.” (2.47) 35

There are a few major takeaways here: First, the key to asana is meditation. Second, Patanjali admits that the mind is naturally restless. So it might be natural to find meditation challenging: It’s in the mind’s nature to constantly move from one thing to another. He is offering asana as a prescription for a busy mind.

In the very next sutra, Patanjali describes the result of practicing asana: “Thereafter one is undisturbed by the dualities.” (2.48) 36 He’s revealing the truth of asana as a technique for handling the ups and downs of life. Asana not only prepares the body to take a comfortable seat in meditation, it also lifts us above the chaos of life to find a still point of peace.

So many spiritual, and even meditative, practices are conceptual, intellectual, and ethereal. They can feel too subtle to grasp. But the practice of asana is different. It is practical and grounded in its ability to give you access to deeper parts of yourself, whether you have the patience for seated meditation or not.

In a flowing asana series, you can explore the interplay of action and stillness in your body while your mind focuses on each movement. Or you can hold a static posture and notice dynamic inner movement. You can experience the lifting and grounding that you find in the flow of the breath. The inhale bringing in new life, the exhalation letting go; inhale lift, exhale release.

Asana allows you to experience the energy of creation moving through the body and mind. By holding steady, or through movement, stretching, or strengthening, asana clears the subtle energetic pathways and allows your prana to flow.

Asana is about expanding your sense of self, yet it’s so often taught in a way that feels limiting or constricting. So many of people struggle with body image, and it’s heartbreaking to think that the way contemporary yoga is taught feeds into this. Instead, yoga can teach you how to cherish your body for giving you this lifetime and for allowing you to engage in spiritual practice. Remember, your body is equally worthy of asana as any other body.

The body has an essential role in the hatha yoga traditions. It’s perceived as either an obstacle that needs to be overcome, or a vehicle that needs to be strong enough to withstand the force of kundalini, potential prana, flowing through it. Some of the more ascetic, or strictly disciplined, traditions focused on overcoming attachment to the body by forcing it into compliance. These hatha yogis would stand for months on end, hold one arm up in the air, or perform some similarly intense practice. This is a way to force the body into compliance in the hope of releasing attachment to it.

Contemporary yoga has maintained a hint of this extreme practice by teaching that the body needs to be whipped into shape in order to be worthy. I don’t think this is a useful idea because it reinforces cultural messages of unworthiness that are used to sell wellness “solutions” back to you. It also feeds the self-hatred and internalized prejudice that flows from ableism, racism, sexism, fat phobia, homophobia, transphobia, ageism, and other kinds of oppression.

You can practice asana as meditation and make movement your practice. You don’t even have to identify as someone who meditates, and you don’t have to sit with your thoughts and feelings. Instead, you can move your body, channel your energy, and find relief from your mind’s ruminations. Your yoga mat or chair is a magic portal to a different land. Take a few breaths, move your body, and then integrate movement with your breath, and poof! Off you go.

Because of the way asana is represented in the media as an extreme sport, it can be intimidating for a lot of people, which is why I’ve spent my entire career teaching people how to make these practices accessible and welcoming. The meditative heart of asana practice is naturally inclusive and accessible. Rather than performing asana or copying what other people do, the question is: Can you listen to your own body? When you practice, let go of your thoughts and focus on how you feel, and what your body needs today.

There are a few different things you can focus on in asana that turns movement into meditation. In fact, you can start by asking yourself what the difference is between asana and exercise. The difference is subtle, and it all comes back to meditation. Is your mind focused when you’re doing asanas? It doesn’t really matter what it’s focused on, but it’s the act of focusing that is key. Approach a short asana session as a meditation. You can do this with any asana practice focused on your breath. If you have a regular asana practice, do some poses that you enjoy—in a chair or on a yoga mat.

If you are new to asana, consider joining a beginner’s yoga class, or look up chair yoga online. It can be helpful to start in a chair, or even better, a couch. Couch yoga is my favorite way to experience the power of asana as a meditation.
