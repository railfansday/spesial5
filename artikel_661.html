Why hydrogen flames can glow orange, then turn nearly invisible

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Hydrogen flames can glow bright orange one day and become almost impossible to see the next, even when the burner settings remain exactly the same. Researchers at Adelaide University have now identified a surprising reason for this dramatic change, offering insights that could help make hydrogen-powered stoves, ovens, and other household appliances safer and more dependable.

The study, published in Next Energy, found that much of the difference comes down to sodium, a naturally occurring element carried through the air in microscopic particles. Sea salt transported inland by the wind is one important source.

When sodium is present in the surrounding air, hydrogen flames can take on a noticeable orange glow. But when sodium concentrations fall, the flames become much harder to see, even though the burner continues operating under the same conditions.

This discovery has important implications for the development of hydrogen appliances. Instead of depending on flame color to show whether a burner is running, engineers may need to incorporate other detection technologies. Improving these systems could help support the adoption of hydrogen as a lower-emissions alternative to natural gas.

Why Hydrogen Flames Change Color With the Weather

Lead researcher Professor Paul Medwell, from the School of Electrical and Mechanical Engineering at Adelaide University, explained that studying hydrogen flames under ordinary environmental conditions is essential for developing appliances that people can safely and confidently use at home.*

"Hydrogen has significant potential as part of a lower-emissions energy future, and we need to understand how it behaves in the real world if we want people to be confident using it.

"Our research helps explain why hydrogen flames can look different from one day to the next. That knowledge gives engineers valuable information to develop appliances and flame-detection systems that work reliably, regardless of changing environmental conditions."

To investigate these changes, the researchers combined controlled laboratory experiments with outdoor testing around Adelaide. They examined how hydrogen flames responded to variations in atmospheric conditions, including shifting weather patterns and changes in the air reaching the burner.

Over two weeks, the team monitored light emissions from hydrogen flames while keeping the burner settings unchanged. Despite the consistent operating conditions, the sodium-related light signal varied by more than 28 times between its lowest and highest recorded values.

Weather played a major role in those differences. Air that had passed over the ocean generally contained more sodium, producing flames with a stronger orange glow. When the air carried less sodium, the flames appeared considerably fainter.

Hydrogen Flames Are Harder to See Than Natural Gas Flames

The researchers also examined how hydrogen flames compared with those produced by a conventional natural gas cooktop.

They found that hydrogen flames emitted less visible light on average, even when atmospheric conditions favored a brighter flame. This difference presents an additional challenge for appliances that use visible flames to indicate whether a burner is operating.

Hydrogen combustion can also produce faint blue and red light. However, the researchers found that these colors were easiest to detect in darkened surroundings, making them less useful as reliable visual indicators under everyday lighting conditions.

Together, the findings show why judging whether a hydrogen burner is operating based on appearance alone may be unreliable. A flame that is clearly visible under one set of conditions could become extremely difficult to detect when the surrounding air changes.

Medwell emphasized that the results do not undermine hydrogen's potential as an energy source. Instead, they demonstrate why appliances must be engineered to accommodate the fuel's particular properties.

"Different energy sources have different properties, and good engineering is about understanding those differences and designing for them.

"Rather than relying on a flame looking a particular color, we can investigate other ways of confirming that a burner is operating. Options include specialized ultraviolet or infrared flame detectors and temperature sensors.

"By identifying the factors that influence flame visibility, we can help inform the development of hydrogen appliances that are fit for purpose in real homes and other everyday settings."

Such detection systems could provide a more dependable way to confirm that hydrogen is burning, regardless of whether its flame appears orange, faint blue, or is barely visible.

By identifying how environmental sodium affects flame visibility, the research provides information that engineers can use when designing hydrogen appliances for kitchens and other everyday environments. That knowledge could help make the technology safer and more practical as interest in lower-emissions energy alternatives grows.

The project brought together researchers from Adelaide University, the University of New South Wales, and Gas Infrastructure Research Australia. Support came through the Future Fuels Cooperative Research Centre, with funding provided under the Australian Government's Cooperative Research Centres Program.

The study, 'The role of sodium on hydrogen flame visibility in domestic settings,' was published in Next Energy

*In 2023, the Australian Gas Infrastructure Group (AGIG) introduced Australia's first 100% hydrogen-powered home in Victoria. The demonstration showed that household gas appliances could operate entirely on hydrogen, a development described as a 'game changer'.
