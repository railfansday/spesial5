Think about what's sitting in your inbox and Drive right now. Client contracts and invoices. Project specs under NDA. Staging credentials a client emailed you. That API key or password you meant to delete.

For most developers and freelancers, all of it lives in Google Workspace or Microsoft 365. Both are excellent tools, and both encrypt your data in transit and at rest. But in their standard setup, the provider holds the encryption keys, which means your provider (and the integrated apps and AI features connected to it) can access what's inside.

Proton Workspace takes a different approach: the same everyday tools, with encryption designed so that not even Proton can read your data.

Here's how the three compare, what each does best, and which makes the most sense for how you work.

Google and Microsoft are built around ecosystem integration. Proton is built around keeping your data yours, without sacrificing productivity tools.

Google Workspace is the go-to suite for startups, agencies and freelancers, and for good reason. Gmail, Drive, Docs, Sheets, Calendar and Meet work together seamlessly, and several people can edit the same file in real time without emailing versions back and forth.

Google encrypts data at rest and in transit, and offers client-side encryption on eligible editions. But that extra layer requires higher-tier plans and configuration, so in the default experience Google holds the keys. A password manager and VPN aren't part of the suite.

Best for: Teams whose top priorities are real-time collaboration and third-party integrations.

If your work runs on Word, Excel and Outlook, Microsoft 365 needs little introduction. Business Standard includes full desktop apps, and Excel remains the benchmark for heavy spreadsheet work.

That breadth comes with complexity. Licensing, permissions and device management can eat into time a small team or solo developer would rather spend shipping. And like Google, Microsoft's everyday productivity experience isn't built around end-to-end encryption, while Copilot and advanced security features add cost.

Best for: Businesses built on desktop Office apps and established Microsoft workflows.

The honest trade-offs: Proton Docs and Sheets cover core editing and collaboration, not every advanced Excel feature. There's no presentation app yet, and the third-party integration ecosystem is smaller than Google's or Microsoft's.
