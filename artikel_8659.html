Keep Rent & Utilities Near 30% of Gross Pay – Then Test Your Real Budget

Start from 30% of your monthly pay before taxes for rent and utilities together. Past that, the federal government counts your housing as a burden. Then add renter’s insurance, parking, and your commute, and check that take-home pay still covers your debt payments and savings. If it doesn’t, aim lower before you sign a lease.

A landlord’s approval won’t tell you whether you can afford a place. Many landlords ask for monthly income of three times the rent, which allows rent up to a third of your pay and ignores utilities.

The federal government’s line is tighter than the landlord’s. The Department of Housing and Urban Development treats housing as a burden once it costs more than 30% of monthly income, utilities included. Rent at 30% of your pay crosses that line once you pay for power and water.

That 30% is also figured on gross pay, before taxes, so rent at that level takes more than 30% of the paycheck that reaches your account.

To test a place, add up everything it costs each month: rent, utilities, renter’s insurance, parking, and the commute from that address. Subtract that total, your debt payments, and your monthly savings from take-home pay to see what’s left for groceries, your phone, and everything else. Then save the security deposit and first month’s rent you’ll need to move in.

In high-cost cities, 30% of your pay may not rent much on your own. A roommate who splits the rent and utilities can put a pricier place within reach, and an address near work can justify higher rent if your commute costs fall by as much as the rent rises. A cheaper neighborhood works too, as long as it saves more on rent than it adds in travel.

Aim lower if you’re paying off student loans or card debt, or building savings. Say you earn $54,000 a year, pay $300 a month on student loans, and spend $150 a month getting to work. Thirty percent of your $4,500 monthly gross is $1,350 for rent and utilities, and with the loans and commute you’d spend $1,800, or 40% of gross pay. Holding rent and utilities near $1,100 instead of $1,350 cuts that total to $1,550 and leaves $250 a month, $3,000 a year, to save.

If what’s left of take-home pay can’t cover groceries and your other bills, the rent is too high, whatever the landlord approves.
