Stone and water governed early human settlement of the desert

Models of early human dispersal rely on climate to predict where populations could settle, with freshwater assumed to govern the timing and geography1,2, particularly across the Saharo-Arabian arid belt, where humid windows episodically opened corridors into the desert interior3. Yet Pleistocene dispersals into ‘Green Arabia’ were brief and geographically constrained4,5, a pattern not explained by water availability alone. Here we show, from an approximately 1,500-km geoarchaeological survey through the Rub’ al Khali of Arabia, the largest sand sea in the world, that the availability of stone for tool manufacture operated as an independent factor on early human settlement. Palaeolake deposits were recorded at 55 of 125 localities investigated, yet no lithic artefacts and no knappable raw materials were found along the transect, with ground-surface visibility exceeding 95%, despite abundant freshwater and contemporary occupation at the desert margins. Spatial modelling of the Peninsula supports the inference that settlement tracked the co-occurrence of water and lithic raw materials rather than water alone. Across the Peninsula, 57% of water-accessible terrain lies on formations that lack knappable stone and adding raw-material availability raises discrimination of known site locations from an area under the curve of 0.695 to 0.768. Convergent patterns from Africa, North America and Asia indicate that the constraint is not peculiar to Arabia. Dispersal models that omit raw-material geology may systematically overestimate the habitable range of stone-tool-dependent populations.

Predictive models of early human dispersal across Arabia, built primarily on palaeohydrological reconstructions of palaeolake and palaeoriver networks and on climate-envelope reconstructions of precipitation and net primary productivity, identify interior basins as high-probability zones for Middle Palaeolithic (MP) and Middle Stone Age (MSA) occupation3,6, the broadly contemporaneous prepared-core industries of western Eurasia and Africa, respectively; because Arabian assemblages show affinities with both, the joint term MP/MSA is used throughout. The expectation that these palaeolakes attracted human settlement reflects decades of palaeoclimatic research demonstrating that orbital forcing periodically transformed the Arabian Peninsula into landscapes of lakes, wetlands and grasslands7,8,9. Geographical Information System (GIS)-based models built on this record have shown predictive success at the Rub’ al Khali margins, where dated MP/MSA assemblages occur alongside lacustrine deposits in the western basin10,11. Yet these models have never been verified against systematic ground survey in the desert interior, nor have they been evaluated against the distribution of knappable raw materials, a resource fundamental to Palaeolithic subsistence12 and consumed at rates that varied with the technology used13,14. Although it has been recognized that water would not be the sole determining factor in site location6, no study has evaluated this other variable.

The Rub’ al Khali occupies around 650,000 km2 of the southern Arabian Peninsula, bounded by the Asir and Yemen highlands to the west, the Dhofar Nejd and Hadhramaut plateaus to the south, the Hajar Mountains to the east and the Tuwaiq escarpment and Ad Dahna desert to the north. Its interior is dominated by longitudinal dune systems separated by interdunal corridors in which Quaternary sediments are exposed by deflation. During Pleistocene humid intervals, precession-driven intensification of the Indian Ocean monsoon raised regional water tables and formed lakes across the basin7,8,9,15, the most extensive of which are documented for Marine Isotope Stage 516. Archaeological evidence for these intervals is concentrated at the desert margins, where MP/MSA assemblages occur alongside dated lacustrine deposits at Mundafan10,11, Jebel Faya17,18, Umm al Sha’al19 and the Dhofar Nejd Plateau5,20. These assemblages are technologically diverse: Levallois and other prepared-core reduction strategies predominate, with Nubian Levallois industries of African affinity documented in the south20,21 and assemblages of western Asian character in the north4. The interior basin itself, however, has never been surveyed systematically and its archaeological status has been inferred from the margins rather than tested.

To test the prediction that interior basins were high-probability zones for MP/MSA occupation, a systematic geoarchaeological reconnaissance of the desert interior was conducted along an approximately 1,500-km transect through the central Rub’ al Khali (Fig. 1). The survey targeted palaeolake deposits correlated to sections independently dated to about 122,000–67,000  years ago16, focusing on the MP/MSA. This period corresponds to both the dated palaeolake chronologies and documented Homo sapiens dispersals into Arabia17,20,22. Because the nearest known knappable outcrops lie more than 200 km from the surveyed basins, the interior decouples water from stone. This study therefore tests whether the co-occurrence of freshwater and knappable stone, rather than freshwater alone, accounts for the distribution of archaeologically visible MP/MSA occupation in the Arabian interior. If freshwater alone governed settlement, MP/MSA populations occupying the adjacent margins would be expected to have left archaeologically visible traces in the interior during humid phases. Abundant evidence for palaeolakes was recorded, but no knappable raw materials, no lithic artefacts and no trace of human occupation were found. An independent spatial model, validated against 301 MP/MSA findspots in adjacent Oman, indicates that settlement was concentrated in zones where both water and raw materials co-occurred; a Peninsula-wide analysis shows the same pattern across 363 documented MP/MSA sites.

The expedition investigated 125 localities along the survey transect, of which 55 yielded palaeolake deposits (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). The eastern localities lie among sections independently dated by luminescence to Marine Isotope Stage 5, between about 122 thousand years ago (ka) and 67 ka (ref. 16). Contemporary occupation is documented at the Rub’ al Khali margins at Mundafan (about 96–77 ka)10,11, Umm al Sha’al (about 87–71 ka)19, Jebel Faya (about 134–80 ka)17,18,23 and the Dhofar Nejd plateau (about 109–95 ka)5,20 (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Table 4 and Supplementary Information, section 6). Freshwater lakes and nearby populations therefore seem to have overlapped regionally across this interval, yet the surveyed interior produced no archaeologically visible occupation evidence. Carbonate lacustrine sediments, ostrich eggshell fragments, and freshwater mollusc assemblages dominated by Melanoides tuberculata and Corbicula fluminalis were observed at palaeolake localities (Fig. 3). Sedimentological features, including bioturbated limestones, dolomitic microbial build-ups, gypsum rosettes indicative of terminal desiccation and trace fossils characteristic of shallow lacustrine environments, are consistent with previous palaeoenvironmental interpretations. These waterbodies ranged from freshwater at highstand, indicated by the mollusc and limestone facies, to saline during desiccation. More localities were recorded at equivalent elevations to the deposits investigated by ref. 16 and represent fragmented remnants of the same palaeolake systems (Extended Data Fig. 3 and Supplementary Information, section 3).

The transect started at isolated outcrops of Jurassic Hanifa Formation, which provided lithic raw material for the MP/MSA occupation at nearby Mundafan10,11. Approximately 80 km further northeast, the last bedrock outcrops were documented (Lower Cretaceous Wasia Formation); these yielded heavily weathered silicified sandstones, of limited suitability for stone tool manufacture (sites 8 and 9; Supplementary Information, section 1). East of these outcrops, the chert-bearing Mesozoic–Eocene formations dip beneath a thick sequence of Miocene to Quaternary siliciclastic, carbonatic and evaporitic sediments. In the central Rub’ al Khali, only small, isolated remnants of these younger basin-fill deposits are exposed between dune fields and none contains knappable lithologies (Fig. 4). Despite systematic reconnaissance and high ground visibility, no lithic artefacts or other cultural materials were recorded at any surveyed locality and no lithic raw materials were found throughout the transect, whether as exposed bedrock or transported clasts, except at its westernmost and easternmost ends. Knappable stone was confined to 10 of 125 localities (8%): two Wasia Formation outcrops in the west and eight eastern-margin localities comprising one Dammam Formation bedrock exposure and seven alluvial gravel deposits carrying reworked chert. No lithic artefacts were recovered at these localities either. Across the intervening around 800 km of interior basin, surface lithologies consist exclusively of carbonatic, evaporitic and siliciclastic facies, none of which possesses the conchoidal fracture properties required for stone tool manufacture (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Information, section 1).

Collectively, these results indicate that the central basin of the Rub’ al Khali produced no archaeologically visible evidence of occupation throughout the MP/MSA, despite (1) the abundance of freshwater lakes, (2) the presence of contemporary human occupations around the desert margins (Fig. 1b) and (3) the demonstrated capacity of MP/MSA populations for long-distance lithic transport24,25,26,27,28. Ground visibility exceeded 95% across deflationary interdunal surfaces and the identification of centimetre-scale gastropods and ostrich eggshell fragments suggests that lithic artefacts would have been conspicuous had they been present. Nor can the vacancy be attributed to a reliance on perishable technologies in place of stone. Organic implements are themselves manufactured and maintained with lithic tools, so any resident toolkit would have generated the durable debris of edge production and resharpening. The same populations, moreover, left abundant lithic assemblages wherever knappable stone was available at the desert margins. Future fieldwork may yet document small logistical or procurement sites in the interior but, against a systematic pattern of absence across 55 palaeolake localities spanning the full transect, such finds would represent low-intensity events rather than evidence of sustained settlement.

This absence reflects the surface geology of the central basin, which is composed exclusively of carbonate and evaporite formations lacking cryptocrystalline silicate lithologies suitable for stone tool manufacture29,30. Interior palaeolakes were fed by groundwater rather than surface runoff, as indicated by fine-grained lacustrine sediments lacking coarse clastic input (Supplementary Information, section 2). Permanent lakes persist in the Rub’ al Khali interior today, fed exclusively by artesian groundwater31 and Nefud palaeolakes were similarly groundwater-fed systems without surface connectivity32. Unlike river-fed lakes, groundwater-fed systems have no fluvial mechanism to transport knappable stone from distant sources.

The vacancy is not confined to the MP. The interior was extensively rewatered during the Early Holocene humid phase, with perennial lakes and river systems reforming across the Rub’ al Khali33, yet the central basin surveyed here yielded no cultural material of any period, Lower Palaeolithic, MP or Holocene. Holocene occupation concentrated instead in the oasis basins and interdune playas of the Nefud to the north34 and on the northern margin the few reported Neolithic traces take the form of ephemeral hearth scatters that remain largely uninvestigated35. Where mobile pastoralists occupied these northern basins, they provisioned their toolkits from small clasts, importing finer raw material from distant and unknown sources and left no structural remains, only lightweight and ephemeral camps35. The southern margin, where knappable stone crops out locally, records a different outcome. At Maitan, on the incised Eocene limestone plateau at the southern edge of the sand sea, Dammam Formation flint nodules occur in the surveyed area and are the probable source of the local industry, alongside finer flint drawn from outcrops of the same formation tens to hundreds of kilometres distant. High artefact densities and grinding equipment indicate recurrent occupation during the Holocene humid phase, although no architectural features were recorded36. This persistence of absence across climatically distinct humid phases supports the inference that raw-material availability, rather than water, was the limiting factor on archaeologically visible occupation.

Occupation around the Jubbah palaeolake in northern Saudi Arabia provides the positive counterpart. Like the Rub’ al Khali basins, Jubbah is an interior palaeolake system isolated from regional drainage. Unlike the Rub’ al Khali, however, it sits on Saq Sandstone that supplied locally abundant quartzite and MP populations occupied its shorelines37,38. The Duqm region of central Oman provides the inverse: chert is abundant and of comparable quality to the Dhofar Nejd, yet MP/MSA sites are rare39, a pattern attributed to the scarcity of freshwater40. Where the Rub’ al Khali has water without stone, Duqm has stone with little water and archaeological site density is very low in both cases. The same asymmetry appears in the transect itself: the ten localities at which knappable stone was recorded, all at the basin margins, likewise yielded no artefacts, indicating that the presence of stone alone was no more sufficient than the presence of water alone. On the Dhofar Nejd, MSA populations occupied chert-rich landscapes during Marine Isotope Stage 5c but occupation appears to have ceased when freshwater failed during Marine Isotope Stage 5b, despite continued access to raw material5. In each case, settlement tracks the co-occurrence of water and knappable stone; where either resource is missing, occupation is rare or absent. The two resources are only weakly correlated across the model domain (Pearson’s r = −0.15), so their joint distribution is not reducible to a single underlying variable such as piedmont setting or drainage incision, because a shared geomorphological control would produce covariance between them and none is present.

The observation that the availability of knappable stone influences settlement independently of water is not unique to Arabia. On the Messak Plateau of Libya, 91% of MSA occurrences concentrate near quartzarenite outcrops despite abundant water across adjacent lowlands41. On the Tibetan Plateau, Terminal Pleistocene occupation was governed by the localized availability of gneissic pebbles at glacial meltwater confluences, the only knappable material in a granite-dominated landscape42. In the Eastern Desert of Egypt, MSA sites are associated strictly with specific sandstone formations and are absent from water-rich but lithic-poor areas43. At Taramsa in the Egyptian Nile Valley and Jojosi in South Africa, MSA groups made repeated, specialized visits to single raw-material outcrops, ignoring other locally available lithologies to export prepared blanks across the wider landscape44,45. In eastern North America, Clovis Palaeoindian settlement systems seem to have been centred around stone outcrops, with raw-material sources functioning as the hubs around which subsistence strategies were organized46. These convergent patterns, across different geological substrates, hydrological regimes and chronological contexts, provide analogical support for a ‘tethering’ effect whereby stone-tool-dependent populations become tied to raw-material sources47. In most of these settings water was widely available, so the distribution of knappable stone alone structured settlement. Arabia represents the more constrained case in which both resources were patchy and occupation required their co-occurrence. The prepared-core technologies that dominated Arabian MP/MSA assemblages sharpened this dependence further, because they consumed substantial raw material in core preparation10,11,23,48.

The Arabian Peninsula seems to have comprised a patchwork of discrete zones where water and lithic raw materials co-occurred, attracting and sustaining MP/MSA settlement. Existing dispersal models prioritize topographic variables, climate and water availability1,2,3,49,50 but do not incorporate lithic resource distributions. Although MP/MSA populations could transport stone over long distances24,26,28, the quantities moved decline steeply with distance. In well-documented MP systems, most raw material in an assemblage (60–98% by frequency) derives from sources within about 5 km, whereas sources beyond 20–30 km rarely contribute more than 5% and usually 1–2% (ref. 51). In western Europe about 100 km represents a characteristic upper threshold on transfer, and in central Europe, where the longest recorded distances reach 300 km, transfers at that range account for 7 of 100 recorded occurrences and exceed 1% of an assemblage in only three cases51. Neutral models of embedded procurement reproduce such distances from ordinary foraging mobility at three to four times the foraging radius, without invoking deliberate provisioning or exchange52,53.

Occasional acquisition and sustained provisioning are distinguished not by distance alone but by the technological stage at which material arrives. Locally procured stone enters sites as nodules or roughed-out blocks and is reduced on the spot, with all stages of the reduction sequence represented. Material from beyond 20 km arrives as blanks or finished tools, heavily resharpened, with the final stages of reduction overrepresented51. The first signature indicates a resident lithic economy, the second occasional acquisition embedded in wider mobility. Sustained occupation of the Rub’ al Khali interior would require the first, across more than 200 km between disconnected groundwater-fed lakes with no intermediate sources. Where MP settlement was drawn to water on stone-poor ground it operated at a far smaller scale: at Kůlna in Moravia, occupied for its mineral springs rather than its lithic resources, local material contributes only 1–3% of the assemblage and the nearest silicate source lies 10 km away, a distance covered in a single day51. Such long-range acquisition can furnish occasional pieces, but it cannot supply the daily tool production of a resident population over generations.

The dual-resource pattern observed here need not have a single cause. If MP/MSA populations entering Arabia were small, as might be expected of foragers colonizing unfamiliar territory54,55, settlement in favourable zones may never have generated the demographic pressure required to push groups into neighbouring regions lacking essential resources, so that populations were drawn into dual-resource zones rather than forced out of them56. Landscape knowledge would have reinforced the same tendency, because foragers accumulate and transmit detailed environmental knowledge over generations57,58 and awareness that the interior lacked knappable stone may itself have functioned as a deterrent59,60. Terrain roughness has been proposed as an influence on habitat selection, because rugged landscapes juxtapose ecotones and so support higher biome diversity and a wider range of foods61. Arabian MP/MSA sites do occupy somewhat rougher ground than background locations, although the effect is small (Cliff’s delta = 0.197) and roughness runs inversely to the distribution of knappable stone across the Peninsula (Spearman’s rho = −0.217), knappable lithologies occurring disproportionately on low-relief terrain, so it cannot generate the observed association and adds no discriminatory power to the dual-resource model (Extended Data Fig. 4). The ecological mechanism itself cannot be tested against the palaeoproductivity record presently available for Arabia. The demographic and landscape-learning processes would have operated together with the targeting of dual-resource areas, concentrating MP/MSA populations in favourable zones rather than in the resource-poor interior (Supplementary Information, section 7). Whatever their relative contribution, the consequence is the same, in that populations occupying northern and central Arabia and those in the south would have experienced limited demographic exchange across the archaeologically vacant interior.

Incorporating raw-material geology substantially reduces the area suitable for settlement. Thus, when raw-material geology is factored into existing climate-based models, more than half the area conventionally classified as habitable was of low potential for sustained MP/MSA occupation under the dual-resource model. This figure is conservative, because formations were counted as raw-material-bearing wherever any knappable lithology is documented, whether or not it is available at the surface across their mapped extent, so the water-accessible area lacking usable stone is unlikely to be smaller than 57%. At the 1:2,000,000 resolution of the source geology and given the binary nature of the raw-material classification, the estimate is necessarily approximate, but the qualitative result, that a substantial majority of the water-accessible Peninsula lacks knappable stone, is not sensitive to it. This correction extends beyond Arabia to any arid landscape on which early humans settled.

These results indicate that ‘Green Arabia’ was a lithic archipelago whose islands were defined as much by geology as by climate; dispersal models that omit lithic raw-material distributions may systematically overestimate the area habitable by stone-tool-dependent populations. For Pleistocene arid landscapes, the viable territory was not merely where water occurred, but the fraction of those areas where water and knappable stone coincided. This fraction was not fixed, however, but would have varied with how much raw material a given tool technology consumed13,14. The settlement patterns documented here are consistent with populations tethered to raw-material sources. More economical reduction systems, or composite technologies that extend the working life of a small amount of stone, may later have eased that dependence, although this lies beyond the record examined here48. This dependence governed not only where people settled but how far they could spread, with dispersal advancing between dual-resource islands and stalling where those islands lay beyond reach of one another5,40. Whether early human populations settled a desert landscape depended as much on the stone exposed across its surface as on the water that filled its basins.

The Rub’ al Khali crossing expedition team conducted systematic geoarchaeological reconnaissance along an approximately 1,500-km transect through the central Rub’ al Khali between 15 November and 20 December 2023 (35 days). Route length was measured from the continuous GPS track recorded throughout the traverse and archived with the survey documentation; the straight-line distance between the western and eastern termini is about 1,070 km. The expedition comprised eight team members using three purpose-built expedition vehicles equipped with fuel, water (about 1,700 litres) and supplies enabling six weeks of autonomous operation. Route planning involved analyzing satellite imagery to chart a passage through the dune fields (Google Earth, Sentinel-2), targeting the palaeolake belt documented by ref. 16, in which several sections had been independently dated by luminescence.

Localities were identified through satellite imagery analysis, searching for visible lacustrine deposits and published palaeolake locations. Survey strategy comprised systematic pedestrian reconnaissance within corridors of about 500-m radius centred on exposed lacustrine sediments. Time allocated per locality varied from 10 min to 5 h depending on exposure extent and accessibility. Survey corridors totalled around 43 km2 of systematically reconnoitred area (calculated as π × (0.5 km)2 per locality × 55 palaeolake localities). Combined with more than 95% ground-surface visibility, this represents survey intensity comparable to published regional investigations. Surface facies and terrain were classified in the field by direct observation, following standard geological criteria for lacustrine, evaporitic, fluvial-alluvial and aeolian depositional environments; determinations were made by a field geologist (R.W.D.) at each locality and recorded photographically. Facies definitions and their distribution along the transect are given in Supplementary Information, section 1.

Survey targets included (1) lithic artefacts or manufacturing debris, (2) mollusc shells, (3) ostrich eggshell fragments, (4) sedimentary features characteristic of lacustrine environments, (5) trace fossils and (6) lithic raw materials suitable for stone tool manufacture. Malacological identifications were made exclusively from field photographs. This is a methodological limitation, because assemblage composition is more informative than the presence or absence of individual taxa and the identifications should be treated as indicative rather than definitive (Supplementary Information, section 8). Geographic coordinates were recorded using GPS (±5–10-m accuracy). All features were photographed with GPS geotagging. Aerial documentation used drone photography. No physical samples were collected.

The Oman dataset derives from 44 archaeological survey campaigns conducted between 2002 and 2024 across six governorates (Dhofar, Ad Dakhliyah, Al Wusta, Adh Dhahirah, Ash Sharqiyah and Musandam), comprising 13 research campaigns, 27 environmental impact assessment surveys and four reconnaissance surveys (Supplementary Table 3 and Supplementary Information, section 4). Coverage encompassed diverse geological and hydrological contexts, including mountain piedmont, plateau, escarpment, interior gravel plain and sand sea settings and recorded both localities at which archaeological material or knappable raw material was present and localities at which neither occurred. This combination of positive and null observations provides the empirical basis for the raw-material probability model. At each locality, the presence or absence of knappable lithic raw material was recorded alongside any archaeological material and locations were logged by GPS. Raw-material occurrences documented across the survey programme are mapped in Supplementary Fig. 4 and provided the training data for the weights-of-evidence analysis; the 301 MSA findspots recorded across the same programme were used to validate the resulting occupation potential model.

Chronological control derives from spatial and stratigraphic correlation with the independently dated sections of ref. 16 (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). Five surveyed palaeolake localities in the eastern interior (sites 61–65) lie in the cluster of dated sections 14.3, 14.7, 15.1 and 15.3, at 6–31 km from the nearest dated section; these sections span Marine Isotope Stage 5, from about 122 ka to 67 ka and sites 61 and 62 lie within 8 km of a dated deposit. Survey localities at equivalent elevations (within ±15–20 m, accounting for Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM) vertical accuracy and postdepositional deflation) and exhibiting identical lacustrine facies, such as grey marls, carbonate platforms and freshwater mollusc assemblages, are interpreted as erosionally fragmented remnants of the same palaeolake systems, correlated to the dated interval by their position on the regional piezometric gradient (Extended Data Fig. 3 and Supplementary Information, section 3).

To evaluate whether dual-resource availability predicts MP/MSA settlement, a spatial model of human occupation potential for the Arabian Peninsula was developed, integrating water proximity and lithic raw-material occurrence. Water source locations were reconstructed by combining the HydroRIVERS global drainage network64, filtered to rivers with upstream catchment areas equivalent to or more than 1,000 km2. Modelled drainage networks in areas with Quaternary aeolian geology were excluded6. Rivers were buffered by Strahler stream order to approximate channel width65.

Raw-material presence is defined by geological maps (Supplementary Information, sections 5.3 and 5.5). Here the Peninsula-wide model differs in construction from the regional Oman model (Extended Data Fig. 1). In Oman, the probability of raw-material occurrence was estimated by a weights-of-evidence analysis informed by systematic survey of raw-material occurrence. No comparable raw-material survey exists at the Peninsula scale, so the raw-material layer was derived directly from the geological map as the presence or absence of knappable lithologies in each mapped formation. Lithic raw-material suitability was assessed on the basis of conchoidal fracture properties characteristic of cryptocrystalline silicates, namely chert, quartzite, quartz, chalcedony, rhyolite and quartzitic or silicified limestone or sandstone. Because this layer is defined from geology independently of the archaeological sites, the distribution of documented MP/MSA sites provides a test of the model rather than an input to it.

Human occupation potential was classified for each resource layer independently. For both the Oman and the Peninsula-wide models, water-based potential was defined by distance thresholds corresponding to the 50th, 75th and 95th percentiles of observed site-to-water distances (high, 3; medium, 2; and low, 1 potential, respectively). In Oman, weights-of-evidence raw-material probabilities were classified into three potential classes by K-means clustering. All raw-material types were treated as equal; in the Peninsula-wide model, geological units with at least one of these raw materials were assigned a value of 1. This represents a necessary simplification of lithic resources, because knappability, nodule size, abundance and surface accessibility differ substantially between lithologies and the equal weighting is therefore a conservative limitation of the classification. These layers were combined into a single human occupation potential surface that is effectively limited to where raw materials are present and scaled by proximity to water resources (Supplementary Information, section 5). Correlation between the two resource layers is weak and slightly negative across the Oman model domain (Pearson’s r = −0.15 across the model domain), indicating that water-proximity and raw-material probability vary largely independently rather than jointly expressing a single geomorphological setting.

The dual-resource human occupation model was validated against the observed distribution of MP/MSA sites using Monte Carlo simulation. For each of 999 iterations, modelled human occupation potential values were sampled at random locations in the study area, equal in number to the known sites. A 95% confidence envelope was calculated from the ensemble of simulated distributions and compared with the observed distribution to test whether sites are preferentially associated with zones of high modelled potential. To further confirm that including raw material availability improves prediction of site locations across the Arabian Peninsula, two logistic regression models were compared. Known archaeological site locations were paired with a set of randomly generated background points (n = 10 × known site count) sampled from the study extent with a 100-m buffer around known sites to avoid pseudo-absences. Of the 363 compiled sites, 362 fall within the modelled extent and enter the comparison. Water-based potential values and raw-material availability-based potential values were independently extracted at each site and background location. A baseline model predicted archaeological site presence based on water proximity alone and a full model added raw-material availability. Model improvement was assessed by likelihood ratio test and discriminative performance of each model was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) (Extended Data Fig. 2).

The sensitivity of the resulting area of human occupation potential to raw-material classification error was assessed by a bootstrap analysis that flipped the presence or absence value of a randomly selected 5% and 10% of the geological context polygons, drawn uniformly across the mapped geology rather than targeted at units of higher classification uncertainty. The percentage of the area with water-based occupation potential retained when filtered for raw-material presence was calculated for each iteration and the observed percentage based on the raw-material availability map described above was compared with the bootstrapped values for the various label error rates (Extended Data Fig. 5).

Topographic roughness was evaluated as a potential confounding variable with raw-material availability. Elevation data from the GEDTM 120 m global model66 were reprojected to a Lambert azimuthal equal-area grid centred on the Arabian Peninsula at 104.2-m resolution and roughness was calculated as the difference between maximum and minimum elevation in a 3 × 3 cell neighbourhood (the queen neighbourhood). Cells of zero roughness accounted for 0.29% of the surface and were retained without transformation. Roughness at the 363 documented MP/MSA sites was compared with 3,630 background points drawn under the same 100-m exclusion buffer applied in the AUC analysis. Roughness on raw-material-bearing and barren geology was compared using 19,982 points sampled at random across the mapped geology, weighting each formation by its mapped area (Extended Data Fig. 4b). Nested logistic regression models were then compared by AUC to test whether roughness improved discrimination over the combined water and raw-material model. Because the AUC is a rank statistic rather than a likelihood, it can decrease when an uninformative predictor is added.

All analyses are observational and correlational; no experimental groups were allocated and no blinding or randomization applied. Monte Carlo validation and the bootstrap sensitivity analysis each used 999 iterations or resamples under a fixed random seed. Background locations for the roughness comparison were drawn at random from the modelled extent and the reported test statistics vary within approximately 0.02 of the values given across repeated draws. Statistical tests are two-sided throughout. The principal field result, freshwater present but archaeologically visible occupation absent, was recorded at all 55 palaeolake localities across the full extent of the transect. The complete R workflow and all input datasets are archived at Zenodo and OSF, with the seed set for the Monte Carlo and bootstrap procedures.

Most MP/MSA findspots used in the spatial analyses are surface occurrences lacking stratigraphic or chronometric control. Technocultural attribution relies on the presence of prepared-core technology diagnostic of the MP/MSA: Levallois reduction systems (preferential, recurrent centripetal and convergent), Nubian Levallois types 1 and 2, together with characteristic flake and point products (Levallois flakes, Levallois points and retouched points on Levallois blanks). Assemblages dominated by bifacial reduction (Lower Palaeolithic) or by blade technology on single-platform cores (Upper Palaeolithic) were excluded. Sites with mixed assemblages spanning several periods were included only where MP/MSA components were demonstrably present as discrete spatial concentrations or where diagnostic MP/MSA core technology was distinguishable from other temporal components. The same typological criteria were applied consistently to the Oman dataset (n = 301) and to the Peninsula-wide compilation (n = 363), following the technocultural attributions of the original publications. The Oman records form a subset of the Peninsula-wide compilation. The two analyses are therefore independent in their raw-material layers, the Peninsula surface being derived from 1:2,000,000 regional geology without reference to the Oman survey, rather than in their site samples. Site records in both compilations derive from published survey and excavation reports; the original publication for each locality is given in the reference field of the deposited database.
