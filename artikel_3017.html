Ford's BlueOval Battery Park Michigan is shipping its first lithium iron phosphate cells to Louisville for Fathom prototypes, a key milestone for the automaker's next-generation EV.

This week, Ford began shipments of the first lithium iron phosphate electric vehicle battery cells from its new BlueOval Battery Park in Marshall, Michigan, to its Louisville Assembly Plant in Kentucky. There, the prismatic cells will power early Ford Fathom electric pickup truck prototypes and validation vehicles. The automaker invited me into the plant to meet the people who build the batteries and to see how the so-called “jelly roll” is made.

In 2025, consumers lost access to the clean vehicle tax rebates of the Inflation Reduction Act, but the program and its incentives remain in the law for automakers and suppliers. So bringing this US-based, domestically owned battery plant online is kind of a big deal for Ford. It’s an important milestone for the automaker’s plan to make its new Universal Electric Vehicle, or UEV, platform a reality and to bring the Fathom — its upcoming electric pickup truck — to market in 2027 at a price that EV-eager customers can stomach.

Ford expects to sell the upcoming Fathom at an estimated price of $28,350 (well, $29,945, including the $1,595 destination and delivery fee), with the first preorder expected to open early next year.

BlueOval Battery Park Michigan is a $3 billion, 2 million-square-foot facility with a targeted annual production capacity of 20 gigawatt-hours. That’s a whole lot of batteries. Ford is expected to employ 1,700 people at the site across two 12-hour shifts, running around the clock to reach that capacity.

The plant is a clean facility, so getting in meant donning a clean suit, mask, hairnet, gloves and two layers of shoe booties. I then crossed special magnetic and sticky floor panels designed to pull any metal particles off the bottoms of those booties, before passing through multiple air-blower airlocks that blasted my body clean of loose dust.

From the outside, the building is huge and difficult to take in completely. On the inside, the scale truly comes into focus as I stare down a massive hallway just under a mile long end to end. Raw materials — rolls of copper and aluminum sheet, plus the chemical powders that coat them — enter the plant at the eastern end, move in a straight line through the facility, and completed battery packs exit at the western end. The first 0.7 mile of the fab belongs to cell production, split between isolated cathode and anode lines that join halfway through for final cell assembly.

Ford engineers, battery experts and executives tell me that the automaker chose lithium iron phosphate, or LFP, chemistry in a prismatic (rectangular) format for its affordability and structural benefits. LFP uses no nickel or cobalt, which keeps costs down, and Ford found the chemistry well suited to bidirectional charging.

To keep costs low enough to deliver a reasonably priced car, Ford couldn’t use a Chinese supplier and still qualify for IRA manufacturing credits. So it licensed the technology from one, working with a company called CATL, which one Ford exec described as “the world’s leading battery manufacturer,” whose batteries power 30 million vehicles worldwide.

Despite working with a Chinese company, Ford says the plant is completely US-owned, staffed and operated and is totally IRA-compliant, with no foreign entity of concern, or FEOC, involvement beyond the licensing agreement.

Charles Poon, who leads vehicle hardware engineering, said Ford didn’t simply copy a catalog part.

“We designed a unique cell that does not exist in the CATL catalog, specifically for the UEV,” Poon said, adding that the taller and simpler cell delivers “the energy density, the affordability, the durability that [Ford is] looking for” and allows the automaker to more completely integrate the cells and battery pack into the vehicle’s design.

Ford also had to build the supply chain, Poon said, sourcing an IRA-compliant stream for a battery chemistry that is only just entering the mainstream in North America, and working with approved global partners and North American suppliers.

On the cathode and anode assembly lines, meter-wide rolls of copper and aluminum are first coated with a chemical slurry that is baked on. The large rolls are then split into 10 smaller rolls, with tabs notched into their exposed edges at set intervals. As the continuous rolls pass through multiple machines like gigantic cassette players, AI-powered vision systems watch the foil for coating defects, scratches and wrinkles, marking problem sections so they can be automatically removed.

Next, the cathode and anode foils are layered and rolled into a cylinder, then pressed flat into what Ford calls a jelly roll. Imagine a roll of toilet paper mashed in a hydraulic press, and you’ll have a good idea of what it looks like: a flat rectangle with rounded ends.

The jelly rolls are then gathered in pairs, and the previously exposed tabs are folded together and ultrasonically welded to more solid terminals, which are in turn laser-welded to a cap. The jelly roll couple is then laser-sealed into an aluminum box, about the size and weight of a masonry brick, to form a nearly complete prismatic cell. Engineers vacuum out the air, check for leaks by pressurizing the cell with helium, and fill it with liquid electrolyte. Bob’s your uncle: you have a finished LFP prismatic cell.

Ford says the assembly lines can deliver 40 cells every minute, which plant manager Tracy Church says makes BlueOval Battery Park “among the fastest LFP prismatic lines outside China.”

From the cell line, completed prismatic cells would normally pass through a door and straight into pack manufacturing, where full battery packs with integrated “e-box” electronics, integrated cooling and wiring are built. That part of the plant is still undergoing validation, so for now Ford is shipping just the cells to Louisville, not complete battery packs. The company plans to have pack manufacturing up and running in Marshall ahead of Fathom’s launch, with full pack deliveries to Louisville planned in 2027. Church told me that the plant aims to produce 50 battery packs per hour.

Ford hasn’t officially specified the energy capacity of the cells or the completed battery packs and wouldn’t comment when asked about expected range for the Fathom. The automaker wouldn’t even confirm how many cells go into the EV’s pack, but I estimate between 90 and 108 cells based on a cutaway of the finalized unit I examined (but wasn’t permitted to photograph) during my tour. I expect Ford is holding off on finalizing details until closer to the EV’s 2027 launch window.

Battery Park Michigan isn’t just sending cells to Kentucky; it’s also sending expertise. Ford’s Glendale Kentucky plant — which used to build batteries for the F-150 Lightning full-size electric pickup — is currently being retooled to build LFP cells for Ford Energy for large-scale energy storage applications, and the lessons Ford learns in Michigan are already being shared with the manufacturing teams there.

Ford has been in the EV business for over 15 years. In 2011, it revealed the Focus Electric at CES. 2019 brought the debut of the Mustang Mach-E — which Ford recently found is holding up well after years on the road with minimal battery degradation — followed by the arrival of the F-150 Lightning in 2021. This isn’t the brand’s first electric rodeo, but executives tell me that the automaker has never had this much control and ownership over its battery production, and it’s hoping that will make the difference as it prepares the next-gen UEV vehicles.
