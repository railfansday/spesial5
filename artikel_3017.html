ULA Vulcan Launch Ends in Disaster for Amazon's Satellite Internet Ambitions

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

Amazon was counting on ULA to get its Leo satellites back on track. It shouldn't have.

By Rich Smith – Nov 21, 2026 at 9:27PM EST

A crisis was forming in the space industry.

For months, Space Exploration Technologies (SPCX -7.69%) CEO Elon Musk had been making noises about ending commercial launches of its Falcon 9 rocket -- the rocket that, in 2025, launched more than 80% of all payloads to orbit worldwide. At the time, rockets from rival companies Arianespace, Blue Origin, and United Launch Alliance were all grounded for a variety of technical glitches.

Nine months ago, United Launch Alliance (ULA) suffered an anomaly with one of its Vulcan Centaur rockets, forcing it to pause operations while it worked out a fix. But at long last, ULA was ready to return to flight. In a mission slated for Nov. 15, Vulcan would return to flight, carrying a payload of 40 Leo communications satellites into orbit for customer Amazon (AMZN -4.10%).

A successful launch would advance Amazon's effort to build a satellite communications system to rival SpaceX's Starlink. At the same time, it would alleviate the concerns of space companies worried that Falcon 9's retirement would deprive them of access to space. With ULA back in the race, there'd once again be an alternative to SpaceX.

That's what a successful launch would have meant -- but it's not what actually happened.

Instead, for the first time in its history as a space company, ULA suffered a total mission failure -- all cargo lost. As ULA explained, its Vulcan rocket "lost telemetry and the mission at approximately 10 minutes into flight," although the real problem occurred before that.

Vulcan Centaur includes two stages: BE-4 engines from Blue Origin power the Vulcan first stage, supplemented by RL10 side boosters from L3Harris(LHX -2.59%). In flight, the RL10s are first to drop off the rocket, followed by the entire first stage detaching approximately six minutes into flight. Then the Centaur second stage takes over and lifts the payload the rest of the way into orbit.

After several days of examining the data, ULA now believes the RL10 boosters and the second-stage Centaur both performed nominally. The problem must therefore lie with the Vulcan first stage -- specifically, preliminary indications as of Wednesday suggest Vulcan's fuel supply to the BE-4 engines, rather than the engines themselves, was the culprit. This first stage performed poorly in the boost stage, failing to accelerate the vehicle sufficiently for the Centaur to lift the payload all the way into orbit.

For Amazon, either culprit would be bad news.

Aiming to accelerate its deployment of Leo satellites so that it can begin competing with SpaceX in satellite internet, Amazon splashed out billions of dollars to reserve launches on 38 Vulcan Centaur rockets, 18 Arianespace Ariane 6es, and anywhere from 12 to 27 Blue Origin New Glenns.

It's also reserved launches on some of ULA's older Atlas V rockets. So far, those have done most of the work of putting nearly 400 Amazon Leo satellites into orbit (with significant help from SpaceX's Falcon 9, which Amazon reluctantly added to the lineup in 2023). The problem is, ULA is just about out of Atlas V rockets (and isn't building more). And SpaceX Falcon 9 launches are all booked up through 2028 (and SpaceX also doesn't plan to add more).

This leaves Amazon largely dependent on ULA's Vulcan Centaur -- which just proved itself unreliable. And this problem may get even bigger; after all, the same BE-4 engine that appears to be at the root of Vulcan's problems also powers Blue Origin's New Glenn rocket. And that could put New Glenn on the sidelines as well.

(Or it may not. While the investigation is still at an early stage, as of Wednesday, ULA believes "preliminary indications" suggest Vulcan's fuel supply to the BE-4 engines, and not the engines themselves, is the culprit. If that's the case, then Blue Origin's New Glenn may not be affected.)

Still, until this is all resolved, it appears that Amazon currently has no reliable means of putting Leo satellites into orbit.

In the meantime, SpaceX Starlink, already the leader in satellite internet, is likely to remain so for the foreseeable future. More than that: the longer Amazon is forced to cool its heels down here on Earth, the wider the gap between SpaceX and [insert name of literally anyone else who might want to compete with SpaceX].

Amazon's pain, I fear, is going to be SpaceX's gain.
