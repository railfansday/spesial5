Sneezing After You Put Up the Tree? You Might Have Christmas Tree Syndrome

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

A Christmas tree may explain those December sniffles. Learn what Christmas tree syndrome feels like and how to reduce common triggers at home.

Being at home during the holidays can be a magical time of year for many. But for some, it also means dealing with allergies. Christmas tree syndrome could be responsible. Sneezing might start soon after the Christmas decorations go up, sometimes happening around the same time every year.

Dr. Racheal Onah, ND, consulting physician with RealTime Labs, explains what causes it, which symptoms to watch for, and how families can reduce their exposure.

Christmas tree syndrome refers to allergy and asthma symptoms that appear after putting up a Christmas tree or holiday decorations. Onah explains that the term is a nickname, not an official diagnosis, though the reactions are real.

Real Christmas trees can bring mold, pollen, and other allergens into the house. A study published in the Annals of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology identified several types of mold on Christmas trees, including some that can cause allergies.

“Outdoors, molds play a part in nature by breaking down dead organic matter such as fallen leaves and dead trees, but indoors, mold growth should be avoided,” explains the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency. “Molds reproduce by means of tiny spores; the spores are invisible to the naked eye and float through outdoor and indoor air.”

Symptoms that get worse around the tree but improve away from home may point to an allergy. Onah says frequent sneezing and itchy eyes without a fever suggest an allergic reaction rather than a virus. A cold is more likely to bring a fever, sore throat, or body aches.

Onah suggests checking for mold or musty smells before buying a real tree. She recommends having the tree lot shake off loose debris, then rinsing the tree outside and letting it dry before bringing it indoors. Once inside, Onah recommends keeping the tree away from heating vents and running a HEPA air purifier in the same room.

Artificial trees can cause problems, too, depending on where and how they’re stored.

“Artificial trees that sat in a garage or basement for 11 months have had plenty of time to collect dust and moisture,” says Onah. “They are often stored in the original cardboard boxes they came in, which is a material that holds onto humidity and can feed mold on its own.”

Wreaths, garlands, and fabric decorations can collect dust or develop mold in storage as well. Scented candles and artificial snow sprays may also bother people with allergies.

Onah recommends unpacking decorations outside and wiping down artificial branches before setting them up. She says glass, metal, and hard plastic ornaments can be washed with warm water and mild dish soap. Porous decorations with visible mold or a musty smell, such as fabric, paper, or wood pieces, should be thrown away.

“When the season is over, make sure everything is completely dry before you pack it away,” says Onah. “Use sealed plastic bins instead of cardboard, add a few silica gel packets to absorb moisture, and store everything off the floor. If possible, opt to store your bins in a climate-controlled space like an interior closet rather than a garage, attic or basement.”

Anyone dealing with recurring allergy symptoms or worsening asthma should check with a healthcare provider.

“I’d pay extra attention if symptoms linger after the decorations come down,” says Onah. “This could be a sign the decor stirred up a bigger mold problem in the home, like a damp storage area or an HVAC system that’s harboring growth.”
