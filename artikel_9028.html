I tried this kids' 3D printer with my daughter – but would I recommend it?

A truly kids-focused 3D printer, which is accessible and simple to use. It's built fairly well, but comes with some key compromises in regard to size and build complexity. It's a fun toy, and a good introduction to 3D printing for younger children.

I was captivated by the idea of a 3D printer designed for children, so I jumped at the chance to let my nine-year-old try the Funbox. She’s wanted a 3D printer for ages. I’ve held out, partly because of the cost and partly because of the inevitable tide of tiny plastic things that would end up around the house.

The Funbox is a compact, good-looking printer made by Ugee. It's fully enclosed for safety, has a clean air filter, is anti-scald and you can create with just a tap via the app. With Christmas coming up, plenty of parents will be hunting for the right option for their kids, so I want to be honest about whether this is it. Does this compare to the best 3D printers?

The external design is delightful. Ugee has nailed the look for kids: it’s cute, it has minimal buttons and fuss, and mine arrived in baby blue. A pack of stickers in the box lets your child decorate it themselves.

The inside is just as accessible. The build plate is easy to put in place and everything is clearly marked to show you how to get started (although we did make a wrong turn by not actually putting the plate in the first time around as you can see above – oops!).

Loading filament is simple, though it can be a bit fiddly for small fingers. It is pretty much plug and play – setup took me about 20 minutes, and my daughter was able to manage the first print pretty much independently with minimal interference, only some instruction. Bear in mind, the build space is small, so this printer is best for smaller prints. You can keep an eye on your print via the camera, which links to the app – my daughter was fascinated by this part.

Designs come from a marketplace in the Funbox app, available for mobile or tablet. It should be simple to use, but it’s glitchy. On our Huion tablet, the ‘start’ button was hidden behind the notch and there was no way to scroll or move the screen to reach it. That forced us onto the mobile app, which wasn’t ideal when I wanted my daughter to use the printer independently, without borrowing my phone.

For the most part, the Funbox worked as expected. My daughter could pick a print and start it herself, and she really enjoyed the process.

The small build volume is the main constraint. Many designs have to be printed in parts and assembled, which is fine if you stick to small prints. For anything more complex, you’ll need to be ready to help with sticking or putting pieces together. It also prints in a single colour at a time.

Most of the toys we printed turned out well, especially single-piece prints with no moving parts. The detail was good, and the results matched what the marketplace listings promised. Highlights include a cat hair comb, a skull and crossbones hair slide and a phone holder, which would hold my phone if it was the right size (note: must check next time).

Functional toys were a different story. We made a couple of fidget toys that couldn’t do their job without breaking. That could be down to the third-party design, or to the filament being too brittle for the purpose. We couldn’t tell which.

That fits what this printer is: a starter machine built for fun, not one that delivers polished, fully functional prints every time.

We haven’t yet tested the AI 3D model builder, which turns your child’s drawing into a printable 3D model. We’ll cover our experience of it in a separate piece.

The Funbox costs $299/£318 but is often on sale for $279/£289. Whether that’s reasonable depends on who you're buying it for.

For my nine-year-old, I’d rather take on the steeper learning curve of a basic ‘grown-up’ printer like the Bambu Lab A1, which I’d expect to give better-quality prints for similar money. It’s less approachable for a child to use alone, but the results would be worth it for her. For a younger child, though, the Funbox’s ease of use and friendly design make a stronger case, and I think they justify the price.

If you want a genuinely child-focused printer, this is a good option. Just be aware you’re trading away print size and complexity.

Choose your designs carefully. Ugee’s own are reliable, but third-party designs from the marketplace are hit and miss.

If your child is at the upper end of the age range (nine to 12), consider a basic printer from one of the adult-focused brands instead. For younger children, the Funbox is the easier fit.
