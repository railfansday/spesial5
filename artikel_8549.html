Give a used wheelchair, walker, or shower chair to a program that cleans it and passes it to someone who needs it, such as your state’s assistive technology program or a local loan closet. Return anything Medicare or another insurer still rents to its supplier, oxygen equipment included, since it isn’t yours to give.

In fiscal 2025, state assistive technology programs got 104,257 reused devices to 68,164 people, saving them about $36 million, according to the Center for Assistive Technology Act Data Assistance. More than half were mobility, seating, and positioning devices.

Programs like these clean and repair a donated wheelchair or walker, then lend or give it to someone who needs it, often for free. Many thrift stores won’t take medical equipment at all, and Goodwill of Northern New England lists hospital beds, wheelchairs, and walkers among the donations it can’t accept.

Before you give anything away, make sure it’s yours. Under Medicare, most equipment starts out rented, many wheelchairs included, and ownership usually passes to the patient after 13 months of rental payments, according to the Medicare Rights Center. Oxygen equipment never becomes the patient’s, since under Medicare’s oxygen rules the supplier owns it for the whole five-year cycle and takes it back once it’s no longer needed. Anything still rented, through Medicare or another insurer, goes back to its supplier, and the University of Michigan’s medical equipment service warns that the rental charges can keep coming until it does.

Deducting any of these gifts follows IRS Publication 526’s rules for noncash gifts. You can deduct one only if you itemize, and only at its fair market value, what a willing buyer would pay for it used. A gift to a person, such as one you arrange through an exchange listing, doesn’t count. The new deduction for people who don’t itemize, which starts with 2026 returns, covers cash gifts only. Everything else, from cleaning and testing each item to getting a receipt, works as it does for donating any used stuff.

If you’re clearing out a whole house, its furniture, clothes, books, and old computers each go to groups of their own.

Call any of these before you load the car, since their rules on hospital beds and breathing machines differ widely. The loan closet in Howard County, Maryland, refuses hospital beds, CPAP and BiPAP machines, and oxygen concentrators, and REquipment takes no hospital beds. A home hospital bed that nobody near you will take can still go to Project C.U.R.E., which ships it to a clinic abroad.
