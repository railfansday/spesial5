Brian Sullivan: Why the 'real' price of oil is likely $30-$40 higher than where it is now

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

Oil, in reality, is $125 per barrel or higher.

That's the real cost of a barrel of oil because buying the physical commodity is a very different story from a number on traders' screens.

Oil futures are just that: paper contracts indicating a price today, tomorrow, next month or further out.  If you want to buy the physical commodity, it may be a totally different story — and price.

As I've reported, prices for oil delivery have skyrocketed. Last week, a VLCC (aka a "supertanker" in non-shipper parlance) was chartered from the U.S. Gulf Coast to China for $76 million. Another very large crude carrier went for $77 million from the U.S. to South Korea. That's 10 times more than the price of the same trip less than one year ago.

Assuming these ships hold about 2 million barrels of oil, that charter price means each barrel on the ship costs about $38 over the price of the oil itself.

I'm certainly not saying to avoid looking at oil futures prices, but you need to know that the price on the CNBC screen or CNBC.com isn't always the full price being paid. These days, any company that needs oil shipped by sea, rail or truck will pay a hefty premium over the price of the oil itself. So, while the volume of oil moving through Hormuz is closing in on pre-war levels, costs are not. And there's no sign that they're going anywhere but up.

You know what else is up? The stocks of the shipowners. Look at their moves this month and this year.

The supercap oil and gas stock to watch right now is Shell (SHEL). Shell is not a shell of its former self. It's back to being what energy investors wanted: a major player in global energy, focused on what it does best. Shareholders are being rewarded.

Shell hit a new high this week. While analysts remain bullish, the stock is getting close to its target price.

Shell may be underappreciated for its huge liquefied natural gas business. RBC analyst Biraj Borkhataria writes: "Without a sustained resumption in Qatari volumes and with European storage levels below seasonal lows heading into winter, European gas prices continued on an upward trajectory in 3Q26. With HH broadly unchanged throughout the summer, export margins have increased to the widest levels seen since 2022, on our estimates nearly double the level in 2Q26 (~$13/mmbtu vs. $7/mmbtu)."

Borkhataria expects Shell and other companies with huge U.S. LNG positions — TotalEnergies (TTE) and BP (BP) — to benefit from wider margins on exports to Europe.

Speaking of RBC and energy, the firm lists ConocoPhillips as one of its top 30 global stock ideas. The company is covered by analyst Scott Hanold, who believes it has a "returns-focused value proposition, a strong balance sheet, and peer-leading distributions." He loves its "global and diverse" asset base and management's clear "investment propositions." Hanold has a $152 target on COP. That's more than 20% upside.

In oil and gas, refiners and big energy stocks are generating cash for investors, and Morgan Stanley is optimistic more is coming.

The firm says the diesel shortage is now spilling over into gasoline, potentially marking another leg of the "golden age" for refiners. Morgan Stanley has been revising its earnings estimates higher for companies including ExxonMobil (XOM), Valero (VLO), Phillips 66 (PSX) and PBF (PBF).

The entire energy world is watching Europe's natural gas storage levels. The question is whether the continent will have enough gas to make it safely through the winter. Power Insider wrote about the topic in the last issue.

The fact that JPMorgan highlights expected weather patterns should tell you how much temperature matters when it comes to gas storage. The early forecast calls for October and November to be average or slightly warmer than the 10-year average. The red line represents the 10-year average.

Europe has been largely "saved" by mild temperatures over the past few years. Natural gas storage levels are lower this year than in years past, so the thermometer will matter a lot when it comes to how well countries can, ahem, weather the winter. Random, but interesting.
