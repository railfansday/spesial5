America’s fastest ocean liner was purposely sunk off the Florida coast, making it the world’s largest artificial reef

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

The SS United States, once the fastest transatlantic ocean liner, was sunk off Florida in November 2026 to become the world's largest artificial reef.

The SS United States, a 990-foot (302-meter) ocean liner, was sent to the bottom of the Gulf on November 11, 2026.

Okaloosa County, Florida, had scheduled the sinking for November 12, about 22 nautical miles (41 kilometers) southwest of Destin-Fort Walton Beach. It bills the wreck as the world’s largest artificial reef.

The county’s plan put the ship in about 180 feet (55 meters) of water. Her upper deck would sit about 55 feet (17 meters) below the surface.

Her keel was laid on February 8, 1950, at the Newport News Shipbuilding and Dry Dock Company in Virginia. Designed by naval architect William Francis Gibbs, she was the largest passenger ship ever built in the United States.

She left New York on her first crossing in July 1952, bound for Le Havre and Southampton. She covered the 2,949 nautical miles (5,465 kilometers) from the Ambrose light vessel to Bishop Rock lighthouse in 3 days, 10 hours and 40 minutes.

Her average speed topped 35 knots (40 mph, or 65 kilometers per hour), according to Guinness World Records. In trials the month before, she had reached 38.32 knots (44 mph, or 71 kilometers per hour).

On that voyage, she broke the transatlantic speed record in both directions. She was also the first American ship in 100 years to win the Blue Riband, says the SS United States Conservancy.

Built to be converted into a troop transport in the event of war, she could carry 14,000 troops 10,000 miles (16,000 kilometers) without refueling.

The liner was withdrawn from service in 1969. In 1996, she was towed to Philadelphia, where she stayed docked on the Delaware River for nearly three decades.

A rent dispute over the ship’s Philadelphia pier ended up in federal court. The Conservancy, which bought the ship in 2011, had been paying $850 a day under a berthing agreement.

A June 14, 2024, ruling rejected the pier owner’s demand to double the rent to $1,700 a day. It also required the Conservancy to remove the ship by September 12.

“The judge’s decision gives us a very limited window to find a new home for the SS United States and raise the resources necessary to move the ship and keep her safe,” Conservancy President Susan Gibbs said after the ruling.

That October, Okaloosa County signed a purchase and sale agreement with the Conservancy, estimating up to $10.1 million for acquisition, remediation, transport and deployment. The county later allocated $13 million to the entire project, paid for with tourist bed taxes and contributions from partners.

After a 1,800-nautical-mile (3,300-kilometer) tow, the ship reached Mobile, Alabama, in March 2025 to be prepared for sinking. Both funnels, the radar mast and a propeller were removed and stored for a planned land-based museum, and more than 400 portholes were kept.

“This will be a historic day for Destin-Fort Walton Beach and for a ship with such a historic story,” Trey Goodwin, chairman of the Okaloosa County Board of County Commissioners, said in October.

Before a ship becomes a reef, fuel, oil, asbestos and polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) are among the materials of concern, the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency notes. Properly prepared, a vessel is meant to be environmentally sound as a reef, unlike wrecks leaking explosives.

All 128 fuel and lubricant tanks on the SS United States were emptied, cleaned and certified clean by a marine chemist. Crews collected more than 200 samples to check for PCBs.

All of the green exterior floor covering, which held high concentrations of PCBs, was removed. Work on all 12 decks also removed ventilation systems, flaking paint and wiring, with no negative findings in final inspections, the county’s tourism department reported.

Plans also called for cutting back the aluminum superstructure to allow a shallower placement. Holes were to be cut so the ship would land upright, and passages opened for marine life.

The project received a 10-year federal re-authorization permit. A December 2025 county presentation also ruled out the use of explosives.

The aircraft carrier USS Oriskany was sunk off Pensacola on May 17, 2006. The Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission lists her as 911 feet (278 meters) long, at a depth of 212 feet (65 meters).

The SS United States is longer, but Guinness World Records still lists the Oriskany as the largest artificial reef.

Officials expect the wreck to draw grouper, snapper, mackerel, tuna and wahoo. Her top deck is meant to be within reach of all levels of certified divers.

Researchers from three institutions have partnered with the project to study the new reef. One will run 10 years of biological, chemical and physical monitoring, and the others will study its reef soundscape and tagged fish.

The ship joins more than a dozen large vessels already sunk in the Gulf by the county’s Natural Resources Team.
