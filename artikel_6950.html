Report: Cyber Criminals Move Toward More Targeted Phishing Attacks

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Cyber criminals are moving away from mass phishing scams and focusing on more targeted attacks, increasingly using trusted websites and services to trick victims, according to a new report from KnowBe4. The security firm found that spear-phishing attacks increased 41% over the past year.

The company's Phishing Threat Trends Report, Vol. 8 found that overall phishing volume increased 29.8% year over year. Organizations received an average of one dangerous e-mail for every 733 messages, but the bigger concern is how attackers are changing their approach.

Rather than sending the same fraudulent message to thousands of people, criminals are spending more time researching their targets and creating convincing messages that appear to come from trusted coworkers, businesses, and technology platforms.

The findings are based on threat data from KnowBe4 Defend, the company's inbound e-mail security platform, which processes approximately 1.15 billion event signals daily.

KnowBe4 found that several traditional phishing methods declined significantly compared with 2025. Mobile-focused scams dropped 47.3%, advance-fee fraud fell 31.3%, and missed-message scams declined 11.3%.

Meanwhile, spear phishing increased 41.1%, pre-attack reconnaissance rose 33.7%, and company impersonation climbed 28%.

The shift suggests that attackers are finding more value in carefully crafted scams that exploit existing business relationships and workplace routines.

Business e-mail compromise (BEC) remains particularly common, accounting for 51.2% of phishing e-mails analyzed in the report. Human resources, finance departments, and executive leadership are among the most frequently targeted groups.

Attackers also appear to be timing their campaigns around normal business activity. KnowBe4 found that 90.4% of phishing messages arrive Monday through Friday, with Monday accounting for the largest share at 20.6%.

One of the report's more troubling findings involves the growing use of legitimate technology platforms to deliver malicious content.

KnowBe4 found that phishing attacks were twice as likely to originate from legitimate infrastructure in the third quarter of 2026 compared with the previous quarter.

That includes AI services, where attackers are taking advantage of trusted domains to host fake developer consoles, login prompts, and credential-stealing pages. The report also identified a 228% increase in AI-brand impersonation attacks between March and August.

Because these malicious pages can operate on legitimate domains with valid security certificates, users may not see the usual warning signs associated with suspicious websites.

"For years, security advice has focused on a predictable checklist: Check the sender address, hover over the link, and look for the padlock," said Jack Chapman, senior vice president of threat intelligence at KnowBe4.

"While still foundational, that guidance is becoming less useful by the day."

The findings echo a recent report about management software and AI e-mail assistants. Research from Fortra and Barracuda showed how criminals are using legitimate administrative tools and hidden AI instructions to manipulate users and compromise systems.

Cybersecurity companies Rubrik and CrowdStrike have announced an expanded identity-security integration designed to move compromised identity environments from threat detection through clean recovery with less manual intervention.

While security teams have invested heavily in e-mail protection, endpoint security, and identity controls, new research from Fortra suggests one challenge remains difficult to solve: users.

As an umbrella term, education technology encompasses everything from learning management systems and student information systems to productivity tools and AI platforms. More specific language clarifying the difference between ed tech and consumer-facing tools would allow state leaders to match the right evidence, expertise, and policy to the right questions.

A new study from consulting firm KPMG found that while AI investment continues to grow, relatively few organizations consistently measure whether the technology's benefits justify its costs.
