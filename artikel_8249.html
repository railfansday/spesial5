Vermilion Valley Resort Receding With Each Step — John Muir Trail Day 16

Tanggal: 2026-11-15

I had a lousy night.  Arriving as late as I did, I didn’t discern that the campsite I’d selected had a slight incline.  It didn’t seem like much at first, but when I tried to sleep, I kept feeling like I was sliding down the side of the mountain.  Not a good way to sleep.

Plus, my flank was really hurting, either my rib, or my pectoral muscle, or whatever the hell it was.  It is strange that it didn’t hurt that much in the immediate aftermath of my fall, but now, a couple of days later, it was hurting a lot.

So, it was a very fitful sleep. When I saw the light coming, I thought, “Well, thank goodness I’ve got a nice easy day today.  I will just cruise downhill for about seven miles to the Vermilion Valley Resort.  It should be easy; I will probably get in around two in the afternoon.”    I didn’t even pack much lunch because I knew I’d have a huge dinner once I got there.

Slowly I put myself together.  I arrived too late to dig a cat hole the night before, but I was lucky and found a nice spot under a huge tree in the morning. It was easy.

At the lower elevations, the trees get a lot bigger.

Despite everything going well, and getting up at seven a.m., I was not on the road until nine.  It just seems to take me a long time from waking up, to actually being on the road and moving.

The Vermilion Valley Resort, or VVR, is an iconic destination on the John Muir Trail.  Among its hiker-friendly amenities is a free beer when you arrive!  In the past, it was usually accessed by a boat ride the length of Edison Lake.  Then last year the famous boatman died.  Whether because of his absence, or the exceptionally low water in the lake, the boat access was not available this season.  To visit VVR I needed to hike in.  Northbound, as I was headed, there were two trail options.  I had heard the better choice was to turn off the John Muir Trail onto Bear Creek Trail.

The day before I deliberately tried to make a lot of distance, so that I could be on the Bear Creek Trail almost right away in the morning.  Indeed, that was what happened.  Very soon I took the left fork and was headed for VVR.

This trail was not quite as well-maintained as the JMT.  I was hiking in shorts and about a half mile down the trail I was getting my legs scratched by wild roses at the trailside.  I thought, “No, this is no good.  This is going to be a big problem.”

So, I pulled off the trail, undid my pack, pulled out my long pants, took off my shorts, put on the long pants, and transferred everything I was carrying into new pockets.  I stowed my shorts and returned to the trail.

Less than five minutes after I resumed, I observed a couple coming toward me from the opposite direction.  They were both wearing shorts.  So, I got to chatting with them, and they said, “Oh, yeah, there are a few roses right here, but after this there are none.  It’s fine.  You can hike in your shorts.”

My first reaction was, “I’ve gone to all this trouble, I’m not changing back.”

A hundred yards later I changed my mind.  After all, unless it’s cold, I really do prefer to hike in shorts.  So, I went through the whole darn process all over again in reverse.  I just wish I had run into those people a little sooner than I did!  But the rest of the day I hiked in my shorts.

I was expecting a downhill cruise, so it was disappointing to find some uphill bumps, even if they were minor.  Shortly after one of these I came  across a woman sitting down whose name was Amy.  We commiserated over the tiny, unexpected pass we had crossed and then I went ahead of her along some granite slopes, where the trail was marked by stones.

Just like the Yellow Brick Road… except without the yellow bricks.

Here the rock path is a little more subtle, but the view beyond is better. You can see I was about to descend.

Then the trail turned sharply downhill for a stretch.  I take those steep downhills slowly, both because my knee is hurting and because I don’t want to injure myself.  At the bottom, Amy passed me, and I said, “Oh, you’re very good at downhills!”

She replied, “Well, I probably have younger knees.”  That was clearly the case.

We ended up leapfrogging each other a couple of times more, en route to a famous waterfall where we both intended to have lunch.

We finally got there and sat together to eat our snacks.  I learned that she had just gotten her PhD from Brown University in biology.  Her thesis entailed studying the sounds of woodpeckers and some other kind of bird.  We had a pleasant chat as we ate.

The waterfall was a wide expanse of rocks with water falling over them through various channels into a large, open, placid pool.

It was an ideal spot for lunch and to go wading to cool off in the water.  Amy chose not to do so, but I enjoyed the cool water on my feet.

Because we were off the John Muir Trail, FarOut was not working for me as a navigation app.  So, I asked Amy how much further we had to go.  I thought she was going to say, “Oh, three miles.”  Instead, she said, “Well, six miles.”

That seemed wrong; it was much further than I expected.  But I bit my tongue and didn’t say anything.  I left first, and as I went further down the trail, I started to encounter a few hikers coming up the other way.

I asked them, “Did you come from Vermilion Valley Resort?”

“Oh, you have to make sure you spot the sign that says Edison Lake, because that’s where you have to turn.  It’s a very small sign. It’s easy to miss.”

“About six miles or so.  It’s not easy, because you have to climb over that ridge that lies between us and Edison Lake.”

When three hikers in a row said the same thing, I took it seriously.

Yes, the sign for Edison Lake was where I needed to turn.  But that sign was still farther away than it seemed it should be from the map.  I didn’t understand it.

I was getting hot and when I stopped to pee, I noticed my urine was getting quite dark.  It was the sign that I wasn’t drinking enough.  So, I pulled over at the next stream access, filled up my water bottles, and added electrolytes.  I also drank a lot of water on the spot, “cameling up” as the hikers’ saying goes.  By the time I got back on trail, Amy had bypassed me again.

I was already tired by the time I finally reached the Edison Lake turnoff.  Amy was there resting and we chatted again.  She admitted, “This is not what I expected.”  I concurred, “This is not AT ALL what I expected either.”  However, hearing it from her made me feel better about it.

From that point, the trail entered a long series of switchbacks all the way up the hill.  Bear Creek Trail is one hill away from Edison Lake, so you’ve got to go over that ridgeline.  I ascended ahead of Amy and made a good pace despite being tired.  At the top, I looked down into Edison Lake and was shocked.  No wonder the lake ferry was not running!  The level of the lake was very low.

The grey in front of the water is the dam, but the grey behind shows exposed sandbanks that are normally fully under water.

Then I cruised down the other side of the ridge and arrived in the parking lot.

It was 3:09 p.m. and a sign there read:  “VVR shuttle:  8:15 and 3:15.”

I thought, “Oh no!  The shuttle is going to come in about six minutes!”

Now, I didn’t mind.  I had told Amy I planned to walk to VVR because I wanted a connected straight line on my thru hike.  She had replied, “I don’t really care about that.  I will take the shuttle.”

How ironic that the guy who didn’t want the shuttle was there in time and the gal who wanted it was running a few minutes late!

The shuttle arrived and the driver, Josh, turned out to be a super nice guy.  I said, “Look, someone else is coming who really wants the shuttle.  Can you wait for a few minutes?”

He said, “No problem.  I’ll just go to the other trailhead, because the other route I pick up from exits quite nearby.  Then I’ll come back here and pick her up.”

So, he went to the other spot and came back.  She still hadn’t appeared.  He said, “Well, you know, I really have to leave by 3:30, but I’ll wait until then at least to see if she comes.”

I went a short way up the trail and shouted, “Amy!”  I heard nothing.

When I returned, it was almost 3:45.  He really did have to go.

Ruefully I said, “Well, thanks for trying Josh.  I’ll wait here until she arrives and we can do the road walk to VVR together.”

Just at that moment, he said, “Oh, I think I see someone on the trail.”   Sure enough, there was Amy.

It turned out I had done her a nice favor by delaying Josh long enough for her to get there.  She hopped on the shuttle, and I began the road walk.

It was another three miles, but on the road it’s pretty easy.  For the first time this whole trip I carried my poles instead of using them.  After a short distance on a forest road it passes along the front of the dam that creates Edison Lake and I got some interesting photographs.  The cover photo is a panoramic view of the dam as viewed from the center, which makes it look like a fisheye shot.

No surprise, the irrigation canal emerging from the lake is dry as a bone!

When I got to Vermilion Valley Resort, the first task was to figure out how the place worked.  You set up a charge account and use it to buy things like dinner, or a token for a shower.  Right away they offer you a free beverage, just for making it there.  I had a beer, an IPA.

Then I bought a two-hour credit on the VVR Wi-Fi.  I thought, “All right, I’ll call Lee and see how she’s doing and send her my love and I’ll call sons Ben and Gabe.”

Drinking my beer, I made all of those calls except for Ben.  I guess it was too late at night in Vermont by then.  So, I just left him a message on voicemail.

I had been warned that dinner at VVR was strictly between five and seven p.m.  After my calls it was just about 5 o’clock.

The tenting at VVR is free, but I thought, “I won’t even pitch my tent or unpack or any of that stuff.  I’ll just have dinner now and take care of all that after dinner.”  You may remember that I had packed a light lunch expecting a much easier day than I encountered?  I was hungry!

I entered the dining room, and the meal included an enormous piece of meatloaf, with a nice salad, and some mashed potatoes.  As I looked around the room, I noticed that the woman I’d met on the trail the day before, Ninja Queen, was sitting having dinner by herself.

Papa George having dinner at VVR (photo courtesy of Ninja Queen)

I said, “May I join you?” and she assented.  We ate and chatted and enjoyed our dinner.  At one point she said, “By the way, do you know that you can stay in the hostel here for $25?”

I had no idea.  But the option of not pitching my tent in the dark had to be worth $25!

I immediately got up and went to the front desk and I asked, “Is it true that you have a hostel for $25?”

They said, “Yeah, but you know, it’s just bunks.  You sleep in your sleeping bag, but you don’t have to pitch your tent or do any of that stuff.”

So, I booked the hostel and it turned out that Ninja Queen and I were the only ones there.  There were maybe six or eight bunks altogether, but only the two of us.  It might be the best-kept secret at VVR!

You can borrow clothes too, for when you shower or need to do ALL of your laundry.  I rummaged through their “free loaner” piles and found a pair of jeans and somebody’s shirt that I put on after my shower, and for the rest of the evening.  Obviously, I intended to give it all back in the morning and to put my own clothes back on.

Ninja Queen and I talked long into the evening.  I think I mentioned she’s from a Korean family background.  Her pack was exceptionally heavy because she was very susceptible to cold.  I don’t think I ever met anyone who carried TWO puffy jackets!

We had some interesting things in common.  For example, we talked about her dogs and about our beloved dog Nilo.  We agreed that when our pet dies, we don’t want another dog, we want the one particular dog that we loved.  That’s who we miss, not a random sort of generic dog.

As we whiled away the time, I charged my electronics.  We discovered we were taking the same route out the next day, over Goodale Pass instead of the more traditional route over Silver Pass.  Since finding the route to the starting point might be challenging, we agreed to stick together the next morning, at least until we got on trail.

Sensing a long day ahead, we snuggled into our sleeping bags – hers being an arctic polar one! – and called it a night.
