Zelda: Ocarina of Time Review: A Modern Remake With an Old Soul

Tanggal: 2026-11-11

Nintendo brings one of gaming's most influential adventures into the modern era without losing the magic that made it special.

I don't think I'll ever forget the first time I played The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time. Not because I remember every detail of that first adventure, but because it was literally the first video game I ever played.

I was just a little kid who barely understood English, sitting in front of a Nintendo 64 with a controller (which, yes, wasn't even connected to the console because I was the younger sister) and a dictionary by my side. I had no idea what half the characters were saying, but I knew I wanted to see what happened next. Nearly 30 years later, I find myself returning to that same adventure, this time with a much better understanding of English and a controller that actually works. And, surprisingly enough, I think this might be one of the most emotionally powerful experiences I've had with a remake in a long time.

From Darksiders to Shinobi: Art of Vengeance, I present to you ten action-adventure games that are unexpected masterpieces.

Sure, we all know how revolutionary Ocarina of Time was back in 1998. Its approach to 3D level design, exploration, combat, and storytelling helped shape the entire industry, establishing a reference that countless games would follow for decades. But this remake doesn't seem interested in proving that all over again.

So, rather than trying to reinvent one of gaming's most influential adventures, Nintendo seems much more interested in touching something deeper than any groundbreaking mechanic ever could: the memories we built with this little boy, his fairy, and a world that once felt impossibly big.

For starters, let's get one thing out of the way: despite Nintendo's pre-release marketing describing this as a ground-up remake a few times, don't expect something along the lines of Square Enix's Final Fantasy VII Remake trilogy. This is actually a faithful recreation of the original, much closer in spirit to what Nintendo did with Ocarina of Time and Majora's Mask on the Nintendo 3DS.

The main story remains practically untouched, the overall structure is familiar, and most of the changes come in the form of visual improvements, quality-of-life adjustments, and a handful of additions that make the experience feel more modern. This means that if you've played the original, you'll recognize almost everything here.

And I can understand that this might disappoint some people. When a remake is announced as being built from the ground up, it's natural to expect substantial changes, perhaps even a new interpretation of familiar locations, mechanics, or story moments. But I think it all comes down to what you expect from this particular adventure in the end.

If you've played the original, you'll recognize almost everything here.

As a longtime Zelda fan who literally learned how to play video games with Link, I found this approach much more effective than I initially imagined, since it allowed the remake to strike a point much deeper than any new fancy level design could ever reach, largely because it understands that Ocarina of Time doesn't need a completely different story to make us feel something all over again.

Instead, it gives us that very same story with a series of carefully considered tweaks. Some are immediately noticeable, while others are the kind of little details you might not even realize have changed until you've spent a few hours exploring Hyrule again. And there's no better place to start discussing these changes than with the story itself.

If you've somehow never experienced Ocarina of Time, here's the short version: you're a little boy without a fairy who happens to oversleep one morning, only to be awakened by a very concerned fairy named Navi. Before long, you're thrown into a journey to save the world, involving a mysterious princess and an evil Gerudo king.

It's a wonderfully straightforward premise that gradually evolves into something much bigger, exploring themes of childhood, growing up, loss, and how the passage of time feels bittersweet for us all. And, nearly three decades later, its emotional impact remains remarkably intact. However, the remake introduces something that makes these familiar moments feel considerably more powerful: dedicated cutscenes with voice acting.

The original Ocarina of Time did an incredible job with the hardware limitations of the Nintendo 64. Seriously, the developers managed to create some wonderfully expressive moments with not much to work with back then. But now, these scenes finally have the cinematic presentation they've always deserved – and boy, does it make a difference!

I honestly lost count of how many times I caught myself wearing the dumbest smile while watching a cutscene. Seeing Princess Ruto slowly abandon her grumpy attitude as she falls for Link, watching Darunia dance to Saria's Song as if he's attending the greatest concert of his life, or witnessing the moment we realize Ganondorf only managed to obtain the power he wanted because we essentially fell for his little scheme.

These scenes have so much more personality now, and the voice performances help give familiar characters a newfound sense of life. More importantly, they made me realize something rather unexpected: deep down, these were already the scenes I had imagined back in 1998. As a child, we had essentially created a full version of these moments in our heads, but the remake gave those childhood interpretations a proper visual and emotional form, and that's a pretty wonderful achievement!

More importantly, they made me realize something rather unexpected: deep down, these were already the scenes I had imagined back in 1998.

And speaking of giving familiar characters more room to breathe, there's another storytelling improvement here that I absolutely love: how NPCs are way more important now.

The remake's dev team included younger folks, several of whom never played the original Ocarina of Time, who provided a much-needed modern view of things.

One of my favorite things about Ocarina of Time has always been its worldbuilding. Hyrule is filled with strange, charming, and occasionally downright questionable people, each with their own little stories and quirks. But the remake takes this aspect of the original and expands it in a surprisingly meaningful way.

Here's a tiny detail that makes a huge difference around here: time now passes continuously within Hyrule's towns. Basically, in the original, the world essentially had four different environmental states: Child Link during the day and night, and Adult Link during the day and night. This allowed certain characters and events to appear depending on the time period and Link's age, but the system was somewhat restrictive because time wouldn't actually progress while you were exploring towns.

Now, the remake changes that! The sun rises and sets naturally as you explore even inside cities, with characters appearing and disappearing according to their own schedules (except in more contained areas such as temples and minigame shops).

It's a small adjustment that makes the towns feel much more organic, almost as if their inhabitants finally have their own agendas, which gives it a lovely Majora’s Mask vibe. Also, this adds additional events and dialogue associated with these new day-night cycles, giving us more opportunities to learn about people we previously knew very little about.

My favorite example of this is Grog, who is arguably one of the saddest characters in the entire game. Remember that punk-looking guy who sits beneath a tree in Kakariko Village at night, complaining that everyone he knows is disgusting? In the original, we only really encounter him twice: once as Child Link and later in the Lost Woods as Adult Link. Well, thanks to the new time system, you can now find him walking around Kakariko Village during the day, too. And he has more dialogue and new interactions, finally giving more weight to fan theories that have circulated about him for nearly 30 years!

And this isn’t an isolated example. Several other characters receive similar attention, making Hyrule feel like a much more interconnected place. I absolutely love this kind of addition because it doesn't just give us more content for the sake of having more content. It actually enriches a world we already know, rewards longtime fans who have spent years discussing its little mysteries, and makes its characters feel like actual inhabitants rather than people waiting for Link to show up. It just makes everything feel a little more real.

Okay, so the remake has the storytelling side covered. But what about the actual gameplay? After all, Ocarina of Time wouldn't have become such an influential title without its approach to exploration, combat, and puzzle-solving.

The good news is that the core experience remains remarkably faithful, although there are plenty of quality-of-life improvements that make returning to Hyrule considerably more comfortable.

For starters, the item menu has been reduced to three main tabs, with a much cleaner organization that makes finding and equipping things considerably easier. I have to admit that I wasn't particularly fond of the menu taking direct inspiration from Breath of the Wild, since different games have very different needs. However, the new quick-access carousel is an absolute lifesaver, and switching between items during exploration and combat feels much more fluid. You no longer have to spend quite so much time navigating menus just to equip something.

And there's also a second quick-access system dedicated to some of your most essential tools, including the Ocarina, Lens of Truth, Happy Mask Salesman's masks, and (yes, you can finally celebrate!) the Iron Boots. This last addition is particularly welcome because it addresses one of the most infamous frustrations of the original game: the Water Temple.

But don't think everything is perfect just because most changes are improvements. Some of the modernized design decisions have introduced a few restrictions that, to be completely honest, I'm not entirely convinced were necessary. For instance, certain pieces of equipment have been tweaked to prevent players from using them in ways the developers seemingly never intended.

This happened with the Hookshot, which was previously a wonderful tool for experimenting with different traversal possibilities, but now only attaches to specific designated surfaces. That means you can no longer use it to reach rooftops, for example. Does this force you to find alternative solutions? Sure. But it also takes away some of the creativity and freedom that made experimenting with the original game's mechanics so much fun.

Still, this is where expectations come into play again. And there is something important to highlight: overall, the remake makes your life considerably easier than it was back in 1998, which means that finding new solutions isn’t actually hard. Around here, NPCs offer more hints and several sequences have been optimized to deliver a more straightforward experience. Oh, and there's also a dedicated quest log that helps you keep track of both the main adventure and your secondary objectives.

While I wasn't particularly happy about the Hookshot changes, I never felt like they significantly compromised my experience. Finding alternative solutions was generally okay, and the improvements elsewhere made the overall gameplay feel much smoother. So it's a trade-off, and one that I think works more often than it doesn't.

Now, here's where things get a little more complicated. As you might expect from a remake that's practically a 1:1 recreation, exploration remains very faithful to the original. And while the quality-of-life improvements make moving around Hyrule much more pleasant, they don't necessarily change how you interact with the world itself.

There are some additional visual indicators to help you find interesting things, though. Poes in Hyrule Field are now associated with specific fences, and Lake Hylia is no longer “separated” from the field, for example, making some environmental details easier to identify locations worth investigating. But the overall level design is pretty much what we experienced back in 1998, for better or worse.

And I have mixed feelings about that. On one hand, revisiting these locations with modernized controls and an entirely new visual presentation is an incredibly emotional experience. I genuinely enjoyed wandering around places I hadn't seen in years, recognizing familiar spots, and discovering little details that I had completely forgotten about. It's the kind of experience that makes you want to stop every few minutes just to appreciate how much care went into recreating this world.

On the other hand, I can't help wondering whether the developers could have gone a little further, especially since Breath of the Wild and Tears of the Kingdom have completely changed our expectations of what exploring Hyrule can mean, giving us approximately 984,396 things to discover while traveling from point A to point B. Compared to those experiences, Ocarina of Time's more structured approach can feel a little restrictive, particularly when you know exactly where everything is supposed to be.

I would have loved to see a few more surprises along the way, perhaps additional secrets or expanded areas that rewarded players for approaching familiar locations from a different perspective. But, once again, I need to consider what this remake is trying to accomplish. As someone with an almost unhealthy amount of nostalgia running through my veins, I honestly didn't mind revisiting a place I consider home, even if the journey remained largely unchanged! However, if you're expecting a completely redesigned Hyrule with new exploration mechanics and substantially expanded areas, I'd recommend keeping those expectations in check.

Now, let's talk about the elephant in the room: the visuals. The remake retains the colorful, whimsical identity of the original, but practically everything has been reimagined from an aesthetic standpoint. Hyrule now looks even more like a fantasy world, with lush environments, intricate textures, expressive character models, and an almost overwhelming amount of environmental detail.

I have to confess that the new art direction took some getting used to. Kokiri Forest, in particular, initially felt a little too dense for my taste. Come on, don't any of these Kokiri kids own a lawn mower? And Young Link's redesigned appearance also gave me some mixed feelings during my first few hours.

But the more I played, the more the new aesthetic grew on me. Before I knew it, I was completely obsessed with how beautiful everything looked. And a big part of that comes down to the character designs. Rather than simply updating existing models with higher-resolution textures, the developers have reimagined several characters and enemies to better fit the remake's more elaborate fantasy aesthetic. The result feels cohesive, and there's a clear sense of inspiration behind practically every design.

Not to mention the little details! You can see (and feel, since this game is all about feelings, right?) just how much attention went into recreating familiar locations. Kakariko Village's well, for example, now features carefully rendered water and a variety of textures that make the environment feel considerably more tangible. Even something as simple as watching Link play the Ocarina becomes a visual treat, since you can actually see his fingers moving to the correct notes.

Also, the developers clearly know which details longtime fans care about. One particularly memorable example is how they've handled one of the game's most terrifying creatures (you know who!), since it is scarier than ever. And several bosses have also received additional visual details and updated animations that make their encounters feel more imposing.

And it's these little touches that make the difference! The remake doesn't just want Hyrule to look prettier; it wants every familiar corner to feel worth revisiting. And, honestly, I could spend hours just walking around and appreciating it all.

Putting everything together, I think it's fair to say that this is a remake that successfully modernizes a classic without losing its old soul. And considering how heavily nostalgia has influenced the gaming industry lately, with remakes and remasters seemingly arriving every other month, it's nice to see one that understands exactly what made its source material so special.

Most of the improvements are welcome, even if a few decisions (sorry, I still won't forgive what happened to the Hookshot) leave something to be desired. The updated gameplay makes the experience feel much more accessible, the visual overhaul is gradually gorgeous, and the additional storytelling touches give familiar characters a newfound sense of personality.

Putting everything together, I think it's fair to say that this is a remake that successfully modernizes a classic without losing its old soul.

There are also several additions that I’m obsessed with, from hearing iconic characters speak for the first time to seeing long-standing fan theories receive more attention, not to mention the extra Ocarina mechanics that let you hum melodies into the microphone to play them. It's a collection of thoughtful changes that, when put together, makes this adventure feel surprisingly fresh without sacrificing what made it so memorable.

And that's precisely why I think this remake works so well for longtime fans. It doesn't ask us to forget the original or replace the memories we built with it. Instead, it gives us an opportunity to experience those same feelings again, now with a presentation and gameplay that feel right at home in 2026. But I also believe it works wonderfully as an entry point for newcomers! Despite its faithful structure, the modernized controls, expanded guidance, and quality-of-life improvements make the experience feel far less dated than the original. So you can still appreciate everything that made Ocarina of Time revolutionary without having to deal with every limitation of a game released nearly three decades ago.

In the end, this remake made me feel like that little kid again, waking up early on Saturday mornings to spend hours exploring Hyrule, controller in hand (and, thankfully, connected this time). And I could feel that this was exactly what Nintendo wanted to accomplish within the first few minutes.

Playing this remake reminded me that video games can be much more than the mechanics we praise, the level design we analyze, the technical achievements we celebrate, or framerates that we fear to drop. Sometimes, their greatest power lies in how they make us feel, even decades after we've first experienced them – and Ocarina of Time still has that magic. Now, if you'll excuse me, I still have a few Golden Skulltulas and Heart Pieces to collect. And then? Well, I think I'm ready for another adventure.

The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time is one of the most beloved games of all time, but these remakes might be just as meaningful to fans.
