AMD Is Down 26% and Intel Is Down 42%. Which AI Stock Is Worth Buying on the Dip?

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

As Wall Street reassesses the economics of the AI boom, the differences between these two chipmakers are becoming increasingly important.

By RJ Fulton – Updated Nov 20, 2026 at 3:30PM EST

10 stocks we like better than Advanced Micro Devices ›

The recent semiconductor sell-off has presented investors with potentially attractive buying opportunities as concerns surrounding artificial intelligence (AI) valuations weigh on the sector. Advanced Micro Devices (AMD -13.25%) and Intel (INTC -7.97%) have fallen approximately 26% and 42% from their respective recent highs.

AMD has established itself as a major supplier of high-performance computing products, capitalizing on growing demand for AI infrastructure. Intel, meanwhile, is pursuing an ambitious turnaround centered on restoring its competitive position in processor design and semiconductor manufacturing.

While both companies stand to benefit from continued investment in AI, their contrasting financial performance, capital requirements, and competitive positions present two distinct investment opportunities.

AMD designs high-performance processors and accelerators for data centers, personal computers, and gaming systems. Its expanding portfolio of AI accelerators and multiyear strategic partnership with OpenAI position the company to capture a greater share of AI infrastructure spending. Meanwhile, established relationships with companies such as Sony and Microsoft (MSFT -2.77%) provide additional revenue streams through its gaming business.

In the 12 months ended Sept. 26, 2026, revenue reached approximately $45.4 billion, representing year-over-year growth of 41.9%. AMD generated $7.6 billion in net income over the same period, translating to a profit margin of roughly 16.8%. Its ability to deliver substantial revenue growth while maintaining profitability is particularly attractive as investors increasingly scrutinize the financial performance of AI-related companies.

As of December 2025, AMD maintained a debt-to-equity ratio of 0.1 and a current ratio of 2.9, reflecting a relatively strong balance sheet. The company also generated approximately $6.7 billion in free cash flow during fiscal 2025, although stock-based compensation accounted for 21.2% of operating cash flow.

Intel designs and manufactures processors for personal computers, data centers, and networking applications. While its established processor business remains a significant source of revenue, much of Intel's long-term investment thesis centers on its foundry strategy. By expanding its semiconductor manufacturing capabilities, Intel aims to compete for manufacturing contracts from other chip designers while reducing its reliance on traditional processor sales.

In the 12 months ended Sept. 26, 2026, revenue reached approximately $59.9 billion, representing year-over-year growth of 12.1%. However, Intel recorded a net loss of roughly $12 billion, translating to a negative profit margin of 20.1%. Despite improving revenue, Intel continues to face substantial financial challenges as it works to regain market share and establish a competitive semiconductor manufacturing business.

As of December 2025, Intel maintained a debt-to-equity ratio of 0.4 and a current ratio of approximately 2.0. However, the company recorded negative free cash flow of $4.9 billion in fiscal 2025, while stock-based compensation accounted for 25.1% of operating cash flow.

One of AMD's primary risks is intense competition from Nvidia (NVDA -14.01%), which maintains an established lead in the AI accelerator market. However, geopolitical and regulatory risks are becoming increasingly significant. On Nov. 19, AMD shares fell nearly 8% following a report alleging ties to blacklisted Chinese companies, claims AMD has disputed. New U.S. export restrictions targeting remote access to advanced AI chips through overseas data centers could also limit international growth opportunities.

Intel faces considerably greater financial and operational risks. Unlike AMD, Intel must absorb the substantial costs of developing and operating its own semiconductor manufacturing facilities. Its foundry strategy requires significant capital investment, with profitability largely dependent on securing external manufacturing customers and achieving sufficient production volumes. Intel must accomplish this while attempting to regain market share in its traditional processor business. Customer concentration adds another layer of risk, with its three largest customers accounting for approximately 43% of net revenue in 2025.

Intel appears to be the cheaper option based on both the Forward P/E, which compares stock price to future earnings estimates, and the P/S ratio, which measures market cap divided by sales over the past twelve months.

Valuation metrics include sourcing from Financial Modeling Prep (FMP) and may differ from other data providers.

From a valuation standpoint, Intel appears to be the more affordable option, trading at 37.2 times forward earnings compared to AMD's 60.6. Intel also trades at 4.9 times sales, considerably below AMD's multiple of 17.7. Although neither stock looks particularly cheap, I believe AMD's stronger financial position and growth prospects make it the more compelling investment following the recent semiconductor sell-off.

With interest rates remaining elevated and investors increasingly scrutinizing AI-related capital expenditures, the financial composition of each company deserves particular consideration. AMD's limited debt burden, positive free cash flow, and established profitability provide the financial flexibility to invest in product development without relying heavily on external financing. In contrast, Intel must fund an expensive manufacturing turnaround while contending with substantial losses and negative free cash flow.

Although AMD's elevated valuation leaves it vulnerable to further selling pressure, I believe its stronger balance sheet, superior revenue growth, and considerably lower capital requirements justify a valuation premium. Given the current macroeconomic environment, I would rather invest in a semiconductor company with proven financial performance than one whose investment thesis depends heavily on a successful turnaround.
