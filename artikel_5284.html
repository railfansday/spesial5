Should You Run Your Own AI or Pay a Provider? Here’s How to Decide.

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

You're viewing the US website. Looking for the Site edition?

Open-weight models finally give businesses a real choice between running AI on their own servers or renting it from the cloud.

Opinions expressed by Entrepreneur contributors are their own.

I’m an AI engineer and I’m currently building ChatMakr, an AI-powered platform that creates multi-agent chatbots in seconds, powered by open-weight models. I also spent time at a consulting firm focused on innovation financing, where I talked with hundreds of Parisian entrepreneurs, and this exact question — run it yourself or rent it from someone else — came up constantly. Open-weight models have made that question sharper than ever, so here’s how to actually think through it.

An open-weight model is a model whose trained parameters are published for anyone to download and run, without necessarily sharing the code or data used to train it. As the Open Source Initiative defines it, “Open Weights refer to the final weights and biases of a trained neural network.” You get the finished brain, not the recipe that built it. That distinction is exactly why open-weight models matter for this decision: because you can download the weights, you’re free to run them on your own hardware, on a cloud provider’s hardware or switch between the two, instead of being locked into one company’s API.

Self-hosting means running that open-weight model on infrastructure you control, whether that’s a GPU server sitting in your own office or a rented GPU virtual machine you fully manage. You pick the hardware, you install the software and you’re responsible for keeping it running.

Cloud means renting inference from a provider instead, whether that’s a managed endpoint hosting an open-weight model for you or a closed API you call over the internet. But cloud isn’t one single choice, because where that provider is headquartered changes what legal system governs your data. A U.S. cloud provider, even one running a data center inside the EU, remains bound by the CLOUD Act.

Here’s how it actually plays out. Below, I’ll call the winner between self-host and cloud on six specific fronts.

Self-hosting looks expensive on paper because you’re paying for a GPU whether you use it or not, but at high enough volume that fixed cost stops mattering. Cloudzy’s cost analysis explains that “self-hosting an open-weight model on a GPU VPS can beat a frontier API at high, sustained volume with good GPU utilization.” The catch is that most businesses never generate that kind of steady, maxed-out traffic. For anyone below that threshold, cloud wins on cost.

Running your own GPUs doesn’t just cost hardware money, it costs people, and that line item is easy to underestimate. Activepieces breaks down the real total cost of ownership and finds that “the payroll for these positions often exceeds the annual budget for the H100 GPUs they manage.” The engineers who keep a self-hosted stack alive frequently cost more than the chips they’re maintaining. Cloud wins here, because the provider absorbs that staffing burden for you.

For businesses handling sensitive or regulated data, who can legally compel access to your information matters as much as who can technically breach it. MassiveGRID lays out the jurisdiction problem plainly: “a U.S.-headquartered provider operating servers in the EU is still subject to the CLOUD Act,” while “a provider incorporated and operating entirely within the EU provides stronger protection against extraterritorial data access requests.” Self-hosting on your own hardware or choosing an EU-only cloud provider closes off that exposure entirely. Self-hosting, or EU cloud specifically, wins on legal exposure.

Demand for AI products rarely arrives in a straight line, and that’s where renting infrastructure pays off. Buzzclan defines the core benefit of cloud elasticity as “the ability to automatically adjust infrastructure capacity based on demand, helping businesses maintain performance without overprovisioning.” A self-hosted GPU server can’t grow on demand during a traffic spike or shrink back down when things go quiet, it just sits at whatever size you bought it at. Cloud wins on scalability.

Open-weight models exist precisely because businesses want to shape the model itself, not just prompt around its limits. A closed cloud API gives you a fixed model behind a fixed interface. Self-hosting wins on customization.

For a business selling into enterprise customers, a provider’s paperwork can matter as much as its performance. Google Cloud explains what its SOC 2 report actually verifies: “the SOC 2 reports are generated by an objective third party attesting to a set of assertions made by Google Cloud about its controls that are in place to protect customer data.” Building that same audited trail yourself, on your own servers, takes real time and specialized staff most startups don’t have. Cloud wins on compliance.

There’s no single winner here, and that’s the actual takeaway. Open-weight models made this a genuine choice instead of a forced one: The same model can run in a rented data center or on a box you own, and the right answer depends on which of these six factors matters most to your business right now.

If you’re regulated or handling sensitive data, lean self-host or EU cloud. If you’re scaling unpredictably or don’t want to hire an infrastructure team, lean cloud. Most businesses will end up doing both — cloud for the unpredictable parts, self-hosting for the parts that need full control — and that’s not indecision; it’s just matching the infrastructure to the actual argument that matters most in each case.
