Burial at sea as a reef: how memorial reefs work, what the law says and what they do

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Free daily briefing on global business news.

A memorial reef sets cremated remains inside a concrete reef module on the seafloor, and this guide covers how it is made, U.S. burial at sea rules and ecological limits

Scattering ashes at sea ends in dispersal. The remains drift or sink with the current and leave no fixed point a family can return to. A memorial reef works the other way. It sets a person's cremated remains inside a concrete structure on the seafloor. That structure stays in one place and can become a surface for marine life to settle on.

The difference turns a farewell into a physical object with a location. Over time, algae, sponges, barnacles and corals can colonize the structure, and fish can shelter in and around it. That habitat comes from the concrete. Cremated remains are mostly bone mineral, and they do not drive the process.

Reef memorials sit within a wider set of choices for people who want a connection to the ocean after death. Ordinary burial at sea covers both scattering cremated remains and placing a body in deep water. In the U.S., it falls under a general permit from the Environmental Protection Agency. A reef memorial follows a different path. The structure counts as an artificial reef. It can be placed only at permitted artificial reef sites, under permitting that differs from the rules for a simple scattering.

The idea has taken more than one physical form. The best-known version is the Reef Ball, a hollow concrete dome with holes through its sides. That design came out of the Reef Ball Foundation, founded in 1993. Off Key Biscayne, Florida, Neptune Memorial Reef takes a different approach. It is laid out as an underwater cemetery, with gates, columns and statuary built from concrete.

This guide covers five parts of the subject. It explains what an artificial reef memorial is and where its most common design came from. It then sets out how cremated remains are cast into a concrete reef module. It describes Neptune Memorial Reef as an example of a planned underwater site rather than a set of individual modules. It lays out the U.S. legal framework for burial at sea and for reef placement. It closes with what these structures can and cannot do for marine ecosystems. That includes the scientific debate over whether artificial reefs add productivity or mainly draw fish from elsewhere.

A memorial reef is an artificial reef that contains a person's cremated remains. The Reef Ball is its best-known form, a hollow, dome-shaped concrete module with holes through its sides.

An artificial reef is a human-made structure placed on the seafloor to give marine organisms a hard surface to settle on. Such reefs are often built for fishing, diving or habitat projects. A memorial reef serves a second purpose as well. It marks the life of one specific person who has died.

That pairing makes the structure part monument and part marine habitat. On land, a headstone stands beside a grave. In a memorial reef, the marker and the remains are the same object, because the remains are built into the concrete.

The Reef Ball design came out of the Reef Ball Foundation. The name describes the shape. Each module is a dome rather than a solid block, and its interior is open. The holes through its walls let water move through the structure instead of only around it.

The design is not limited to memorials. Reef Ball modules are also used in artificial reef projects that have no connection to a burial. A memorial reef takes that existing form and attaches it to one person's remains.

Eternal Reefs, a company based in Florida, offers memorials built on the Reef Ball format. In its version, the cremated remains are part of the module, not stored in a separate container inside the dome. Nothing sits loose within the structure.

A memorial reef also asks more of the people arranging it than a scattering does. Scattering needs no manufactured object. A reef memorial needs a module to be produced and a permitted artificial reef site to receive it.

The Reef Ball is not the only shape memorial reefs take. Some sites arrange larger concrete forms into a planned underwater layout. That approach works more like a cemetery than a single monument.

Cremated remains become part of a memorial reef when they are mixed into concrete as the reef module is cast. Once the concrete sets, the remains are part of the structure itself rather than loose material in the water.

Eternal Reefs, a company based in Florida, uses this method. It adds the cremated remains to the concrete at the casting stage, when the module is formed. The concrete it uses is pH-neutral.

The remains are bound into the concrete, so they stay with the module through transport and placement. They are not released into the water at any point. What goes down to the seafloor is a single solid object that holds them. There is no separate urn or container. The reef module itself serves that role.

After casting, the finished module is taken out to sea and set on the seafloor. It goes only to an artificial reef site that has been permitted for that use. A memorial module is not lowered at an arbitrary point chosen on the day.

Relatives often join this final step. Placement is commonly paired with a boat trip that brings family members out to the site. They can be present when the module goes into the water.

Arrangements beyond the casting itself vary from provider to provider. Each program sets what a family can add to a module or arrange around its placement. No single package of options applies across every memorial reef service.

Neptune Memorial Reef is an underwater cemetery off Key Biscayne, Florida. It is built from concrete and arranged as a single designed site, with gates, columns and statuary on the seafloor.

Key Biscayne is a barrier island in Miami-Dade County. The Rickenbacker Causeway links it to the mainland at Miami. The cemetery occupies one site in the waters off that island rather than a scattered set of locations.

The layout borrows the forms of a cemetery on land. Gates, columns and statuary are familiar parts of above-ground memorial architecture. At Neptune Memorial Reef, those forms are made of concrete and set underwater as parts of one plan.

That approach separates it from a memorial reef built from individual modules. The Reef Ball, the best-known module design, is a single concrete unit placed on the seafloor as its own structure. Each module stands apart from the others, even when several share a reef site.

Neptune Memorial Reef works as an architectural whole. Its pieces follow a common design. They are not stand-alone units added one at a time and arranged after the fact.

Both forms rely on concrete. The difference lies in scale and design intent. A single module marks one memorial at one spot. A designed underwater cemetery sets each memorial inside a larger shared space. A cemetery on land does the same thing when it gathers many graves within one plan.

For a family weighing a burial at sea reef, the choice comes down to those two kinds of place. One is a standalone marker on the seafloor. The other is a place within a planned site shared with other memorials.

A memorial reef is legal in the U.S. when it goes through the proper approvals, but it follows a different path from ordinary burial at sea. Scattering ashes or burying a body falls under a general permit from the Environmental Protection Agency. Placing a reef module requires artificial reef permitting instead.

The ordinary route rests on the Marine Protection, Research, and Sanctuaries Act. Under that law, the EPA issues a general permit for burial at sea. A general permit covers everyone who meets its conditions. A family does not apply for individual approval before a scattering or a burial.

Cremated remains carry the lightest requirements. They must be released at least three nautical miles from land. The EPA must then be notified within 30 days. The notice comes after the scattering, not before it.

Non-cremated remains face stricter conditions. The burial must take place at least three nautical miles out. The water at that spot must also be at least 600 feet deep.

A memorial reef sits outside that framework. The remains are not scattered or released into the water. They are fixed inside a concrete structure that is set on the seafloor and meant to stay there. The placement becomes a matter of putting a lasting object into the ocean. Objects of that kind fall under the rules for artificial reefs.

Artificial reef permitting involves both state and federal approval. The requirements differ from one site to the next. Memorial reef modules go to sites that already hold artificial reef permits. A family does not pick an unpermitted spot on its own.

The two routes also differ in who handles the process. A scattering under the general permit ends with a single notice to the EPA. A reef placement depends on a provider working within an approved reef site and that site's conditions.

Rules outside the U.S. differ. The U.S. framework described here does not carry over to other countries.

The concrete structure in a memorial reef, not the cremated remains inside it, is what gives marine life a place to settle. An artificial reef can host organisms and shelter fish, but it does not replace a natural reef.

A concrete module on the seafloor offers a hard, fixed surface. Over time, algae, sponges, corals and barnacles can colonize that surface. Fish can use the structure, and the openings through it, as shelter.

The remains themselves play little part in that process. Cremated remains are mostly bone mineral, a form of calcium phosphate. Once mixed into concrete, they become one more ingredient in the cast. The habitat comes from the module's shape, surface and placement, not from the remains sealed inside it.

Scientists still debate whether artificial reefs add to marine life. One position holds that a new structure adds productivity. It gives organisms more surface to settle on and more places to feed and hide. The opposing position holds that artificial reefs mainly draw fish away from other areas. Under that view, a reef concentrates existing fish rather than producing new ones.

That debate applies to memorial reefs as much as to any other artificial reef. A module that holds cremated remains is built from the same material as one that does not. It serves the same physical function on the seafloor. The memorial purpose does not change how fish or invertebrates respond to the structure.

A concrete reef is also not a substitute for a natural one. Natural coral reefs are built by living corals that lay down calcium carbonate skeletons. A memorial reef does not restore a damaged natural reef, and placing a module nearby does not repair harm already done.

Two practical concerns remain open. The first is site selection. Where a module goes determines what kind of seafloor it covers and what already lives around it. The second is long-term maintenance. A concrete structure placed on the seafloor is meant to stay there long after the memorial service ends.

Families considering a cremation reef are choosing a permanent structure that marine organisms can use. They are not choosing a way to create or rebuild a reef ecosystem.
