FDIC and NCUA coverage explained: 10 things to know when your bank goes down

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Free daily briefing on global business news.

A bank outage vs. bank failure explainer covering FDIC insurance, the $250,000 limit, credit unions and what happens to your deposits in each case

A banking app that will not load and a bank that regulators have shut down can look alike from a phone screen. They are different events with different risks. One is a problem of access. The other is a problem of solvency.

During a bank outage, apps, websites, ATMs or card networks stop working for a time. The money does not go anywhere. Balances are still recorded on the bank's books, and the deposits are there when service returns. The disruption can still cause trouble when a bill is due or a card is declined at the register. But an outage on its own does not put a deposit at risk.

A bank failure is a separate matter. It happens when a bank can no longer meet its obligations and regulators close it. In that case, whether a customer gets money back depends on deposit insurance.

In the U.S., that protection comes from the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation. The FDIC was created in 1933, during the Great Depression, under the Banking Act of 1933. The FDIC states that no depositor has lost a penny of insured funds since it began operating. Credit unions have their own insurer, the National Credit Union Administration, which backs member deposits through the National Credit Union Share Insurance Fund.

Deposit insurance has limits. Coverage stops at a set dollar amount, and it applies to some products but not others. How an account is titled can change how much of a balance is protected. Stocks, bonds and other investment products are not deposits, even when an insured bank sells them.

The bank outage vs. bank failure distinction matters in the moment. A frozen app usually calls for patience and good records. A failure calls for knowing exactly how coverage works.

The 10 slides that follow explain what an outage is and what a failure is. They cover how FDIC insurance works, the $250,000 limit and the ownership categories that can extend it, and what insurance leaves out. They also explain credit union coverage and what happens to accounts when a bank closes. They show how regulators handled the 2023 failures of Silicon Valley Bank and Signature Bank. The final slides cover how to tell a glitch from something more serious and how to check coverage before it matters.

A bank outage is a technical failure of access, not a loss of money. When a bank's app, website, ATMs or card network stops working for a time, the deposits behind those systems are still recorded on the bank's books. The balance owed to each customer does not change.

An outage affects the tools customers use to reach their accounts. A mobile app might refuse to load. Online banking might show an error page. An ATM might not dispense cash, or a debit card might not go through at a checkout. Any one of these channels can go down on its own, or several can fail at once.

None of those channels is the account itself. The app and the website display information drawn from the bank's records. The ATM and the card network send instructions to those records. When the connection breaks, the customer loses the view and the ability to give instructions. The underlying record of what the bank owes that customer stays in place.

That is the central difference between an outage and a bank failure. An outage is an operational problem. A failure is a financial one. A bank fails when it can no longer meet its obligations, and regulators step in to close it. An outage alone does not close a bank or change what it owes its depositors.

Deposit insurance reflects this split. Federal deposit insurance in the U.S. exists to protect customers when an insured bank fails. An outage does not trigger it, because the bank has not closed and no deposits have been lost. The money remains the bank's obligation throughout the disruption.

The practical cost of an outage is lost access. Customers may be unable to move money, pay a bill or withdraw cash until service returns. A payment that cannot go out during an outage is delayed, not lost from the account.

A bank failure is a solvency problem. Regulators shut a bank down once it can no longer cover what it owes, and that closure marks the failure.

An outage is a different kind of event. It is a technical breakdown that knocks apps, websites, ATMs or card networks offline for a time. Balances remain recorded on the bank's books while systems are down. A failure concerns the bank's finances, not its computers.

To a customer, the two can feel similar. Either one can leave a person unable to reach an account for a while. The causes differ, and so does the response.

An outage ends when the bank brings its systems back. A failure does not resolve that way. The decision to close a bank rests with regulators, not with the bank's technology staff.

The two problems can also occur independently of each other. A bank with every system running can still be insolvent. A bank whose app has been down for hours can remain financially sound. A working screen is no sign of solvency, and a frozen one is no sign of insolvency.

For depositors, the practical question in a failure is what protects their money. In the U.S., the answer is federal deposit insurance. The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation insures deposits at member banks. Credit unions have separate coverage through the National Credit Union Administration.

Deposit insurance is designed for failures, not outages. A bank that is temporarily offline has not failed, and its customers' deposits are still there. Insurance comes into play only after regulators have closed a bank that cannot pay what it owes.

A closure is also distinct from everyday payment trouble. A declined card, a delayed direct deposit or a transaction stuck in pending status is usually a processing issue. None of those, on its own, signals that a bank has failed.

The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation, or FDIC, protects customers of its member banks by insuring their deposits. If an insured bank fails, covered money is protected rather than lost along with the bank.

That protection is designed for one kind of event. Deposit insurance responds to a bank failure, which happens when a bank can no longer meet its obligations and regulators close it. It plays no part in an outage. While systems are down, the customer's balance stays recorded on the bank's books.

The agency dates to the Great Depression. It was created in 1933 under the Banking Act of 1933. The FDIC states that no depositor has lost a penny of insured funds since it began operating. That record is the agency's own claim, and it covers insured funds only.

Membership is the starting point. The FDIC insures deposits at member banks. A customer's first check is whether the bank holding the money is an FDIC member. Deposits at a bank outside the FDIC's membership get none of this coverage.

Within a member bank, protection has a ceiling. It applies per depositor, per insured bank and per ownership category. Each insured bank carries a separate limit. Money held at two insured banks is covered at each one.

The insurance also depends on the type of product. It applies to deposits such as checking and savings accounts. Investment products fall outside it, even when an insured bank sells them. Together, the ceiling and the product rules decide how much of a given balance the insurance reaches.

When a member bank fails, the FDIC delivers that protection in one of two ways. It often arranges for another bank to assume the deposits. If no buyer is found, it pays depositors directly.

Credit union members fall outside the FDIC. Their deposits are insured by the National Credit Union Administration through the National Credit Union Share Insurance Fund.

FDIC insurance covers up to $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category. The limit applies to each category separately. A depositor who holds money in more than one category at the same bank can therefore be insured for more than $250,000 there.

Each part of that rule does a different job. "Per depositor" means the limit attaches to the person, not to an individual account. "Per insured bank" means each FDIC member bank carries its own separate limit. "Per ownership category" means coverage is counted within each legal form of ownership, not across everything a person has on deposit.

The number of accounts matters less than the category they sit in. Consider a checking account, a savings account and a certificate of deposit, all held in one person's name at the same bank. All three fall into the single account category. Their balances are added together and measured against one $250,000 limit. Opening a fourth or fifth account of the same type at that bank does not create new coverage.

The ownership categories recognized by the FDIC include single accounts, joint accounts, certain retirement accounts and trust accounts. Each represents a different way of owning money. Each also carries its own limit for the same depositor at the same bank. Take a person with $250,000 in a single account and $250,000 in a qualifying retirement account at one bank. That person could be insured for $500,000 there, because the two balances fall under separate categories.

Spreading money across banks follows the same logic. Each insured bank is treated on its own. A depositor might hold $250,000 in a single account at one FDIC member bank. If that person holds another $250,000 in a single account at a different member bank, $500,000 would be insured in total.

The $250,000 figure is a ceiling on insurance, not on deposits. A bank can hold far more than that for one customer. Only the insured portion is capped.

Money above the limit within one category is uninsured. In an ordinary bank failure, a depositor has no guarantee of recovering that excess. Knowing which category each account falls into is the starting point for working out how much of a balance is protected.

FDIC insurance protects deposits, not investments or stored valuables. Stocks, bonds, mutual funds, annuities, cryptocurrency and the contents of a safe deposit box all fall outside its coverage. That stays true even when an FDIC-insured bank is the one selling or holding them.

Coverage applies to four deposit products: checking accounts, savings accounts, money market deposit accounts and certificates of deposit. With each of these, a customer places money with the bank and the bank records what it owes back. That recorded obligation is what deposit insurance stands behind if the bank fails.

Investment products work differently. A stock is a share of ownership in a company. A bond is a loan to a company or a government. A mutual fund pools investors' money to buy securities. None of these is a deposit, and their value can rise or fall with the market. FDIC insurance does not make up losses on any of them.

The names can blur the line. A money market deposit account is a deposit product and is covered. A money market mutual fund is a mutual fund, which puts it on the uninsured side. The two can appear next to each other in the same banking app.

Annuities follow the same rule. A bank branch can be the place of purchase, but that does not change what the product is. An annuity bought there is still not an insured deposit.

Cryptocurrency is excluded as well. A bank or a bank-linked app may let customers buy or hold digital assets. Those holdings are not deposit products, and they carry no FDIC coverage.

Safe deposit boxes invite confusion because of the word "deposit." The box is a storage service, not an account. Cash, jewelry, documents or anything else inside it is not counted as a deposit. FDIC insurance does not pay for lost contents.

A bank statement can be sorted into two groups. Balances in checking, savings, money market deposit accounts and CDs count toward deposit insurance in a failure, up to the coverage limits. Investment holdings and box contents do not, whatever logo appears at the top of the page.

Credit union deposits are protected by the National Credit Union Administration, not the FDIC. The NCUA insures members' money through the National Credit Union Share Insurance Fund, with standard coverage of $250,000 per member, per insured credit union, per ownership category.

The NCUA is a federal agency that is separate from the FDIC. It does for credit unions what the FDIC does for banks. A member who keeps savings at a federally insured credit union relies on the NCUA's fund if that credit union fails.

The coverage formula has three parts. The limit applies per member, so it attaches to the person who holds the money. It also applies per insured credit union, so money at two different insured credit unions is counted separately. The third part is the ownership category.

That third part means one member can be insured for more than $250,000 at a single credit union. For that to happen, the money has to be held in different ownership categories. Two accounts in the same category at the same credit union share one $250,000 limit.

This structure matches the FDIC's rules for banks. A member who compares a bank account with a credit union account faces the same standard dollar limit and the same three-part test. What differs is the agency and the fund that stand behind the money.

Share insurance comes into play only when a credit union fails, meaning it can no longer meet its obligations. A credit union whose app or ATMs stop working for a time has an access problem instead. Members' balances stay recorded on the credit union's books during that kind of disruption. Share insurance plays no part in that situation.

Members who want to check their own position can use the NCUA's share insurance estimator. It estimates coverage under the same rules: per member, per credit union and per category.

When the FDIC closes a failed bank, customers typically regain access to their insured deposits within a few business days. The agency settles those accounts in one of two ways. It either moves them to another bank or pays depositors directly.

Closures usually take place on a Friday. On that schedule, the weekend falls between the closure and the next business day. For a customer, the wait before insured money is reachable again is usually counted in days, not weeks or months.

One outcome is an assumption. The FDIC often arranges for another bank to take on the failed bank's deposits. Customers in that case do not need to open a new account or move money themselves. Their accounts move to the acquiring bank automatically. The balance remains a deposit. What changes is the institution that holds it.

The second outcome applies when no buyer is found. The FDIC then pays depositors directly for their insured balances. In this case, the money comes from the FDIC itself rather than through an account at a new bank. With no acquiring bank, there is no new account waiting for the customer. Deciding where to deposit that payout next falls to the customer.

Either way, the process deals with insured deposits. How much of a balance counts as insured depends on the coverage limit and on how each account is titled.

Records help during the changeover. A customer who keeps recent statements and a note of current balances has a reference point. Those records can be checked against the figures the acquiring bank or the FDIC reports after the transfer or payout. An acquiring bank, like any bank, generally has a duty to correct errors on accounts. A record made before the closure gives the customer something specific to point to if a balance looks wrong.

Every depositor at Silicon Valley Bank and Signature Bank was made whole in 2023, including those holding more than the $250,000 insurance limit. That outcome came from a systemic risk exception regulators invoked for those two banks. It was not a change to the standard rules.

Silicon Valley Bank failed on March 10, 2023. Signature Bank failed two days later, on March 12, 2023. Regulators applied the same exception to both closures.

The exception mattered because of how ordinary coverage works. Standard FDIC insurance stops at $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category. Any balance above that line is uninsured. In a typical failure, a customer holding more than the limit has no guarantee of getting the excess back in full.

In these two cases, the line did not decide the outcome. Depositors with balances above $250,000 were made whole alongside those with smaller accounts.

The decision covered Silicon Valley Bank and Signature Bank. It did not raise the coverage limit for anyone else. It also does not commit regulators to take the same step the next time a bank closes. A customer who keeps more than $250,000 in one ownership category at one bank still relies on the standard limit, not on the 2023 precedent.

That distinction matters for planning. The default protection for a large balance comes from the standard rules, which count coverage separately for each insured bank and each ownership category. Spreading money across categories, or across more than one insured bank, can keep more of it within insured limits. A balance above those limits carries risk that the 2023 response did not remove.

The two failures also show where the line between an outage and a failure falls. Neither bank had a technical glitch that would clear once apps and websites came back online. Both were bank failures, the kind of event in which regulators close a bank that can no longer meet its obligations. Restored service would not have changed what happened to depositors' money. For customers of those banks, deposit insurance and the systemic risk exception decided the outcome, not the bank's systems.

A payment glitch, a bank outage and a bank failure can look alike at the checkout counter, but they are three different problems. A glitch is usually a processing issue with a single transaction. An outage cuts off access for a time. A failure means regulators have closed the bank.

The narrowest of the three is the processing issue. A declined card, a direct deposit that has not arrived or a charge stuck as pending usually reflects how a payment is moving through the system. On its own, it does not mean the bank's systems are down. It also does not mean the bank is in financial trouble.

An outage is broader. The bank's app or website stops loading, ATMs stop dispensing cash or card networks stop approving purchases for a period. These are technical breakdowns of access. The deposits themselves stay recorded on the bank's books while the systems are offline.

A failure belongs in a separate category. It is a financial problem, not a technical one. A bank fails when it can no longer meet its obligations, and regulators close it. For deposits, the protection that matters at that point is federal deposit insurance. The FDIC covers member banks, and the NCUA covers credit unions. A glitch or an outage does not bring deposit insurance into play, because no bank has closed.

The practical test is what has stopped working. If one payment misbehaves while everything else runs normally, a processing matter is the likely cause. If every channel to the bank goes dark at once, an outage is more likely. A failure does not show up as an error message. It shows up as a closure by regulators.

Good records help in all three situations. Keep track of your balance and save recent statements. During an outage, pending transactions can appear differently from posted ones. The balance on screen may therefore not match the figure you expect.

If a number still looks wrong after service returns, raise it with the bank. Banks generally have a duty to correct errors on customer accounts. Saved statements make it easier to show what the balance should have been.

Two FDIC tools answer the core coverage questions. BankFind shows whether a bank is FDIC-insured, and the Electronic Deposit Insurance Estimator, known as EDIE, estimates how much of a depositor's money falls within the insurance limit.

BankFind comes first. FDIC insurance applies only to deposits at member banks. A BankFind search confirms whether the institution holding an account belongs to that system. Without that membership, FDIC deposit insurance does not protect the money held there.

EDIE handles the arithmetic that follows. The estimator asks how each account is titled. It then sorts accounts into ownership categories, such as single accounts, joint accounts, certain retirement accounts and trust accounts. Each category is measured against the limit separately. That is how one depositor can hold insured balances above the standard limit at a single bank.

The per-bank part of the rule works in two directions. Deposits at two different insured banks count against two separate limits. Several accounts in the same ownership category at one bank are added together and measured against a single limit. A depositor who opens a second savings account in the same name at the same bank does not gain extra coverage.

Only deposit products belong in an estimate. Checking accounts, savings accounts, money market deposit accounts and certificates of deposit are the balances to enter. Brokerage or crypto holdings do not belong in the calculation, even when they were bought through the same insured bank.

Credit union members need a different tool. Their deposits are insured by the National Credit Union Administration, not the FDIC. EDIE does not apply to those accounts. The NCUA offers its own share insurance estimator for that purpose. It works from the same structure of coverage per member, per insured credit union, per ownership category.

Recent statements make these estimates easier to run. They list the exact account titles and current balances an estimator asks for. Account titles matter because the wording on an account determines which ownership category it falls into.

Accounts held outside the U.S. fall under other systems. Other countries run deposit guarantee schemes, but their limits differ by country. The scheme in the country where an account is held sets the terms for that money. FDIC tools do not measure that coverage.

The results of these checks do not depend on whether an app or ATM is working. Coverage rests on a bank's membership and on how accounts are titled. Running a check in advance means the answer is already known before any disruption.
