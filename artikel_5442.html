Dell puts a single gateway between enterprise AI agents and the data they crave

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Data intelligence is becoming the deciding layer of the enterprise AI factory as companies learn that racks of graphics processing units deliver little value when agents lack trusted, governed context.

Neocloud providers built the first wave of large-scale AI systems, and core enterprise customers now want the same capabilities inside their own organizations. Dell Technologies Inc. recently extended its AI Data Platform with semantic and knowledge graph features aimed at that market shift. But both the challenge and the opportunity lie in pairing structured and unstructured data so agents get full context while maintaining guardrails and human oversight, according to Srinath Alapati (pictured, right), senior director of product marketing for AI Data Platform at Dell.

“The bottom line is if you feed the wrong data, you get the wrong outcomes,” Alapati said. “What we’ve found is in our own testing — by giving the right context — you can improve the accuracy from roughly 60% to like 94%.”

Alapati, Alison Biers (center), senior director of product marketing for compute, networking and global industries at Dell, and Adam Glick (left), senior director of AI solutions product marketing for compute at Dell, spoke with theCUBE Research’s John Furrier at SC26, during an exclusive broadcast on theCUBE, SiliconANGLE Media’s livestreaming studio. They discussed how Dell is tying data, infrastructure management and validated designs together to move enterprise AI factories into production. (* Disclosure below.)

Enterprises can’t give agents unfettered access to their systems, which is where Dell’s newly announced Agentic Data Gateway fits. The gateway sits in the orchestration layer as a single point of control, backed by a Model Context Protocol server, Alapati explained. Storage intelligence tells customers what data they hold, who owns it and how large it is, while Dell’s new data intelligence capabilities track lineage and provenance. Hybrid workflows extend that model across the edge, the cloud and the data center, where companies can struggle to meet the data consolidation requirements of other data platform providers.

“They want you to bring all the data to wherever the platform is. That’s not always possible,” Alapati said. “Number one, it’s super expensive. Two is data is always being generated. It’s a never-ending game. You can’t always keep bringing in data, consolidating data. What we do is we only send metadata … That way your data stays where you want to be.”

On the infrastructure side, Dell OpenManage Rack Scale extends management beyond compute endpoints to the network fabric, power and cooling. That gives operators a single operational plane from day zero through day two, according to Biers. A rebuilt, intent-based Dell Fabric Manager replaces the company’s SmartFabric Manager, and Dell’s PowerRack systems treat the full rack as the unit of deployment.

“It is literally six hours from the time it hits your loading dock to full production,” Biers said. “Something that used to take maybe months and months [is] now cut in half, maybe even less.”

More than 6,500 customers now use the Dell AI Factory, and a growing share are core enterprises asking for help putting AI to work, Glick noted. Validated designs built with Nvidia Corp. and other partners cover both the hardware and the full software stack, and Dell is expanding its labs to keep pace with independent software companies joining the ecosystem. Compute still draws most of the attention, but that is only one part of a larger system.

“If you’ve got a factory, compute is your assembly line. It’s the important part,” Glick said. “It’s what you think of when you think of ‘factory’, but that assembly line doesn’t run if you don’t have a warehouse that’s feeding it the raw materials — that’s the data that goes into it.”
