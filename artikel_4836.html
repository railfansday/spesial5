The Universality of Privilege and Inheritance of Excellence

Posted November 12, 2026 | Reviewed by Michelle Quirk

The word privilege has somehow evolved linguistically to be viewed negatively. It has also come to imply an unearned advantage or an unfair benefit held by one group at another's expense.

Throughout human history, privilege has meant something quite different: privilege has emerged when individuals and/or groups apply merit, achievement, competence, and excellence in ways that expand possibilities for everyone. I refer to this as the MACE Principle.

Research shows that high levels of expertize and exceptional performance emerge through sustained practice, learning, and skill refinement rather than arising spontaneously. In this sense, such accomplishments and the benefits they create for subsequent generations are among the oldest features of human civilization (Ericsson, 2008; Srivastava & Das, 2015).

Nearly three million years ago, our distant ancestors fashioned the first stone tools. The first person to shape stone into a cutting implement created something far more significant than a simple object. That innovation gave the entire group a practical advantage.

The collective also gained a privilege. The tribe could now cut hunted and gathered food and, self-evidently, share their food far more efficiently and much more quickly. Survival itself became easier.

Consequently, the privilege created by that stone-tool innovation was not confined to the inventor; it became a significant benefit and privilege enjoyed by the collective (Kivell, 2015; Stout, 2011).

The same pattern recurs throughout history. The invention of the wheel transformed transport and trade. Once developed, the wheel's benefits and the privilege it created extended far beyond its creator.

Entire communities far removed from the original invention gained the privilege of moving goods and people more efficiently. What began as an achievement of human ingenuity became a shared privilege and an immense social advantage beyond its original location (Kivell, 2015; Stout, 2011).

Perhaps no invention demonstrates this process more dramatically than the printing press. Developed in the 15th century by Johannes Gutenberg in Mainz, within the Holy Roman Empire (present-day Germany), the printing press fundamentally altered humanity's relationship with the development and advancement of knowledge.

Reading, writing, collective information, ideas, and knowledge, once largely confined to a comparatively small number of individuals, could now be reproduced, preserved, and distributed on a scale that was never before possible.

Reading and writing ceased to be “a mystery” for a select number of individuals or a “select” group. As with the stone tool and the wheel before it, the printing press created new benefits and privileges, consequently moving these benefits and privileges from the few to the many.

Although history informs us (as with all inventions and achievements), the resulting benefits and privileges were not always distributed equally.

However, even with this being the case, with the wider dissemination of knowledge (following the invention of the 15th-century printing press), the benefits of this invention extended far beyond Gutenberg and transformed what future generations could achieve (Jara-Figueroa et al., 2019; Triggle & Triggle, 2017).

Consider what followed. For approximately three million years, humanity crafted tools, built settlements, established civilizations, constructed cities, developed metallurgy, crossed oceans, fought wars, and created empires.

During this period, countless innovations delivered immense benefits and privilege. Yet the accumulation and transmission of knowledge remained comparatively limited.

Then, within about five centuries of the invention of the printing press, humanity witnessed an unprecedented acceleration in the growth and development of knowledge (Jara-Figueroa et al., 2019; Triggle & Triggle, 2017).

In the 18th century, Edward Jenner laid the foundations of modern immunization with the first successful smallpox vaccination (Riedel, 2005).

In the 20th century, the United States sent three men to the Moon: Neil Armstrong, the first human to walk on its surface; Buzz Aldrin, the second; and Michael Collins, who remained in lunar orbit as part of the mission.

These achievements produced further benefits and privilege, as earlier innovations had. They reflected humanity's expanding capacity to preserve, transmit, and build upon knowledge. The printing press transformed reading, writing, ideas, and information into a far more widely shared social benefit and privilege for the many (Triggle & Triggle, 2017).

In doing so, it expanded the benefits and privilege available to successive generations and helped create the conditions for scientific, technological, and civilizational advancement to proceed at a pace previously unknown in human history (Jara-Figueroa et al., 2019; Triggle & Triggle, 2017).

The same principle applied across medicine, engineering, agriculture, chemistry, and countless other fields.

Vaccines, clean water systems, supermarkets, automobiles, telecommunications, digital technologies, and now artificial intelligence all exist because individuals pursued excellence in their respective domains.

The benefits of those achievements are enjoyed not merely by their creators but by millions, and even potentially billions, of people (Tewksbury et al., 1980; Yu & Liu, 2021).

Throughout history, parents have generally sought to provide advantages, opportunities, benefits, and privilege to their children, just as previous generations sought to provide them to those who followed.

Civilization itself can therefore be understood as the cumulative result of countless individuals attempting to improve the circumstances inherited by the next generation (Gulyas & Powell, 2019; Stout & Hecht, 2017).

This does not self-evidently mean that every benefit or privilege is distributed equally; it never has and it never will.

It does mean, however, that inventions and achievements, no matter what they were, enriched lives, from the smallest tribes to the largest civilizations, because there were individuals who invented something that changed what previously existed, and with this, benefits and privilege arose (Dean et al., 2014; Tewksbury et al., 1980).

The story of human civilization is the story of expanding everyone's potential for privilege, but not a guarantee. Through innovation, discovery, and excellence, however, humanity has repeatedly turned extraordinary achievements into benefits that potentially eventually become available to the broader populace.

The greatest privilege is that each generation inherits the achievements of those who came before and can contribute new ones for those yet to come (Dean et al., 2014; Stout & Hecht, 2017).
