Can There Be Democratic Socialism Without Wokism?

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

A Jacobin editor decries the excesses of "Woke I," and warns that it's still here.

Jacobin senior editor Meagan Day takes to the pages of the NYT to decry woke excess, and argue that it need not be a part of democratic socialist politics. Indeed, she fears wokism will "pull the left off course."

To hear many conservatives define it, I would be considered woke. I've been a member of the Democratic Socialists of America for 10 years. I believe this country needs deep criminal justice and police reform. I am galled by ICE agents ethnically profiling and brutalizing people. I not only support gay marriage but am an enthusiastic participant in one.

But wokeness is not about a basic concern for social justice. It hyperfocuses on language and identity, fights dirty from the moral high ground and mandates deference to self-appointed spokespeople for marginalized groups. It conflates disagreement with harm, and keeps careful watch for heretics.

Day's comments are not merely based upon her experience during the Great Awokening, but also as a student at Oberlin where she saw what she calls "Woke Beta." Now she fears wokeness could derail the ability of Democrats to advance socialist priorities.

President Trump's re-election quieted calls to defund and diversify. But wokeness persists today. In D.S.A., which I joined on the heels of the first Bernie Sanders presidential campaign, one still encounters debates about whether clapping is ableist or whether socialists should commit to "decolonizing 'Minnesota.'" At its best, D.S.A. strikes me as our greatest shot at building a broad-based and politically serious organized left. At its worst, the energy reminds me of a scene from an Oberlin dining co-op, where a group of members filed in during a meal and silently unfurled a sign reading, "This co-op is messed up." (They were protesting the privilege inherent in cliques; the sign's wording was in fact less polite.)

The next Congress can't let these debates trickle up again. The freshman representatives from the bluest districts, such as those in Brooklyn and Los Angeles, will still face pressure to spend their time denouncing offensive language and censuring members to their right. Instead, they should focus on making life better for working-class people — working toward Medicare for all, labor law reform, maybe even a Green New Deal. For the first time in a while, the moderate members of their party might actually be open to compromising on these issues.

As for wokeness, I suspect that many progressive elected officials are privately rankled by its excesses, but publicly inclined to ignore it, worried about inviting moral hectoring from the left. The most productive thing they can do now is grow a backbone. They need to take it upon themselves to elevate the best, most compelling ideas even when doing so earns them some pushback.

Day's arguments, including her suggestion that DSA types should embrace "carceral minimalism" instead of prison abolition--what she calls a "nonsensical position"--is facing pushback from the Left. We will see whether the new Democratic majority takes her advice.
