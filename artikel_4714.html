Anxiety Is More Complex Than We Might Think

Anxiety can be a necessary response to potential threats, but also a serious chronic condition that negatively affects our functioning and well-being. In either case, we feel wary and on edge, scared or irritable, with overactive thoughts. We are tense and easily startled into more powerful fear responses.

The key factor is whether our fear-system, our biological responses to threat, can ‘switch off’ once the threat has passed. If we hear a strange creak alone in bed at night, we might tense up, hold our breath and listen carefully; our heart beating faster. If we hear it again and recognise the hot water pipe noises, we can relax and quickly fall back to sleep.

However, if anxiety is a habitual state, we might still guess that it was the hot water pipes, but we can’t relax—we keep imagining an intruder breaking in—staying awake for hours in a state of fear.

What is anxiety? The biological response is at the core, what I call fear-alert, our instinctive reaction to danger sensed at a distance.[1] Our hearing, eyesight, and sense of smell become more acute to evaluate the threat, and our startle response is readied, and the nerve signal regulating our heart rate is withdrawn. The ‘brake’ that holds our fear responses in check is removed. We are now ready for extreme life-saving action.

This means our nervous system is highly volatile now; even minor or imagined threats can put us in a high-energy state of fear-arousal—fight, flight, or freeze. In this state, the heart's pumping capacity shifts up a gear, and a flood of hormones enriches the bloodstream with glucose and other nutrients, providing sustained energy.

Because fear-alert frequently switches into fear-arousal, our experience of ‘anxiety’ covers fear-arousal symptoms, such as a racing heart, dry mouth, sweaty palms, nausea or numbness, as well as the wariness, irritability and worrying thoughts of fear-alert.

None of this would be a problem if our fear responses deactivated once the threat had passed. However, this can only happen when we feel safe. If we are under threat and don’t feel safe for long periods, the biological processes that switch off our fear responses start to lose effectiveness, and we can get stuck in fear-mode, a chronic state of fear-alert that frequently tips us into fear-arousal or fear-collapse [2] [3]. This is another way of talking about complex trauma, which affects many areas of life, as we can see in the following example:

Adrian was a young man with a responsible managerial job who suffered from constant high levels of anxiety and was unable to make decisions about work and relationships, leading to constant self-criticism. He worked for an aggressive boss who treated him like a slave.

He found all social interactions stressful and exhausting; he felt the need to please everyone and keep them entertained. This burden led him to avoid intimate relationships, and the relationships he had didn’t last long.

He experienced all his pain and confusion as being "in his head," with his mind always overactive but his body “numb from the neck down.”

I worked with Adrian for nearly five years. He had previously tried CBT and found it unhelpful. I felt under huge pressure to alleviate his anxiety quickly, which triggered my own fear responses, but our work was painfully slow.

It emerged that he had suffered from anxiety since he was 3 years old. He had been highly disturbed by his mother’s acute distress at the death of her own mother. This loss of his caregiver was compounded by a sense that he had caused her collapse, and throughout his childhood he constantly tried to make her happy, becoming overburdened by responsibility.

Understanding the impact of his early history interested Adrian; however, it did not alleviate his anxiety, and realising that he didn’t have to take responsibility for the happiness of others also changed nothing. Being stuck for many years in a continuous state of fear activation had created a complex tangle of problems: nervous system volatility, chronic bodily tension, the numbing of sensation, deeply ingrained patterns of thought and behaviour towards others, habitual self-criticism and an inability to seek care.

We explored all these areas with little success at first. What started to shift things, however, was understanding and regulating my own fear-system. I stopped reacting with anxiety to Adrian’s pressing need for a cure and stopped overworking. I became a calmer presence, and he responded, eventually able to engage with ways of working that were not initially helpful. We worked on regulating breathing and using a focus-oriented approach that gradually helped him become reconnected with his bodily sensations. [4]

By the time Adrian finished work, he had become far less of a people-pleaser, had changed his boss and asserted himself more at work. He was in a relationship where he was gradually allowing himself to accept care as well as give it, and his generalised anxiety had greatly diminished.

Anxiety can be highly complex, and treating it at the level of a thought disorder that needs correction can lead to disappointment and frustration. Treatment may take time, and it may consider many aspects and use several different approaches. It also requires the therapist to understand and regulate their own anxiety in the work.
