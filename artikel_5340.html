Amazon CEO Andy Jassy checks his watch and ends the interview on the spot if you can’t answer his go-to question, says $55 billion EA cofounder

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Amazon’s CEO Andy Jassy has one go-to interview question, and if you can’t answer it on the spot, he’ll look down at his watch and end the interview. At least, according to Bing Gordon.

Gordon was a founding exec at $55 billion EA Games—where he helped create The Sims, the game that had millennial girls around the world hooked—and Audible. He also served on Amazon’s board of directors from 2003 to 2017.

And one time, he was sitting at dinner with fellow Amazon execs, including Jassy before he became chief exec, when the conversation turned to who had the best go-to interview question.

“My go-to interview question is, ‘What would it take to keep you in a company like ours for 20 years? And who’s your business hero?’ And Andy Jassy had a better one,” Gordon told Fortune. “He said, ‘Mine is: what’s your strategy for constant learning?’”

Gordon instantly listed the various ways he learns, from reading books to watching people—which Jassy said was an instant green flag.

“He said: ‘Good, you know it off the top of your head. When I ask that question, if somebody goes, what do you mean? He says, as far as I’m concerned, the interview is now over, and I look at my watch and try to figure out how to get out of it.’”

Fortune reached out to Amazon for comment.

Gordon, who has just published a book, Everybody Wins, on what games and decades in Silicon Valley taught him about leadership, has some advice for getting ahead of an interviewer’s make-or-break question. “My advice to kids is, you know that in the interview process everybody who is interviewing you has an interview strategy they do again and again and again,” he said. “So take control of the interview.”

How? Just outright ask the hiring manager at the start of the interview what their go-to question is—and what they’re looking to learn from that.

“Just say, ‘I know you have one.’ Let’s talk as peers about the interview process because, frankly, I’m interviewing you as well. So I want to know how you decide who ought to be here.”

The second question to ask, after “What’s your go-to interview question?” is: What’s the best performance you’ve ever seen in this job, either at your company or in a company like this, and why?

“Now you’ve done two things: You’ve made the interviewer think because nobody’s ever asked him that before. You’ve also communicated in a really nice way that you have high standards, and you want to know what the best is because you’re going to try to get there.”

And finally, come extra prepared. Gordon borrows a rule from advertising legend David Ogilvy: “Know more about the client’s business than anyone else in the room, including the client CEO.”

Even if you get the job, Gordon says twenty-somethings should be “highly prepared” if they want to keep impressing their boss, including coming to their first day of work with a one-year plan of action.

“If you want to get promoted in your 20s, which is the top 10% every year, always ask good questions, always have an idea, know as much about the topic as everybody in the room, always get your job done and always know what the A plus is.”

Gordon and Jassy aren’t the only leaders with signature questions.

IBM’s president Dave McCann asks candidates whether they think we’re in an AI bubble. “Right now I must tell you, I’m always asking candidates if they think we’re in an AI bubble or not,” he said at the Fortune CEO Forum. “We’ll see how long that question lasts.”

There’s no right or wrong answer. McCann doesn’t mind which side you pick, as long as you’ve thought it through.

LinkedIn’s Sue Duke asks candidates what they’d do with the platform’s data. “If you had all of that data and all of our economists and data scientists with you in the morning, what’s the one thing you would want us to do with the graph to have a real impact out there in workers’ lives, in companies’ lives, and in the economy more broadly?” There’s no wrong answer, she said, but she’s looking for “people who dream big.”

And some executives are even asking candidates how they’d explain the rules of tennis to a 10-year-old.

“It’s not one that I came up with, but I heard somebody else ask this very interesting question,” Lilika Beck, president of the $14.5 billion healthcare giant, Sonova, previously told Fortune. “I think what’s cool about that question is it’s a little bit surprising, but it also shows how an individual can simplify and communicate well.”

Others like Bupa CEO Iñaki Ereño and Twilio CEO Khozema Shipchandler switch up the settings of their interviews to see how candidates act when the hot seat is at a restaurant. Ereño is watching whether the candidate is confident enough to order wine and how they treat the waiter.

Meanwhile, Shipchandler interviews senior candidates over 45-minute dinners and listens for how often they say “I.” He also sets aside around 20 minutes for the candidate’s questions. If they have none? “That’s a pretty big red flag.”
