Skilled trades leaders admit ‘nothing is future-proof’—but AI can’t replace America’s growing demand for blue-collar workers like auto technicians

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

As AI forces office workers to rethink how tasks get done, skilled trades jobs have largely been seen as a more insulated, safer path to a six-figure career. However, hands-on roles like plumbers, electricians, and auto technicians aren’t exactly immune to the changes sweeping the workforce, either.

“Nothing is future-proof,” Mary Culler, president of Ford Philanthropy, said at the Fortune 500 Innovation Summit on Tuesday. “But I do think that a lot of the skilled trades are just those jobs that are going to be required to have a human there.”

Auto technicians—who are in short supply, for example —could increasingly use AI to help diagnose vehicle problems, she said, but still need a human to do the actual manual labor.

Carhartt CEO Linda Hubbard echoed that AI-powered efficiency will change how skilled trades workers do their jobs—but she said that the industry’s labor needs—fueled by the data center boom and broader infrastructure investments—will continue to outpace any productivity gains from the technology.

“No matter how good we get at encouraging people, I think we’re always going to be challenged to get the number of people that we need into the trades to just rebuild the infrastructure of the country,” Hubbard said on stage in an interview with Fortune’s Kristin Stoller.

Employers are expected to fill approximately 1.7 million skilled trades openings annually between now and 2034—but roughly a quarter of the industry’s existing 18 million workers are over 55 and nearing retirement.

Skilled trades work isn’t for everyone. The jobs can be physically demanding, require working outdoors in difficult conditions, and sometimes depend on project availability. But they can also offer something increasingly attractive to workers: relatively strong job security, salaries that can reach six figures, and even a pathway to owning a business.

Getting more people to recognize those opportunities, however, has been a challenge.

“We’re seeing pushback both on the cost of college, what jobs really require college degrees,” said Maria Flynn, CEO of Jobs for the Future. But decades of trade policies and other economic shifts have also left some communities with fewer skilled trades opportunities than they once had, she said.

“We’re having to rebuild trust, rebuild that awareness, and helping parents, young people, school counselors really understand what these 124 different skilled trades occupations offer and what the paths are into those roles,” Flynn said.

That perception gap is particularly evident among the people responsible for advising students on their next steps. While 95% of counselors agree trade school is a valuable and credible post-high school option, just 54% say they often recommend vocational or trade school, and only 28% often recommend an apprenticeship, according to a report released earlier this year by the Alliance for America’s Skilled Trades.

“We need to change the perception and get the guidance counselors to start talking about the opportunities,” Culler said.

Even after convincing someone that a skilled trades career might be right for them, the industry still has to get them through the training process and into a job.

The Alliance’s September report found that among every 100 who start an apprenticeship, just 48 complete the program, and only 29 enter a skilled trade occupation within five years of leaving it. The numbers are similarly challenging for related postsecondary programs: Of every 100 entrants, 48 complete their programs and just 34 go on to work in a skilled trade.

Flynn described the problem as “pipeline leakiness,” which can often come down to a lack of support for workers navigating training programs. That includes seemingly basic but consequential barriers like transportation and childcare.

“We really need to be working on this together,” she said.

Preston Fore is a reporter on Fortune's Success team.
