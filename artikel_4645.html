There has been a theme in my practice recently: friend grief. This entails grieving the loss of meaningful friendships and connection.

As we get older and life happens, we naturally change and evolve. We move away. We focus on or change our careers. We struggle with and prioritize our mental health. We navigate difficult life events and trauma. We partner up or get married. We have children or choose to be childfree. We take on new interests and move on from old ones. We have new priorities. We make changes to our lifestyles. We start to develop other friendships or connections. And with these life events and experiences come changes and shifts in our relationships.

Oftentimes, these changes in our friendships are not spoken out loud. But they are felt. Sometimes it's the physical distance that makes it hard to stay connected in the same way. Sometimes a friend slowly becomes less available. Sometimes you start having less and less in common with someone you used to have so much in common with. Sometimes you simply just don't like who this person has become and feel less inclined to maintain the relationship because of how it makes you feel.

Regardless of the circumstances, friend grief can be painful. Even if this friend is still in your life, but to a different degree, it's normal to mourn the relationship you once had and the closeness you previously experienced. Some of the things you might grieve or miss include the phone calls, the text chains, the inside jokes, the memes exchanged, the belly laughs, the experiences shared, and the support you offered one another.

Additionally, friend grief is often accompanied by other feelings, which can be overwhelming and/or confusing. You might also feel relief, abandonment, guilt, or shame—or some combination of these emotions. Again, this is normal and a common experience.

There are different reasons why people struggle with processing their friend grief. Sometimes it feels silly or insignificant compared to grieving the death of a loved one. And in these instances, it's common to minimize this type of grief. Other times, it may feel uncomfortable, or even intolerable, to confront this loss. This may result in avoidance and denial. Regardless of the reason, it's incredibly important to honor and make space for grief and any other feelings that arise from this loss.
