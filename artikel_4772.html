Ambience Is the Silent Influencer

“No object is so ugly that, under certain conditions of light and shade, or proximity to other things, it will not look beautiful.” —Oscar Wilde

Ambience is defined as an atmosphere, or a surrounding influence. It involves subjective experience of the outside world through sense perception. The atmosphere or ambience of a certain situation contributes to the aesthetic experience.

We appreciate the way in which various elements come together to influence the aesthetic experience. For example, our appreciation of food is inseparable from the whole ambience orchestrated by a few other ingredients: table setting and decoration, the environment in which we are eating, music, the occasion, time of the day, and so on.

Our ordinary experience is seldom obtained through a single sensory source. What we appreciate is not any single sensation (a smell and texture) but the way in which various elements come together to provide ambience. That is, taste is inseparable from smell and texture. The rich aroma of Starbucks coffee would likely clash with the delicate, fresh scent of sushi, resulting in a mismatched sensory experience. The taste of a very expensive wine wouldn’t be the same if we drank it out of a paper cup.

In the context of restaurants, consumer perceptions are shaped by a confluence of sensory inputs (sight, sound, touch, taste, and smell), each contributing to the overall perception of value and quality. Taste is rarely experienced in isolation. Visual presentation, aroma, and texture all play pivotal roles in shaping flavor perception (Spence, 2016).

Aesthetic experiences are also socially framed, such as viewing a painting or theatrical work in an appropriate setting (museum or theater). Artworks in museums are rated as more pleasant and evoke longer viewing times, whereas those viewed on computer screens are rated as less interesting, memorable, and arousing (Pelowski, 2017). Art collectors know that museum exhibitions can add prestige to an artist or an artwork’s value.

An amusing illustration of this occurred when, in 2007, acclaimed violinist Joshua Bell agreed to participate in an experiment conducted by The Washington Post. In the experiment, Bell, wearing jeans and a baseball cap, nonchalantly played his $3.5 million Stradivarius violin during rush hour in the subway. Most passersby barely noticed him and gave no indication of realizing that they were in the presence of an accomplished artist. However, when people see Joshua Bell dressed up in a concert hall, they highly appreciate his talent, even though he is playing the same instrument and the same music. This discrepancy arises because our brain evaluates the same sounds differently in different situations.

The importance of ambience in mood regulation encourages one to be more attentive to their surrounding atmospheres. For example, coffee shops often play laid-back folk music or chill-out electronic music to calm their patrons without interfering with their conversations or other quiet activities. In the context of a dining experience, soft, warm lighting often evokes intimacy and comfort, fostering a sense of luxury and exclusivity.

Research in consumer behavior has shown that creating certain atmospheres in cafés, restaurants, or shops can influence sales (North, 1999). For instance, music plays a pivotal role. The pace of background music can influence how customers eat and drink in a restaurant. When a song plays at a fast pace, we move faster. When slower music is played, customers spend more time browsing. Slower tempos encourage diners to linger, enjoy, and spend more. Classical compositions often convey sophistication and have been shown to encourage patrons to linger longer and spend more.

North, A.C., Hargreaves, D.J., McKendrick, J. (1999). The influence of in-store music on wine selections. J. Appl. Psychol., 84 (2), pp. 271-276.

Pelowski, M., Forster, M., Tinio, P. P. L., Scholl, M., & Leder, H. (2017). Beyond the lab: An examination of key factors influencing interaction with ‘real’ and museum-based Art. Psychol. Aesthet. Creat Arts. 11, 245–264.

Spence, C., Okajima, K., Cheok, A.D., Petit, O., Michel, C. (2016). Eating with our eyes: From visual hunger to digital satiation. Brain Cogn. 110:53-63.
