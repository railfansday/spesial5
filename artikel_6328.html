Cat urine may hold an early warning of kidney disease

Tanggal: 2026-11-11

A protein in cat urine may help detect kidney disease earlier, while other protein patterns could reveal different types of damage.

Kidney disease is common in cats, but vets often find it late. The usual blood test may still look normal after a cat has lost more than a third of its kidney function.

Now, researchers have found a possible early warning sign in cat urine. A protein called MMP-7 was higher in cats at the earliest stage of the disease.

The study also found three groups of sick cats with different patterns of urine proteins. This suggests that cats with the same diagnosis may have different kinds of kidney damage.

Katelyn Brusach led the work in Jessica Quimby’s feline kidney research program at The Ohio State University College of Veterinary Medicine.

Her team compared urine from 30 healthy cats and 34 cats with chronic kidney disease, including just five at the earliest stage.

Estimates of how common chronic kidney disease, or CKD, is in cats vary widely. It is especially common in older cats.

CKD means the kidneys have lost some of their ability to work, and that loss lasts over time.

The damage can begin in the kidneys’ filters, their tiny tubes, or the tissue around them.

Vets usually measure creatinine in the blood to diagnose CKD and judge its stage. This substance comes from muscles, so when a sick cat loses muscle, the reading can look falsely low.

Creatinine and another blood marker called SDMA can reliably identify middle and late stages, the researchers wrote, but early disease is harder to spot.

Knowing exactly what kind of damage has occurred requires a kidney biopsy, which is not always possible.

Vets use substances in blood or urine as clues to disease. Researchers call these substances biomarkers.

“The search for more precise biomarkers is not unique to feline CKD,” Brusach said. “This is a common problem in biomarker discovery across other chronic diseases.”

Her team turned to urine because it comes directly from the kidneys. Changes in its proteins could offer clues about changes inside those organs.

All 64 cats in the study were pets enrolled during clinical exams at Ohio State. The cats with CKD had stable disease.

The researchers classified the sick cats using the International Renal Interest Society‘s four-stage system.

Five had stage one disease, 15 had stage two, eight had stage three, and six had stage four, the most severe.

They examined each urine sample with a mass spectrometer, a machine that identifies molecules by their mass. This let them look at many proteins at once.

The team identified 2,219 different proteins. As far as the researchers knew, no earlier study had used this method to identify so many proteins in cat urine.

The team first compared the five cats at stage one with the 30 healthy cats. Several proteins differed between the two groups.

Only MMP-7 also tracked creatinine levels across all 64 cats. Cats with higher creatinine, a sign that their kidneys were working less well, tended to have more MMP-7 in their urine.

MMP-7 is an enzyme that breaks down material around cells.

Researchers think its family of enzymes plays a role in kidney scarring, but they disagree about whether these enzymes add to scarring or help limit it.

An earlier study of cat kidneys found that genes for three enzymes in this family were more active in diseased tissue, but only MMP-7 was linked to creatinine.

Brusach’s team suggested that MMP-7 may offer an early sign of kidney scarring.

Kidney disease can look different from one cat to the next, making it hard for one protein to tell the whole story.

“Chronic diseases are often heterogeneous, which makes them challenging to encapsulate with one protein,” Brusach said.

The team compared the full mix of urine proteins from each cat. Healthy cats grouped together, but some cats with early CKD fell into that same group.

The researchers suggested that early disease may not yet change many urine proteins, or that standard tests missed disease in some apparently healthy cats.

Some cats initially classified as healthy later received a CKD diagnosis.

The remaining cats with CKD fell into three groups separate from the healthy cats. These groups offered possible clues to different kinds of damage.

One group included five cats: four with stage two disease and one with stage three. The researchers found no distinctive pattern of urine proteins in this group.

The researchers suggested that these cats might have a more stable form of the disease, might not yet have worsened, or might have normally aging kidneys.

Scientists still do not fully understand why some cats get worse while others do not. In a 2012 study cited by the team, 81% of cats at stage two did not reach stage four before they died.

A second group contained five younger male cats with advanced disease.

Their urine had higher levels of proteins involved in immune responses and blood clotting, and all five had excess protein in their urine.

The team suspected that the immune system was causing inflammation in the kidneys’ filters, an uncommon problem in cats.

Without biopsies, though, they could not confirm that explanation.

The third group included seven cats with high levels of a different set of proteins. Some of those proteins occur in the membranes around cells.

This pattern may point to damage in the cells lining the kidneys’ tiny tubes. The researchers suggested it could reflect the usual form of late-stage CKD.

The study included few cats at stage one or stage four and few elderly cats with healthy kidneys. Healthy cats averaged 6.4 years old, while cats with CKD averaged 11.3.

Age or sex may have influenced the groups, but the study was not designed to test either factor.

The researchers also cannot yet say how well MMP-7 or any of the protein patterns would work as a test.

The team called for future studies that directly measure the proteins it identified.

For MMP-7, a larger and more varied group of cats would help show whether it can reliably tell early disease apart from healthy kidneys.

Zoetis Inc. funded the work through a sponsored research agreement with Quimby’s lab.

The study is published in the Journal of Proteome Research.
