Habits like nail biting may be clues to hidden mental health struggles in teens

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

About one in eight students surveyed in England reported significant distress over nail biting, skin picking, or hair pulling.

Nail biting and skin picking are widely regarded as nothing more serious than bad habits. In a survey of students in England, most teens who reported these behaviors said they caused little or no distress.

The survey was answered by 5,437 students. More than half of them reported sometimes picking, pulling, or biting at their hair, skin, or nails.

The behavior was described as difficult to control, although little or no distress was reported by most of these students.

About one in eight students gave a different answer. These students said the behavior upset them quite a lot or a great deal. This distress was closely associated with anxiety, depression, and bullying.

The study was led by Clare Mackay, a professor in the Department of Psychiatry at the University of Oxford. The behaviors are familiar to her from her own life.

Researchers have a name for them: body-focused repetitive behaviors, or BFRBs.

“BFRBs aren’t simply bad habits. They’re often signs that a young person is struggling,” Mackay said.

Because the students were surveyed only once, the answers can indicate which problems occur together. The order in which those problems appeared cannot be established from the data.

The questions formed part of the 2025 OxWell Student Survey, which reached 32,102 students in England.

A total of 49 secondary schools and colleges took part, and most were state schools in Oxfordshire and Merseyside.

The survey was usually completed online during a lesson. An optional set of questions was then offered, and some students continued to it.

Within that group, the questions about picking, pulling, and biting were answered by 5,437 students aged 11 to 18.

Three questions were written for the survey by Mackay’s team. Before being sent to schools, the questions were tried by 26 people with personal experience of the behaviors. Those people also commented on the questions.

In the first question, students were asked about picking, pulling, or biting at hair, skin, or nails. Only behavior that feels difficult to control was covered by this question.

In the second question, students were asked how much the behavior upset them. Four possible answers were offered, ranging from not at all to a great deal.

The third question asked whether the behavior interfered with home life, friendships, leisure activities, or classroom learning.

Overall, at least one such behavior was reported by 58.5% of the students. Nail biting was the most common at 43.6%. It was followed by skin picking at 31.3% and by hair pulling at 14.5%.

For most of these students, the behavior was not experienced as a source of distress.

Among students who reported a behavior, 35.2% said it didn’t upset them at all. Another 42.6% said it upset them only a little. One of the two highest distress ratings was chosen by the other 22.2%.

The researchers then focused on that smaller group. It was made up of 691 students, or 12.7% of everyone surveyed. The highest rating was chosen by 182 of them.

The lowest levels of distress were reported by students who only bit their nails. One of the two highest distress ratings was chosen by 11% of them.

The share was 29% among students who picked their skin. Among students who pulled their hair, a share of 37% was recorded.

Hair pulling and skin picking are listed as disorders in psychiatry’s diagnostic manuals. No separate category has been given to nail biting.

Considerable overlap between the behaviors was also observed.

At least one other behavior was reported by 84% of the students who pulled their hair. Among students who picked their skin, the corresponding figure was 76%.

The authors suggested that the behaviors may share one underlying process. On this account, they may not be separate conditions.

Interference with daily life was also reported more often for hair pulling than for nail biting.

Depending on the area of life, interference was reported by 5% to 13% of students who only bit their nails. For students who pulled their hair, the range was 18% to 39%.

At least one behavior was reported by 45.6% of boys and 64.6% of girls.

A third group consisted of 272 students who identified as trans or gender diverse, or who were unsure. Within that group, a share of 84.2% was recorded.

Distress was also lowest among boys. Distressing behaviors were reported by 5.6% of boys and 16.4% of girls. In the trans, gender-diverse, or unsure group, the figure was 25%.

The team then accounted for anxiety, depression, bullying, and age. The gaps between boys and the other two groups remained.

In studies of adults seen in clinics, about four women have been reported for every man with these conditions. A much smaller gap between girls and boys was observed among the students.

According to Mackay’s team, these behaviors had not previously been reported in trans and gender-diverse adolescents.

A standard 25-question checklist of anxiety and depression symptoms was also completed by the students.

Distressing behaviors were reported by 6.3% of students whose scores fell in the normal range. The share was 19.4% in the borderline range. In the highest range, which is labeled clinical by the checklist, it was 34.2%.

Distressing behaviors were also more common among students who reported bullying.

Among students who reported no recent bullying, 9.6% had distressing behaviors. A share of 27.1% was recorded among students bullied about once a week or more.

The team then analyzed these factors together. Anxiety and depression symptoms showed the strongest link to distressing behaviors. Gender and frequent bullying also remained linked.

Distressing behaviors were reported about twice as often by students who said they had autism, ADHD, or both. The comparison was made with students who reported neither condition.

Once anxiety, depression, gender, and bullying had been accounted for, that difference was no longer statistically significant.

The authors wrote that picking, pulling, and biting may work as a way of managing difficult feelings. The behaviors can then become a source of shame.

Young people may stay silent about the behaviors because of shame and stigma, said Polly Waite. For the same reasons, help may not be requested.

Waite is a co-author from Oxford’s Department of Experimental Psychology.

That question cannot be answered by the survey. Since each student answered only once, it cannot be shown whether anxiety leads to picking and pulling.

It is also possible that anxiety is increased by the behaviors.

Earlier work suggests that the relationship may run in both directions.

The students also may not represent English teenagers as a whole. Participation in the optional questions was a matter of choice.

Compared with the full survey, this group included more girls and gender-diverse students. Higher anxiety and depression scores were recorded in it as well.

As a result, students with emotional difficulties may have been over-counted.

The three questions are new and have not yet been formally tested. It also cannot be determined from them which students would meet the criteria for a diagnosis.

Mackay’s team wrote that these behaviors are rarely asked about in schools and clinics. A single screening question was therefore suggested by the authors.

It asks whether picking, pulling, or biting upsets a young person. It also asks whether the behavior stops them from doing things they want to do.

The next question is whether distress tied to these behaviors would be eased by reducing anxiety, depression, or bullying.

The authors wrote that this could be tested in future studies, especially among girls and gender-diverse young people.

The full study was published in the journal BMJ Mental Health.
