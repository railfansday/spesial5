Even California’s Biggest Ports Aren’t Betting On Hydrogen For Ships

Tanggal: 2026-11-11

California has been one of hydrogen’s most enthusiastic supporters for two decades. The state has subsidized hydrogen vehicles, backed refueling networks, supported fuel-cell buses and trucks, invested in hydrogen production and kept the technology prominent in its zero-emission transportation policies. Los Angeles and Long Beach, the two enormous ports that anchor America’s Pacific container trade, have been part of that enthusiasm. So when these ports produce a comprehensive assessment of future marine fuels and leave hydrogen out of their detailed deep-sea shipping plans, it deserves attention well beyond California.

The Clean Marine Fuel Feasibility Assessment, released in October, is the product of a substantial examination of shipping fleets, carrier preferences, fuel availability and infrastructure costs. The ports recommend developing commercial methanol bunkering demonstrations, continuing existing ship-to-ship LNG bunkering arrangements and avoiding major new fuel terminals until demand becomes credible. Hydrogen, the molecule that California has spent so much money promoting for transportation, did not make the cut for detailed quantitative deep-sea fuel planning.

This isn’t a state government announcing the end of hydrogen subsidies. It is arguably more revealing. Two commercially important institutions have examined what the ships serving them are likely to use, considered the infrastructure they would have to provide and declined to spend the next decade preparing a major hydrogen bunkering business.

California’s hydrogen story has repeatedly confused technical possibility with commercial demand. Fuel-cell passenger cars proved they could operate, but struggled with expensive fuel, unreliable stations and battery-electric competition. Hydrogen buses and trucks have faced many of the same issues, often compounded by the economics of supplying fuel to fleets. Ports have supported hydrogen demonstrations because eliminating diesel exhaust matters enormously to nearby communities, but a successful demonstration does not establish that the resulting system is the best commercial solution.

Deep-sea shipping adds a particularly difficult set of constraints. A container vessel requires immense amounts of energy over long voyages. Hydrogen’s low volumetric energy density, expensive handling and demanding onboard storage make the fuel system difficult to accommodate economically. The problem extends beyond the ship to production, transportation, storage and bunkering equipment. There is little reason for a port authority to build a hydrogen fuel network when shipping lines are not ordering a commercially significant fleet that would use it.

The ports’ survey reinforces the commercial judgment. Of 38 shipping lines contacted, 19 responded. Nine expressed varying degrees of interest or commitment concerning methanol, but only one did so for hydrogen. That is a survey of carrier intentions, rather than binding fuel contracts, and the researchers examined other evidence as well. Still, it is difficult to turn one interested carrier into a compelling investment case for expensive hydrogen infrastructure.

The contrast with methanol is instructive. Methanol is a liquid under ordinary conditions and fits into a mature global chemical-handling industry. Existing storage and bunkering equipment can be adapted instead of requiring an entirely new cryogenic or high-pressure fuel network. Long Beach is already offering a $1 million incentive for an initial commercial-scale methanol bunkering operation, an approach that can test operational feasibility without committing to an enormous permanent facility.

I have a personal interest in that choice because I had to correct my own shipping-fuel forecast. Last year, I published Mea Culpa: Biomethanol Will Be A Major Shipping Fuel. I had previously given too much weight to vegetable-oil-derived fuels for shipping and insufficient weight to aviation’s competition for those feedstocks. I had also underestimated how far cheaper batteries could displace marine fuel demand. My revised assessment favours electricity for inland and short-sea shipping, with sustainable biomethanol becoming a primary liquid fuel for the remaining deep-sea requirement. The ports’ decision is consistent with preparing infrastructure for that possibility, although it does not establish that their future fuel supplies will be renewable.

The distinction is important because methanol is not inherently a climate solution. It can be manufactured from natural gas, coal, biomass or electricity-based production pathways, and those routes have radically different greenhouse gas consequences. California’s ports can build a technically successful methanol bunkering service without achieving substantial shipping decarbonization if the fuel delivered is fossil-based. That question belongs alongside the commercial case, not behind it.

LNG presents another caution. The ports are accommodating existing LNG-capable ships without recommending major new shoreside LNG infrastructure. It is a conservative capital decision, although fossil LNG’s climate case remains weak when methane leakage and engine slip are included. Reductions in sulfur and particulate emissions are useful, but they are not the same as meaningful lifecycle greenhouse gas reductions.

The larger significance of the study is the change in what gets taken seriously. For years, California’s hydrogen ambitions helped keep the molecule at the centre of transportation discussions. At Los Angeles and Long Beach, the planning process has produced a different hierarchy. Existing demand and affordable infrastructure determine the near-term investment choices. Hydrogen enthusiasm has not produced enough evidence to justify a deep-sea bunkering buildout.

There are weaknesses in the ports’ assessment, including the separation between bunkering readiness and verified emissions reductions, and the question of how electrification will change future fuel demand. Those deserve more scrutiny than a short article can provide. But the decision to leave hydrogen outside detailed deep-sea fuel planning is already consequential. California’s most important maritime gateways have applied a commercial filter that much of the state’s transportation hydrogen strategy has resisted.
