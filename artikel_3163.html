If I Could Tell All S&P 500 Investors Just 1 Thing Right Now, It's This

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

If you can do this, you have a good chance of improving your returns.

By David Dierking – Nov 20, 2026 at 4:45PM EST

At one point in 2025, the S&P 500 (^GSPC -3.70%) fell 19% below its high. It still finished the year up 16%.

In 2020, the index dropped by nearly 35% during the COVID pandemic. It also closed out the full calendar year with a gain of 16%.

In 2026, stocks are sliding again. Right now, the S&P 500 sits about 6% below its all-time high, but the Nasdaq-100 is down nearly 13%.

I don't tell you this to suggest that U.S. stocks are poised for a rapid recovery like they experienced earlier this decade. It's to tell you that episodes like this are part of the experience when investing in stocks.

If you own the Vanguard S&P 500 ETF (VOO -3.42%), here's the one thing I'd tell you right now. These declines are no doubt painful, but they're the price of admission when accumulating long-term wealth.

Here's an interesting bit of stock market trivia. Since 1980, the average intra-year drop in the S&P 500 has been 14.2%. But despite this, the S&P 500 has produced a positive calendar-year return in 35 of those 46 years.

In other words, market corrections are a normal and regular occurrence for the S&P 500. But so are the recoveries from them. And the fact remains that stocks spend much more time rising than they do falling. The short-term losses are part of the path to long-term gains.

One of the most underrated moves an investor can make is making no move at all.

When stock prices are falling, many people rush to sell their stocks, fearing that more losses are on the way. They may say something like they're going to wait for things to get "better" before they get back in.

There are a couple of things wrong with that thinking.

First, selling stocks after they've fallen only locks in the loss. It doesn't erase it. Second, stock market rebounds are often well underway by the time investors feel that things have gotten "better."

The sum total of these actions means that a lot of folks lock in the loss, miss out on the rebound, and end up damaging their long-term returns. If the S&P 500's long-term annualized return is around 10%, the average investor usually ends up getting less than that.

That's why the one thing I like to tell investors is: Get used to the stock market's volatility. It's normal and it's a daily occurrence. Corrections of 10% or more are fairly frequent and should be expected from time to time. It's how you handle them that makes the difference. If you can buy and hold instead of giving in to the temptation to sell, you have a better chance of reaching your financial goals.
