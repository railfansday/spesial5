Daniel Jones has been released by the Colts. Should the Patriots go after him?

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

You heard about a Friday news dump, but have you heard about a Friday QB dump? That's precisely what the Indianapolis Colts did when they released their starting quarterback, Daniel Jones, earlier today.

Jones, 29, was in his second season with the franchise and just eight months removed from signing a two-year, $88 million contract extension. However, he and the Colts as a whole have struggled mightily this year, leading to some sweeping changes within the organization. First, they fired the GM who signed him to that deal, Chris Ballard, and now they let go of Jones himself.

His release comes with the Colts at 3-8 and Jones having completed 205 of 351 pass attempts (58.4%) for 1,974 yards with seven touchdowns and 13 interceptions. A year removed from tearing his Achilles tendon, he is ranked 30th among 31 qualifying quarterbacks in EPA per play this season (-0.139) and 31st in both net yards per attempt (4.8) and completion percentage above expectation (-6.1).

Long story short, he has been dreadful. And yet, the New England Patriots might just keep a close eye on him.

The Patriots, after all, saw their own starting QB, Drake Maye, suffer a fractured fibula during last Sunday's loss versus the Detroit Lions. While it remains to be seen how long he will be out, Maye missing most if not all of his team's seven final regular season games this year would not come as a surprise.

The question now becomes whether or not the Patriots should pursue Jones as a replacement. Let's make the argument in favor of and against such a move.

Before making this argument, it needs to be prefaced with this: everything in this section only applies to Jones going through the waiver wire unclaimed and making it to the open market. According to Jason Fitzgerald of Over the Cap, a team would need $6.35 million in salary cap space to claim Jones — financial flexibility the Patriots do not have at the moment. If he passes waivers, he could be signed to a deal worth as little as $505,554.

With that out of the way, let's take a look at why brining Jones in would make sense for the Patriots. It all starts with one question: would he be an upgrade over Tommy DeVito for however long Drake Maye will be sidelined?

His play this season so far suggests that that might not be the case. However, a change in environment might help Jones regain some of the confidence he had in his first year with the Colts, when he played some of the best football of his career and finished the season sixth in the league in EPA per play (0.201). If the Patriots can get a fraction of that, they should be happy with Jones as a better alternative than DeVito.

In addition, Jones also has some familiarity with the Patriots scheme. While he never played directly under offensive coordinator Josh McDaniels, he did spend the better part of three seasons under former New England assistant Brian Daboll. The offense Daboll ran with the Giants has similarities to McDaniels' in terms of terminology, quarterback responsibility and play designs, meaning that the lead-up time for Jones to get ready would be comparatively small.

Inserting Jones would likely also not come with significant philosophical changes. In essence, after all, he is a similar player type as Maye is: somebody capable of making plays with his arm as well as his legs, as evidenced by his career rushing average of 6.1 yards per carry.

While his highs have not been close to Maye's, he has shown that he can be an adequate NFL starting quarterback and somebody able to elevate the talent around him. That obviously was not the case in Indianapolis this season, but if a change of scenery and move to a better-run team can get him close to that, it would be precisely what the Patriots need right now if there are any doubts about DeVito.

And even if not, what do the Patriots have to lose? They are 5-5 and fighting for their playoff lives, and signing Jones would not automatically disqualify them from starting DeVito. At the very least, he would provide veteran depth better suited to challenge the post-Maye hierarchy than sixth-round rookie Behren Morton.

On Friday, Jones became the first quarterback in the NFL in more than three decades to get released less than 24 hours after starting a game. That plus the fact the Colts are willing to cut their losses despite the financial investment they made in him just this offseason should tell you all you need to know.

While there might be a snowballing effect to Jones' struggles and a fresh start could indeed help him regain the form he previously showed, there is no denying he has played some of the worst football in the NFL this year. The numbers mentioned above are an illustration of that.

Some of it had to do with the surroundings and a Colts team that a) lacking quality across the board, and b) is in a free fall since October. In addition, his injury situation also seems to have played a part in it: 12 months after his torn Achilles, he still does not look like he is close to 100% and fails to consistently to step into throws, maneuver the pocket and pick up yards on the ground, averaging just 3.8 yards per carry.

On top of it, Jones has been a streaky player throughout his career. While there have been stretches of play where he lived up to his former first-round status, the lows have been underwhelming, including this season.

Given all of that, expecting a quick turnaround in New England would be naive, particularly considering that the Patriots are missing several offensive pieces because of injury. But even if they didn't, Jones would not have any familiarity with them: despite some experience in a McDaniels-style system, he would have to build chemistry with his receiving corps, offensive line and coaching staff on the fly.

Tommy DeVito, meanwhile, has a two-year head start in that regard. Speaking of DeVito, he was signed to a contract extension in the offseason to serve as QB2 and has, by all accounts, been a model citizen deserving of his shot. Obviously, the job should go to the best option regardless of contract situation. That said, all things considered — including what a change might mean for the locker room — DeVito still has a strong case to be just that.

Even though they are still in a competitive position on paper, the Patriots are fighting for their playoff lives with Drake Maye sidelined for the foreseeable future. Their margin for error, meanwhile, is, to quote safety Kevin Byard, "basically at zero."

Would adding Jones increase it and allow New England to play winning football during Maye's absence? Ideally, that is not a question they would have to ask; Tommy DeVito stepping in and leading the offense at an adequate level would be the best-case scenario. Of course, as the Lions game showed, there is a realistic chance the lights are too bright for him.

The problem is, they also might be for Jones. So far this season, after all, he has not been good and not been able to hold up to the pressure of being the guy at quarterback in Indianapolis. He wouldn't quite be that in New England, sure, but the eyes of one of the NFL's biggest markets would still be on him.

Would that prevent the Patriots from signing him? Probably not. Still, it is one of the factors to consider alongside the arguments outlined above. And putting all of those together, it is clear the decision whether or not to sign Jones as a potential stand-in for Maye is quite nuanced. At the end of the day, Mike Vrabel, Eliot Wolf and Ryan Cowden — the latter of whom spent time with Jones in New York — will have to see their way through it.

And once they do, they very well might find that the potential disruption caused by adding Jones might not be worth the theoretical gains.
