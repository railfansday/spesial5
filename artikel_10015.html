Anthropic mathematician claims to prove Riemann hypothesis, most famous problem in all of math

An AI-generated solution of the 167-year-old problem, long considered far out of reach to human mathematicians, would cap off the technology’s year-long conquest of math in spectacular fashion

By Joseph Howlett edited by Claire Cameron

Levent Alpöge, a mathematician and employee at artificial intelligence company Anthropic, claimed on Friday to have proven the most famous problem in all of mathematics using an LLM.

“I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis,” Alpöge wrote in a statement posted on his website.

The Riemann hypothesis was first proposed in 1859 by the mathematician Bernhard Riemann. It claims that the locations of the prime numbers along the number line, which appear random at first blush, are actually determined by an intricate, meaningful order.

The conjecture has since become the North Star for the entire field of number theory, and is the most famous open problem in math. Progress toward solving the Riemann hypothesis was so rare that most top experts spent little of their time actually working on it, because there was no obvious path to proving Riemann’s guess. The first chink in the problem’s armor appeared in October, when one of the proofs in OpenAI’s massive dump of math resultsmade significant progress on making sense of the primes.

Now the long struggle appears over. Alpöge posted his Claude proof coded in the programming language Lean, which verifies the mathematical correctness of each argument. Issues can arise in Lean, but it shouldn’t take long for experts to verify whether the hypothesis is properly coded, and if the proof is correct.

If that happens, it will cap off a year in which AI’s mathematical capabilities ascended from unreliable college student to a superhuman force, leaving no part of the discipline undisrupted.

Alpöge says his proof confirms the “Generalized Riemann Hypothesis,” a gargantuan expansion on Riemann’s initial guess that undergirds the entire mathematical universe with untold hidden structure. There is no superlative strong enough for how this would transform mathematics, instantly unlocking a deluge of truths contingent upon the ambitious conjecture.

Alpöge’s statement is elegiac—recognizing the sense of grief many mathematicians are feeling—but also optimistic. “It is natural to feel that this is an end, in my case a loss of a principal guiding light,” he wrote. “But what I saw during the course of progress so amazed me that I believe it is one dawn across science of something far greater.”

The Anthropic mathematician has been the subject of headlines since he tweeted a Claude-generated solution to the famous Jacobian conjecture in July, kicking off math’s unprecedented summer.

“I hope the argument provides a glimpse towards the miracles that lie ahead,” Alpöge wrote, “and shows us the joy and wonder we are about to find in the simplest things.”
