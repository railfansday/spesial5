U.S. airlines are largely back to normal after Friday's four-hour FAA ground stop, but the financial and political bills are coming due. Airlines canceled close to 18,000 flights from Friday through Sunday, and Raymond James estimates the four largest U.S. carriers could take a combined fourth-quarter hit of $370 million to $964 million, with American ($136 million to $354 million) most exposed after scrapping 28.5% of its weekend schedule. JetBlue canceled 44.1% of flights, while Delta, United, and Southwest limited cancellations to 5%, 3%, and 4%. The episode has intensified the funding fight over air traffic control: Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy has raised his ask from $10 billion in August to $30 billion in September to $50 billion on Friday, while House Democrats on the Appropriations Committee said $9.1 billion of last year's $12.5 billion remains unspent, and a September GAO report found the FAA lacked a well-documented lifecycle cost estimate for its new air traffic control system. United CEO Scott Kirby called the moment "a call to action" and backed the Modern Skies Act, which would add $30 billion but is unlikely to pass this year. Demand looks intact — AAA is holding its forecast of 5.96 million air travelers between November 24 and 30 — though Amtrak searches jumped tenfold, rental car searches nearly tripled, and a December 11 government funding deadline leaves open the possibility of another round of airport disruption two weeks before Christmas.
