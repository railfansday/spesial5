Borussia Dortmund's Felix Nmecha and the question of religion

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Felix Nmecha's "Crowns down" celebration has drawn criticism from a Borussia Dortmund fan club, reopening debate over faith, inclusion and religious messaging in football.

Felix Nmecha's renewed use of the "Crowns down celebration" — where a crown is pretended to be removed from someone's head and placed at Jesus' feet — has sparked a reaction from Borussia Dortmund's official supporters' group.

"Faith does not justify exclusion. Faith does not justify division. Borussia Dortmund is a club for everyone," read a statement after the Bundesliga club beat Elversberg, a game in which Nmecha scored twice. Nmecha's first documented "Crowns down celebration" was at the 2026 World Cup, for which "The King's Return" campaign was launched.

"In the past, the the Borussia Dortmund supporters club has deliberately refrained from commenting on sports-related issues and the athletic performance of individual players. We remain committed to this approach," the statement continued.

"However, when it comes to our club's values, discrimination, and the question of what view of humanity is fostered within and around Borussia Dortmund, we cannot and will not remain silent."

The fan club states their issue is not with Nmecha being a devout Christian, but rather his public support for "The King's Return" campaign, which the supporters club said "gives us cause to reassess the situation."

This campaign, according to research by German public broadcaster ARD in the summer of 2026, was designed by the "Ballers in God" (BIG) network. BIG was founded in 2015 by footballer John Bostock, a devout Christian who wanted to create a community where professional footballers could strengthen each other in their faith, pray together, and read the Bible. In over a decade since, the BIG community has grown to nearly 800,000 followers on Instagram.

Nmecha issued a response on social media not long afterwards, writing:

"I've been told that my most recent goal celebration has led to some misunderstandings," Nmecha wrote.

"My message was never intended to spread intolerance, discrimination, or hatred in any form. As Christians, our message is one of love and respect for every person—regardless of where someone comes from, what they believe in, or what worldview they hold."

Reports suggest that both the board at Dortmund and Germany head coach Jürgen Klopp will speak to Nmecha on the matter in the coming days.

The fan club say their criticism is "directed at religious networks that, under the guise of faith, promote a worldview in which people are devalued or marginalized because of their sexual orientation, gender identity, lifestyle, or personal choices... When religious beliefs are used to deny people their dignity or equality, that is where our tolerance for intolerance ends."

Here, they are referring to the recent ARD investigation. That work revealed the homepage for "The King's Return" campaign featured a link to a live counseling chat run by US-based evangelical nonprofit Groundwire. During the investigations, counselors told reporters that abortion is "Satan's plan" and that homosexuality is a sin.

Borussia Dortmund's statement of core values commits the club to diversity, human rights, and inclusion regardless of gender, sexual orientation, faith, or background. The club's largest and most influential fan group also promotes progressive, anti-fascist, and inclusive values. It is also part of a wider fan landscape in Germany where club members are more involved in club decisions than in many other countries. This was evidenced when the club signed a deal with arms manufacturer Rheinmetall two years ago and in the case of Nmecha as well. When the midfielder signed for Dortmund in 2023, many of the club's fans protested because the player had previously shared or liked several transphobic and homophobic posts on his Instagram account. In response, Nmecha deleted some posts and instead issued a statement emphasizing "I do not discriminate against anyone."

Article 4 of German Basic Law guarantees that each individual is allowed to decide how strongly, why and which god to believe in. In football, players cannot display political, religious, or personal slogans, statements, or images on their jerseys, undershirts, or gear. However, physical gestures are allowed so long as they don't delay the game or antagonize the crowd.

The use of physical religious gestures has history in sports, as professor Paul Putz described in his book "The Spirit of the Game: American Christianity and Big-Time Sports". Putz describes how the the pioneers of the Christian athlete movement realized they: "could influence others to Christian ideals more effectively on the field than in the pulpit."

And yet, such gestures can also be at odds with the atmosphere of modern sports arenas, particularly football. In their book "Sports and Christianity: Historical and Contemporary Perspectives", Nick J. Watson and Andrew Parker explain how modern sport has become a substitute for traditional faith.

"Sport seduces the teeming 'global village'; it is the new opiate of the masses; it is one of the great modern experiences … Sport, for many, has replaced religion as a source of emotional catharsis and spiritual passion," they wrote.

With the 26-year-old Nmecha a key player for both club and country, the conversation around faith and football looks set to continue for some time yet.
