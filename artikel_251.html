Natalie Tran is turning short-form comedy into a filmmaking career

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Natalie Tran had already spent most of her life performing by the time one of her videos unexpectedly took off online.

She started acting at five, first through musical theater and later through commercials and indie films. Content creation came much later — initially as something she did for fun, not as an attempt to build an audience.

Then, at the end of 2023, one of her videos went viral. "I was gaining so many followers, I couldn't even believe it," Tran told Mashable at VidCon 2026.

But going viral was only the beginning. Tran took the comedic persona and format that audiences responded to and kept building, eventually turning short-form sketches into another arm of a career that still revolves around her original love: acting. Now she's trying to bring those worlds even closer together.

Tran recently wrapped Passion Project, a roughly 17-minute short film she wrote, directed, produced, and acted in, with hopes of using it as a proof of concept for a future feature. And rather than seeing her years making social video as separate from filmmaking, she says the skills she developed online — from finding the hook to obsessing over an edit — made directing feel surprisingly familiar.

"It feels like one of my skits, just higher budget," she said.

For Mashable's Creator Playbook, Tran talks about what happens after a viral moment, why short-form comedy taught her to be a better filmmaker, the changing relationship between creators and Hollywood, and why sometimes ambition just has to be louder than insecurity.

Mashable: You started acting when you were five. How did content creation eventually enter the picture?

Natalie Tran: I auditioned for a play called The King and I because I'd always been singing. That's actually where I started — as a singer. Surprisingly, I'm mainly an actor now, but I thought I was going to be a singer initially.

I got into musical theater and, from there, moved on to commercial work, indie films, all that jazz. That's what led me into content creation because I really like performing. I like making funny videos, things like that. I'd been making videos here and there, but never to get an audience, just kind of for fun.

At the very tail end of 2023, I made a video that went viral. It blew up, and then I kind of took the bit and ran with it. Now I'm here. It's been interesting to see how acting and content creation can work together. Every day I'm kind of building that brand and making it work.

Anyone can have a viral video. What did you do after yours took off to turn that moment into something bigger?

At first, I was just shocked. I was like, "There's no way this is happening." I was gaining so many followers; I couldn't even believe it. Then I started making videos. My favorite thing ever is when people comment that I'm funny. I love being called funny. It's my favorite compliment. That made me really happy to be recognized for that because I'm actually a pretty shy person. I like that I'm now recognized for my personality.

I think I can show my personality not just through my videos, but also when I direct something, make movies, or do long-form work. It's also helped me become a more sociable person in general. Everything kind of works together to build my career.

Your comedy works really well in short-form. Do you think those skills translate to long-form filmmaking?

Definitely. I just wrapped a short film, and that'll be about 17 minutes. The goal is for that to be a proof of concept for feature films in the future.

I think short-form has really helped me because, you know, how in school there's always a word count? They're like, "Get below the word count." You can get the idea faster. With short-form, it's: Get the audience hooked in the first three seconds. I think that still applies to long-form because you want to make it engaging.

Now that I have this short-form experience, I can kind of be like, "OK, this can be a bunch of different short ideas that I can stitch together." That's kind of how I think about it now.

How much intention goes into those first three seconds?

When I make a video, I make sure my face is the first thing they see. I think about high energy right away. Maybe not the best joke — you want to keep the punchline for the end — but you want to have something really engaging.

You think of an idea and then ask, "OK, what can make it even funnier? What's going to make someone stop scrolling and be like, 'Oh, I want to watch this whole video?'" Sometimes it sucks because it's like, "I don't want to have to make the first thing a clickbait reaction." So it's a matter of thinking about what's worth it.

There's a balance. You don't want to draw your audience in and then have the rest of the video be something they didn't actually want to watch.

Why do you think comedy works so well in short-form?

The biggest thing about comedy is timing, and that's really important with shorts. It's like boom, boom, boom. I'm a very percussive comedian, I would say. I'm so picky with the edit.

It depends because I also love comedy that's dry — no edit, literally nothing. But then I also love the comedy where it's cut, cut, cut, cut, cut. That's why it does so well in short-form. With these short attention spans, it works because it keeps you engaged the whole time.

Tell me about Passion Project. What made you finally decide to write and direct something yourself?

I've been acting since I was young, and people would ask me a lot, "Do you ever want to write or direct something?" And I would think, "Yeah, yeah, yeah." Then I was in a feature film last year called Frogman Returns, and I was really drawn to the way it was shot. It was a lot of one-ers, and I saw the way the actor chemistry worked on camera.

It was generating ideas, so I came up with the idea of Passion Project on that set. It was very early stages, but I remember I still have the voice memo where I was like, "Oh my gosh, it could be this thing where it's a lot of one-ers and really good actors."

Acting is the main thing. That's my love. I sat on it for half a year. Then over winter break, my friend was at a party, and she said, "Natalie, someone has the same idea as you." And I went, "Nope. That's not gonna happen. I'm making it."

That was the kick in the butt I needed to start writing it. It was about five months of pre-production and rewrites, and then we shot it in May. It was a dream come true. I'd been on camera a lot and worked as an actor my whole life, but this was my first time really being the producer, director, and writer behind everything. I found that I really love it.

How did being a content creator prepare you to direct a film?

I asked in my producing class one day, "Have you ever had a director who is also an actor in it?" Because everyone wants to say that it's very hard to direct and act in your own movie. I disagree.

The reason is because I have all this content creation experience where I'm filming with a friend, I ask them to record me, I watch the playback, I go, "OK, redo it. Change the angle. I'm gonna change my acting." And that's exactly how it was on set. I kept telling everyone, "This feels like one of my skits, just higher budget."

Content creation helped me so much because I already had that acting background, but filming my skits and finding out what I like in my own videos really helped me. Now I know how to communicate that to a team.

There used to be this idea that creators used the internet as a stepping stone to Hollywood. Does that distinction still feel relevant to you?

I think it's a lot easier now. I think it's the time where creators are bursting out of their bubble. Before, it was hard to get discovered. I'm lucky because I did have the traditional acting background, but now that I'm able to put my own stuff out there, it's an extra push.

Because of that, I'm able to put myself out there. I've gotten with a great agency and management, and all these things have been helping me build my career.

It's a really interesting time. I feel like a lot of people didn't expect it to be this way. It's growing every day, and more people are talking about creators getting into Hollywood.

Social following is even becoming part of traditional casting now. How have you seen those worlds collide?

I'll get auditions now, and sometimes they'll ask for your socials. I just think that's interesting, how it's a factor now. It's hit-or-miss because you don't want to cast people solely for their following, of course.

With Passion Project, I purposely sought out creators who are actors but also do content because that's where I come from. It was really fun to have that room of people who had the same understanding of this industry. People recognize the faces in the movie, but I'm not just hiring any Joe Schmo off the internet. I had a casting process as well. It's interesting to see how those worlds collide.

Acting and content creation used to feel like two separate sides of your career. Do they still?

When I started social media, it was almost a battle. If you even look at my Instagram feed, I had my pictures and it was all acting, modeling, acting, modeling. Then you swipe over and it's me being like — what is this weird juxtaposition?

Before, it was kind of battling with each other. Now I'm learning to marry the two and really have them work together.

Authenticity gets talked about constantly in creator spaces. How do you stay authentic when so much of your content is satirical?

I blew up kind of for satirical videos, and a lot of people got it. They were like, "Oh, I know you're not actually like that." And then some people thought I was like that, and I'm like, "Wait, but now I'm not being authentic." It's hard. I consider myself pretty multifaceted. I'll livestream, and I never really like to play characters in my livestream. I'll do accents all the time — I love to do an accent — but I'm not gonna fake stuff.

That's where my authenticity comes from. I'm always gonna be real. Someone asks me a question, I'll answer real. I'm not gonna give you a fake answer. It can get difficult with content because you do want to play things up sometimes for the video. But luckily, since I come from the sketch comedy world, it's always going to be a bit. You can tell it's not real.

What do you think keeps people coming back to your content?

I think it's how weird I am. I talk in accents all the time. My manager literally went, "Who is this?" It's just fun. I like putting on silly voices and making weird faces and just being myself on camera. But it's also funny because when people meet me in real life, they're like, "Oh, you're way more chill." It comes out in bursts. I think that's what makes my videos fun.

What's your advice for someone who's scared to put themselves out there?

Just put yourself out there. If you are confident in what you do, just do it. Don't care about what other people think. And I'm saying that, and I do care about what other people think. I get scared to post things sometimes because I'm scared of people thinking it's stupid or commenting something mean.

But luckily, I think my drive is higher than my insecurity. I don't know what to call it. Put yourself out there, because if you're passionate about what you do — I've used the word passion over and over again, especially because of Passion Project — it's gonna pay off. If you love what you do, it's worth it to pursue it.

When I first blew up, I would read every single comment. Even on the video that I always talk about that blew me up — I think that one got over 20 million views — I think I literally read every comment. I was on my phone because it's a dopamine rush. It's honestly pretty shocking initially.

Now I've gotten to the point where once in a while I'll check my DMs or look at the comments. It depends on my mood. There are some videos where maybe I've read every comment. There are some videos where I don't think I even opened the comment section. I'm kind of sporadic.

Do those metrics mean less to you now than they used to?

Yes. I think I'm more prone to looking at the comments on videos that I'm really proud of because those are always really nice. But there are some videos — and I don't make these as much anymore — that were a little more rage bait. A lot of the comments were mean, and so I take one look, and I'm like, "Never mind. I'm done."

There are days when I'll open my comment section, and it ruins my day. That'll happen. That's another piece of advice to creators. I'm one to say it because I do let it ruin my day sometimes, but try not to. That's all you can do.

So: have fun, put yourself out there, don't get consumed by the comments or views, and maybe do a funny little accent.

Do a funny little accent. Everyone will love it.
