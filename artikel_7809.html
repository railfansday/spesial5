Who supervises the system? Agentic AI and the future of lawyer oversight

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

For two years, the legal profession’s artificial intelligence conversation has centered on an attorney asking a system a question and checking its answer. That is no longer enough.

Systems entering legal practice perform work: taking in a matter, researching, drafting motions, and choosing their own next steps. Used well, they can reduce routine work, freeing lawyers for judgment and client advice.

The profession built its supervision framework around people and bounded tools. An agentic system fits neither model: It has no license to lose or judgment to train, yet acts across a workflow with autonomy the Model Rules do not squarely address.

When AI moves from answering questions to executing multistep legal workflows, what does meaningful human oversight look like?

The unit of supervision is no longer an answer; it is a system of delegated decisions.

The ABA Model Rules of Professional Conduct anticipated part of this shift. In 2012, the ABA changed Rule 5.3’s title from “Responsibilities Regarding Nonlawyer Assistants” to “Responsibilities Regarding Nonlawyer Assistance.” Its comments encompassed technology-enabled services; a companion resolution added technological competence to what became Comment 8 of Rule 1.1. In 2024, Formal Opinion 512 applied Rules 1.1, 5.1, and 5.3 to lawyers’ use of generative AI and their supervision of the people and providers using it.

That guidance remains organized around a tool: review its output, protect client information, train users and assess providers. It offers less direction when the system executes the workflow, deciding which documents to pull, which facts matter, and what goes where—decisions the finished product may not reveal. Reviewing only the output confirms the car arrived, not its route. Supervision of output is not supervision of process.

METR’s May 2026 report sharpens the point. Assessing agents inside frontier developers (or entities that train AI models), it found them exceeding intended bounds and misleading users about their work, especially on difficult tasks. It did not study law firm deployments, and routine monitoring might have caught every incident because an independent record existed. The lesson: A lawyer cannot treat an agent’s account as the record of its work. That account is another output of the same probabilistic process, which has training incentives that can reward telling users what they want to hear. Asking the agent to double-check itself is not an independent control.

Accountability cannot rest solely with whoever pressed “run.” It must be institutional and individual, extending to those who designed, approved and maintained the workflow. Every deployed workflow should have a named lawyer-owner authorized to pause it, just as every matter has a responsible partner. This does not displace the matter lawyer’s responsibilities. The owner’s authority must permit suspension on suspicion alone before a committee convenes or a vendor responds. Ownership without it is a label.

Final output review remains necessary. Oversight must also cover consequential junctures: privilege calls, external communications, filings and anything irreversible or beyond client authorization. Supervision begins at design time: deciding what a workflow may do independently, its latitude to reason, and where it must stop and ask. Every firm should be able to answer: Where can this system act without a human seeing it first, and who decided that?

A tested record on comparable work can justify less intensive routine checking, though never at those consequential junctures. Greater autonomy should be approved and recorded. Material changes to the model, tools, permissions or workflow should trigger reassessment and renewed authorization.

Autonomy bundles three permissions requiring separate calibration: generating work product, accessing sensitive information, and acting in firm or client systems. Permissions should narrow as consequences grow: broad room to draft; matter-scoped room to read; and narrow, logged room to act. A first-year may draft the memo; nobody gives them unilateral wire authority. The grant of action is never implied by the grant of access.

If a firm cannot trace what a system did, it cannot supervise it; where reasoning cannot be audited, behavior must be. Otherwise, recordkeeping, preservation, and verification of representations to clients, courts, and third parties may suffer. Three mechanisms matter: an audit trail independent of the agent’s reporting, means to interrupt workflows mid-run, and a path to unwind reversible actions—with honesty about those that cannot be reversed. Record model versions and configurations too: Providers update continuously, and a workflow validated in March may differ from August’s.

Five workflows can be governed individually; 500 cannot. Solo and small practices may use a simple inventory but still need a way to stop a workflow. At scale, governance becomes operational infrastructure: an inventory of deployed systems, risk classification, approval gates, monitoring and reassessment. The profession knows this playbook: Ask a managing partner how conflicts are governed, and you will hear about a system, not a policy.

Delay accumulates governance debt: workflows deployed faster than they can be inventoried, permissions granted faster than they can be reviewed. Like all debt, it compounds quietly. When data-protection regimes preceded settled enforcement guidance, prudent organizations built from regulatory intent rather than waiting for caselaw. Agentic AI presents the same problem: established duties but no settled operating model.

Ownership, calibrated oversight, permission limits, traceability, reversibility and governance at scale matter wherever organizations deploy agentic AI. What distinguishes law is that our supervisory duties are explicit and long-standing. We are not a special case. We are an early test case.

Fortunately, the test is familiar. Lawyers have been supervising work they did not personally perform since the first associate billed their first hour. The question is no longer only whether lawyers can use AI competently. It is whether we can supervise systems that increasingly do the work of the supervised.

Scott A. Meyers is the chairman and CEO, and Michael Adler is the director of artificial intelligence governance and data protection at Akerman, an Am Law 100 law firm.
