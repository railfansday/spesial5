This three-part series examines the informational divide in how we conceptualize issues some view as psychological concerns but which are seen by others as practical challenges with solutions.

We are inundated with advice. Social Media Influencers have swayed people to change their mindsets based on current trends toward actionable solutions like "You should be treated like a Queen," "Stop talking to your toxic parents," and "Why struggle with yo-yo weight cycles when GLP medications are available?" Life coaches see clients as whole persons with goals, not problems to fix. AI chatbot advisors have added another dimension; why explore your psyche if AI can offer suggestions on how to approach people differently and advise you on how to think?

At the other end of the continuum are psychotherapies that vary in their understanding of the human condition. Here-and-now therapies like cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) focus on reframing negative thoughts about why someone remains stuck in life, love, or work and how to cease self-defeating behaviors. Longer-term therapies steeped in classical theories like Freud's and relational theories are seeing renewed interest as people search for greater meaning in understanding themselves, their conscious and unconscious motivations, and their roles and needs in relationships.

There is a difference between understanding psychological intent and motivation and coming up with solutions on how to move forward by shifting narratives in your head, making lists of goals and timelines, and taking a 'no one is going to stand in my way' approach. Psychoanalytic theory examines intent and motivation, narcissistic injury and loss, sex and aggression, and their spectacular influence on how we live. CBT teaches us about the prefrontal cortex and reframing past thoughts and behaviors, focusing on positive change rather than their origin. Influencers promise that willpower and mindset problems can be fixed with the right message or program they promote. Life coaching is not therapy; it is unregulated, and coaches employ many techniques similar to those used in CBT to help clients achieve goals, usually without training, degrees, or personal therapy. These divergent approaches reflect fundamentally different assumptions about human change and whether transformation emerges primarily from deeper self-knowledge and awareness or from deliberate action and behavioral modification strategies moving forward.

Determining why a person chooses one approach over another involves overlap and contradiction, complicated by the impossibility of generalizing mindsets across categories; we are individually unique with distinct historical, familial, experiential, and internal details. Delineating how each approach addresses similar issues is more reliable, and this series examines these mindsets and interventions for addressing two specific psychological issues.

The focus remains on human interventions.

Parts I, II, and III will define terms, share facts, explore perspectives of influencers, life coaches, and two psychotherapies, and examine their approaches to two psychologically impactful issues some consider diagnosable mental health conditions. This series will also ask readers to reflect on their own self-concept and consider what makes sense when seeking help or advising someone on approaches to the issues detailed in two vignettes.

The questions raised are whether all, some, or one available option, like therapy, life coaching, influencers, and AI, might be beneficial for our individual goals, well-being, personal struggles, and relational concerns.

Psychoanalytic thinking teaches us that connection, love, and loss are inherent human needs. We find ourselves in a reductionist, transactional world where quid pro quo never heals a lonely heart and contradicts being cherished for who we are, not what we provide.

The challenges posed by AI are significant. A 'reliable' partner through automation is not better than the real thing, despite what social media asserts. People are imperfect and flawed, not fabrications by AI providing superficial solutions to heal internal unrest, dissatisfaction, and pain. Influencers, life coaches, and short-term therapies suggest human suffering amounts to mere obstacles and shifting narrative is a powerful tool for changing direction.

Tina, a 30-year-old woman who has struggled with weight and yo-yo dieting since adolescence, acknowledges emotionally eating and purging. She wants a relationship but is compelled to stay home eating.

Sam is a 45-year-old man proud of sexual conquests who sees this as masculinity and dominance. He wants a relationship but accepts cheating as inevitable.

Influencer: Since the term Influencer is social media-created, its definition is shaped by content creators like Wikipedia and Merriam-Webster. An influencer is someone who can alter attitudes, behavior, or decisions through authority, knowledge, or relationship with audiences. Their power comes from self-promotion through media outlets, usually for financial gain and fame.

Life Coach: An unregulated industry that helps individuals identify personal and professional goals, navigate transitions, improve communication, take action, and challenge belief systems. The focus is on the future, using present issues to guide advice, create goals, and confront obstacles. (Whitworth et al.)

Psychodynamic Psychotherapist: A licensed mental health professional who works with patients through talk therapy based on established classical theories like Freudian, Interpersonal, Object Relations, and Ego Psychology. Typically, a psychodynamic therapist is trained in multiple clinical theories and has undergone personal psychoanalysis or intensive psychotherapy. These therapists help people explore unconscious thoughts, feelings, and motivations to explain current behavior and relationships, and examine childhood relationship history that shapes their self, interpersonal, and worldview today. People often become stuck due to unconscious fears about confusing, painful, and vulnerable emotions and thoughts. The psychodynamic therapist uses the relationship with the patient as a primary vehicle through which the unconscious reveals itself.

CBT therapist: CBT is a time-limited, structured, problem-solving approach helping people identify and change dysfunctional thinking and behavior. Treatment is based on empirical, hypothesis-based evidence. CBT is skills-based, time-limited, and goal-oriented..

About 14% of US adults received counseling or therapy from mental health professionals.

Data from the International Coaching Federation reports that about 17% of people worldwide have participated in coaching. The most common issues people seek coaching for are health, career, and relationships.

About 40% of US adults get health information from Influencers, with closer to 50% for those under 50. Yet younger adults rate their mental health as fair or poor. (Pew Research Center).

Consider the vignettes above. What approach might be right for you, or how might you advise a friend? Stay tuned for Part II as you consider what the professionals and non-professionals say.
