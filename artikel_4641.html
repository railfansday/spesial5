Finding Solace and Comfort in Listening to Trees

As we approach the holiday season, each of us needs to find ways to take time for ourselves to recover from the activities and responsibilities of the season. Attention restoration theory, proposed by Kaplan and Kaplan (1989), suggests that if we are able to spend time in natural spaces, we can feel rested, renewed, and better able to cope with the stressors and demands of the season. Listening to trees is one healing practice that offers such a possibility by calming the mind and body. Through this practice, we can develop a deeper understanding of ourselves, where we are in life, how we feel, what is important to us, and what we need during this season.

Listening to trees is one contemplative, meditative practice that invites us to slow down, become more attentive, and be more fully present in our everyday lives. At the same time, spending quiet, contemplative time in natural spaces can lead us to experience feelings of transcendence. Transcendent experiences deepen our sense of connection to ourselves, to the natural world, to other people, and to something larger than our individual lives.

Each person experiences transcendence differently. For some, it is connected to religious beliefs and spiritual practices; for others, it emerges through mind-body practice; for others, creative engagement can lead to transcendence. Experiencing transcendence does not involve the withdrawal from the trials and tribulations of everyday life. Instead, it helps us to be more deeply engaged with life by opening ourselves to a greater sense of connection, meaning, and belonging. Transcendent experiences serve dual purposes. They help us not only to ground ourselves more fully in the moment but also to connect to something that surpasses the immediate moment.

The theory of transcendence (Tornstam, 1977) suggests that as we age, we tend to shift our perspective toward more spiritual and cosmic concerns. According to this view, transcendence includes a shift in meaning by recognizing that time is limited, and understanding and accepting the past while at the same time being more fully in the present. It also includes integrating and accepting all aspects of the self, and placing greater emphasis on connections and relationships (social transcendence), and the well-being of the planet. Transcendence in later life, or gerotranscendence, encompasses all three dimensions and focuses on a deep connection with the natural flow of life, with natural spaces, with the future of the planet, and future generations. As we acknowledge that our time is limited, transcendence can help us heal from loss, cope with bereavement, and navigate day-to-day expectations. Transcendence prompts us to shift our focus more fully to what truly matters, while at the same time connecting us to something beyond our individual lives.

One therapeutic way of promoting transcendence is to regularly engage with nature — to listen to trees. Natural spaces provide solace, comfort, and healing, particularly in later life as aging adults contemplate the world they will leave behind. We also know that our individual well-being and happiness are intricately connected to the well-being of the planet. Listening to trees, walking through forests, spending time in parks and on beaches, or simply viewing nature are all physically and psychologically restorative and healing. The Biophilia Hypothesis (Wilson, 1984) even suggests that our connection with nature and natural spaces is fundamental to our survival. Natural immersion, even a few minutes daily in natural spaces, can also deepen our sensory awareness and draw our attention beyond our worries and anxieties toward a transcendent view in which we reflect on who we are, our place in the world, and what will be left when we are no longer here.

Certainly, human beings are not able to survive if nature does not. Our connection to nature is deeply rooted in our biology. Consciously or unconsciously, we have psychological, emotional, and physical connections to the natural world. I first learned about biophilia, our innate human tendency to be connected to nature and living organisms, in graduate school, when I studied the work of psychologist Erich Fromm. Fromm used the term in relation to a love of life and all that is alive (Fromm, 1973). This view has continued to inform my work and my personal life. In my ongoing work on well-being in later life, it has often been evident that our relationship with nature can sustain us throughout our lives, particularly during periods of vulnerability and loss.

In a culture of haste, in our busy lives, and particularly during the winter holidays, when the days are short and light is limited, burnout is an ever-present possibility. Psychological theories have suggested that if we can find the time to connect with natural spaces during this time, we can manage our stressors and demands more effectively and feel more restored. Attention restoration theory (ART) states that time in natural spaces can replenish our ability to manage our attention, concentrate, and be mindfully present in our daily lives and in our holiday gatherings — and in the process help prevent feelings of loneliness and isolation.

The American Psychological Association has stated that the healing and therapeutic benefits of spending time in natural spaces are available even after only spending two hours each week in nature. By setting aside less than 30 minutes a day to walk, unplug, breathe deeply, and mindfully take in our surroundings, we can begin to experience the restorative energy that nature has to offer. Even limited exposure to nature can be beneficial. Simply viewing nature can have a calming and positive influence on both physical and emotional well-being (Tahmaseb McConatha et al., 2023).

As the holidays approach, one way to stay grounded and prevent burnout is to reconnect with natural spaces. One simple practice is to listen to the trees. This experience can be deepened by finding a comfortable place near or beneath a tree and sitting quietly for a few moments. Even five minutes can help calm the mind and body.

There is no need to find the perfect spot or the perfect tree, or to set expectations or goals for your practice. Instead, give yourself permission to pause, be present, and take in the world around you. Simply engage your senses by noticing the sounds, scents, colors, textures, and gentle movements of nature. Allow yourself to become more aware of your surroundings and embrace the quiet of the moment.

When you are ready to move on, thank your tree for being there and offering stillness, comfort, and a place to simply be.

Renewing transcendence can be a regular practice. It does not require anything other than slowing down, connecting with nature and senses, and allowing ourselves to be fully present.
