In August 2017, Myanmar’s military launched sweeping “clearance operations” against alleged insurgents in the country’s Rakhine state. Within just three months, security forces had killed thousands of people, razed hundreds of villages, committed widespread sexual violence, and forced some 700,000 Rohingya Muslims to flee into neighboring Bangladesh.

Nine years later, the International Court of Justice (ICJ) found Myanmar guilty of genocide. The landmark ruling, delivered Thursday, marked the first time that the United Nations’ top court has found a country directly responsible for violating the 1948 Genocide Convention.

Foreign Policy spoke with Tom Andrews, the former U.N. special rapporteur on human rights in Myanmar, a few hours after the ruling came down. He discussed the decision’s implications, including where justice lies for the world’s largest stateless population and how the ruling could affect similar genocide cases linked to the wars in Gaza and Ukraine.

This interview has been edited for length and clarity.

Foreign Policy: Earlier today, the ICJ found that Myanmar’s security forces had committed genocide against the country’s minority Rohingya Muslim community. For those who aren’t aware of the case, could you give a brief primer on the court’s findings?

Tom Andrews: They were very explicit in their findings. They talked about the thousands and thousands of people who were killed, the thousands of women who were raped, the more than 2,000 children who were killed, the hundreds of thousands of homes that were burned.

I was in Bangladesh when people had arrived [in 2017], and I sat and talked to many people who had literally been running for their lives. And the stories they told me were just absolutely incredible. I mean the horror, the brutality, the systematic nature of the killing, of the rapes, of the torture, was just absolutely extraordinary. And, of course, more than 700,000 people escaped into Bangladesh; we know that 1.3 million are now in Bangladesh, in the largest refugee camp in the world.

But what [the Rohingya] did was they pushed for justice and they pushed for accountability. And there were people who were victimized by this brutality, who came to the court, the International Court of Justice, and provided their own personal testimony, told their own stories in a private session, which I believe was a very powerful, powerful part of this whole process, where it allowed the judges to focus on the actual consequences, the human element of this.

FP: This is the first time that the U.N.’s top court has found a nation directly responsible for genocide. What is the significance of that ruling?

TA: History was made today. The standard for genocide is very, very high. And that is a reason why this is so remarkable. This is the first time it’s ever been made, because the standard not only includes the acts but also the intention behind those acts—that [the state] designed to harm a population in whole or in part.

But the question that it begs is, well, OK, now what? Now that we’ve established genocide, are there consequences for genocide, and what are they? And the court has ruled that the regime in Myanmar is responsible for providing relief and compensation for the victims.

I believe that that needs to occur both within the United Nations and outside of the United Nations. Within the United Nations, I believe that the United Nations Security Council should take this up. I think they should pass a resolution. I think that these individuals who were responsible for this genocide should be criminally prosecuted, and so there should be a reference to the International Criminal Court for that criminal prosecution.

I think it’s also important for us to recognize that the killings that continue to go on are happening because the military junta—the generals and [junta leader] Min Aung Hlaing—has access to sophisticated weapons of war that it’s using to attack civilians. I think it’s really important that the international community do everything that it can to cut off that access to those weapons and those weapon materials, and the financing that’s being used to acquire those weapons and those weapon materials, so that we can stop that flow.

Another step that they can take is to reject this rehabilitation campaign that Min Aung Hlaing has been involved in now for the past few months. He’s been traveling to states in the region, to his neighbors, to ASEAN [Association of Southeast Asian Nations] states. He’s been invited into these capitals, welcomed into these capitals. He’s been seeking to gain greater acceptance within ASEAN and the structure of ASEAN. And I think what’s really important is for those member states who have been moving in that direction of accepting or softening their relationship to Min Aung Hlaing and this genocidal regime to reverse those steps and that the regime be as isolated diplomatically as it deserves to be.

FP: Do you think that the ruling will be enough, though, to actually push member states to take that next step and start to reverse the junta’s efforts to cast off its diplomatic pariah status?

TA: I certainly hope so. I mean, this is unprecedented. This is a powerful, powerful moment for the world. I think it’s incredibly important for the world to respond to that finding in a responsible way and that member states and their citizens need to stand up and make their voices very, very clear and very strong that their government needs to do everything that it can to isolate this regime, weaken this regime, take away this regime’s capacity to continue to commit murder against its own people.

I mean, this is a moral imperative, and it is a call for everyone to make a moral stand for not only the Rohingya, not only for the people of Myanmar, but for justice and humanity around the world. Because the thing is, if we establish that there is no consequence for genocide, then we can be guaranteed that genocide is going to happen again. And it’s only by imposing consequences that we can begin to see justice prevail, not only in this case but in future cases.

FP: What’s next for the Rohingya community themselves? The ICJ doesn’t have an enforcement mechanism, but could this case have a tangible impact on the future of that population?

TA: It can, and it should. First of all, the world has turned its back on the Rohingya. Support for the conditions in which they’re living, the refugee camps, has been declining. There have been increased difficulties in just being able to survive in these camps, in part, certainly, by the fact of the international community reversing commitments that it had made and support that it provided to people who are in these conditions for no fault of their own.

Now, I think, with respect to reparations, the idea that the government of Myanmar—the current illegitimate regime of Myanmar—would accept the order of the court to provide compensation is, at best, a very, very remote possibility. In fact, I think that the chances of this happening are exactly zero. So, until there is a new regime that actually is legitimate and believes in justice and accountability and human rights, until that day happens, I think the world has to step forward, and I think there are options the world can take.

For example, the United Nations can establish a reparations fund and a mechanism to manage that. The United States, for example, seized over a billion dollars in assets from this regime. And that billion dollars can be used as part of a compensation fund, and that can then be built upon with governments coming forward to provide additional resources so that there is some form of justice and compensation for those who were victimized by these attacks.

FP: What is the view coming out of Myanmar right now? Is there talk of this case possibly leading to something like regime change?

TA: I’ve heard reports of additional hate speech that’s beginning to be drummed up, some of the nationalists that have used hate speech against the Rohingya in the past, that they are beginning to once again generate that kind of hatred against the Rohingya. It’s a dangerous moment in the country.

FP: Obviously, this case has an outsized impact on Myanmar’s future, but could this ruling affect similar cases at the ICJ on the conflicts in Gaza and Ukraine?

TA: It remains to be seen. The U.S. ambassador to the United Nations [Mike Waltz] posted a tweet in which—I’m paraphrasing here, but [he] accused the court of being poised to lower the standard for genocide, which could have an impact on future cases. And what’s happened is that the court was very explicit in saying that they used the current standard for genocide, including the standard of genocidal intent, in making this judgment. So, in fact, there was no lowering of the standard for genocide that some have accused the court of preparing to do.

I think what’s important is to focus on this case, to focus on this community and on people who were wronged, and to focus on our responsibility to them and to accountability and justice for them and to make sure that there is a consequence for genocide. So I’m hoping that we’re not distracted by what might happen in the future, that we’re focused on what’s happening right now.

I saw a report that a child is killed in these attacks every 29 hours in Myanmar now. So, yes, we need to stop the atrocities from continuing, but we need to focus on responsibility and accountability for these acts and these acts of genocide.
