Sea otters are adorable and iconic, but few people understand how important they are to ocean ecosystems

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

Otters eat urchins, and urchins eat kelp. On Southern California reefs, a study suggests, competition among grazers and seaweeds weakened that chain.

Sea otters eat sea urchins, and sea urchins eat kelp, so otters are famous for bringing kelp forests back. Off Vancouver Island, it happened so fast that a reef was almost always one or the other: covered in urchins or covered in kelp.

Around San Nicolas Island, off Southern California, otters, urchins, and kelp lived together for years.

A study of both coasts suggests that on the southern reefs, competition among other species weakened the otters’ effect on kelp.

The fur trade wiped out sea otters across most of the places where they once lived. Otters were brought back to Vancouver Island from 1969 to 1972 and to San Nicolas Island from 1987 to 1990.

Researchers surveyed the same marked sites year after year, eight off Vancouver Island and six around San Nicolas. The records run through 2017.

A team led by Ryan Langendorf of the Department of Environmental Studies at the University of Colorado Boulder built a statistical model from those records. The study was published in March 2025.

The model’s numbers are estimates of how strongly species affected one another, not direct measurements.

Ecologists call the sea otter a keystone species. That means it has an unusually large effect on the other species around it.

“We always thought keystone species control their ecosystem the same way, regardless of where they are or what else is in the ecosystem,” Langendorf said.

“A more modern view is that they are still very important, but they can have different effects in different places.”

Otters reduce urchins, and urchins reduce kelp. Two negative effects in a row make a positive one for kelp.

Competition can add a third negative step, and three in a row make a negative one.

At San Nicolas, one of the strongest chains of that kind involved turban snails. Otters eat the snails, and the snails compete with red urchins. With fewer snails, red urchins can do better, and red urchins eat giant kelp.

The model estimated that these extra chains often made little difference. In some years at some San Nicolas sites, though, they reduced the otters’ effect on giant kelp by 15% to 20%.

Off Vancouver Island, the extra chains made almost no difference.

Fish aren’t in the model, because the team lacked the data. Records of what the otters ate are missing too.

Around San Nicolas, grazing animals didn’t eat giant kelp down to nothing, even before otters were brought back. The authors wrote that their models don’t adequately explain why.

Future analyses could include otter diets, the authors wrote. That may help explain how much the mix of urchin species shaped the otters’ effect.

The full study was published in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.
