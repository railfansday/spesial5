Power shutoffs meant to prevent wildfires may carry unexpected health risks

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Planned power shutoffs in California were linked to more respiratory-related hospital visits, particularly when wildfire smoke was also present.

When the wind picks up on a hot, dry day in fire country, the power company may switch off the electricity on purpose. A line with no current in it can’t start a fire when it drops into dry brush.

A planned shutoff has a cost of its own, though. In California, on days when a large shutoff reached a neighborhood, people there were more likely to turn up at an emergency room with a breathing problem.

The rise was largest among people with chronic lung disease, and wildfire smoke in the air at the same time made it larger still.

Lauren Blair Wilner, an epidemiologist at the University of Washington, led the analysis with colleagues at Scripps Institution of Oceanography and Columbia University.

Utilities call these planned outages public safety power shutoffs, or PSPS, and no study before this one had measured what they do to hospital traffic.

In an interview with Earth.com, Wilner pointed to the days when the two overlapped.

“Surprisingly, the effects of a PSPS event and wildfire smoke co-occurring were not simply additive – we found evidence that their effects on health were multiplicative,” she said.

Three major California utilities serve 88% of the state’s electric customers. They have run these power shutoffs since 2012 and report each one to the California Public Utilities Commission.

The research group PSE Healthy Energy compiled those reports.

Wilner’s team matched the reports for 2013 through 2019, by zip code, against every emergency room visit and unplanned hospital admission by adults across 1,504 California zip codes.

Those records came from the California Department of Health Care Access and Information and included more than 6.6 million visits for breathing problems.

Each visit day was compared with the other days of the same weekday that month, so every patient served as their own comparison.

A large shutoff meant more than 593 customers in a zip code without power.

On those days, the odds of an emergency room visit or admission for a breathing problem were about 6% higher. Smaller power shutoffs made no measurable difference.

Shutoffs and smoke arrive together more often than not. Of the 11,293 zip-code days with a shutoff, 7,656 also had wildfire smoke in the air.

Asked by Earth.com what else the study showed, Wilner came back to those overlapping days. “Co-occurrence is what we really need to think about here,” she said.

Because days with both outnumbered days with a shutoff alone, she added, shutoffs should be studied together with the fires they are meant to prevent.

Picture a day with a large shutoff and 10 micrograms of smoke particles in each cubic meter of air. The odds of a breathing-related visit that day were 16% higher than on a day with neither.

The shutoff alone added about 6% and that much smoke alone about 1%, so the pair did more than add up.

The pattern was strongest among people with COPD, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, a long-term illness that narrows the airways and makes breathing hard work.

On days with a large shutoff, the odds of a COPD visit were 29% higher. With smoke on top, they were 37% higher.

Many people with COPD rely on an oxygen concentrator, a machine that pulls oxygen out of room air.

It runs on electricity, and its battery typically lasts three to four hours. A typical shutoff in this study lasted 86 hours, more than three days.

Wilner said planners should take simultaneous outages into account as they prepare for wildfires and expand healthcare capacity.

“It is essential to put measures in place to ensure that people relying on electricity dependent medical equipment have access to power and can readily access healthcare, if needed,” she said.

Visits for heart problems didn’t rise during shutoffs, with or without smoke, and neither did visits for mental health crises.

Earlier studies of blackouts in New York State and Florida largely agree, suggesting that outages may be more strongly associated with respiratory problems than heart problems.

How long a shutoff lasted also mattered less than how many people it reached.

When the team counted total hours without power over the previous week instead, they found no link between duration alone and hospital visits.

Each additional 1,000 customers without power, on the other hand, was linked to about 2% higher odds of a breathing-related visit. For COPD visits, the figure was 6%.

Utilities report outages by circuit, so the team can’t say who inside a zip code lost power.

The researchers assumed customers were spread evenly along each circuit, which means some people counted as exposed weren’t.

Nor can the records show whether a patient was home during a shutoff. People may travel to a place that still has power, and breathe more smoke on the way.

Only adults 20 and older who reached a hospital are counted, so anyone who got sick at home and stayed there is missing.

The study covered 2013 to 2019, when planned power shutoffs were less common than they are today.

Since 2022, utilities in Washington, Oregon, Idaho, and Hawaii have used shutoffs too. California requires utilities to warn customers first and to open centers where people can charge devices.

Wilner’s team didn’t test whether those centers keep anyone out of the emergency room.

Wilner’s team is turning to what happens after a wildfire or other disaster, when outages and evacuations overlap.

“There are not complete datasets on historical evacuations and power outages after disasters, making it impossible to study the ways in which these things play out and affect health after an event,” she told Earth.com.

Those records, she said, would show what affected communities need and how to reach them.

For now, seven years of California records offer valuable insight into the potential health consequences of planned power shutoffs.

The full study was published in the journal PLOS Medicine.
