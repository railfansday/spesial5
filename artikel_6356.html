A refrigerator is intended to do only three things: preserve food, store it properly, and supply enough lighting to see all contents. We also want our fridge to be reliable and last a long time so we don’t have to go through this exhaustive shopping exercise again until it’s absolutely necessary.

When shopping for a refrigerator, we're not just looking for something that keeps our food cold. We require aesthetic appeal from our primary kitchen appliance. We also crave convenience in the form of spacious and adjustable shelves and bins, temperature or humidity-controlled spaces, an ice maker so we can ditch the refillable trays, and whatever other clever additions fridge makers come up with to lure us into spending more cash.

If you haven’t shopped for a refrigerator in a while, we’ve got some good news and some bad news. The bad news is that refrigerators may be more expensive due to ongoing tariffs on both foreign steel and aluminum used to manufacture models in the U.S. and on wholly imported fridges. The good news, though, is that there is a wider variety of designs and sizes to match your family’s specific needs, plus features like exterior or interior water and ice dispensing options, separate food-specific cooling spaces, and an abundance of smart tech.

To help you find the perfect fridge for your kitchen, we researched a variety of options based on specific criteria and found the best of the best. We also made sure our picks all varied in terms of style, so below, you'll find our top recommendations for French door refrigerators, side-by-side fridges, and a number of other types, as well.

As with any other appliance, our consideration for best models was primarily based on value—capacity and features for the money. Value also incorporates the reputation of the manufacturer and opinions from both retailers and consumers (from online reviews). We also compared capacities and feature sets of various models and took into account our own hands-on experiences at appliance stores and trade shows over the years.

When faced with seemingly equal choices, especially on price, we always leaned toward higher-capacity models since we know there’s no such thing as too much refrigerator space. However, we also recognize that higher-capacity refrigerators are usually full or standard depth models that may be too big for many kitchens, so we’ve tried to add less pricey, smaller alternatives where we found them.

Each fridge form offers varying cubic-foot capacity, usually expressed as total space. Until recently, inside capacity was somewhat restricted by the need for refrigerant circulation and insulation inside a fridge’s thick walls. But many newer models with updated cooling and insulation technologies have slimmed these inside walls to create so-called “max,” “mega,” or “XL” capacity models, creating more inside capacity without increasing exterior dimensions.

Bear in mind, some vendors fudge on capacity, often rounding up to the nearest whole number in the name of the fridge. For instance, a “23” cubic foot model may actually only contain 22.5 cubic feet of storage.

So how do you decide how big your new fridge needs to be? Obviously, how much capacity you can buy depends on how much space you have in your kitchen for a refrigerator.

Figure 4- to 6 cubic feet per family member, leaning toward the higher end for a lot of shared items. Alternatively, you can start with ten cubic feet for the household, then add 1.5 cubic feet per household member to get a minimum capacity.

In terms of fridge-freezer split, most models assign 65 to 70 percent of space to cooling and the remaining 30 to 35 percent to freezing. If you’re a bulk buyer or frequent meal preparer, we recommend you find a model with more freezer space.

You want shelves and inside door bins to offer varying height adjustments to accommodate the tallest types of containers such as opened wine bottles. Some fridges offer shelves that slide out and door bins that can accommodate bulky gallon jugs or tilt out for easier access.

All refrigerators include storage drawers, but the best are those models that offer drawers with some sort of temperature and/or humidity control. When shopping, check the drawers to make sure they slide in and out smoothly, come out easily for cleaning, and don’t feel cheap.

Refrigerators with twin doors—side-by-side and French—are the only models in which you’ll find exterior in-door water and ice dispensers. Most in-door water dispensers also dispense ice—often both cubed and crushed—but some don’t offer ice, so double-check this before you buy. If a twin-door model doesn’t include an exterior water dispenser, there’s likely one inside the fridge.

If you’re replacing a fridge without an in-door water dispensing with one that does, you (or, more likely, a plumber) will likely first have to install a separate water line that connects from your under-sink pipes to your new refrigerator. You’ll also occasionally have to change the refrigerator’s water filter—usually around twice a year—but ask first to make sure of the timing. Our advice: order in a supply of filters so you’re sure you’re always dispensing the freshest filtered water.

A growing number of “smart” refrigerators include interior cameras to let you view your fridge’s contents to confirm if you need something while grocery shopping. A camera-equipped smart model with a smart screen on the door can also scan your remaining ingredients and create an edible recipe, assemble a shopping list and create an order from a grocery delivery service, and even identify what food items have reached their expiration date.

Yes, refrigerators still come in basic white, but you also can choose from a growing variety of subtleties such as gray and black tones in shiny or matte finishes (with smudge- or finger-resistant coatings). But a few fridge makers also offer custom-made colors, including finishes to match your kitchen cabinets or decor.

Do note that a model listed as having a “stainless” finish does not necessarily mean you’re getting an actual stainless steel door. In most instances, just the word “stainless” likely means the door is some other material, not stainless steel. To make sure you’re getting a true stainless steel door, first feel it, as stainless steel is cooler to the touch than other materials. Also, take along a refrigerator magnet—most stainless steel doors are not magnetic.

There are, essentially, only five forms of fridges, all defined by how the doors and drawers are arranged. In order of price, features available, and capacity (low to high), the five fridge forms are:

This is the most familiar no-frills fridge format with two doors: a top freezer compartment and a larger fresh food compartment below. Top freezer fridges are usually the least expensive fridge option, landing somewhere between $500-$900. They also range between 10 to 24 cubic feet of space, and are best suited for single folks or couples on a budget, or as a second fridge for a recreational basement or garage.

No frills means you’re unlikely to find any top freezer models with in-door water dispensers or an ice maker. Most models come with reversible doors that open left-to-right or right-to-left, however.

A.k.a. “bottom freezer” models, these refrigerators are equipped with a single door up top and a freezer drawer usually encompassing two shelving tiers below. Bottom freezer models are presumably more attractive to those with fewer freezer needs since you have to kneel or bend over to rummage through it.

Bottom freezer models generally provide a bit more freezer space than a top freezer— around 18 to 22 cubic feet of total capacity—and typically cost more than a top freezer model. Like a top freezer model, you’re unlikely to find bottom freezer models with in-door water dispensers, but some pricier versions do include ice makers.

Rather than a horizontal freezer, side-by-side models offer vertical freezing and cooling compartments. Side-by-side interior horizontal space, however, is narrower than a top- or bottom-freezer model, so wide platter trays or frozen pizzas may not fit. Side-by-sides are more likely to offer in-door water or ice dispensing, which can further reduce freezer space.

Essentially a side-by-side with a bottom freezer, except the two top French doors open out to reveal a single large and wide cooling compartment. Since there’s no wall dividing the top section, French door models offer the highest capacity—up to around 30 cubic feet worth of fresh food room—which makes these the best option for a large and active family.

French door models usually include either in-door or interior water and ice dispensers. Higher capacity, of course, means higher prices—French door models start at around $1,400 depending on interior space and features, such as more convenient separate dual freezer drawers.

Four-door French-door fridges replace the bottom freezer drawer(s) with freezer doors that open into separate compartments. However, you’ll see models with French doors with dual drawer freezers referred to as “four-door,” which they are not since a drawer is decidedly not a door. More expensive four-door models allow you to convert one of the freezer sections into an extra refrigerator compartment if you need extra cooling space.

Most refrigerators last around seven years. Spending a bit more now on a better model means you can go a lot longer without having to buy a replacement. Models with long warranties usually signal a longer expected lifespan.

Our general refrigerator shopping advice: you already know your current refrigerator’s shortcomings (that’s presumably why you’re looking to buy a new one). Match your known needs with the features outlined above that you find attractive before you go to a store and are confronted by a pushy salesperson.

It's not recommended to turn your fridge on and off for short periods of time because it can actually increase wear on the compressor, but powering down during longer absences helps save energy and prevents issues like mold.

Before heading out for vacation or an extended trip, you should remove all food, clean and dry the interior, unplug the appliance, and keep the doors propped open for air circulation.
