Berkshire Has Owned These 2 Consumer Stocks for Decades. I'd Buy Both Before December and Never Sell.

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

These two Berkshire holdings have outlasted almost everything else; here's why I'd own both before the calendar flips.

By Micah Zimmerman – Nov 23, 2026 at 10:45AM EST

10 stocks we like better than American Express ›

Berkshire Hathaway (BRKB +0.59%)(BRKA +0.55%) filed its latest list of stock holdings this week, and I skipped straight to two lines. Coca-Cola (KO -1.20%): 400 million shares. American Express (AXP +1.13%): 151.6 million shares.

Those numbers barely ever move. Berkshire completed its Coca-Cola purchase in 1994 and essentially finished buying Amex in 1995. Greg Abel, who now runs Berkshire, named both among the businesses "we understand well, have a high regard for their leaders, and expect will compound over decades." Warren Buffett said it best back in 1988, the year he started buying Coke: "Our favorite holding period is forever."

I'd buy both before December. One has a hard deadline, and the other is heading into its favorite time of year.

Coca-Cola will pay its next quarterly dividend to shareholders of record on Dec. 1. That makes Dec. 1 the ex-dividend date, too, so you'd need to buy by the close on Monday, Nov. 30. One payment won't make anyone rich, but Buffett wrote in 2022 that Berkshire's Coca-Cola dividend grew every year after 1994, "just as certain as birthdays."

What I like more is how Coca-Cola is growing. CEO Henrique Braun said on the earnings call that after visiting markets around the world, "affordability is working" and "both Zero Sugar and Original Taste are recruiting consumers." That means 500ml Super Cans plus low-cost single-serve packs in places like Vietnam and South Africa, while Coca-Cola Zero Sugar grew 16% last quarter. Closer to home, Braun pointed to strong volume from fairlife protein shakes, Powerade, and smartwater, so this isn't just a soda story anymore.

Coca-Cola has now raised its full-year outlook three quarters in a row, but CNBC noted demand in its home market has been lackluster. Braun still sounded confident about North America, saying the price increases sticking there show "that the brands have the equity and the right to take price." That's pricing power.

There's also a long-running tax fight, with CFO John Murphy saying Coca-Cola is managing its balance sheet "as we await a court decision related to our ongoing dispute with the IRS." And the stock sits near its 52-week high, so this isn't a bargain buy.

American Express (AXP +1.13%) is technically a financial stock, but it lives and dies on consumer spending.

Its "before December" moment is Small Business Saturday on Nov. 28. "Since creating Small Business Saturday in 2010, we've encouraged consumers to discover and support the businesses in their neighborhoods," said Chief Marketing Officer Elizabeth Rutledge in a press release.

The pitch to shop owners is simple: Amex says the average U.S. card member spends 3 times as much as people carrying cards on other networks.

The bigger story is who's signing up. CEO Steve Squeri said new card members are "predominantly millennials and Gen Z," and a year after Amex refreshed its Platinum card, CFO Christophe Le Caillec said, "Spend retention remains at 99%."

Amex keeps piling on reasons to stay, from a new Airbnb partnership to Resy Rewards, launched this week in New York City, which gives card members extra points for dining at participating restaurants. "Rewarding diners while driving demand and revenue to Resy restaurants is a win on both sides of the table," said Resy CEO Pablo Rivero. That's a network effect in action, with more diners drawing in more restaurants.

It hasn't been a spotless year. Regulators hit Amex with a consent order and a civil money penalty, and Squeri admitted, "The deficiencies cited in the consent order should not have occurred." It's also cutting about 5% of its workforce. And rivals like Chase, Citigroup, and Capital One are chasing the same wealthy customers, a fight Squeri expects to remain fierce because premium cardholders are such a profitable slice of the market.

Berkshire never needed perfect years from either company. Both have weathered recessions, fads, and plenty of rivals since the 1990s. What Berkshire needed was brands people kept choosing, decade after decade, and a buy-and-hold strategy patient enough to let the dividends pile up.

Coca-Cola and Amex are still those brands, and I plan to own them the way Berkshire does.
