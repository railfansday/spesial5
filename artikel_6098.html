FAA’s Flight Shutdown Fuels A $30 Billion Funding Push—Most Of The Money Isn’t Even For Air Traffic Control

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

After the FAA communications breakdown brought departures across the country to a halt on Friday, the travel industry has its answer: give air traffic control another $30 billion. Most of the money in the bill they’re pushing wouldn’t even go to air traffic control. The U.S. Travel Association says Friday’s ground stop demonstrates that the FAA “lacks the resources necessary to modernize America’s aging aviation infrastructure.” It wants Congress to pass the Modern Skies Act sponsored by Senator Eric Schmitt (R-MO) before the end of this Congress. That legislation would provide $30.1 billion, including $15 billion for airport terminals. It would leave the FAA running air traffic control under essentially the same institutional structure that’s spent decades failing to deliver promised improvements. We need modern equipment. We also need to modernize the organization buying and operating it. Using this failure to sell airport terminal subsidies does neither particularly well. A Local Equipment Change Disrupted Most Of The National Network The nationwide departure stop began at 3:54 p.m. Eastern Friday and lasted until 8:15 p.m. The airline recovery took much longer. There were over 18,000 cancellations from Friday through Sunday. Planes and crews wound up in the wrong places. Crew duty limits and missed connections kept the disruption going after communications stabilized. The FAA identifies two sites: a radio facility in El Dorado, Arkansas, where contractor L3Harris was installing a router, and a radar facility in Amarillo, Texas. Excessive connection attempts flooded their ancient private network, disrupting aircraft communications and radar feeds at 17 of 22 en route centers and spreading into approach control facilities. Blocking the two sites stabilized the network. Officials also disabled the broadcasting function on about 60 similar routers. Some radio frequencies and radar feeds kept working, allowing aircraft already airborne to land. FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford describes the work as routine maintenance, separate from the new $12.5 billion modernization program. That makes the question about managing the system day to day even more pressing if it can destabilize the whole system with only the third nationwide ground stop in U.S. history. Most Of The $30 Billion Would Go To Airports John Kefaliotis at Reason Foundation breaks down the spending. The introduced bill, S. 5572: Purpose Funding Terminal rebuilding pilot for up to three airports $10 billion Other airport terminal grants $5 billion Discretionary airport improvement grants $3 billion Common automation platform and controller workstation $7.2 billion Cloud computing and associated telecommunications $2 billion Air traffic control facilities, including digital towers $2 billion Air traffic flow management technology $600 million Consolidating terminal air traffic control facilities $300 million That’s $12.1 billion for air traffic control systems and facilities, versus $18 billion for airports. About 40% goes to air traffic control. The administration’s announcement includes building airports, but the sales pitch after Friday’s failure is that Congress needs to fund the air traffic control fix. The $10 billion terminal pilot is particularly brazen. It would cover up to 90% of costs at no more than three medium hub airports. Eligibility requires a principal terminal built before 1990 that hasn’t undergone comprehensive redevelopment since then, plus a project with environmental review complete and final design underway. The Transportation Secretary picks the recipients. The bill’s sponsor is a Missouri senator. At St. Louis airport last month, Duffy was asked whether the program appeared tailor made for the airport’s single terminal project. His answer: “That’s a fair assessment.” It’s not even clear whether other airports even qualify given the Rube Goldbergesque rules in the bill. It’s pure pork barrel spending. The same local report notes that Southwest Airlines, which operates more than half of Lambert’s flights, would otherwise bear the largest share of the terminal project’s cost. Moving that bill to federal taxpayers is a windfall for Southwest. The separate $3 billion airport grant pot also bypasses normal allocation formulas and minimum shares. Politicians get more discretion over where the money goes. Bundling that with air traffic control improvements makes it easier to portray objections to the airport spending as opposition to aviation safety. The FAA Has Already Been Spending Billions Congress provided roughly $12.5 billion in the One Big Beautiful Bill Act last year, also from taxpayers rather than airlines and passengers. That came on top of billions in earlier modernization spending largely lit on fire. The Transportation Department’s inspector general reports that the FAA spent more than $15 billion on NextGen through the end of 2024. The effort began in 2003, with completion originally envisioned for 2025. Major capabilities slipped to 2030 or later, costs rose, and the agency reduced what it would deliver. There are improvements underway. Duffy says 69% of copper communications have been replaced and electronic flight strips are installed at 25 towers. But he also acknowledged the FAA’s history during Saturday’s briefing: “They haven’t spent the money well.” Secretary Sean Duffy’s November 14 briefing on the ground stop and modernization funding The FAA set out a plan in 1983 “to equip 150 to 250 airport control towers by 2000.” They went way over budget and didn’t accomplish much. Meanwhile, all of Nav Canada facilities went electronic 15 years ago (and all control towers and TRACONs even earlier). Their solution is used in Australia, Italy, the U.K. and Dubai. We could license the Canadian solution, or other commercial ones, but instead the FAA has been contracting for their own solution since three years before the Beastie Boys were fighting for your right to party. When I reviewed the administration’s plan in May 2025, my concern was that the same institution that had failed to deliver for decades would be asked to execute another enormous upgrade, faster, without fixing its underlying incentives. And there wasn’t a plan to make real changes there.

I’ve laid out the Canadian alternative. NAV CANADA has operated since 1996 as a private nonprofit without equity shareholders. It funds operations and capital investment through customer charges and issuing bonds for long-term investments. Transport Canada regulates its safety.

Commercial airlines elect four of its 15 directors. Government, employees, general aviation and independent directors also have roles. There’s no need to hand the system to an airline monopoly to get it out of congressional budgeting.

The FAA, by contrast, regulates itself – which is a recipe for unaccountability. An outside regulator would have a clearer job: demand safe performance from an operator whose failures it isn’t also responsible for defending.
