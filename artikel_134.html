The leave campaign has been resurrected, so how should Burnham respond?

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

No 10 is confident the PM can make the case for closer EU ties, while others worry about leaving the campaign solely in his hands

The launch of a pro-Brexit campaign was one of the few genuine surprises this week in Westminster, even if the arguments were straight out of a familiar playbook.

The video launched on X over the weekend, with scenes of riots in France, champagne flowing in Brussels, and European politicians saying how desperate they were for Britain’s cash.

Among Westminster’s anti-Brexit veterans, there was immediate chatter about whether a counteroffensive was needed. “I think the immediate reaction was – are they off to the races while we are still putting our pants on?” one said.

For now, the rebuttal of Stay Out’s claims – including that returning to the EU would cost £750m a week – is mostly being done on social media by interested pro-rejoin freelancers, without any kind of central organisation.

One MP said: “It is hard for us to begin any kind of new pro-rejoin campaign until we know exactly the direction the government is heading.

“There are organisations that exist already, like Best for Britain, but I think making the case to the public would need a fresh movement. I think we want to stay away from the kind of marches that People’s Vote movement did with the blue berets if we can possibly avoid it.”

Inside No 10, there is a confidence that Andy Burnham can make his own case for a closer relationship with Europe, without the need for any external pressure group. Stay Out – which is run by the former deputy chief Brexit negotiator Oliver Lewis – will be dubbed “the status quo campaign”.

There is reason to be confident. Strategists have seen Labour polling that shows almost three-quarters – 71% – of all voters support a closer relationship with Europe if it helps bring down prices and grow the economy.

It will be the prime minister, they say, who can front any campaigning that is needed, who can argue that Brexit has done more harm than good, damaging the economy, making it harder for British people to travel and businesses to trade and fuelling the small boats crisis.

However, there will be some fears that this has been tried before when David Cameron attempted to make the case for remain in 2016. But Labour strategists say this is a fundamentally different political moment. “Cameron could not make this kind of emotional connection with people, it was business leaders, facts and figures, and he was arguing for the status quo,” one said. “We are the change campaign now.”

While some cabinet members urged caution last week over Burnham’s uncertain direction on Europe, most trust the prime minister can set out a viable post-summit plan. “All of us, honestly, are slightly running to catch up with a different way of doing things,” one said. “We all easily lapse into the worries about leave-voting seats.

“But there is obviously an argument to be made that the old rules don’t apply. And Andy’s way of doing things is laying out political argument. And we have to believe in that as the way to affect public sentiment.”

There are some close to Labour who worry about leaving the argument solely to Burnham. “Can they do this from the bowels of Downing Street?” one asked. “You do need rebuttal out there and people ready to go out and fight and say, this is bollocks. And it felt like that infrastructure is not there yet.”

Another veteran of the remain campaign said there was concern about being “left out on a limb” if a rejoin campaign was resurrected. “I think whether they like it or not, there will be an apolitical campaign trying to push the maximalist position on Europe. They have people willing to help but it’s not organised in any way.”

Many of those who worked on the remain campaign and the subsequent People’s Vote are scarred by the experience, particular the final months which descended into bitter infighting. But the same is also true for some of the most high-profile veterans of Vote Leave – who also want to steer clear of Stay Out.

As in the pro-Brexit campaigns, there are often willing funders to push pro-European campaigning. “It will happen because there will be money for it,” one campaign veteran said. “But you could not pay me any money to go back to that issue.”
