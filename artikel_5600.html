Midterm Takeaways: Democratic Subpoenas, State ‘Trifectas’ and Bi-Coastal Ballot Wins

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

A midterm election that saw both historic turnout and spending ended with big wins for the Democratic party nearly across the board. While voters clearly went to the ballot box seeking change, can real estate expect the landslide result to boost a sluggish housing market, bring down rates or spark more regulatory scrutiny into housing practices?

At this very early stage, the answer to most of these questions is, unsurprisingly, a big maybe.

While the mainstream focus is always on Congressional elections (where Democrats made historic gains), impactful federal legislation for real estate appears unlikely. President Donald Trump promised to leverage his veto powers if Democrats won, previewing even more gridlock in Washington, but also told the Wall Street Journal on election night that he would work with Democrats.

Realtor.com® Senior Economist Jake Krimmel said in a statement that relief for the housing market at large “relies on a calmer geopolitical environment,” saying that it was unclear whether Democratic control of Congress could deliver this.

Real estate professionals understand well, though, housing is a local issue, and the results most likely to be impactful for practitioners are coming in lower-profile state elections. At the same time, though, a strongly Democratic congress in D.C. offers potential new wrinkles to high-profile real estate controversies and investigations.

As the dust settles on ballot initiatives, and new lawmakers set agendas for 2027, here are the biggest takeaways from the midterms:

Florida and California voters back major housing changes

Two of the most populous states in the country—which also have faced some of the most acute issues with housing supply and affordability in the past few years—overwhelmingly approved two unique initiatives that proponents say will offer some immediate benefits to buyers and homeowners.

In California, Proposition 37 was on track to be approved by around two-thirds of voters. The initiative, designed to simultaneously address the state’s long-running housing affordability and supply issues, creates a special “secondary mortgage” loan for down payments, and hopes to incentivize the construction of around 200,000 new homes at (relatively) attainable price points.

Speaking to RISMedia before the election, housing experts were cautiously optimistic the initiative could move the needle in the medium-term, though critics note the extra down payment loan means higher monthly costs for borrowers.

In a statement after the election, one of the initiative’s architects, Bob Hertzberg, thanked the California Association of Realtors®, among other supporters, for “a decisive first step to create generational homeownership opportunities in California.”

Simultaneously in the Sunshine State, voters strongly backed Amendment 3, which creates new tax relief for many property owners. The initiative makes several changes to how taxes are calculated, aiming to lower—or even eliminate—property tax bills for many homeowners.

The change has sparked some controversy, with concerns that it would deprive local governments of significant revenue. Supporters say it provides vital relief for homeowners struggling with rising homeownership costs, and opens up homeownership to more buyers (though notably, only people who establish state residency before December 2026 will be eligible for the exemption).

Both the Florida and California initiatives were supported by state Realtor® associations.

The result of another housing-focused ballot initiative in Massachusetts that would reduce minimum lot sizes for single-family homes was too close to call at press time Wednesday morning (Realtor® associations have supported that initiative as well).

Democratic Senator Elizabeth Warren has demanded investigations into real estate practices—focused on Compass’s acquisitions, and role in building off-MLS “private” inventory.

With Democrats taking control of the Senate, Warren is now the highest-ranking member on the powerful Banking, Housing and Urban Affairs Committee, which wields the power to issue subpoenas and hold high-profile hearings.

Whether Democratic control of Congress results in any new pressure on Compass or its policies remains to be seen. But back in February, Warren signed a letter with 17 other Democratic lawmakers that called the process by which Compass received approval for its acquisition of Anywhere “corruption,” citing media reports that the brokerage had bypassed antitrust concerns with back-channel lobbying.

Another signatory to that letter, Democratic Senator Ron Wyden, simply posted the word “Subpoenas” on social media as election results poured in last night. Wyden is currently the ranking member on the Senate Finance Committee.

Two Democrats (and one Republican) in the house also penned letters to the FTC this year focusing on “lead diversion” practices used by portals like Zillow, which they called deceptive. Lawmakers largely framed these practices as harming consumers amid larger affordability struggles.

With a significant focus on housing, it appears possible that Democratic oversight could very well shift more scrutiny onto real estate, even though enforcement power largely still rests with the executive branch.

State houses flip amid focus on ‘private’ listings, affordability

At least six state governments (Nevada, Wisconsin, Arizona, Michigan, Pennsylvania and Minnesota) appear on track to flip to a Democrat “trifecta” this year—meaning the party controls both the lower and upper legislative chambers, and the governor’s office. Other states saw significant Democratic gains, rebalancing legislative power that (often) has more of an impact on real estate issues.

Notably, a handful of (mostly) Democratic states passed laws over the last year or so restricting “private” marketing, though at least some of those bills had bipartisan support.

A trifecta, at least on paper, allows more legislation overall to move through state governments, and can result in previous shelved legislation getting another shot at becoming law. Governors also have significant executive power to regulate issues that impact housing in those states.

Among the many regulatory and legislative issues previously or currently being considered in these now-Democrat controlled states are rent control (Nevada), denser housing in suburbs (Arizona), state-wide accessory dwelling units (Pennsylvania), zoning reform for starter homes (Minnesota) and property tax cuts or refunds (Michigan and Wisconsin).

None of these issues are guaranteed to remain in the spotlight once lawmakers are seated and legislative sessions ramp up, but real estate advocates have pointed out that housing issues remain top-of-mind for many voters, who will likely expect substantive action and reforms in places where Democrats are newly empowered.
