Lost your vaccine card? What to know about measles immunity and MMR

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

Free daily briefing on global business news.

A measles booster isn't needed for adults with two documented MMR doses, but people in several groups should check their vaccine records and may need another shot

For most adults, the answer to whether they need a measles booster is no. The harder question is whether they can prove they are already protected. The shot in question is MMR, which covers measles, mumps and rubella. The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention does not recommend a routine extra dose for adults with two documented MMR doses. Producing proof of measles immunity is often the sticking point.

The vaccine works well. Two doses are about 97 percent effective against measles, and one dose is about 93 percent effective, according to the CDC. The agency still recommends two doses for certain adults, including college students, health care workers and international travelers. For people in those groups, one documented dose falls short of that recommendation.

Records go missing for ordinary reasons. Childhood shot cards get lost in moves. Pediatric offices close or merge. School files stay with the school. An adult who has lived in several states or countries may have records spread across many places, or none at all. Without written proof, a clinician may suggest a blood test or another dose.

Birth year also matters. People born in the U.S. before 1957 are generally considered immune, because measles circulated widely at the time. Adults born in 1957 or later who lack evidence of immunity should get at least one dose, according to the CDC. The agency also advises revaccination for people who received the inactivated measles vaccine used from 1963 to 1967, or a vaccine of unknown type in that period.

MMR is not right for everyone. It is a live vaccine, and it is not recommended during pregnancy or for people with severely weakened immune systems. Anyone in those situations should talk with a clinician before acting on a missing record.

The 10 slides that follow are a selection, not a complete guide. Six cover specific groups, defined by birth year, vaccine history, schooling, work or travel. The other four cover practical steps. They explain where to find old vaccination records, how a blood test can show immunity, who should skip the shot and why two documented doses settle the question.

People born in the U.S. before 1957 are generally presumed immune to measles and do not need a measles booster. The CDC counts birth before 1957 as one accepted form of evidence of immunity, alongside vaccination records and laboratory results.

The cutoff reflects history rather than any medical test. Measles circulated widely in the U.S. during the years when this group was growing up. Exposure was so common that a person born before 1957 was very likely to have caught the disease as a child. A measles infection generally leaves lifelong protection. Public health guidance therefore treats that birth year as a stand-in for past illness.

No vaccine was available to this generation in early childhood. The first measles vaccines came into U.S. use in 1963, years after this group had passed infancy. Their protection, in most cases, came from the virus itself.

The presumption is practical. People in this age group may have no childhood vaccine card or record of a measles diagnosis from decades ago. Birth year spares them from having to reconstruct paperwork that may never have existed.

The 1957 line works in one direction only. Anyone born in 1957 or later cannot rely on birth year and needs a different form of evidence, such as written vaccination records.

The rule has limits. It is a general presumption, not a guarantee that every individual had measles. The CDC's guidance frames it as evidence of immunity, which is different from proof that a specific person carries antibodies. A laboratory test can confirm that directly.

The presumption also rests on measles circulating in the U.S. People born before 1957 who grew up in another country, or who are unsure about their own history, can raise the question with a clinician.

Some workplaces and institutions set their own requirements for documented immunity. Anyone in this group facing such a requirement should check what that employer or school accepts before assuming birth year alone will satisfy it.

Adults born in 1957 or later who cannot show evidence of measles immunity should get at least one dose of the MMR vaccine, according to the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. For this group, the absence of proof is enough reason to vaccinate.

The 1957 cutoff reflects how measles spread before a vaccine existed. People born earlier in the U.S. grew up while the virus circulated widely. Adults born in 1957 or later cannot lean on that presumption of immunity. Their protection depends on vaccination or on a lab-confirmed past infection.

The CDC accepts four forms of evidence of immunity. The first is written documentation of vaccination. The second is a laboratory result showing immunity. The third is laboratory confirmation that the person had measles. The fourth is birth before 1957, which by definition does not apply to anyone in this group.

That leaves three routes for adults born in 1957 or later. Each one depends on a written record or a lab result. A memory of getting shots as a child does not appear among the CDC's accepted forms of evidence. Neither does a parent's recollection of a childhood measles case.

The one-dose recommendation is a baseline, not a ceiling. Some adults in this birth range belong to groups for which the CDC recommends two doses. Those groups include college students, health care workers and international travelers. Adults in any of them should confirm whether their situation calls for a second shot.

MMR is a combined shot. An adult who gets a dose to fill a gap in measles records also gains protection against mumps and rubella.

For many adults, the practical first step is to search for old records before booking an appointment. A clinician can review whatever turns up and decide whether a dose or a blood test makes more sense. People who are pregnant or have a severely weakened immune system should mention that at the start. MMR is a live vaccine, and it is not recommended in either situation.

Adults who received the inactivated measles vaccine used in the U.S. from 1963 to 1967 should be revaccinated, according to the CDC. The same advice applies to anyone vaccinated during that window who cannot confirm which type of measles vaccine they received.

Two kinds of measles vaccine were in use in the U.S. during those years. An inactivated vaccine contains virus that has been killed. A live vaccine contains a weakened form of the virus that can still replicate in the body. The CDC's revaccination advice applies to the killed version.

Today's MMR shot is in the live category. It protects against measles, mumps and rubella in a single injection. The combined MMR vaccine did not exist during the inactivated-vaccine years. It was licensed in the U.S. in 1971, several years after that window closed.

That history shapes who belongs in this group. Many people who got a measles shot between 1963 and 1967 were children born in the late 1950s or early 1960s. People born in 1957 or later do not fall under the CDC's birth-year presumption of immunity. For them, a mid-1960s shot may be the only measles vaccination on record.

The unknown-type clause covers a common gap in old paperwork. A childhood card from that era may list a date and the word "measles" without naming the product. A parent's memory of a shot does not identify the vaccine either. Under CDC guidance, a dose of unknown type given in that window calls for revaccination, just as an inactivated dose does.

The remedy is a dose of the current MMR vaccine. That shot replaces an uncertain mid-1960s dose with one whose type is documented.

Anyone who is pregnant or has a severely weakened immune system should raise that with a clinician before getting the shot.

Anyone who finds a measles vaccination dated between 1963 and 1967 on an old card or school file can bring it to a doctor or local health department. A clinician can review the record and decide whether another dose is needed.

The CDC recommends that students at colleges, universities and other schools after high school have two doses of the measles, mumps and rubella vaccine, known as MMR, or other evidence of immunity. For this group, one dose falls short of the CDC recommendation.

The campus setting is the reason for the higher standard. Residence halls, lecture rooms, dining halls and gyms put large numbers of people in shared indoor spaces. Students arrive from many states and countries, and they mix closely for months at a time. Measles spreads through the air and is one of the most contagious infectious diseases.

The recommendation is not limited to 18-year-old freshmen. It covers students in education after high school, which includes community colleges, graduate programs, trade schools and adults returning to study.

Documentation carries practical weight for this group. Many colleges and universities ask incoming students for proof of MMR vaccination before enrollment or before they move into campus housing. Requirements vary by school and by state. The student health office or admissions office can confirm what a specific campus accepts.

A parent's memory that the shots were given does not count as proof. The CDC accepts written documentation of vaccination as evidence of immunity. The record should show which vaccine was given and when.

Students who received both doses on the standard U.S. childhood schedule may already have the proof a college wants. Pediatric records and high school health files are common places for that history to sit.

Students who grew up abroad or moved between health systems may find only one dose recorded, or no record at all. A student with one documented dose generally needs one more to meet the CDC recommendation for this group. A student with no documented doses needs two. When two doses are needed, the CDC calls for them to be given at least 28 days apart.

MMR is a live vaccine. A student who is pregnant or has a severely weakened immune system should talk with a clinician before getting it.

Health care workers need two documented doses of the MMR vaccine under guidance from the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. That is a higher bar than the single dose the CDC sets for most adults born in 1957 or later who lack evidence of immunity.

The shot covers measles, mumps and rubella together. There is no separate measles-only vaccine in routine U.S. use. A second measles dose means a second MMR shot.

Hospitals and clinics are places where measles can spread quickly. The virus travels through the air when an infected person coughs or sneezes. It can linger in a room after that person has left.

Some patients in those settings cannot rely on the vaccine themselves. MMR is a live vaccine. The CDC does not recommend it during pregnancy or for people with severely weakened immune systems. Staff immunity is part of how those patients are protected.

The same limit applies to staff. MMR is not recommended for a health care worker who is pregnant or has a severely weakened immune system. That worker should talk with a clinician or occupational health office about immunity and job duties.

In clinical work, immunity has to be shown on paper rather than assumed. Under CDC guidance, written documentation of vaccination counts as evidence. A worker's memory of childhood shots does not.

A worker with one documented dose meets the general adult minimum but not the health care standard. A second MMR dose closes that gap. A laboratory result showing immunity is another accepted route.

Birth before 1957 also counts as evidence of immunity under CDC guidance. Health care workers born before that year should ask their employer how the rule applies to their role.

Workers who switch employers may need to show proof again. Keeping copies of vaccination records or lab results in one place makes that step faster.

Adults born in 1957 or later should have two documented MMR doses before traveling abroad, the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention advises. That is a higher bar than the single dose the CDC asks of most other adults who lack evidence of immunity.

Travel raises the chance of exposure. Measles still circulates in many countries. Airports, long flights and busy tourist sites put travelers in close contact with people from around the world.

The U.S. declared measles eliminated in 2000. Elimination means the disease no longer spreads continuously within the country. It does not mean the virus is gone. Cases still appear when someone catches measles abroad and carries it home, where it can spread to people who are not protected.

Measles spreads through the air. The CDC reports that the virus can remain in the air of a room for up to two hours after an infected person has left. A traveler does not need direct contact with a sick person to be exposed.

Timing matters for anyone who needs both doses. The two shots must be given at least 28 days apart. An adult with no record of vaccination should plan the first dose well ahead of departure. A traveler who already has one documented dose needs only the second.

Checking records early also leaves room for other options. A traveler who cannot find paperwork can ask a clinician about a blood test for immunity, or get vaccinated. The CDC states there is no harm in getting another MMR dose for someone who may already be immune. The same shot also protects against mumps and rubella.

MMR is a live vaccine, so it is not recommended during pregnancy or for people with severely weakened immune systems. Travelers $TRV in either group should discuss measles risk at their destination with a doctor before departure.

Families traveling with babies and young children face separate considerations. Parents should ask a pediatrician about their child's vaccination timing well before an overseas trip.

Measles vaccination records are most likely held by a state immunization registry, a doctor's office, a school or a parent's files. Under CDC guidance, written documentation of vaccination counts as evidence of immunity. A dated record can settle the question before anyone books another shot.

Start with the state immunization information system. That is the CDC's term for the confidential databases that record vaccine doses given within a state. Every U.S. state has one. Access rules differ by state. Some let residents request a copy online, while others route requests through a health care provider or the state health department.

These registries have limits. Many began operating after older adults were children. Doses given decades ago may never have been entered. A record missing from the registry does not mean the shot was never given.

Family papers are the next place to look. Many households kept a paper vaccination card from childhood doctor visits, sometimes stored with birth certificates or other important documents. If a parent is available, asking where those papers ended up can shorten the search.

Schools are another source. U.S. states require proof of certain vaccinations for school entry. As a result, primary and secondary schools may have kept a copy on file. Colleges and universities that checked immunization status at enrollment may hold records too. How long institutions keep these files varies.

Past doctors and clinics are worth contacting. A pediatrician's office or family practice may still have older charts. Former employers that required vaccinations may have kept a file. People who served in the military may find doses listed in their service health records.

Memory alone does not count. Under CDC guidance, a person's recollection of a past vaccination is not accepted as proof, and neither is a parent's. The record needs to be written and show the date of each dose.

Once a record is found, keeping a copy saves the same search later. A photo or scan stored with other health documents works.

A measles titer is a blood test that checks for antibodies to the measles virus. Under CDC guidance, a laboratory result showing immunity counts as acceptable evidence of protection. That makes the test a substitute for vaccination records that have gone missing.

A doctor or other clinician usually orders the test, and the blood is drawn at a clinic or lab. It measures measles IgG antibodies, the kind the immune system produces after vaccination or a past infection. Results generally come back as positive, negative or equivocal.

A positive result is the outcome that helps someone without paperwork. It gives them written, lab-based proof to keep on file. They can show it to a school, employer or program that asks for evidence of measles immunity. It also settles the question for adults born in 1957 or later who cannot find a record of their shots.

A negative or equivocal result means the test did not confirm protection. The CDC treats an equivocal result as lacking evidence of immunity. In either case, the usual next step is MMR vaccination. The number of doses follows the recommendation that fits the person's age, job or plans.

The blood test is not the only route for people without records. Getting an MMR dose is an accepted alternative. There is no harm in receiving another dose when a person may already be immune, according to the CDC. Some people choose to skip the blood draw and get vaccinated. Others test first to avoid an extra shot if they are already protected.

Antibody testing has limits for people who already have records. The CDC does not recommend routine blood testing after two documented MMR doses. Under CDC guidance for health care personnel, two documented doses count as protection even if a later test is negative or equivocal. Extra doses are not recommended in that situation.

Insurance coverage and cost for titer testing vary by plan and lab. People weighing a blood test against vaccination can ask a clinician or their local health department which option fits their situation.

The MMR vaccine is not recommended during pregnancy or for people with severely weakened immune systems, according to the CDC. Both limits come from one fact: MMR is a live vaccine.

A live vaccine contains weakened forms of the viruses it protects against. Those weakened viruses multiply in the body at a low level. That brief activity trains the immune system to recognize measles, mumps and rubella without causing the full diseases in a healthy person.

That same mechanism explains the restrictions. A healthy immune system keeps the weakened viruses in check. A severely weakened immune system may not be able to do so. That risk is why MMR is not recommended for people with severely weakened immunity.

Severely weakened immunity can have several causes. Some people are born with immune disorders. Others develop it through disease or medical treatment, such as certain cancer therapies or drugs that suppress the immune system after an organ transplant. The degree of suppression matters. A clinician who knows the specific condition and medication is the person to judge whether MMR is appropriate.

Pregnancy is the second situation the CDC names. A pregnant person should not receive MMR while pregnant, even without evidence of measles immunity. Anyone in that position can talk with an obstetrician or other clinician about their measles protection.

These limits do not make vaccine records irrelevant. Someone who cannot receive MMR now may already be protected by doses given earlier in life. Written vaccination records or a laboratory result can confirm that protection without a new shot.

People who cannot be vaccinated rely on the immunity of those around them. Measles spreads easily from person to person. When family members, coworkers and caregivers are protected, the virus has fewer paths to someone who has no protection of their own.

Anyone unsure about their own situation can bring a list of current diagnoses and medications to a doctor, pharmacist or local health department before deciding on vaccination.

Adults with written records of two MMR doses do not need a measles booster. The CDC treats a completed, recorded two-dose series as enough and does not advise adding a third shot as a matter of routine.

The second dose is not a booster in the usual sense. It does not top up protection that has faded with age. It is there to catch the small share of people whose immune systems did not respond fully to the first shot. Two doses give the vaccine two chances to produce that response.

The U.S. childhood schedule places both doses early in life. The first is given at 12 to 15 months of age. The second follows at four to six years. An adult who received both on that timetable, and still has the paperwork, has already finished the series the CDC recommends.

A dated record from a clinic, school or immunization registry is what makes the series count. A parent's memory or a general sense of having had the shots is not written proof. The record should show the vaccine and the date of each dose. Written documentation of vaccination is one of the forms of evidence of immunity the CDC accepts. With it, a doctor, employer or university can confirm the series without ordering another shot.

Two documented doses also meet the CDC's two-dose recommendation for college students, health care workers and international travelers. People in those groups who already hold that record do not need to repeat the series to satisfy the guidance. The same record covers mumps and rubella as well. MMR protects against all three diseases with each dose.

A complete record does not guarantee immunity. No vaccine is 100 percent effective, and a small number of fully vaccinated people can still get measles. Anyone who is exposed to measles or develops symptoms should contact a clinician or local health department for advice on next steps.
