Vanja Matijevec Gave Up His Own Everest Summit to Save Three People

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

Vanja Matijevec on the Lhotse wall during his 1981 Himalayan expedition (Photo: Aleš Kunaver, kept by the National Museum of Contemporary History of Slovenia)

Vanja Matijevec belonged to a generation of Slovenian alpinists who pushed onto some of the biggest walls in the Himalayas, but one of the defining moments of his climbing life came when he chose not to summit.

On his legendary 1979 expedition to Everest, Matijevec gave up his own planned summit attempt to help Stipe Božić, Stane Belak and Ang Phu, who had become stranded above 8,000 meters during their descent. Božić later remembered Matijevec’s selflessness as helping him survive after he lost a crampon high on the mountain.

Born in Ljubljana on October 14, 1946, Matijevec first encountered the Slovenian mountains as a child, tending cattle on pastures above Tržič. He came to alpinism later, after meeting climber Jože Hočevar through his field work, and began training as a mountain guide in 1975. In 1979, Matijevec, Lado Vidmar and Blaž Oblak made the first ascent of Lucifer (WI4+), a roughly 200-meter frozen waterfall in Gozd Martuljek. The climb, completed with equipment Matijevec later remembered as rudimentary, helped usher in the era of modern ice climbing in Slovenia.

But Matijevec’s ambitions stretched far beyond Slovenia. In 1981, Matijevec and Franček Knez climbed to the rim of Lhotse’s immense South Face, then considered one of the great unsolved problems of Himalayan alpinism. Over the course of his life, Matijevec accumulated more than 30 first ascents, including a direct new route up the West Face of Trisul in 1987. Despite his achievements, his peers remember his kindness more than anything. Fellow Slovenian alpinist Viki Grošelj, who had known Matijevec since their early climbing days, remembered him after his death as a distinctive personality and, simply, “the right man in the right place,” he told Slovenian newspaper Delo.
