What Is an Upper Bleph? Here’s What to Know About the Eyelid Surgery

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Doctors explain how the procedure works, when a brow lift may be needed, and nonsurgical options.

In the world of plastic surgery, the nose, breasts, and butt have commanded a lot of time and attention under the knife. But as tech advances and patients seek out more subtle ways to manage aging skin, doctors have shifted their focus to other areas, some once deemed too risky to touch. Blepharoplasty, or bleph, for short, is a plastic surgery procedure that trims, redistributes, or removes excess skin on the eyelids.

The procedure can be done to help improve peripheral vision or for more cosmetic purposes, like removing hooded eyelids. Blephs can be done on the eyelid itself, which is known as an upper bleph, or a lower bleph, which is done below the eye to treat puffy or saggy skin. Even the most effective eye creams and potent skin care formulas sometimes may not be enough to correct excess skin around the eyes, but before you schedule your consultation, see what doctors have to say about the procedure to determine if it’s the best option for you right now.

An upper bleph specifically addresses the eyelid, where skin and sometimes fat closest to the nose are removed. Dr. Lyle Leipziger, chief of plastic surgery at North Shore University Hospital and LIJ Medical Center in New York, explains that the procedure involves removing skin and excising or repositioning deeper fatty tissue to address droopiness, puffiness, and under-eye bags. “As we age, the skin around the eyes naturally loses elasticity, which can cause the upper eyelids to droop, look heavy, or create a tired appearance. This can also be exacerbated by excess upper eyelid fatty tissue,” says Leipziger. During surgery, he explains, excess skin is removed, and surgeons may also remove or reposition small amounts of fat to create a smoother, more defined upper eyelid contour. “The goal is to open up the eyes and create a brighter, more refreshed appearance while preserving the patient’s natural eye shape,” he adds.

Dr. Lesley Rabach, a board-certified facial plastic surgeon and co-founder of Rabach Aesthetics in NYC, says that the best candidates for an upper bleph are people who have a “normal” eyebrow position with excess skin, also known as dermatochalasia. “When considering an upper bleph, your surgeon needs to look at the eyebrow position, eyelid skin, and fat, as well as how much the eye opens when determining if an upper bleph is the correct procedure,” she notes. Rabach says that sometimes a brow lift and/or ptosis repair—which reconstructs the eyelid muscles or connects it to the forehead muscle to lift it—is part of the surgery as well.

Additionally, Leipziger adds that blephs are ideal for individuals who have excess skin or fullness in their upper eyelids that makes their eyes look heavy, tired, or aged. “This is especially common as we get older and lose elasticity in the delicate skin around the eyes,” he points out. “Ideal candidates are generally in good overall health, have realistic expectations, and are bothered by drooping upper eyelid skin and possible fatty tissue.” He recommends having a consultation with a board-certified plastic surgeon to determine whether overhanging skin, excess fatty tissue, brow position, or another anatomical factor is contributing to the appearance of the upper eyelids or if it’s something else.

Rabach says the results are both subtle and obvious at the same time. “They remove the extra skin that often has a crepey texture while restoring the youthful appearance of the eyelid,” she says. “An upper bleph should restore the natural youthful architecture of the upper eyelid that is natural to the person.”

By removing excess skin and, according to Leipziger, when appropriate, repositioning small amounts of fat from the upper eyelids, the procedure helps create a more open and defined eye area. “Patients should experience a significant improvement with their eyes looking brighter and less tired, without ever looking ‘done’” says Leipziger. He also notes that while an upper bleph usually lasts a long time, it doesn’t slow down or stop the way your skin will naturally age. So you may see some sagging or loss of elasticity return over time. “The goal is a natural-looking enhancement rather than a dramatic change in appearance,” Leipziger expresses.

The eye area is very delicate, but blephs are less invasive than you’d think. Rabach performs the procedure at her practice several times a week using just local anesthesia. “However, we use very thin stitches that stay in place for close to one week and must be removed. While there is little to no pain, it’s hard to camouflage the stitches, so we tell our patients they will feel perfectly fine to work from home even after the surgery that day, but not be working from the office,” says Rabach.

Leipziger agrees that recovery from a blepharoplasty is generally minimal compared to other cosmetic surgeries. He says most patients experience some swelling, bruising, and mild discomfort during the first week, which gradually improves. “I typically recommend taking about five to seven days off from work and social activities for an upper blepharoplasty, and a little longer when a lower blepharoplasty is performed,” says Leipziger, adding that most people can resume normal activities within two weeks.

For long-term eye lifting and opening, blephs are the way to go, but Leipziger says several nonsurgical treatments can help improve the appearance of the upper eyelids—even if only temporarily. It all depends on your specific concerns. “Botox can provide a subtle brow lift by relaxing specific muscles, which can help open up the eye area. Certain laser and radiofrequency treatments can also improve skin quality and mild skin laxity by stimulating collagen production and tightening the skin. These options can be beneficial for patients with early signs of aging or minimal excess skin,” says Leipziger. The biggest difference is that the nonsurgical options cannot remove excess eyelid skin or fat. “For patients with significant skin laxity or hooding, an upper blepharoplasty is typically the most effective solution for achieving a more open, refreshed appearance,” Leipziger clarifies.

According to Rabach, a fat transfer is another option to restore hollowness along the orbital bone. This technique can help smooth crepey texture that can set in with age. She also recommends lasers such as CO2 to help improve skin quality, but specifies they aren’t a replacement for surgical removal of excess skin.

As previously mentioned, eyelid skin—even if there’s less of it—will continue to age after an upper bleph. “Long-lasting results come from general health and care of the area. Always wear sunscreen and wear sunglasses to protect the skin, as it’s the thinnest skin on the body and very delicate and prone to sun damage,” says Rabach.

Along with the non-negotiable sunscreen, Leipziger recommends medical-grade skin care products and ingredients such as retinol or peptides to support skin quality. “Additionally, patients may benefit from complementary nonsurgical treatments, such as Botox, laser skin rejuvenation, and/or chemical peels. While surgery cannot stop the aging process, proper skin care and maintenance can help patients enjoy their refreshed appearance for many years,” Leipziger concludes.
