What if shifting your relationship with discomfort could simultaneously ease its intensity and create room for learning, growth, and transformation?

Our culture values convenience, comfort, speed, short-term gains, accumulation, and immediate relief.

What I am suggesting is that sometimes, in trying so hard to find relief, we unintentionally intensify our discomfort and prolong its effects.

My relationship with physical and psychological discomfort was profoundly transformed during my initial 10-day silent meditation retreat. Participants entered the retreat center with a commitment to stay for the entire 10 days. We were required to relinquish our technology, assigned a cell-like sleeping space, and provided with a brief retreat overview.

Imagine a peaceful minimalist meditation hall, assigned meditation mats for each participant, and an outside shed filled with extra mats, pillows, and blankets. Fourteen hours of daily meditation with brief breaks. Two vegetarian meals per day. No technology, talking, reading, exercising, or familiar distractions allowed. In short, your basic meditation bootcamp.

As the days passed, my physical and psychological discomfort grew. The “silent” retreat hall included a soundtrack comprised of frequent outbursts of belching, passing gas, desperate shifting bodies, and deep sighs and moans.

Despite decades of meditation practice, I soon felt the physical and psychological strain and pain of sitting cross-legged for hours. My body was shouting “enough,” while my mind intensely revisited past challenges, failures, and unfinished business.

I had no idea how to manage the pain. During each brief break, I rummaged in the shed for the perfect combination of pillows to ease my rapidly expanding physical discomfort and psychological distress. I was determined and optimistically certain that the ideal pillow combination existed and that I would find it.

By day four, 20 pillows of various shapes and sizes crowded my meditation mat. Increasingly desperate for comfort, I fervently continued my pillow arrangement mission, unsuccessfully creating and assessing one combination after another.

None of the pillow combinations worked. My physical discomfort and psychological stress kept growing. Although I remained committed to staying for the entire retreat, I had no idea how I would make it through. And then something miraculously shifted.

On day five, my pillow quest unexpectedly gave way to releasing. My body and psyche elegantly eased into the meditation sessions. The quality of my discomfort and my relationship to it changed. During the breaks, I found myself returning my stash of pillows, one by one, to the shed.

Rather than finding the perfect pillow combination, I discovered something more powerful: Discomfort and comfort are always in flow. As my relationship with discomfort changed, my experience of it shifted, and my suffering was diminished.

Our instinct and attempts to mitigate or eliminate discomfort often serve to intensify it. We tense and adjust, ruminate, distract, seek immediate relief, make premature decisions, and attempt to control. Our vigorous efforts merely serve to intensify our suffering.

Allowing means acknowledging our experience without immediately judging it or trying to fix, change, or escape it. It does not mean resigning ourselves to our experience or even liking it.

Releasing cannot be forced. It unfolds as we loosen our attachment to a specific goal, outcome, expectation, timing, or meaning. As I naturally let go of my quest for the perfect pillow combination and sat with my discomfort, its intensity eased.

Discomfort does not automatically provide a direct path to wisdom. It can, however, provide the opportunity for understanding and transformation. Through releasing resistance to discomfort, room is created to pause, listen, and allow something better suited to emerge in our various life realms.

At the beginning of the retreat, I naively thought the perfect combination of pillows would empower me to eradicate my discomfort. I searched, collected, experimented, and assessed. The harder I tried to control discomfort, the more powerless I felt. As I began to release control and sit with my experience, the intensity, quality, and meaning of my discomfort began to shift and transform.

How often do we try to manage life’s discomforts by accumulating “pillows”—material goods, distractions, quick fixes, expectations, control attempts?

I’m a fan of comfort, not an advocate for doing without it. My invitation is simply to notice when seeking comfort serves us and when it gets in the way of learning, growth, and transformation.

Trying to avoid discomfort at all costs can be physically and emotionally exhausting. Approaching it with curiosity, awareness, openness, flexibility, and a sense of humor about our shared humanity can create the requisite time and space for transformation.
