Sotheby’s $180 M. Van Gogh Sale Faces Eleventh-Hour Nazi-Era Restitution Claim

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

With a $180 million painting by Vincent van Gogh due to go under the hammer Wednesday, Sotheby’s is pushing back against a last-minute restitution claim, citing details of a 1936 sale that the house says show the painting was sold voluntarily and for a good price.

The auction house told ARTnews that the archives of the art dealer Paul Cassirer confirm that van Gogh’s Châtaigniers en fleurs (1890), also known as Blossoming Chestnut Trees, sold for 68,000 Swiss francs, or about $22,000, in June 1936. Sotheby’s considers that a strong price for a van Gogh at the time. (Compare that to Verger avec cyprès (1888), a painting from the Paul Allen collection that fetched $117.2 million at Christie’s in 2022. That picture sold at Parke-Bernet in 1935 for $15,000.) The new details come after the New York Times reported Friday that the heirs of Samuel Fischer, a Jewish publisher whose family fled Nazi Germany, had asked Sotheby’s to withdraw the painting from its November 18 auction. The heirs say the family was forced to sell the painting because of financial pressure brought on by Nazi persecution.

Their lawyer, Gunnar Schnabel, challenged Sotheby’s to prove that the family received fair market value, according to an email shared with the New York Times, and would have sold the picture even if the Nazis had never come to power. “Otherwise,” he wrote, “no auction may take place.”

He also wrote that his client “is prepared to work toward a fair and just and just solution under the Washington Principles,” the guidelines accepted internationally for handling claims involving Nazi-looted art. ARTnews reached out to Schnabel for comment on evidence of duress, the family’s earlier restitution efforts, and what his client’s ideal outcome would be, but Schnabel did not answer before publication time.

The van Gogh is the star lot of the Blaquier-Arrieta collection, assembled by the Argentine collectors Carlos Pedro Blaquier and Nelly Arrieta de Blaquier. The collection’s 12 Impressionist and Post-Impressionist paintings are expected to bring around $450 million. Sotheby’s has guaranteed the van Gogh, though the amount for that guarantee hasn’t been disclosed.

Sotheby’s wasn’t the only auction house eager to land the collection. According to people familiar with the negotiations, Christie’s also offered guarantees covering all the works, including the van Gogh. The figures are rumored to have been competitive with Sotheby’s and, in some cases, even higher.

For Sotheby’s, which has spent months promoting the collection, the timing could hardly be worse.

Samuel Fischer, who died in 1934, founded one of Germany’s most important publishing houses, with authors including Thomas Mann and Hermann Hesse. His son-in-law, Gottfried Bermann Fischer, took over as the Nazis began banning many of the house’s authors. The family moved the business to Vienna in 1936, then fled Austria after the Nazi annexation in 1938. The Gestapo seized their Vienna home, library, and remaining art collection.

But the van Gogh’s history is different from that of the pictures the family lost in 1938.

According to the picture’s provenance, the family placed it with Cassirer’s Amsterdam gallery in 1932 or 1933, along with two Cézannes. It remained on consignment for nearly three years and traveled to both Rotterdam and Bern for exhibitions before being sold. The Netherlands was outside Nazi control at the time.

Luke Nikas, a lawyer representing Sotheby’s, told ARTnews that the van Gogh was sold to an independent buyer at fair market value. He also pointed out that the family held on to other paintings after the sale. Those were the works the Nazis eventually confiscated.

“The family had pursued and recovered works from the 1938 forced sale, but never the works they sold in 1936, such as this van Gogh,” Nikas said. He also described a postwar brochure published by Gottfried Bermann Fischer listing the paintings the family wanted to recover. Nikas said he has seen the document, and the van Gogh isn’t in it.

The two Cézannes tell another interesting part of the story. One is now at the Harvard Art Museums, where it has been on public display for years without a claim from the Fischer heirs. The other was offered at Christie’s in 2007, and again in 2017 when it sold for almost $6 million. In 2007, Gisela Bermann Fischer, who handled the family’s art claims until her death in 2014, told Christie’s that she wouldn’t oppose the sale.

There was a catch, though. In the same letter, as reported by the Times, she specifically reserved the right to challenge the sales of other family paintings she believed had been sold under duress. Sotheby’s told ARTnews that it had looked into the 2007 Cézanne sale but didn’t know about that reservation, which appeared in confidential correspondence between Bermann Fischer and Christie’s.

Sotheby’s restitution team has come in for scrutiny, too. As Julie Brener Davich noted in her Substack, the Appraisal, and as the New York Times reported in a follow-up Sunday, Lucian Simmons, who established Sotheby’s restitution department in 1997 and ran it for years, left for the Metropolitan Museum of Art in 2024. Richard Aronowitz, who headed Sotheby’s European restitution work for 15 years, joined Christie’s in 2022. Both were also specialists in Impressionist and modern art. Two people familiar with Sotheby’s restitution operation told ARTnews that the department has almost doubled since it was run by Simmons.

The question here is whether a painting sold outside Nazi Germany to an independent buyer and at a good price can still be considered a forced sale. Nikas says New York law distinguishes between the terrible conditions Jews faced under the Nazis and a sale in which the buyer actually pressured the seller.

He pointed to two cases involving Picassos sold by Jewish families during the Nazi era.

In Zuckerman v. Metropolitan Museum of Art, a federal judge ruled in 2018 that a 1938 sale didn’t meet the legal standard for duress. An appeals court upheld the dismissal in 2019, though for a different reason: the family had waited too long to bring its claim. In Bennigson v. Solomon R. Guggenheim Foundation, a New York judge similarly ruled in 2024 that economic pressure caused by Nazi persecution wasn’t enough to establish duress when the buyer hadn’t contributed to it. The dismissal was upheld on appeal in 2025, again because of the delay.

That last point is interesting because Congress changed the rules earlier this year. In April, lawmakers amended the Holocaust Expropriated Art Recovery Act, or HEAR Act, eliminating laches, a legal defense that had allowed courts to throw out restitution claims because heirs had waited too long to pursue them. That makes it harder for museums, collectors, and auction houses to win qualifying cases simply by arguing that too much time has passed. It doesn’t change what counts as a forced sale, however.

Nikas said that distinction is crucial. Congress removed a defense based on delay, he said, but left the legal test for duress alone. He believes the 1936 sale fails that test.

Judd Grossman, a New York art lawyer who isn’t involved in the dispute but has experience with restitution cases, thinks the change has tipped the balance considerably toward claimants. “With this year’s amendments to the HEAR Act eliminating the equitable laches defense, which had prevented heirs from unfairly sitting on their rights before making a claim, the pendulum has swung dramatically in favor of claimants regardless of whether they have ever taken steps to vindicate their rights,” Grossman told ARTnews.

“Whatever the merits of the underlying title dispute, under this new statutory regime, we will see more of this sort of gamesmanship by claimants waiting to the very last minute to come forward before seeking their slice of the pie.”

With a $180 million van Gogh heading to auction Wednesday, Sotheby’s finds itself having to negotiate with the heirs of its former owner, or risk upsetting the biggest sale of the season.

Schnabel isn’t new to restitution claims. In 2016, he and provenance researcher Monika Tatzkow were already investigating Nazi-era losses involving paintings by Piet Mondrian when they approached the artist’s heirs and offered to look into other works. The family hired them, and their research eventually led to a restitution claim against the Philadelphia Museum of Art, according to a 2022 federal court opinion recounting the heirs’ allegations. Whether Schnabel approached the Fischers this time, or they approached him, isn’t clear.

Which brings us to the timing. Sotheby’s says it first heard from representatives of the Fischer heirs on Thursday morning, November 12. The Times contacted the auction house the following morning and published its story that evening. By then, the van Gogh had been publicly advertised for more than six weeks. The auction was only five days away.

Nikas questioned why the claim had arrived so close to the sale–the sale was first publicized in early October–though he said Sotheby’s was taking it seriously. The heirs may have good grounds for questioning what happened in 1936, but the timing gave Sotheby’s little room to respond before the allegations became public, which could have been the point.

There’s another question, too. Schnabel has asked Sotheby’s to establish not only that the family received a good price, but that it would have sold the painting even if the Nazis had never come to power. This may be difficult to prove in the case of a transaction that took place 90 years ago.

For now, Sotheby’s says the painting is still scheduled to be offered Wednesday night. The Blaquier family, meanwhile, says it is willing to talk. In a statement to ARTnews, the family said it had owned the painting for decades and respected the historical concerns involved.

“While the information available to date has not established that the painting was sold under duress, the family welcomes constructive dialogue with the Fischer heirs and supports efforts to reach a mutually satisfactory resolution,” the family said.

Whether that happens before Wednesday remains to be seen.
