Black Friday is bad for the planet. These brands are opting out

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Black Friday is an environmental disaster.

Researchers from the University of Leeds in the U.K. found that up to 80% of items bought on that day, along with the plastic packaging they come in, end up in a landfill, incineration, or at best, low-quality recycling. All this after a very short time of use, sometimes as little as a few weeks. Shipping contributes to the damage; emissions from trucking spike 94% during the week including Black Friday.

The term “Black Friday” goes back to the 1950s, when the police in Philadelphia used the term for the chaos after Thanksgiving when suburban shoppers flooded the city. In 1961, the city’s merchants tried and failed to rename it “Big Friday.” In the 1980s, the retail industry tried to reconceptualize it as the day that they go from being in the red to being in the black.

Today, however, Black Friday is no longer a day. It’s an entire season stretching from late October to early December. And as a marketing ploy, it works well: Americans spent a record $11.8 billion online on Black Friday last year. The urgency of shopping these deals often leads to regret: 42% of shoppers in one survey conducted by Self Financial, a credit building company, said they wished they hadn’t made at least one Black Friday purchase. According to the National Retail Federation, retailers expect 17% of holiday sales to be returned, and roughly 9.5 billion pounds of returned merchandise goes into U.S. landfills every year.

Opting out is bad for business. Black Friday weekend is one of the most lucrative stretches in the retail year, as consumers are primed to open their wallets. But a handful of brands is choosing to take a stand and abstain from sales. Here are brands that are confirmed to opt out on November 27.

REI

The outdoor co-op first chose to skip Black Friday in 2015 with its “Opt Outside” launch, starting a national conversation about Black Friday’s impact. And while the brand lost sales that Friday, it led to a major brand boost in the form of social impressions.

In 2022, REI decided to make its store closure on the day permanent. All REI stores, distribution centers, call centers, and headquarters will be closed. Customers can still order online, and fulfillment resumes the next day. On its website, REI offers ideas for opting outside. Instead of shopping, it encourages customers to go on a hike, a neighborhood walk, or a picnic in the park. That sure sounds more pleasant than endlessly refreshing social media for new deals.

Patagonia

Patagonia declared war on Black Friday in 2011 when it took out a full-page New York Times ad telling people “Don’t buy this jacket.” This year, it has no Black Friday sale. It has a Black Friday microsite that pokes fun at the false sense of urgency the day often entails. It says that its emphasis on creating high quality gear “is an unlimited time deal that has nothing to do with Black Friday or Cyber Monday. Honestly, the best jacket is the one you already own.”

Its website remains open on Black Friday, but there is no sale. Instead, the brand encourages you to shop its secondhand “Worn Wear” collection. In a radical move Patagonia recently put its secondhand products alongside its new products on its website. And if you do choose to buy something new, it encourages you to buy products that will last a lifetime, and offers care and repair guides. More importantly, it lays out ways to give back, including its network of grassroots environmental nonprofits.

The Ordinary (Deciem)

When Deciem first went dark on Black Friday in 2019, it called the closure “a moment of nothingness” and encouraged customers to “buy less, but better.”

On November 27, the websites for all of Deciem’s brands—The Ordinary, Niod, Loopha and The Chemistry Brand—will be closed, as will its standalone stores. Instead, it will transform stores, including those in New York and Toronto, into sites for a blood drive. In its London and Copenhagen stores, it will invite customers to identify their blood type, so they can be equipped to participate in other blood drives.

Lush

Lush doesn’t run Black Friday sales. The company says its prices reflect the true cost of its ingredients, such as the organic Peruvian jojoba oil in its Gorgeous moisturizer. Instead, it has long used the day for activism. In 2016, it “switched off” its websites around the world on Black Friday, drawing people’s attention to the nonprofit Access Now. In 2021, it chose Black Friday to announce that it was quitting a number of social media channels; Mark Constantine, the CEO, said he was “happy to lose” $13 million. In the U.K., Lush stores will be using the Black Friday and Cyber Monday period to raise money through Charity Pot, a lotion that funds grassroots groups.

Freitag

The Zurich bag maker, which launched in 1993 creating bags from old truck tarps, has been saying no to Black Friday since 2019, encouraging customers: “Don’t shop, just swap.”

Since then, its online store has been closed every Black Friday, and visitors are redirected to S.W.A.P. (Shopping Without Any Payment), its bag exchange platform. Over the years, Freitag has offered fun swap events at its stores. The company hasn’t announced its 2026 plans yet, but it’s a good bet that it won’t be running a Black Friday sale.

Veja

The French sneaker brand hasn’t held a Black Friday sale since 2017 and apart from its outlets, it doesn’t offer any discounts. Instead, it has turned the day into “Repair Friday.”

In 2024, it invited people to its Paris headquarters to have things repaired by experts for free, from shoes and clothing to furniture, computers, and phones. In 2025, every Veja cobbler offered two hours of free repairs, and anyone could drop off one pair of sneakers from any brand. (Veja provides shoe repair in nine stores in Europe, North and South America.) Veja hasn’t announced its 2026 plans yet.
