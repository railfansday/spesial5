Scientists just broke through a major barrier in weather forecasting

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Scientists have developed an AI tool that improves weather forecasts three to four weeks ahead, offering earlier warnings of extreme weather.

Check the forecast for ten days out and you’re already taking it with a grain of salt. Past two weeks, the world’s best weather models have done worse than a simple guess that the weather will match the long-term average for that date.

A new machine-learning tool learns from past forecasting mistakes and corrects them, helping models predict broad weather patterns three to four weeks ahead more accurately than long-term averages.

The add-on doubled the skill of a leading AI weather model at those lead times.

It also improved the European model that forecasters treat as the gold standard in about nine of every ten grid squares on the map. Entered in an international competition, it beat every rival.

The team included Hannah Guan of Harvard College, Soukayna Mouatadid of the University of Toronto, Judah Cohen of MIT and Lester Mackey of Microsoft Research.

Several of the authors work at Microsoft or own its stock, and the code is free for anyone to use.

A forecast model simulates the atmosphere step by step, and small errors at each step pile up.

By the third week, accumulated errors can overwhelm the useful information in a forecast.

Forecasters call the stretch from two to six weeks out the “predictability desert.” Models there routinely score below zero on the standard skill scale, meaning a long-term average would have served you better.

Weeks three and four are also where warnings would be most useful. Three winter storms in February 2021 caused the worst energy failure in Texas history and killed at least 246 people.

Earlier warnings could have given officials more time to prepare energy systems and emergency responses.

The two-week limit goes back to MIT’s Edward Lorenz, who argued more than 50 years ago that the atmosphere is too chaotic to predict past that point. Cohen said he never fully accepted it.

The team’s tool, called probabilistic bias correction, doesn’t forecast the weather itself. It takes a model’s forecast, in the form of probabilities that a week will be unusually warm, cool, wet or dry, and adjusts those probabilities.

One half of the tool compares 20 years of the model’s past forecasts with what actually happened, spot by spot and season by season. Then it subtracts the error the model keeps making.

The other half blends the forecast with the weather observed over the past few weeks and with the long-term average. The two corrections are averaged.

The tool retrains itself every day as new forecasts and observations arrive, and it runs without a supercomputer.

“We know AI models have a lot of errors, so we thought maybe we can quantify those errors and remove them,” Cohen said.

The team tested the add-on on three systems. One was the physics-based ensemble from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts.

The others were the center’s new AI model and a hybrid that already uses AI to clean up that ensemble.

Each system forecasts weekly temperature, rainfall and air pressure three and four weeks ahead. The scores compare those forecasts with the real weather from 2016 to 2024.

On the European ensemble, the add-on tripled the model’s small rainfall skill.

It also turned temperature and pressure forecasts that were worse than the average into ones that beat it. The center’s own daily error correction, by contrast, left rainfall skill near zero.

The add-on doubled the AI model’s average forecasting skill and improved the hybrid model’s rainfall skill by 68% to 98%, depending on the forecast period.

The biggest gains came in rainfall, which every model handles worst.

In 2025 the center opened a competition in which teams submit real-time forecasts each week.

The team entered under the name MicroDuet and finished first for every variable and both lead times in the fall season.

Behind it were the operational models of six government forecasting agencies, including NOAA in the United States, and 34 other teams.

“We consistently beat climatology averages with our models,” Cohen said. “That’s one of the more important findings, because it’s so difficult to accomplish.”

Averages matter to farmers and power companies, but the costly events are the rare ones. The team retrained the tool to flag weeks in the hottest, coldest, wettest or driest 5% for each location.

The add-on raised the European model’s skill at spotting extreme heat by 51% over the center’s standard correction. The gains were spread across most of the map rather than concentrated in a few regions.

To test performance during floods, the team examined rainfall forecasts around events recorded by an international disaster alert system over the past decade.

The add-on raised rainfall skill around those floods by 76% to 92%, while the center’s standard correction pushed it to near zero.

The 88 severe floods, those with more than 100 deaths or 80,000 people displaced, showed the biggest gains, and there the standard correction did no better than the raw model.

In November 2025, the team’s week-four forecast called for a cold outbreak across the eastern United States in mid-December. It arrived, and the European model’s corrected forecast had missed it.

“It’s really important to predict both, but extreme events might be the most impactful part of this for society,” Cohen said.

The add-on fixes the errors a model repeats. It can’t recover a signal the model never had, so the authors describe it as a post-processing step rather than a replacement for better models.

The skill scores are positive but modest: corrected forecasts performed better than long-term averages overall, but individual predictions can still be wrong.

Kenya’s meteorological office has asked about using the tool to forecast its monsoon season for farmers, Cohen said.

Evidence that a few extra weeks of warning changes what a utility or a farm actually does comes mostly from isolated case studies, the authors wrote.

The next test is whether agencies plug the code into their own models and the gains hold outside a competition.

The full study was published in the journal Nature.
