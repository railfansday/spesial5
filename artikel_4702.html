My Health Anxiety Frustrates My Doctor; Now What?

When the cardiologist frustratedly told me, “Look, I can’t tell you what every little thing you feel in your chest is,” I immediately felt invalidated, shamed, and guilty for having wasted his time. But I had been feeling discomfort in my chest for a while, and my primary care physician had referred me to a cardiologist as a precautionary measure. I was only following doctor’s orders. While the wisdom of hindsight tells me that I was experiencing anxiety, not a cardiac problem, I did not know it at the time, and I was relying on the cardiologist to give me some peace of mind, not to make me feel silly.

Many individuals who experience health anxiety have been in similar situations where a medical professional’s reaction has left them feeling worse rather than better at a medical appointment. There is a substantial body of research to support the idea that provider invalidation, dismissiveness, and lack of empathy are not only not uncommon but also make an already stressful situation for a health-anxious person even worse. A 2022 study concluded that “patients felt dismissed when they perceived that providers were rude or did not take action, provided poor information, did not believe patients, rushed the visit, or were uninformed" (Hildenbrand et al., 2022). A systematic review of 57 qualitative studies on the topic concluded that “due to the asymmetric physician-patient relationship, physicians should strive to treat patients with dignity and respect in order to alleviate the harmful effects of negative experiences” (Rocque & Leanza, 2015). Provider invalidation, such as that displayed by my cardiologist, is a serious problem for individuals who are already anxious about their health status.

So, what can a patient do if they find themselves in a clinical relationship with an invalidating provider? First, a recognition of a patient’s autonomy is necessary; patients must view the relationship from a place of equal footing and reframe the aforementioned “asymmetric physician-patient relationship.” Two human beings are in a medical office, both of whom deserve common courtesy. I like to remember that my medical appointments are about me, not the doctor. With this mindset, I can be less concerned about “annoying” or “bothering” my provider. My provider is there to provide services to me, and I have the freedom to ask what I need to ask, to express concern, or to seek reassurance. The doctor, in turn, has an obligation to treat my concerns respectfully and seriously, and to utilize a bedside manner that is appropriate and ethical.

We sometimes feel that once we have established a clinical relationship with a provider, we should maintain it even if we are unhappy with the service. Health-anxious people can feel a sense of obligation to their provider and a hesitation to question them or to seek out other opinions. If, however, you feel that your provider is not being appropriately attentive to your concerns or is behaving in an invalidating or dismissive manner, you have the freedom to change providers or seek a second opinion. Often, individuals feel that they are doing something “wrong” or “betraying” their doctor if they make a change, but the reality is that those who provide our services are not always the best match for us. And, particularly in medical matters, we have the autonomy and freedom to change course or go a different route to find a medical relationship that better aligns with our needs.

When it comes to your health, your medical care belongs to you. If, for instance, you wanted all your medical records, you are allowed to request them, and they must be provided by your provider—they belong to you by law. This sense of full autonomy is often forgotten by anxious patients, but reframing the experience of being a patient through a lens of autonomy and agency can be helpful in restoring a sense of self-governance. I am proud to say that my response to that cardiologist’s behavior was to seek a different one, and it was my self-determination that allowed me to recognize that I had no obligation to stay with an invalidating doctor and that it was my decision to go somewhere I would be treated more empathetically. Do not forget that, as a patient, you possess full autonomy.

Anxiety can rob us of our voice, make us feel that we should “hold it together,” and keep us from advocating for ourselves. But just because you have health anxiety does not mean you need to have a doctor who makes you feel worse. Our physical health is precious, and we all deserve to feel that it is being treated in a respectful and ethical way. Please remember that you deserve empathic care—even if you are an anxious patient. Many researchers agree that there is a “need for better support in developing empathic skills” among physicians (Gössi et al., 2025). In the meantime, you—the patient—can ensure that your medical care is reassuring, not triggering or invalidating.
