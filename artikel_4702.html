Reconciling With an Estranged Child: Listening Matters Most

From my work with parents who have been estranged by their adult children, I’ve learned that few experiences match the pain that is felt when you’ve been cut off by someone you love. It may be difficult to believe, but parents are often taken by complete surprise when an adult child steps out of their life. Some children announce their intentions directly to their parents, but others are more subtle, and just stop responding to calls, texts, or emails. When the relationship breakdown resembles being “ghosted” by a child, parents tend to replay their most recent interactions repeatedly, trying to find a reason for the break.

However, when working with the adult children who decide to break away from their families-of-origin, their explanations tend to be very clear. They describe years of fraught relationships or share their frustration that their parents refused to understand the pain or distress they caused. Adult children often become exasperated when efforts to communicate their experiences or their needs to their parents are ignored or pushed aside. That can drive an adult child to simply walk away from the relationship. Parents may be persistent, at first, in their requests for explanations. But if their child's explanations are not received with the openness that the invitation seemed to imply, the estrangement grows more entrenched.

If a person is asked to think back to an event where others were present and asked to share their memory of the event, there’s a good chance that each person who was present is going to share a slightly, or markedly, different description of what happened. And each person is firmly going to believe that their memory of the event is accurate and valid. And for each person, it is their truth. People create their own realities from their own perspectives, and this leads to challenges in resolving family conflicts in which multiple realities compete.

When adult children recall having experienced the parent-child relationship differently than their parent does, it can be difficult for a parent to accept their child’s experience as a lived reality. They do not want to accept the possibility that their parenting strategies or relational behaviors may have been experienced as anything less than appropriate parenting. Parents, on the whole, make the best decisions they can. Unfortunately, sometimes the mistakes that they make arise from intentional efforts to “do the best that they can.” Parents may never have had a reason to consider any other perspective than their own – even when their adult child has made the decision to cut off contact.

When a child cuts off their parents, the child carries the power in the relationship, and it their choice whether to attempt reconciliation or not. In many cases, what estranged adult children need most before considering reconnection is for their parent to understand the impact their behavior had on them. They need to be able to share their experiences without a parent rushing in to deny what the child knows as their truth.

It’s helpful when parents recognize that intentions and impact are not the same thing. A parent’s best intentions do not erase the hurtful impact of their behavior. When an adult child shows up and expresses willingness to work through an impasse, they need their parents to acknowledge their experiences and their feelings. Unfortunately, parents may immediately move to a defensive position. Some may try to deny the reality that their child describes. Others leap to defend their choices and try to force their child to accept their own perspective. Common responses I’ve heard parents use include, “They’re remembering it wrong,” “They just won't understand why I did what I did,” or “I won’t take the blame for something that happened decades ago!” These types of responses do nothing to heal a ruptured relationship and are almost guaranteed to make the situation worse.

It can be undeniably unsettling to recognize that your actions have been experienced so differently from how you intended by people you love. That leads many parents to fear that if they validate their children’s feelings, they are accepting blame for the estrangement. When parents seek to defend their actions to, it leads them in a very different direction than if they seek to understand their child’s point of view. Accepting that multiple realities exist and that each person’s perspective is valid is a big step toward a new way of relating to your child.

When parents seek support and guidance about the best response to their child’s request that they be willing to listen to, validate, and understand their side of the relationship, they often express hesitance or resistance. Some believe that if they are not allowed to defend themselves, they are admitting they are to blame. In these cases, it can be helpful for a parent to ask themselves questions like: What feelings do I experience when my child shares their concerns? What hurt might they most want me to acknowledge? And what does it mean if I acknowledge their pain?

Adult children do not want their concerns to be minimized or met with with explanations; they need their parents to accept that their own experiences are valid. Parents may need to focus less on being right about the past and more on understanding how their children were hurt.

While parents may deeply long for reconnection, this outcome is never guaranteed. An estranged child has the power to decide whether to work toward reconciliation or continue the estrangement. This underscores the importance of being willing to listen to your child’s side of the situation. If you refuse to listen, without rebuttal or denial, the chances for reconciliation drop dramatically. Accepting that your actions may have been received differently than you intended is not a sign of weakness or an admission of blame. It's an act of humility. For parents hoping to reconnect, when the focus becomes less about feeling understood and more about understanding their adult child’s perspective, true understanding can begin.
