Why a House speaker needs a majority of votes cast, not always 218

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Free daily briefing on global business news.

How the House elects a speaker, from private party nomination votes to the Clerk-run roll call, the majority threshold, repeated ballots, the oath and the motion to vacate

The top job in the House of Representatives is filled by a vote the Constitution barely describes. The founding document says the House shall choose its Speaker. It says nothing about nominations, ballots, majorities or what happens when no one wins. Those details come from party custom and House precedent.

Understanding how the House elects a speaker starts with a simple point: the process happens in two stages. The first is private. After a November election, each party's House members gather behind closed doors to pick a nominee. The second is public. On the opening day of a new Congress, the full House votes by roll call, with each voting member answering aloud. Only that second vote makes someone Speaker.

The stakes are practical as well as political. Until a Speaker is chosen, the House does not swear in its members or adopt the rules that govern its work. A smooth vote takes one ballot. A divided majority can turn opening day into a days-long standoff.

Recent history shows both ends of that range. In January 2023, Kevin McCarthy needed 15 ballots to win the gavel. That was the most for a Speaker election since before the Civil War. Mike Johnson won on the first ballot on Jan. 3, 2025. The same rules produced both results.

The process does not end with the vote. A sitting Speaker can be removed through a motion to vacate the chair. Once in office, the Speaker presides over the House floor and stands second in the line of presidential succession, behind the vice president.

This guide walks through each step in order. It starts with the constitutional text and the party nomination votes. It then covers the opening-day roll call run by the Clerk of the House, the majority a candidate needs and what happens when ballots fail. It closes with the oath, the rules for removal and the powers that come with the office.

The Constitution gives the House of Representatives the power to choose its own Speaker, and it says very little else about the job. Article I, Section 2, Clause 5 states that the House "shall chuse their Speaker and other Officers."

The spelling "chuse" was a common 18th-century form of "choose." That short clause settles one question: who makes the choice. The House picks its presiding officer. No other branch of government has a role in the selection.

The same clause gives the House one more power. It closes by granting the chamber "the sole Power of Impeachment."

The Senate works under a different arrangement. Article I, Section 3 makes the vice president the President of the Senate. Senators choose their other officers, including a president pro tempore to preside when the vice president is absent. The House has no outside presiding officer of that kind. Its leader comes from its own vote.

The text also leaves gaps. It does not name anyone to lead the chamber before a Speaker takes the chair.

The "other Officers" are left undefined as well. The Constitution does not list who they must be or what duties they carry. Today, House Rule II provides for the election of a Clerk, a Sergeant at Arms, a Chief Administrative Officer and a Chaplain.

The text sets no membership requirement for the Speaker. Nothing in the Constitution says the person chosen must hold a House seat. Under the plain wording, the House could elect someone from outside the chamber.

The House has never done so. Every Speaker has been a member of the House at the time of election. The House elected its first Speaker, Frederick Muhlenberg of Pennsylvania, on April 1, 1789, during the First Congress.

The constitutional basis for the office is a single line. Everything else about the Speaker election, from party nominations to the floor roll call, was built by the House itself.

Each party picks its candidate for Speaker in a private vote of its own House members, held after the November election. That nomination is only a first step. It does not make anyone Speaker. The post is filled by a vote of the full House on the floor.

The two parties run this step through separate organizations. House Republicans meet as the House Republican Conference. House Democrats meet as the House Democratic Caucus. The names differ, but the job at this stage is the same: settle on one person the party will back when the new Congress opens.

These meetings happen behind closed doors. The public learns the outcome, not the internal debate that produced it. The floor vote that follows is different. On opening day, each member's choice becomes part of the public record.

The November 2022 Republican race shows how a party nomination works in practice. Kevin McCarthy, then the House Republican leader, faced a challenge from Andy Biggs of Arizona. Biggs was a former chairman of the House Freedom Caucus. McCarthy won the conference nomination 188 to 31.

That margin gave McCarthy his party's nomination. It did not settle who would hold the gavel. A conference vote measures support inside one party. The floor vote measures support across the entire chamber. The two results do not always match.

The gap matters most when the majority party holds a narrow edge. A nominee in that position has little room to lose votes from within the party. Members who oppose their party's pick in a private meeting are free to carry that opposition into the public roll call.

For the minority party, the private vote serves a different purpose. Its nominee cannot win the speakership while the majority party stays united behind its own candidate. The vote instead settles whose name the party will place in nomination when the House convenes.

A new Congress convenes at noon on Jan. 3, the date set by the 20th Amendment, unless Congress passes a law choosing another day. Until the House elects a Speaker, the Clerk of the House runs the chamber and confirms a quorum before any vote begins.

The Jan. 3 start date goes back to 1933, when the 20th Amendment was ratified. Before then, the Constitution directed Congress to assemble on the first Monday in December. Under that schedule, a newly elected House often did not hold its first regular session until more than a year after its members won their seats.

The amendment's exception clause gets regular use. Congress has used it to set a different opening day by law. The 112th Congress convened on Jan. 5, 2011, and the 114th Congress convened on Jan. 6, 2015.

An officer who is not a member of Congress presides over the opening session. The Clerk is an officer elected by the House to keep its records and manage much of its legislative and administrative work.

Federal law keeps the Clerk from the previous Congress on the job when the new House assembles. That continuity fills the gap before the new House has chosen any officers of its own.

The same law assigns the Clerk a first task. The Clerk prepares the roll of members-elect from the certificates of election that states send after the November vote. That roll is the official list of people who may answer when the House is called to order.

Before the Speaker election starts, the Clerk confirms that a quorum is present. Members-elect record their presence, and the Clerk announces whether enough have responded for the House to proceed. Only then does the chamber move to nominations.

The Clerk's job on opening day is limited to keeping order and conducting the election. If the voting stretches over several days, the Clerk stays in the chair for each session until a Speaker is chosen.

Speaker candidates enter the House contest through formal nominations made on the floor. The chair of each party's conference or caucus rises to place the party's nominee in nomination. The minority party nominates a candidate too, usually its own leader.

Nominations come at the start of the opening-day proceedings, before any member casts a vote. The Clerk of the House recognizes each nominating member in turn. Each name usually comes with a short nominating speech that lays out the party's case for its candidate.

Republicans give the task to the chair of the House Republican Conference. Democrats give it to the chair of the House Democratic Caucus. Each chair is a party officer elected by the party's own House members. A nomination from that officer presents the nominee as the choice of the party as a body, not of a single ally.

The minority party goes through the same formal step. Its nominee is typically the party's top leader in the House. At the opening of the new Congress on Jan. 3, 2023, Hakeem Jeffries was the Democratic nominee.

The minority holds fewer seats than the majority, so its nominee usually lacks the votes to win. The nomination still gives the party's members a named candidate to support when their names are called. It also puts the party's leader on the record as its choice for the chair.

A nomination is not a vote, and it does not close the field. Party chairs make the main nominations, but other members may also rise to place a name before the House. Members can even answer the roll call with the name of someone who was never nominated. Those answers are recorded like any other vote for a named person.

The nominations frame the contest without deciding it. They tell members and the public which candidates the parties are formally backing before balloting begins. Once the names are in, the Clerk moves to the roll call that settles the result.

The House elects its Speaker through a spoken roll call. The Clerk of the House calls each member's name in alphabetical order, and each member answers aloud with the name of the person they want as Speaker.

That spoken format sets the Speaker election apart from most House business. Most recorded votes in the chamber are cast electronically, with members using voting cards at stations on the floor. The Speaker vote does not use that system. Every choice is said out loud, one member at a time.

The call runs through members' surnames from A to Z. Party and seniority do not affect the order. A Republican and a Democrat with neighboring last names answer one after the other.

Members answer with a name rather than "yea" or "nay." The vote is therefore not a simple up-or-down decision on one nominee. A member can name the majority party's candidate or the minority party's candidate. A member can also name someone who was never formally placed in nomination. Whatever name a member gives is recorded as a vote for that person. A member may also answer "present," which records attendance without backing any candidate.

Tellers keep the count as the names are called. These tellers are House members, typically drawn from both parties. A bipartisan count gives each side a direct view of the tally as it builds.

Once the call is complete, the Clerk announces the result. The announcement lists each person who received votes and the number each received. It also states whether any candidate reached the majority needed to win.

The spoken format also leaves a full public record. Every member's choice is heard in the chamber and captured in the official roll call. The Congressional Record prints each member's name alongside the candidate that member supported. Constituents, party leaders and reporters can see exactly how each member voted.

A candidate for Speaker wins by taking a majority of the votes cast for a named person, not a fixed number of seats. The figure of 218 applies only when all 435 members vote and each one names a candidate.

That makes 218 a figure tied to one set of conditions rather than a permanent requirement. The real target can sit below it.

The base of the calculation is the group of members who actually vote for someone by name. When the 435-member House has every member naming a candidate, a majority of that group is 218.

Absences change the math. A member who is not on the floor during the roll call casts no vote. That member drops out of the total that the majority is measured against, and the number required falls with it.

A "present" vote has the same effect on the count. A member who answers "present" takes part in the roll call but backs no named candidate. That answer does not name a person. It is left out of the base used to set the majority. The bar drops again.

This gives a reluctant member a choice beyond backing the nominee or voting for someone else. A lawmaker who will not support the leading candidate can answer "present" or stay away instead of naming a rival. Either step lowers the number the leading candidate needs. It does not add a vote to that candidate's total.

Votes for other named candidates work differently. A member who names someone other than the front-runner stays in the base. That vote keeps the threshold where it is.

The exact number a Speaker needs is set by the votes actually cast. It can be confirmed only once the roll call is complete and every response has been counted. A candidate can therefore win with fewer than 218 votes when absences or "present" answers shrink the base.

If no candidate wins a majority on the first ballot, the House votes again. It keeps holding new roll calls until one candidate wins a majority, and no rule caps the number of ballots.

Each new ballot starts from scratch. The House does not drop the last-place candidate. It does not hold a runoff between the top two finishers or hand the job to whoever leads. The same majority rule applies on every round.

Members are free to change their votes between rounds. A member can stay with an earlier choice, move to another candidate or name a person who drew no support on the previous ballot. Candidates can also be placed in nomination again before a later roll call.

The Clerk of the House stays in the chair through every round. The House can adjourn between ballots and return later. That pause can give party leaders time to negotiate with holdouts away from the floor.

Kevin McCarthy's election at the start of 2023 shows how long the process can run. The California Republican became Speaker on the 15th ballot, on Jan. 7, 2023. Fourteen roll calls had ended without a winner before that one.

A century earlier, the House faced a similar deadlock. Frederick Gillett won the speakership on the ninth ballot in 1923. Eight rounds had failed to produce a majority before members settled on him.

A first-ballot win remains the usual result. Repeated ballots typically arise when the majority party's own members do not unite behind its nominee.

With a narrow majority, a small group of dissenters can withhold enough votes to block their own party's nominee. That can happen even after the nominee has won the private party vote. A dissenter can vote for another person, vote "present" or skip a ballot. Each of those choices changes the count on that round. The number of votes a candidate needs can therefore shift from one ballot to the next.

The Dean of the House, the chamber's longest-serving member, administers the oath of office to a newly elected Speaker. The Speaker then swears in the rest of the members, and the House turns to adopting its rules package.

Before taking the oath, the Speaker-elect is escorted to the chair. By custom, the leader of the other party introduces the winner and hands over the gavel.

The Speaker goes first in this sequence. Until the Speaker vote ends, the people seated in the chamber are members-elect, not sworn members. The new Speaker becomes the first of them to take the oath.

The oath itself traces to Article VI of the Constitution. It requires senators and representatives to be bound by oath or affirmation to support the Constitution. A member-elect becomes a sworn member only after taking it.

The Dean's part in the ceremony rests on seniority, not party. The title goes to whichever member has the longest continuous service in the House. The member who swears in the Speaker can therefore come from the party that lost the gavel.

Once sworn, the Speaker administers the oath to the other members-elect. They take it together as a group on the floor, not one at a time. That single oath turns the members-elect into a sworn House.

With its members sworn, the House also elects its officers, including the Clerk, the Sergeant at Arms and the Chaplain. Those posts are usually filled by adopting a single resolution rather than by separate roll calls.

The rules package comes next. It sets the procedures the chamber will follow for the two-year Congress that has just begun. Adopting it is a separate vote of the full House.

That vote is needed at the start of every Congress. Unlike the Senate, the House is not treated as a continuing body. Its rules do not carry over on their own. Each new House adopts a set, usually building on the previous Congress's rules with changes.

A motion to vacate the chair is the House's way to remove a sitting Speaker. Under the House rules in force in 2023, any member could offer one. On Oct. 3, 2023, the House voted 216 to 210 to remove Kevin McCarthy. He was the first Speaker removed this way.

The motion puts one question to the full chamber: should the office of Speaker be declared vacant? It does not name a replacement. A successful vote ends the sitting Speaker's tenure. Choosing a successor is a separate step.

That split shapes how a removal vote comes together. Members who back a removal do not have to agree on who should hold the gavel next. They only have to agree that the current Speaker should go. A small group of majority-party members can join with the opposing party to reach a majority. Neither side needs a shared plan for what follows.

Six votes separated the 216 members who backed the motion against McCarthy from the 210 who opposed it. The vote came about nine months into his tenure. The motion needs only a simple majority of the members voting. A narrow margin is enough to carry it.

Removing McCarthy left the chair without an elected Speaker. A Speaker pro tempore, drawn from a list McCarthy had filed in advance, presided in the meantime. The House had to run the full election process again, starting with new party nominations. Mike Johnson won the office on Oct. 25, 2023, by a vote of 220 to 209. His election came 22 days after the removal vote.

The two tallies measured different things. The first showed a majority willing to end a speakership. The second showed a majority able to agree on a replacement. Assembling that second majority took more than three weeks.

Under the 2023 rule, a Speaker could face a removal vote at any point in a Congress, not only on the day of the election. The possibility of removal stayed open for the full term.

The Speaker of the House presides over floor proceedings, decides which members are recognized and sets the floor schedule. The office also ranks second in the line of presidential succession, behind only the vice president, under the Presidential Succession Act of 1947.

Each of the three floor powers covers a different part of the chamber's work. Presiding means the Speaker runs House sessions from the chair. Recognition decides which member gets the floor at a given moment.

Control of the floor schedule decides which measures come up for consideration and when. A bill that never reaches the floor cannot get a vote of the full House. The person who sets the calendar shapes what the chamber takes up at all.

These powers put order in the chamber and the flow of its business in the same hands. The member who wins the opening-day roll call gains control over what the House debates, not only a title.

The succession role reaches beyond the House. If both the presidency and the vice presidency were vacant, the Speaker would be next in line. That ranking links a post filled by a vote of House members to the executive branch.

The Speaker's place in the order comes from a statute, the 1947 act, rather than from the Constitution's text. The place in line belongs to the office, not to any individual. Whoever holds the Speaker's chair occupies that position in the succession order. When a new Speaker is elected, including after a removal, the place in line passes to that person.
