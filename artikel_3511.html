See Bruce Springsteen, Martin Scorsese, and Surprise Guest Tom Cruise Honor Steven Spielberg at MoMA

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

Bruce Springsteen and Steven Spielberg are good friends. They’ve vacationed together a number of times on the director’s yacht, the Seven Seas. They went to Portofino in 2019, Sicily in 2017, and to Saint-Jean-Cap-Ferrat in 2024—the latter time with Bono and The Edge. But when Springsteen took the stage at MoMA Thursday night to toast Spielberg at the museum’s annual Film Benefit, he had a bone to pick with the director.

“In June of 1975, I went to a small theater directly across from the Atlantic Ocean and I saw Jaws. I was 25 years old,” Springsteen said. “Now, one of the great pleasures on the Jersey Shore in 1975 was night swimming, preferably naked, on one of those 90-degree, 80% humidity August nights, preferably with someone you wanted to have sex with. There was nothing like after the bars closed, you had a few drinks in you—naked night swim.”

“Steven Spielberg fucked that up,” Springsteen continued. “He fucked it up good. Now, many times over the years, I asked myself, Why? Why would a nice man like that—why would he do that?”

Here at MoMA, a guitar slung around his back, Springsteen then attempted a bit of playful psychoanalysis. He recalled his trips on the Spielberg family yacht. Maybe, thought the guy who wrote “Born to Run,” the guy who directed Jaws is scared of swimming.

“I’ve been on that boat many times and I can tell you: Steven Spielberg does not go in that water— ever. Steven’s boat is so fucking big it has water on it. There’s a pool on it. Steven Spielberg does not go in that fucking pool—and I mean ever,” Springsteen said. “I’m not sure I’ve seen him in a bathing suit. So the reason night swimming is fucked—not just on the Jersey Shore, but every fucking place in the world—is because that nice man, Steven Spielberg, is very afraid of the water.”

“The name of that boat is the Seven Seas,” Springsteen finished. “And I guarantee you, Steven Spielberg has not been in any of the water on any of those fucking seven seas.”

Springsteen added, of course, that he loves Spielberg and his wife, the filmmaker and artist Kate Capshaw. And he wanted to sing a song for them. So he broke into “Dancing in the Dark.”Ch

The whole night was like this. I’ve been to the MoMA Film Benefit a number of times, and it’s always a celebrity-stuffed evening at the museum, always sponsored by Chanel, a highlight of the season. But this was on another level. The museum was honoring a director synonymous with the modern era of blockbuster movies: the avatar of American celluloid excellence, the closest thing Hollywood has to a Michael Jordan. What’s more, there’s a true connection between the art institution and the director. His first theatrical feature, The Sugarland Express, had its New York premiere at MoMA, in 1974, as part of a series called New Directors/New Films.

“This is where it all began, here at MoMA,” Spielberg said. “Why did you wait so long to invite me back?”

He went on to praise the institution’s herculean efforts to restore films and add them to the collection. He also thanked Chanel for dressing him in a look way fancier than his usually quite casual (but fawned over) director ’fits, and for supporting the benefit over nearly two decades.

“MoMA’s dedication, since 1935, to curation, preservation, and restoration is what makes this honor so deeply personal for me, for all of us who love film,” Spielberg said.

Earlier in the night, Spielberg caught up with Amy Sherald, showing the artist some pictures on his phone, as Jeff Goldblum, who played Dr. Ian Malcolm in a little movie called Jurassic Park, pulled up to his table. Nearby, Christopher Walken—who had a great turn in Spielberg’s Catch Me If You Can—was wandering toward his seat, looking a bit lost, when another slightly older man came up to say hello. It was Robert De Niro.

The artist Taryn Simon and her husband, Jake Paltrow, were chatting with Christy Turlington Burns at the bar when Stephen Colbert went up to get a drink. Studio Museum director Thelma Golden was chatting with MoMA board president Sarah Arison, and Jennifer Lawrence strolled in with her husband, the art dealer Cooke Maroney. Michael Ovitz, the collector and MoMA board member for nearly four decades, who happens to also have a long history with Spielberg—“Michael Ovitz, my former agent from a long time ago!” Spielberg said onstage—invited the artist Nicolas Party and the novelist Sarah Blakley Cartwright to his table, with Larry Gagosian sitting nearby.

Lorne Michaels was chatting with collector Ray McGuire—though when everyone sat for the opening remarks, the seat across from Michaels remained mysteriously empty. Soon, a montage of iconic Spielberg clips filled the room. As the lights came up, a figure walked across the stage, mouthing the word “wow” over and over. Surprise: It was Tom Cruise. A MoMA rep, sitting near me, confirmed that Spielberg had no idea that the star of Minority Report and War of the Worlds would be attending the event.

“Since I was a young man, you were very generous with me, always on movie sets—and at your home with Kate, the meals that we had together, the hours that you shared talking about your films and what inspired making them,” Cruise said from the stage. “The stories about you as a young boy with that eight-millimeter camera. You were gracious enough to listen to my stories about the jobs that I took, so that I could buy the tickets to go see your movies.”

Cruise later waded into the crowd to hug his director and take that empty seat across from Michaels. MoMA director Christophe Cherix then got up to gush about his favorite Spielberg movies before introducing the next speaker: Martin Scorsese.

“The films he has made have changed the world. So art does matter. It always will,” Scorsese said. “We’re here tonight surrounded by art in the museum, and it’s really the appropriate setting all the more to honor Steven. And so Steve, I don’t want to… Listen, we’ve known each other since 1972, and how do we get to be in our 80s? What happened?”

To introduce Spielberg himself, the museum managed to rope in Steve Martin and Martin Short, who have never been in a Spielberg-directed movie. Actually, they have no obvious connection to the honoree, except for the fact that they’re two of the funniest people alive.

“You know Steven is very proud that he has never used AI in any of his films. By the way, AI is Steven’s nickname for us,” Steve Martin said.

“I’ll never forget the first time I saw Saving Private Ryan,” Martin Short said. “I found the film so mesmerizing that, halfway through, I whispered into my phone, ‘I’m going to have to call you back.’”

Spielberg was dying in his seat, just losing it laughing, and then had to follow up Martin and Short, which he compared to playing after a Taylor Swift concert. “Marty and Steve, I’d happily follow you anywhere, except up here,” the director joked.

He looked even more moved as Springsteen ripped through a no-bullshit, full-throated performance of “Dancing in the Dark,” which is not something you usually get to see at a museum that’s got The Starry Night and Les Demoiselles d’Avignon hanging upstairs. And then it was time to leave the museum, to go back to the reality of a stormy New York where you can’t catch a cab. In the coat check line, Spike Lee turned to Donna Langley, the studio head at Universal, for whom he’s made a number of pictures.

Then Lee bounced outside, giving a fist bump to every single security guard working the door at MoMA, greeting each of them with what’s going to be a lifelong mantra: “Knicks in five.” “Knicks in five.” “Knicks in five.”
