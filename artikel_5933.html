Unsealed warrant documents for Ilhan Omar's DC home raid reveal decades-long scope of investigation

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Fox News legal analyst Gregg Jarrett joins 'Fox & Friends' to explain what a federal judge's expected release of redacted search warrant records could reveal about the investigation into Rep. Ilhan Omar, D-Minn.

Heavily redacted versions of documents used to justify a search warrant and raid on the Washington, D.C. home of Rep. Ilhan Omar, D-Minn., were released to the public Monday evening.

Despite the level of redaction, the documents provide more insight into the scope of the investigation into the Somali-born congresswoman.

In an ex parte brief in support of an affidavit for a search warrant, federal prosecutors say they're searching for all "records and information that constitute fruits, contraband, evidence, and instrumentalities of violations of [redacted], those violations involving Ilhan Nur Said Elmi a/k/a Ilhan Abdullahi Omar, and occurring between 1980 to the present (the 'Target Offenses)."

While accounts vary, even from the congresswoman herself, Omar was born in 1981 or 1982. She does not have a birth certificate. She came to the United States in 1995 with her father. Fox News Digital confirmed just after the Oct. 12 raid of her home, that investigators were looking into alleged immigration fraud, potentially related to how Omar and her father filled out her immigration paperwork.

In another document, arguing that the court mischaracterized the government's arguments in ex parte proceedings, prosecutors make it clear that the investigation into Omar spans decades.

"The United States believes that ILHAN has committed [REDACTED] crimes over decades— beginning long before she was ever in Congress," the document says.

Fox News Digital reached out to a spokesperson for Omar.

In a rare move, Obama-appointed federal Judge James Boasberg ordered the documents' release last week, telling the Department of Justice (DOJ) to submit proposed redactions before the materials were made public on Monday. Judge Boasberg, in order dated October 9, 2026, agreed initially to seal the documents due to "such reasonable grounds to believe the disclosure will result in flight from prosecution, destruction of or tampering with evidence, intimidation of potential witnesses, and serious jeopardy to the investigation."

Search warrants typically remain sealed until there is an arrest in a criminal case.

Fifteen media outlets, led by The New York Times, along with watchdog group Judicial Watch, sued President Donald Trump's DOJ for the release of the documents used to conduct the raid of a sitting congresswoman's residence. The outlets claimed that viewing the documents was in the public interest, while the government attempted to block the release by arguing that it was a violation of investigative protocol.

Omar's Washington, D.C., home was raided on Oct. 12, 2026 — an unprecedented action to take against a sitting lawmaker, especially when there is less than a month until the Election Day where they are seeking reelection. The raid did not impact Omar in winning a fifth term in the House of Representatives.

Homeland Security Investigations (HSI), the chief investigative body of the Department of Homeland Security (DHS), conducted the raid last month. Omar's personal phone, computer and passport were seized as evidence in the search. Authorities are also in possession of her DNA.

Additionally, the lawmaker's sister, Sahra Noor, voluntarily submitted to questions from investigators during the week of the raid. Investigators are probing whether Omar or her late father — or both — lied on her citizenship paperwork, sources told Fox News Digital.

In a motion seeking to prevent the federal search warrant for the raid from being unsealed, the U.S. Attorney's Office for the District of Columbia referred to Omar as "Ilhan Nur Said Elmi, a/k/a Ilhan Abdullahi Omar." Sources later told Fox News Digital that Ilhan Omar is not the congresswoman's "real name."

That news caused speculation that Omar married her brother, Ahmed Nur Said Elmi, to resurface. The pair married in 2009, and divorced in 2017.

Omar has not been charged with a crime and has repeatedly denied wrongdoing. She has characterized the investigation as politically motivated, saying President Donald Trump is targeting her.

"For a decade, Donald Trump has relentlessly targeted Congresswoman Ilhan Omar with inflammatory rhetoric, repeatedly putting a target on her back," her office said in a statement after the raid. "Now, those attacks have escalated to a pointless federal raid. The Congresswoman has done nothing wrong. No president should be able to use the power of the federal government to intimidate his political enemies."

The Somali-born congresswoman came to the United States as a child in 1995, after decades of civil unrest in her home country. Her father, Nur Omar Mohamed, served as a colonel in the Somali National Army under the brutal Siad Barre regime.

Barre's regime systematically killed between 50,000 and 200,000 members of the Isaaq clan in what is known as the Isaaq genocide. He was deposed in 1991.
