The Best iPad to Buy (and What to Avoid) in 2026: Air, Pro, Mini

Tanggal: 2026-11-11

It’s a weird time for the iPad. Between the introduction of the MacBook Neo and the iPhone Duo this year, the iPad feels like it’s heading for an identity crisis. It doesn’t help that it’s been a relatively light year for iPad updates overall. It can still be a good time to buy an iPad, though, as the current options are still quite good. We’ve tested every iPad model to help you choose the right one for your needs and budget.

Consider the iPad’s Compatible Operating System

The current version of the iPad operating system is iPadOS 27, announced at Apple’s Worldwide Developers Conference (WWDC) in June 2026. The public beta for iPadOS 27 dropped in July and officially rolled out to compatible devices on September 14.

Much of the update focuses on Siri. iPadOS 27 introduces a dedicated Siri AI app that uses Google Gemini’s world knowledge and context. The update also improves integration with apps including Camera, Mail, Photos, Messages, Calendar, and more. The most interesting AI feature is in Shortcuts, which lets you create an automated task using natural language that can trigger actions across multiple apps.

iPadOS 27 also refines the highly controversial Liquid Glass design from last year’s iPadOS 26, adding a slider in Settings that allows you to change the opacity. Other notable changes include faster, more efficient performance; user-made Custom Extensions you can create through natural language; custom equalizer (EQ) settings with AirPods; dramatically extended parental controls; and much more. The iPad Mini 1 through 6 and iPad 1 through 10 have either low-resolution screens or are simply too old to support the latest software. Forget buying a 2014 iPad Air 1 or a 2015 Air 2, as those are simply too long in the tooth. As a general rule of thumb, avoid buying a device that came out before 2020.

There’s a chance an older iPad could perform decently, but unless someone is giving you one for free, you’re better off hunting for a newer model at a reduced price. Many older iPads no longer receive software updates, and they’ll feel quite slow compared to your phone and computer.

Apple has always packed a little extra into its iPad Pro tablets compared to its base, Air, and Mini models. It’s still totally fine to buy the iPad Pro M4, iPad Pro M2, or iPad Pro M1 if you can find them, but make sure you’re not paying close to MSRP. If it’s close to what you would pay for a brand-new M5 iPad Pro, the older model isn’t worth the small savings. If you can snag a good deal on one of the aforementioned slightly dated models, you’ll get a powerful machine that can match the current Pro slates in many ways; these devices also work with the first-generation Magic Keyboard with Trackpad.

Older iPad Pro models, like those from 2018 or 2020, aren’t worth buying (if they’re even available). They’re just too old.

By design, the iPad is primarily for entertainment and content consumption, while the MacBook is for work, school, and content creation. You can use both devices for any of those purposes, but each iPad type shines brightest in its intended uses. That’s why iPads work so well for kids, retired folks, and travelers.

The iPad Pro, however, may be an outlier, as it is truly a pro-level content-creation device. Depending on the task, it could be a laptop replacement. Some power users have a use for its tandem OLED display, faster ports, and M5-level graphics, but it’s a much smaller niche.

In March 2025, the long-awaited iPad (11th-generation, A16) refresh came. It had been three years since Apple released a new base iPad, and the update brought many modern features. It used the same modern design as its predecessor, complete with uniform bezels around a slightly larger 11-inch LCD. There’s a USB-C port, a Touch ID sensor integrated into the power button, a 12-megapixel rear camera, and a 12-MP selfie camera in landscape mode (with support for Center Stage). The cellular model supports 5G networks.

The A16 chip (the same one as in the iPhone 14 Pro, iPhone 15, and iPhone 15 Plus) is plenty powerful, but it doesn’t have enough RAM to support Apple Intelligence, making it the only iPad in the lineup without access to Apple’s AI features. (You might see that as a perk.) You’ll get 128 GB of storage, up from 64 GB, with the option to increase it to 512 GB.

This iPad doesn’t have a fully laminated display, meaning there’s an air gap between the screen and the glass. Because this gap can make Apple Pencil interactions feel a smidge imprecise, it isn’t our pick for artists. You also won’t get second-gen Apple Pencil support; this iPad only works with the first-gen stylus or the USB-C Apple Pencil.

Even though it’s over a year old, the iPad is still the best tablet for most people. Even at its new $449 price, it’s a well-rounded slate that can easily tackle most tablet-appropriate tasks, like gaming, streaming movies and TV, and reading. If you connect a keyboard or the Magic Keyboard Folio, you may be able to use it for work, too (depending on what you do). It’s also perfect for folks who want an entertainment device bigger than a phone, but not as small as an iPad Mini. —Julian Chokkattu

The classic iPad was never meant for serious work—at least not in the conventional sense. But over the past decade, iPad software and accessory upgrades have made the devices more capable of tasks formerly reserved for a laptop or desktop. This evolution started with cursor and mouse support, then came the Magic Keyboard with a full trackpad and an extra port. Then, iPadOS 26 brought windowed multitasking. For students or anyone else who wants to use those aforementioned features to do more with their iPad, I recommend the M4 iPad Air (7/10, WIRED Recommends).

Contrary to its name, the iPad Air isn’t much thinner or lighter than the base iPad. Instead, the iPad Air lets you use an iPad as your primary work or school device. It offers significantly better performance than the base iPad for tasks like video editing or gaming, and it comes in a 13-inch screen size for a larger canvas to work with. Whether you use your iPad for art in Procreate or just write emails, you’ll appreciate that extra screen space. The 12 GB of RAM means you can keep many apps open, and you can even dabble in Apple Intelligence or other on-device AI tasks.

Another reason to get the iPad Air is its compatible accessories. The large Magic Keyboard that comes with the 13-inch iPad Air offers a spacious typing experience. The key spacing and keycap size are close to those of a MacBook’s keyboard. The trackpad and palm rests are still a little small, but the keyboard itself is great.

With such a light update in this latest model, and given the recent price increase, you might also consider buying last year’s M3 iPad Air instead. It’s a valid sentiment; you can buy the M3 iPad Air 11-inch for $499 right now, which is a great deal, especially since that chip is plenty powerful for iPad Air use cases.

The 2026 iPad Mini upgrades nearly every aspect of Apple’s smallest tablet, making it feel increasingly like a “Pro” tablet. Compared to the previous 2024 model, the screen is bigger, it is thinner, and it has more memory. It even has a feature never before seen on an iPad: IP67 water resistance. It’s begging to be taken poolside or to the beach. Another point in its favor for using this iPad Mini outside is the display. It’s now OLED and offers the nano-texture display option, which dramatically reduces glare and reflections. It costs an extra $100, but it’s well worth it.

But the price is definitely of concern. It now starts at $699, up $130 from before. All the features, performance, and tech upgrades are worth that price, but the form factor feels limiting. This tablet size is better suited for leisurely activities like reading, gaming, or casual browsing. The use case for an expensive, super high-end tablet of this size that can handle serious workflows feels far more niche. I have an inkling that if you are integrating an iPad into your creative process, you will want a larger display—and probably one that can be connected to a Magic Keyboard, like the Air or Pro variants.

If you can afford it and just want the best small tablet ever made, the iPad Mini is as good as it gets. And fortunately, you can still buy the previous-gen iPad Mini for around $599. For the price, it’s a solid option for anyone who wants a simple device to play games, browse, and read books.

Most people don’t even need to consider the M5 iPad Pro (9/10, WIRED Recommends). It starts at $1,199 and $1,499 for the 11- and 13-inch models, respectively, and those prices don’t include Apple’s Magic Keyboard, which adds $350. You can buy a very powerful MacBook for that kind of cash. Don’t get me wrong, this tablet is exceptional. The tandem OLED screen is one of the best in the business: it’s bright, sharp, and colorful; it has excellent black levels; and it’s the only iPad with a 120-Hz screen. The speakers sound fantastic, and even the webcam is pretty sharp. You get all of that in an incredibly thin design, though it’s not much different from the M4 iPad Pro.

The M5 chip powering this iPad offers a small performance boost over the M4, though most folks won’t notice it in general apps. If you frequently use AI apps like Draw Things or graphically intense apps like DaVinci Resolve, that’s where the speed improvements matter. The iPad Pro is for people who use these kinds of powerful apps for content creation and work, whether that’s creating 3D objects in Blender or producing music. You still have to decide whether you want the benefit of a tablet experience or, say, the MacBook Pro M5, which can deliver slightly better performance and is overall a better machine for getting stuff done.

The latest iPadOS windowed-apps multitasking system is a great benefit that makes this iPad Pro more functional as a computer. While this is available on all iPads running iPadOS 26 and iPadOS 27, it shines best on the biggest iPad models, which are the 13-inch iPad Pro and iPad Air. When in doubt, try the iPad Air first; if that feels more than sufficient, you'll save yourself a wad of cash. —Julian Chokkattu

The Apple Pencil is a must-have for any iPad owner who takes a lot of notes or likes to draw. (You can even use it to sign documents.) iPadOS also supports handwriting in certain search fields and apps (like Notes), so you don’t need to switch to the keyboard every time you want to type. Apple debuted a new Pencil in 2024, so you now have four options to choose from, depending on the iPad you have. Here are the key differences:

Apple Pencil (USB-C) for $79: The USB-C Apple Pencil is a bit perplexing. It launched in 2023 to support the switch to USB-C across iPads, but it lacks some features. It doesn’t offer pressure sensitivity, so it’s better suited for note-takers than artists. It can attach magnetically to the sides of an iPad, so you don’t need to find a place to store it. However, it doesn’t support wireless charging, so you’ll have to pair and charge it via USB-C. It supports Hover Mode, which allows you to preview what will happen before touching the display. This feature is exclusive to select iPad Pro, iPad Air, and iPad Mini models. The USB-C Apple Pencil is compatible with iPad (10th gen and A16), iPad Air (fourth gen, fifth gen, M2, M3, and M4), iPad Mini (sixth gen and later), iPad Pro 11-inch (first gen and later, including M4), and iPad Pro 12.9-inch (third gen and later, including M4 and M5).

Apple Pencil Pro for $99: Apple’s Pro Pencil is the most feature-packed. It looks identical to the second-generation model, complete with a matte finish, but it also has a few new tricks. A “squeeze” feature opens up a tool palette, and a new haptic engine gives feedback as you squeeze. A built-in gyroscope lets you change the orientation of the tools as you twist them (for finer control), and it supports Apple’s Find My network to track the stylus if you lose it. It’s only compatible with the iPad Air (M2, M3, and M4), iPad Pro (M4 and M5), and 8th-gen iPad Mini. (C’mon, why isn’t it backward-compatible?)

Apple Pencil (first gen) for $99: The first-generation Pencil is quite old (it came out in 2015!) but still works well—as long as you have an older, compatible iPad. It offers both pressure sensitivity (lines become thicker as you press down harder on the screen) and tilt sensitivity (for shading). It has a glossy finish instead of matte (if that matters), and it is also cylindrical, meaning it will roll off surfaces. It doesn’t feature magnets, so you can’t stick it to the tablet for safekeeping. It has a Lightning connector, so to charge it, you’ll have to remove the cap and plug it into the iPad’s charging port. If your iPad has a USB-C port, you can buy an adapter. However, it’s only compatible with the iPad (sixth gen and later), iPad Air (third gen), iPad Mini (fifth gen), iPad Pro 12.9-inch (first and second gen), iPad Pro 10.5-inch, and iPad Pro 9.7-inch.

Apple Pencil (second-gen) for $129: The second-generation Apple Pencil has the same capabilities as the first-gen and USB-C versions, plus wireless pairing and charging (it’ll charge when it magnetically sticks to the top edge of supported iPads). It also adds a double-tap feature that lets you quickly switch between tools by tapping on the Apple Pencil twice. It’s compatible with the iPad Air (fourth and fifth gen), iPad Mini (sixth gen), iPad Pro 11-inch (first gen and later), and the 12.9-inch (third gen and later). Unfortunately, Apple officially discontinued it, so newer iPads—including the iPad Air (M2, M3, and M4) or iPad Pro (M4 and M5)—don’t support it.

Accessories for the iPad are endless. Below, I’ve highlighted some of my favorites to round out your tablet experience. You can find even more options in our Best iPad Accessories guide.

Twelve South Curve Mini for $50: An iPad can be a great tool to use alongside your MacBook or PC, but it needs a proper stand with plenty of adjustability. The Curve Mini is simple, but it’s stable and attractive, especially in this Dune color that I sampled.

Apple Magic Keyboard for $200: It’s expensive, yes. But it’s worth every dollar. It really does transform the iPad Air into a capable laptop replacement, with a keyboard and trackpad that’s every bit as good as a high-end laptop. It also adds a secondary USB-C port. The magnetic design does make it quite heavy, but I love how easy it is to pick up the iPad right off the case. There’s also a Magic Keyboard available for both versions of the iPad Pro, though they’re more expensive.

Zugu Case for $50+: This is my favorite folio case for the iPad for multiple reasons: It’s durable (with a rigid bumper), has a magnetic cover that stays shut, and has a flap that lets you position the screen at eight angles. The case is magnetic, so you can stick it securely to the fridge. It’s also reasonably priced, comes in an array of colors, and has a spot for your Apple Pencil.

Satechi M1 Wireless Mouse for $30: We’re already big fans of Satechi’s accessories at WIRED, and this mouse didn’t disappoint. It has a comfortable ergonomic design, a sleek aluminum finish, and smooth scrolling. It also has great battery life, thanks to its built-in lithium-ion battery. I’ve been using it for the past four months and have yet to charge it.

Mageasy CoverBuddy Case (iPad Pro) for $70: The CoverBuddy lets you magnetically connect your iPad to Apple’s Magic Keyboard case without removing the case each time. It feels durable and doesn’t add too much bulk to the iPad. There’s also a slot for the Apple Pencil Pro or the USB-C version. The company also offers the CoverBuddy Lite for the iPad Air (M2).

Logitech Combo Touch (10th-gen) for $260: The Combo Touch (8/10, WIRED Recommends) comes with a built-in keyboard, trackpad, and kickstand, making it ideal for getting work done on your iPad. You can also easily remove the detachable keyboard when you don’t need it. The Combo Touch connects via Apple’s Smart Connector, so you never need to tinker with Bluetooth or charge it. It’s also available for the iPad Pro (M4) and M5 (although these add a bit of weight) and the iPad Air (M2).

Casetify Impact Screen Protector for $56: If you’re worried about damaging your iPad screen, I recommend this Casetify protector. It’s super thin, has excellent touch sensitivity, and is mostly fingerprint-resistant (I’ve wiped off some smudges here and there). It’s painless to apply—the company supplies a microfiber cloth, a de-dusting sticker, and wet and dry wipes.

Paperlike Charcoal Folio Case for $70: Paperlike is known for its screen protector, but the company also offers a great case. It’s designed to feel like a sketchbook, complete with a polyester fabric cover that feels lightweight and high-quality. You can also prop up your iPad at two levels. This case doesn’t include an Apple Pencil slot, but a large flap closure keeps it from falling out. I tested the case using the iPad Air, but it’s also available for the iPad Pro (both sizes).

Twelve South HoverBar Duo 2.0 for $80: The HoverBar serves two purposes. You can mount it to the side of your bed, kitchen counter, or shelf (for comfortable, hands-free viewing), or use the included stand at your desk. With the second-gen version, you can now remove the arm from the clamp and attach it directly to the stand, making it easier to swap between the two modes.
