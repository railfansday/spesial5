US Supreme Court rebuffs White House effort to cut research grants

The Supreme Court’s decision not to take up a dispute over federal research grants hands a win to scientists.Credit: Kevin Dietsch/Getty

The US Supreme Court has dealt a blow to the Trump administration’s efforts to terminate research grants it deemed at odds with its political priorities.

The court said today that it would, for now, not intervene in a landmark legal battle over the cancellation of research grants by the US National Science Foundation (NSF) and four other federal agencies. The court’s decision leaves in place several lower-court rulings that came out this year and last and required the reinstatement of hundreds of cancelled awards while a lawsuit brought by a group of scientists against the government proceeds.

Since early 2025, US President Donald Trump’s administration has cancelled thousands of grants from agencies such as the US National Institutes of Health (NIH) and the NSF. Many of the cancelled grants were for studies that contained terms that the administration said ran counter to its priorities, such as “climate change,” “equity” and “gender”. In response, a group of University of California researchers whose grants had been terminated sued the administration. Lower courts have repeatedly sided with the scientists, finding that the government had penalized academic researchers based on their viewpoints and that this likely violated the US Constitution’s First Amendment, which protects freedom of speech and other activities.

The Supreme Court’s refusal to hear the case is a relief to scientists whose work was left in limbo, and leaves protections for their reinstated funding in place. “The denial is terrific news,” says Claudia Polsky, a lawyer for the University of California researchers. The denial came only three days after the case was considered on 13 November, and none of the nine justices publicly dissented from the decision. “Those are all of the best signs one could have that the Supreme Court really did not buy the government's argument,” Polsky says.

The White House referred Nature to the US Department of Justice, which said that it would “continue to litigate” the order by one of the lower courts that “currently prevents the cancellation of certain federal research grants supporting diversity, equity, and inclusion initiatives within the University of California system”.

The Supreme Court’s decision not to take up the case now is not the final word on grant terminations. The lower courts found that the researchers were likely to succeed, rather than issuing a final judgment. Once a final ruling is issued, it could be appealed, potentially bringing the case back before the Supreme Court.

The case kicked off in June 2025, when Neeta Thakur, a physician who studies marginalized communities, and five other researchers at the University of California who had had federal grants terminated sued the Trump administration. The researchers argued that the cancellations were arbitrary and unlawfully penalized scientists whose work clashed with the White House’s political agenda.

The same month, judge Rita Lin at the US District Court for the Northern District of California ordered the NSF, the Environmental Protection Agency and the National Endowment for the Humanities to reinstate the University of California researchers’ grants. The plaintiffs had a “substantial likelihood that their funding was terminated based on the viewpoint expressed in their grant proposals”, Lin wrote.

In August 2025, the Supreme Court issued a ruling in a separate case about the termination of around 800 NIH grants. The court ruled that federal district courts — such as the one that originally ruled in favour of Thakur — cannot reinstate cancelled grants by invoking a law called the Administrative Procedure Act (APA), which requires federal agencies to follow proper procedures and justify their decisions. Some lower courts had used this reasoning to reinstate terminated grants.
