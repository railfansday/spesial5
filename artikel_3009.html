18 Ways To Win Executive Buy-In For Technology Investments

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

When you’re asking fellow executives to fund a major technology investment, a compelling list of features rarely makes the strongest case. Decision-makers need to understand what the investment will change for the business, how much value it could deliver, and why the timing makes sense. That means bringing credible evidence that connects the proposed technology to clear business needs and priorities.

Even a promising solution can be a difficult sell if its benefits remain abstract. Here, Forbes Technology Council members share how to build a stronger business case for new tech and earn executive support.

Bring a portfolio displacement map. Show which already-approved projects lose people, vendor slots or launch dates if this investment takes priority, and ask those sponsors to validate the trade. A proposal that counts its upside but hides the work it pushes aside is incomplete. Executives need to compare the new capability with the best alternative use of the same engineers and capital. Make the postponed outcomes visible before approval, not after. Set each tradeoff. - Jagadish Gokavarapu, Wissen Infotech

Bring evidence that a business owner will change the operating process, not just approve the software. Show the current baseline, the decision or handoff that will improve, and who owns the result after launch. A credible investment case links value to an accountable behavior change. Features alone cannot prove the organization is ready to capture it. - Rahul Saluja, WinWire

Evaluate an investment based on where the market is heading, not where it stands today, and weigh how significant a competitive advantage it will create. Begin with optimism, but identify potential roadblocks and be prepared for each scenario. A useful test is whether the investment would still be worthwhile if it took twice as long to deliver. This discipline matters most in the high-paced environment of AI, where the market will reward those who can forecast accurately. - Andy Tsen, Ramen

Bring proof that you are using what you already paid for. Before approving anything new, executives often silently ask why the last platform did not solve the problem. The Salesforce organizations that we audit typically use under 40% of the features in their current licenses. Show that usage report first. If the gap is real after that, your request reads as the next step, not another experiment. - Sergii Grushai, Peeklogic

Bring a staged value realization scorecard with an off-ramp plan. Show fellow executives the exact business metric that defines success, the early signals proving you are moving in the right direction, and agreed-upon pivot points if benchmarks aren’t met. Proving you know how to validate progress and when to cut losses builds far more trust than a list of features. - Sandipan Bhaumik, Capgemini

Time saved is often the easiest metric to present, but it doesn’t tell the full story. Technology leaders should bring evidence that an investment will reduce errors, improve decision making or help manage risk. Faster workflows matter, but lasting value comes from measurable improvements in business outcomes, not productivity gains alone. - Matt Wielbut, Openly

A technology investment shouldn’t be justified by what it can do but by what it will change for the business. Leaders need to show how it supports the company’s long-term strategy and delivers measurable results. Will it accelerate growth, improve efficiency or create a competitive advantage? If you can’t explain how it moves the company forward, you’re selling technology, not a business case. - Marie Aiello, ContinuumGlobal

Bring evidence from a small, real-world pilot. A narrow trial with live users and actual results beats any slide of capabilities because it shows the investment works in your environment, not a vendor’s demo. Share what worked, what didn’t and what scaling would take, and the ask becomes a lower-risk decision. - Aruna Veerappan, Upwork Global Inc.

Bring proof that time saved can become capacity the business can use. A pilot might shave 10 minutes off each case without changing staffing or throughput. Show how the operating team will turn that time into more completed cases or shorter queues or a cost it can actually eliminate. Otherwise, the ROI assumes a benefit nobody is positioned to capture. - Nicholas Domnisch, EE Solutions

One piece of evidence you should bring is the expected business value. While each organization defines business value differently, some common elements include improved customer experience, faster time to market, and lower operating costs. - Peter Karlson, NeuEon, Inc.

Bring evidence of the pain disappearing, not a PowerPoint parade of features. In hospitality, nobody funds a faster check-in system because it has 47 buttons. They fund it because guests stop queuing and staff stop apologizing. Run a small pilot, and measure the hours reclaimed and the customer frustration eliminated. Executives invest faster when they can see the headache leaving the building. - Joel Frenette, TravelFun.ai

Show me the economic leakage—the revenue lost, the cash trapped, the productive hours consumed, or the risk that’s unmanaged. Establish a measured baseline, the value that’s realistically recoverable, and the executive accountable for capturing it. A vendor’s ROI slide is not evidence of enterprise value. If nobody can name the business number that will move, the board is funding conviction rather than a credible investment thesis. - Kelvin Cheema

The evidence you need to bring is the cost of one successful outcome today and what happens to it if the underlying technology gets 10 times cheaper. Features describe what you’ll build. That number tells you whether the investment compounds or gets commoditized as the market moves. If cheaper compute widens your lead, fund it. If it mainly helps competitors catch up, you’re renting a trend. - Vatsal Bhardwaj, Jabali.ai

Explain where your existing systems are holding the business back. Where are you adding people or manual processes to compensate for technology that can’t keep up? What does that cost in time, money or risk? Executives need to see an operational problem firsthand and understand why the current approach can’t solve it. That’s a much stronger case for investment than a list of capabilities. - Greg Brown, Illumia

Provide the cost of not making the investment in numbers the CFO already tracks: hours lost, releases delayed, revenue stuck behind a bottleneck, and churn tied to a defect. A feature list asks executives to imagine value; a cost of inaction shows them money they’re already losing. With this data, the investment stops being a bet on new capability and becomes a fix for a known leak. - Mayank Bhola, Testmu AI (Formally Lambdatest)

Bring evidence of real ROI, not estimated ROI. Most products demo well and work perfectly in a sandbox with a business case shaped like a hockey stick, but what matters is what happens in your real environment. What convinces executives is a baseline of what the problem costs today and what changed once you started using the product. Without that proof, you’re pitching a shiny list of features. - Yasmin Rajabi, CloudBolt

Bring proof that your own people will use it. A feature list says what the tool can do. A small pilot with real users says whether anyone will do it, and that is what decides the return. If 30 people were given the tool and 25 drifted back to spreadsheets, no feature list changes that outcome at scale. I would bring the pilot’s usage curve to the meeting before the demo. - Brandon Fuchs, Balenor Pharmaceuticals Inc.

Bring evidence of what the organization will stop doing. A major platform that leaves every old system, workflow and manual step intact is usually added cost disguised as transformation. Name what will be retired, when it disappears and who owns the switch-off. Investment becomes credible when replacement is explicit. - Akhilesh Sharma, A3Logics Inc.
