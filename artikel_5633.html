Pondering The Potential Of Plug-In VPP

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

I recently discussed the potential of the plug-in VPP from Jackery and Every Electric to transform urban energy storage. But the current program could be just the start. The more I discussed it at RE+, the more it started making me thing about where it could go in the future.

Essentially, customers of the plug-in VPP receive a battery, plug it into the wall and their window air conditioner, and connect to Wi-Fi. Then they use AC as they normally would, the VPP shaves off demand peaks on the grid, and the customer gets paid. It is likely the fastest, easiest and cheapest way to implement urban BESS in the US. And regular people living in apartments benefit. You can read more about the plug-in VPP in my previous article for context. Or you can watch the video below.

Both Jackery and Every Electric are collaborating and working to create innovative solutions for customer needs. Plug-in VPP makes the benefits of battery storage accessible to people in apartments. However, the potential could go much farther. Of note, much of what I am discussing here is not available yet. But the plug-in VPP sparked my imagination, and I wanted to expand on what might be possible.

Initially, the system is being deployed in New York City, which has a large number of apartments that can be aggregated to create a meaningful amount of combined storage. Of course, the potential can be realized in areas outside of NYC. Every Electric is already looking to expand into the Boston area. There are several other areas where the existing system based on window air conditioners in urban areas could apply, particularly along the Northeast corridor. However, as the program evolves, there is tremendous potential outside of the region.

In Jackery’s home state of California, 4.5 TWh of solar and wind power was curtailed in the first half of 2026. More electricity wasted in six months in one state than many developing countries consume in an entire year. Residential solar often feeds into the grid when it just leads to curtailments elsewhere. Even with recent ESS installations, the Duck Curve is still deepening. If home batteries can help “decapitate the duck,” customers and the utility both stand to benefit. And all that renewable energy currently being wasted can contribute to reducing emissions.

In addition, every Jackery battery can operate as a solar generator, with a DC input. People can simply plug in a solar panel or two to recharge the battery. No additional inverter needed. As it does not send energy back into the grid, it also avoids the cost and complication of traditional grid-tied systems. Sending solar power directly to the battery would avoid having excess solar generation fed into the grid that contributes to overcapacity, deepening the back of the duck curve. Demand response could then come from a battery charged largely from solar, rather than from the grid. As the US is still trying to figure out balcony solar regulations, this would let people enjoy many of the benefits of small scale, plug-and-play solar now. No electricians needed.

Of course, when balcony solar regulations are figured out, Jackery has solutions like the SolarVault3 that I saw at IFA Berlin. Potentially, this system could integrate with point of use battery storage to form a kind of hybrid plug-in system. In the process, solar power could be utilized when it does the most for the grid and offers the greatest potential payback for the customer.

There is also a lot of potential for this concept to work outside of the US. In many parts of the world, most people live in apartments. As the planet warms, world cooling needs recently overtook heating for the first time. As the VPP is monitored via a Wi-Fi connection, there is no need to upgrade to a smart meter to take advantage of the capability. As the storage is already distributed, there is also reduced need to provide the grid upgrades to distribute the energy stored in a large battery during peak energy demand.

Currently, the plug-in VPP is only working with one model of battery in one application. That makes the deployment and scaling of the plug-in VPP easier. Large urban areas offer the clearest path to aggregate the storage needed for a plug-in VPP to work.

However, Jackery has sold over 8 million units over the past 15 years. This has established a level of customer trust and a reputation for reliability. Many of those portable power stations and solar generators can connect to Wi-Fi. That is potentially GWh of deployable storage.

In addition, portable power stations are very popular now. Many Jackery models are selling out. Preparedness is a major driver. Climate-related disasters amplify the fear and uncertainty. However, the batteries that people are buying to be prepared for emergencies offer far greater potential capability. The more batteries that are out there, the greater the potential.

As the software and capability of the VPP develop, multiple batteries have the potential to connect to amplify value, even if that capability isn’t being offered yet. Those batteries, in coordination with a VPP, could earn people money. Currently, the system is being run with one battery model, but Jackery offers a wide range of batteries with Wi-Fi connectivity that might be able to participate in the future. Once the program has proven itself with the initial application in urban areas, there is potential for expansion.

As people start earning money, even if they purchased the battery out of preparedness concerns, they would also start to see the broader benefit. When they gain both confidence and financial benefit from battery storage and renewable energy, they will tend to rationalize that benefit. That can lead to a broader shift in mindset. When you try to start with a rational argument, especially when that argument involves added costs, people are less likely to be supportive than if you start with the benefit.

Many of the pieces to make this happen have already existed. People have the ability to program Jackery batteries through the app to charge and discharge during certain parts of the day. This can work with TOU utility plans, where available. But it doesn’t take full advantage of the capabilities of a VPP. Customers do not get full credit for the storage capability. The true innovation comes from putting the pieces together, between the Jackery hardware, Every Electric software and the utility. If one of those pieces of the collaboration was missing, a plug-in VPP wouldn’t have the same potential.

One of the things that has impressed me about Jackery since I was first introduced to the company was their focus on solving customer problems. However, to create solutions, they also recognize that collaboration is often necessary. I already saw this with the flexible solar panels of SunBooster at IFA Berlin for balcony solar applications, and then again with Every Electric for plug-in VPP.

I asked Jackery founder and CEO Jack Sun about the value of collaboration in creating an environmentally friendly ecosystem:

This is also very important, because we have an open model, and we are more willing and more innovative…

We will actively collaborate with partners in areas such as power chips and more proactively in co-creation. We aim to accelerate the application of their innovations into our solar generator solutions. Our goal is to achieve faster and more efficient solar energy development through joint innovation. The generator can be installed in every household to help more families achieve energy independence more cost-effectively, while also reducing carbon emissions more quickly.

This focus on collaboration makes meaningful innovation happen. Just as I speculated here on the potential of plug-in VPP, other people will have their own ideas. Some will be different than this VPP concept. Many will be better ideas than the ideas I can come up with. Having innovative companies like Jackery and Every Electric actively seeking to work with partners creates potential for those ideas to come together. I look forward to seeing what new solutions come together next.
