The Thousand Year Old Trail

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

“If from today I do not continue walking on your path,.. I have arrived nowhere.” Prayer of La Faba (Pilgrims Prayer)

Europe was in the middle of a record-setting heatwave, and with limited  air conditioning in local lodgings, our room had none. It was a warm, restless night. Knowing we had to climb 2,000 feet in the first 4 miles today, we set off early—though we didn’t quite realize how hard that climb would be.

We started our climb at 7:00 a.m. accompanied by the soothing sound of a bubbling creek about a hundred feet below us.

At first, we walked up a steep, narrow paved road with deep scratches in the asphalt. “Snow chains?” I wondered. I’d soon realize what actually caused those marks.

Before long, the Camino veered off onto a beautiful forested dirt path. Birds sang in the trees, and the creek continued to bubble below. The trail took a steep turn, leveled off briefly, and then pitched sharply upward. This section felt similar to climbs on the Appalachian Trail:, steep, deep woods, dirt, and rock strewn paths.

The key difference from the AT? This path was over a thousand years old.

Massive trees with 15-foot-diameter trunks had guarded the trail’s edge for centuries. The path was worn so deep into the earth that the dirt walls on either side rose up to 20 feet high, fortified in places with ancient rock retaining walls. It was steep, dark, and beautiful.

I stopped to admire one massive old tree that had sheared off about 10 feet above ground. Refusing to give up, a dozen new shoots flared into the air from its stump—some a foot thick on their own. Trees are truly amazing.

Popping out of the forest, we entered the ancient medieval village of La Faba. The village church sat on the hill above—mostly in ruins  except for its standing bell tower.

It was here that we saw the horses that scratched the pavement earlier today. Hikers can schedule horses rides up this steep section of the Camino, something we definitely plan to try if we return. Riding up this ancient road and exiting the forest into a medieval town on horseback must be an extraordinary experience. So this is what caused the scratches on the pavement earlier today

La Faba was built directly into the mountain, featuring thick stone farmhouses with cow barns on the lower level and living quarters above. Potted plants decorated the rock porches of this working farming town.

Leaving town, we recentered the forest on an old cobblestone trail. The path climbed steeply before opening into a broad, rolling mountain meadows. Cowbells rang off in the distance from grazing herds. The views were expansive, revealing valleys and peaks.

Moving on, we stopped in Laguna, a tiny hamlet high up in the hills. We found a small café for caffeine, fresh-squeezed orange juice, and toast.

After our break, we hit a dirt farm path lined with stone walls and soon crossed the boundary into the province of Galicia—our home on the trail for weeks to come.

By 10:00 a.m., framed by a dark spruce forest and a welcome cool breeze, we reached O Cebreiro. A man in a kilt playing bagpipes greeted us as we walked into town.

O Cebreiro features classic Celtic oval structures with thatched roofs (pallozas). Though ancient, the town looks immaculate. Tourists milled about alongside pilgrims—many day-visitors skip the hard climb we had just completed to come straight to this village.

We visited the simple cathedral in town. Something about it made me deeply emotional. It may have been the grueling climb we had just finished, or perhaps it was reading the Prayer of La Faba displayed inside. I’ve seen grand, elaborate cathedrals on this trip, but this humble one touched me the most.

Before leaving, I tried a cortado—Spanish espresso with a splash of milk to lower the acidity—and we stopped to chat with several pilgrims we hadn’t seen in days.

Leaving O Cebreiro, the path meandered through woods with sweeping valley views before following a paved road down to a small crossroads called Linares. We grabbed yogurt and popcorn outside a local store to refuel.

The Camino then diverted back onto a dirt path through the woods, climbing to Alto San Roque. Here stands a 10-foot-tall bronze statue of a pilgrim facing into the wind. He looks as though he had just completed the same grueling walk from Herrerias.

Pilgrims had decorated the statue’s feet with bandages—including heavy tape over his heels to represent blisters.

Moving along the ridge, fields of white daisies swayed in the wind on both sides of us.

We passed through Hospital, another small farming hamlet where locals carried on daily life—driving tractors and hanging laundry—just as villages have done for a thousand years while millions of pilgrims walked past.

The final climb of the day was brutal: just two-tenths of a mile long, but climbing at a slope equivalent to over 1,500 feet per mile. Exhausted, we took tiny baby steps to make it up the incline.

Over the crest, we were delighted to find a small bar waiting right on the trail at Alto de Poio.

We sat down and had our first truly great pilgrim’s meal of the trip:

When we asked for two glasses of red wine, the owner brought us a full bottle! We relaxed and watched other exhausted pilgrims crest the hill, looking just as bewildered as we were by this trail side oasis.They all smiled brightly when I clapped for them as they reached the top.

Feeling rejuvenated, we hiked mostly downhill into Fonfria, a working medieval village where we’d spend the night. We carefully dodged piles of fresh manure on the narrow cobblestone streets.

In Spain, checking into accommodations requires registering your passport and signing paperwork. When we arrived, the internet was down, and the sweet host spoke no English. After some patient gesturing, we got everything sorted and headed to our room to drop our packs.

With little else to do in town, we spent the afternoon hand-washing our clothes and resting in the warming sun.

That evening, our host—who reminded me so much of my Italian grandma—cooked us another incredible meal: steak, fresh salad, fries, Galician cake, and another bottle of wine.

Over dinner, we had a wonderful conversation with Chung Sow, a fellow pilgrim from South Korea, while a herd of cows wandered past us in the street outside.

It was a physically taxing day, but we fell asleep grateful for this little corner of Galicia. We vowed to always seek out cow-poop-covered streets in our future stays!

Although I may have travelled all the roads, crossed mountains and valleys from East to West,  if I have not discovered the freedom to be myself, I have arrived nowhere.

Although I may have shared all of my possessions with people of other languages and cultures; made friends with Pilgrims of a thousand paths, or shared albergue with saints and princes, if I am not capable of forgiving my neighbour tomorrow, I have arrived nowhere.

Although I may have carried my pack from beginning to end and waited for every Pilgrim in need of encouragement,  or given my bed to one who arrived later than I, given my bottle of water in exchange for nothing; if upon returning to my home and work,  I am not able to create brotherhood or to make happiness, peace and unity, I have arrived nowhere.

Although I may have had food and water each day, and enjoyed a roof and shower every night; or may have had my injuries well attended, if I have not discovered in all that the love of God, I have arrived nowhere.

Although I may have seen all the monuments and contemplated the best sunsets; although I may have learned a greeting in every language; or tried the clean water from every fountain; if I have not discovered who is the author of so much free beauty and so much peace, I have arrived nowhere.

If from today I do not continue walking on your path, searching for and living according to what I have learned; if from today I do not see in every person, friend or foe a companion on the Camino; if from today I cannot recognize God, the God of Jesus of Nazareth as the one God of my life, I have arrived nowhere.
