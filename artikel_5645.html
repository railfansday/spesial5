<a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/providers/how-messaging-could-be-next-source-clinician-burnout" hreflang="en">How messaging could be the next source of clinician burnout</a>

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

How messaging could be the next source of clinician burnout" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Physician burnout may be falling nationally, but healthcare leaders are noting a potential new source of overwhelm: messaging overload.

And, it’s not affecting all providers equally.

Leaders at Ochsner Health, per the American Medical Association (AMA), found female physicians receive about 30% more staff messages and 30% more patient messages than their male counterparts through an analysis of electronic health record (EHR) data.

Though the direct causes are unknown, TigerConnect Chief Growth Officer Sheeza Hussain said general messaging overload is a “symptom of a coordination problem.”

“Healthcare slows down when people are waiting for information, decisions, consults, transfers, and discharges and all the handoffs in between,” Hussain said. “At TigerConnect, we focus on helping health systems remove those bottlenecks by connecting people, systems and workflows so communication moves care forward.”

Hussain continued, “When communication is orchestrated well, clinicians can focus on care, and patients can move through their care journey more smoothly and effectively.”

Hussain and Nikki Manuel, TigerConnect clinical solution design director, spoke with Fierce Healthcare about the factors driving this trend, impacts on patient experience and how health systems can mitigate it.

Editor’s note: The following Q&A has been edited for length and clarity.

Fierce Healthcare: Why does message overload land unevenly in hospitals and health systems?

Nikki Manuel: Nurses are at the center of patient care and care coordination, so a significant share of the communication burden often falls on them. Think about a nurse trying to reach the right provider about a change in a patient’s condition. They may need to review the EHR, confirm who is on call, determine the preferred communication method and follow up if there is no response—all while managing other patients. The burden grows when communication crosses departments or shifts and ownership is unclear. The clinicians coordinating those handoffs can end up spending valuable time navigating the communication process instead of moving more quickly to the next step in care.

Sheeza Hussain: In my conversations with health system leaders, it comes back to fragmentation. Healthcare has never lacked technology, but more technology hasn’t created more connected care. Hospitals run single-purpose communication tools, fleets of devices, and disconnected biomedical equipment, putting out huge volumes of alerts and alarms. None of it coordinates, which puts clinicians in the role of middleware. It’s not a tool shortage. It’s a coordination problem.

FH: As this trend takes shape, who are the clinicians most affected by potential overload?

Hussain: Nurses, first. They’re asked to manually bridge disconnected systems and respond to alarms. Most alarms don’t need a response, but a nurse still has to hear every one to find out. Hospitalists and on-call physicians feel it too, especially when coverage isn’t clear. Clinicians should not be middleware.

FH: How, if at all, is messaging overload shaping up to be the next source of burnout for clinicians?

Manuel: I don’t think messaging itself is necessarily the next source of burnout. In many ways, messaging has made communication easier. As a bedside nurse, I remember relying on pagers and phone calls and having to stop what I was doing when a provider called back. Messaging gives clinicians more flexibility to communicate without constantly interrupting patient care.

The burden comes when we layer messaging onto communication processes that haven’t been standardized. Nurses are already surrounded by alerts, messages, and notifications coming from multiple systems, and they’re often the ones deciding what needs action, who needs to receive it, and what to do when there isn’t a response. That’s where the cognitive load starts to build.

Health systems should be looking at where communication can be standardized and where alerts, routing and escalation can be automated. Technology should support that process by identifying the appropriate person and helping get the right information to them without requiring a nurse to manually connect every step. Nurses shouldn’t have to serve as the middleware holding the communication process together.

Hussain: Because it adds to a load clinicians already carry. They’re surrounded by beeps, pings, messages and alerts, and most of it does not need action. The requirement to still read these messages and make that decision adds to cognitive overload and burnout.

It’s a thousand small interruptions compounded by the need to choose which tool to use to communicate. And the risk goes beyond burnout. When people tune out the noise, a real emergency can slip past. That’s a patient safety issue.

FH: Why do some organizations not have clear communication rules?

Manuel: Communication practices often evolve over time as organizations grow, add new technology and adapt workflows across different departments and care teams. That can naturally lead to different ways of communicating depending on the team, the workflow or even the situation. The important thing is establishing clear communication standards and governance around those practices.

Hussain: Many hospitals do not have clear communication rules because communication has evolved department by department and tool by tool, rather than as an enterprise-wide workflow strategy. Health systems have invested in a lot of technology, but more technology has not always translated into more coordinated care. The result is that a nurse, physician or operator may have to decide which system to use, who to contact, how urgent the issue is and what to do if there is no response. That is a lot of operational burden being pushed onto clinicians.

Clear communication rules are important, but they also have to be supported by technology that can operationalize those rules in real time.

FH: As messaging providers has become more commonplace at health systems, some have begun to charge for the service. How has that impacted overload, if at all?

Manuel: Research has shown that charging for certain patient messages can reduce message volume, but I don’t think we can assume that fewer messages automatically translates to less burden for clinicians.

We also have to consider the potential impact on patient access. We don’t want patients hesitating to reach out with questions or concerns because they’re unsure whether they’ll be charged. From a clinical perspective, the bigger question is whether we’re reducing the work associated with managing communication. If clinicians are still sorting through unnecessary messages, tracking down information or following up on requests that weren’t routed appropriately, we haven’t really addressed the underlying burden.

Charging may influence volume, but improving how communication is managed is what can make a meaningful difference.

FH: How does message overload ultimately affect patient experience and engagement?

Manuel: When we talk about message overload, I think the impact on patients is really about whether communication is helping care move forward or creating additional work and delays. When information is clear, actionable, and reaches the right person at the right time, clinicians can make decisions faster, reduce unnecessary delays, and keep care moving forward. When communication becomes fragmented or creates unnecessary noise, it can have the opposite effect.

Communication is also an important part of patient engagement. Patients want to understand what’s happening with their care, have their questions answered, and feel involved in decisions. When communication processes work well, clinicians have more time for those meaningful interactions and to apply their clinical judgment where it’s needed most. There’s also an element of confidence and trust. Patients may not see all of the communication happening behind the scenes, but they experience the result of it. When the care team is aligned, information is consistent, and next steps are clear, patients have greater confidence in their care and a better understanding of what to expect.

FH: How can leadership change communication without disrupting patient care?

Hussain: Leadership can start by treating communication as a patient-care-affecting workflow, not just a technology or etiquette opportunity. The goal is not to slow communication down or create more rules for clinicians to remember. The goal is to make communication more reliable so [that] the right information reaches the right person with the right level of urgency. That requires involving frontline clinicians, understanding where delays and interruptions are happening, and then standardizing the workflows that create the most friction. Done well, this should reduce disruption because clinicians are no longer forced to manually manage every handoff, escalation, or follow-up themselves.

FH: More broadly, what can hospitals and health systems do to mitigate this overload?

Hussain: Hospitals and health systems need to look at overload as a coordination problem. Clinicians are receiving messages, alerts, alarms and requests from many different systems, but those systems often are not working together. The first step is understanding where volume is coming from and which communications are truly actionable. From there, organizations can clarify escalation paths, reduce duplicative tools, and route communication based on role, schedule, urgency and context. Technology can help, but only if it is designed around the workflow.

FH: How could AI or other kinds of health tech provide relief for clinicians?

Hussain: AI and health tech can provide relief when they are built into real clinical workflows, not layered on top of them as another tool to manage. Clinicians do not need more alerts, inboxes or systems to check. They need technology that can reduce noise, understand urgency, identify the right person, and help activate the next step without forcing nurses, physicians or operators to manually coordinate it all.

This is where communication orchestration matters. The goal, and much of the work we focus on at TigerConnect, is to help health systems move from fragmented communication to smarter, role-based workflows that reach the right person at the right time. Used well, AI can help filter low-value communication, route information more precisely and automate routine coordination so clinicians can spend less time managing the work around care and more time with patients.
