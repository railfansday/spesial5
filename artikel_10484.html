Why the smartest AI strategy starts with a little laziness

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

My employees know that I have an unconventional take on laziness: A lazy mindset doesn’t mean being unproductive. Often, it means finding a faster way to get something done. As Bill Gates famously said, “I choose a lazy person to do a hard job. Because a lazy person will find an easy way to do it.”

That’s why I encourage a lazy mindset, insofar as it means looking for more efficient ways to get things done—like leveraging the latest AI and automation tools. As I’ve seen in leading a company of more than 800 employees, people are eager to streamline their work. Studies confirm they’re doing it, whether their employers know about it or not.

One recent report found that while only 40% of companies surveyed had purchased official subscriptions to large language models, employees at more than 90% of those companies reported regular use of artificial intelligence. That often leads to piecemeal adoption and missed opportunities to realize AI’s full potential. Studies suggest AI can absorb 60% to 70% of busywork, freeing employees to focus on more meaningful tasks, like strategizing and coming up with innovative solutions.

Leaders should recognize this reality and adopt AI initiatives at the enterprise level. But the challenge isn’t finding opportunities—there are countless AI tools on the market, and more rolling out every day. It’s deciding which are worth pursuing. Every AI initiative comes with trade-offs, requiring time, budget, talent, and executive approval. Here are some tips for taking a more strategic approach to AI adoption in your company.

Start with business goals, not AI tools

Estimates of the number of AI tools on the market vary widely, from around 2,500 to more than 50,000. (If you look at live, available products, the lower figure is likely more accurate.) But one thing is indisputable: There are a ton, more than anyone could possibly test while working a full-time job. Amid this dizzying array of options, the first step in integrating AI into an organization is top down, clarifying your broader business goals.

Maybe your primary objective is to make it easier for users to incorporate your software into their everyday lives. AI-powered chatbots that field recurring user questions could be a wise investment. Or maybe your goal is to analyze users’ circumstances quickly and provide help faster. A tool that uses AI to analyze and synthesize vast amounts of data could transform your business. In healthcare, for example, IBM’s Watson has been used to analyze patient data and medical literature and provide doctors with evidence-based treatment options.

The key is to start with the problem you want to solve, not the technology you’re excited to use.

When leaders define their business goals, they can focus on AI opportunities that align with those goals and avoid the myriad distractions out there. Rather than dedicating resources to tools that merely speed things up, they can focus on uses with the greatest potential impact. They can deliberately pass on AI tools that don’t align with business objectives and concentrate their efforts where AI can make the biggest difference.

Let employees lead the way

If you were developing new cooking technology for a bakery, you probably wouldn’t begin with the product designer. You’d start by asking the baker how they work, where things tend to go awry, and how you might improve their processes. The best ideas for improving how people work often come from those on the front lines.

At my company, we invite all employees to share the AI tools they’ve discovered that have helped, or could potentially help, during our weekly demo days. It’s a valuable exchange of ideas—for example, customer service reps advising designers on how to better program help chatbots.

Experts have observed the power of peer-to-peer learning networks, where employees develop and share ideas for AI adoption. Take an article in Harvard Business Review about AI usage at the European bank BBVA: “Instead of relying on the core IT and operations functions, the organization empowered the people who understood business processes best to design the solutions.”

While leaders can create a framework for AI adoption, starting with clear business priorities, they also need to tap employees for their ideas and insights into where and how tools can truly improve internal processes and the customer experience. Whether it’s an online forum, a Slack conversation, or a weekly all-hands meeting, make sure there’s a channel where employees can contribute their perspectives on AI development and share best practices. 

Make trade-offs deliberately

Organization-wide change requires investments of both time and money. That’s why I recommend encouraging employees to build slack into their schedules to test and experiment with new tools and figure out how to streamline their workflows. Each step eliminated, each minute of busywork recaptured makes a difference—and the benefits compound over time.

Recognizing that every AI initiative competes for limited time, budget, and attention helps organizations decide where to invest more wisely. Experts recommend managing AI investments like a portfolio, evaluating each one against objective criteria. Consider whether an investment serves broader business goals, whether additional training is needed, what other resources are required, and the cost of diverting those resources. As they assess potential investments, leaders can also track whether their overall AI strategy is delivering returns. As noted in another HBR article, “The goal is to ensure that individual projects advance business strategy and that the overall mix of investments creates sustainable value.”

Ultimately, this means being as deliberate about what to deprioritize as what to pursue. Success with AI depends less on which tools companies adopt and more on their willingness to stay focused on the initiatives that matter most. Be lazy about how much you take on, and smart about what you choose.
