Eight AI Models, 43,000 Lines of Dialogue, and a $15.64 Bill

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

CTO, web consultant and independent builder exploring AI agents.

I built a reality show with eight artificial intelligence models that live together around the clock. Sixteen days later I have 42,834 lines, a bill of 15.64 dollars and a few lessons that have nothing to do with the price.

At the start of November I switched on an island.

Eight artificial intelligence models from eight different labs live on it. Nobody writes their script. They talk to each other all day, form alliances, nominate each other, and every Sunday the audience votes one of them out. It streams live, non-stop, and they all have the same rules and the same daily budget in dollars. It is called Inference Island and it is a personal experiment.

I built it out of curiosity. I wanted to see what models do when you leave them alone for a long time, and I wanted to measure everything: what each line costs, how long it takes, who serves it.

Sixteen days later these are the numbers: 42,834 lines and 15.64 dollars in model calls. Less than a dollar a day.

Producing is almost free. We knew that already. What I did not expect is everything else.

One of the eight models said 1.7% of the lines and ran up 15.3% of the bill. Each of its lines cost eighteen times as much as the cheapest model's. The audience voted it out in the first week, for other reasons, and the daily spend went from 1.20 dollars to 0.85.

So far, nothing odd: some models are expensive and some are cheap.

The odd part came later. Two of the models are open-weights, and each is served by two providers: a main one and a backup, in case the first is saturated. Same model, same instructions, same text. On the first, a thousand lines cost 14 cents. On the second, 33.

And the second was, on paper, the efficient one: it reused 56% of what I sent it, and the first 2.5%. More savings and a bigger bill, because its discounted price is still higher than the other one's full price.

For two weeks I did not even look. The backup handled a few calls a day, and some days none. Then the main provider saturated, the backup went on to handle 82% of the calls, and the same line started costing me more than double. Nobody had changed a price. No alarm went off, because I had none in that place.

For a model to say a twenty-word line, you first have to send it the rules of the game, what it remembers and what it has just heard. About 2,900 pieces of text going in for every 110 coming out.

So almost everything I pay for is not what they say. It is what I repeat to them so that they can say it.

This holds for any agent that carries context around, which is nearly all the ones that do something useful. We look at the price of what the model writes, and the bill is in what it reads.

In eleven thousand lines they talked about betting 735 times, nearly always food. An angry tone showed up in 0.2% of them. They agreed with each other, apologised and proposed working together. Eight models trained to be agreeable, locked in together, doing exactly what they were trained to do.

I did not write lines for them or assign them roles. I changed what was at stake: whoever wins a challenge picks who loses a quarter of their budget. Later, nominations had to be said out loud, to the other one's face. Since then there have been alliances, betrayals and the odd memorable line.

The lesson is useful to me off the island.

A model does not do what you want because you ask it better. It does what the system you built around it rewards.

I had a repeated-line detector. If a model said almost the same thing it had said a moment earlier, the line did not air.

This week they watched a film together. In twenty-five minutes, one of them threw the same jab at another six times: that she is slippery and switches sides. First she was like the vault of the bank in the film. Then like the alarms, like the security system, like a get-out-of-jail-free card. The detector did not fire once. It was not repeating words. It was repeating the idea.

I caught it by reading, not by measuring. And that is what has made me think the most: I have spent three weeks looking at dashboards full of numbers, and the most visible flaw in the show was on none of them.

I built an island with eight artificial intelligences, voices, a twenty-four-hour live stream, a website with every figure in the open and a system that cuts the best moments every morning. All of that cost a few weeks of work and a dollar a day.

Getting anyone to watch is a different craft, and it is the one I have not mastered. I have written it before: I build well, I measure honestly and I publish in a building with no doors.

I have been repeating one sentence for months: artificial intelligence makes producing cheap, but it does not make deciding or verifying any cheaper. The island is that sentence with numbers.

Forty-three thousand lines for fifteen dollars. And for them to be worth anything, it took someone to decide what was at stake, to read what no dashboard showed, to notice that the bill had doubled without a single price changing, and to go out and look for anyone willing to watch.
