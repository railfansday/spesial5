Your $230 payment: $65 of it is interest in month 1, $0.32 in month 22 — where each payment actually goes

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Same debt as the earlier examples: $4,100 on a card at 18.9% APR, paying $230 a month, no new charges. Paid on time, that takes 22 months and $751 of interest, $4,851 total. Those are the totals. This post is the month-by-month split underneath them: how much of each $230 payment goes to interest, and how much actually takes the balance down. The split changes every month, and the first month is the worst one you will ever see.

The interest on a $4,100 balance at 18.9% is 1.575% a month, which is $64.58. Your $230 payment covers that first, and the remaining $165.43 is what the balance falls by: $4,100 becomes $3,934.57. More than a quarter of the first payment — 28 cents of every dollar — goes to interest, and it buys nothing. The balance falls by less than three quarters of what you paid.

That is the same first month whatever the payment is. The interest charge does not depend on how much you pay; it depends on the balance. At $130 a month, the first payment is $64.58 of interest and $65.42 of principal — almost exactly half the payment is interest. At $200 a month it is $64.58 and $135.43. At $300 a month it is $64.58 and $235.43. The interest is fixed by the balance. The payment decides how much principal sits on top of it.

Three things to notice. First, the interest falls every month because the balance falls every month — the payment stays $230, but more and more of it becomes principal. By month 12, $196.45 of the $230 takes the balance down. By month 18, $215.76 does. Second, most of the interest is front-loaded: by month 12 you have already paid $593.58 of the $751 total, 79% of it, while the balance is still $1,933.58 — nearly half the original debt, with only 21% of the interest left to pay. Third, the last payment is $20.59, not $230: $0.32 of interest and $20.27 of principal, because there is only $20.27 left to pay.

The monthly interest drops below $30 for the first time in month 14: $27.31 of interest, $202.69 of principal, balance $1,531.35. It drops below $10 for the first time in month 20: $7.39 of interest, $222.61 of principal, balance $246.39. Between month 1 and month 20, the interest share of the payment falls from 28% to 3%. Nothing about the payment changed. The balance did the work.

That is also why the payment size matters more than anything else in this series. A bigger payment does not change the first month's interest — $64.58 either way — but it takes a bigger bite of principal from month 1, so the balance falls faster, so every later month's interest is smaller. At $300 a month the first payment takes $235.43 off the balance instead of $165.43, and the whole payoff finishes in 16 months with $554 of interest instead of 22 months and $751. At $130 a month the first payment takes $65.42 off, and the payoff takes 44 months and $1,612. The first month's split is the whole story in miniature: same interest, different principal, different ending.

The statement shows the interest charge and the new balance. It does not show the split as a story — it shows one month at a time, and one month at a time the split looks like nothing is changing. Month 2 is $61.97 of interest and $168.03 of principal; month 3 is $59.32 and $170.68. The principal creeps up by about $2.60 a month. The change is real but slow, and slow change is easy to miss while the balance still starts with a 3.

The number worth watching is the balance, not the payment. The payment is the same $230 every month. The balance is the thing that decides next month's interest, and it is the only thing the payment can move. Every dollar of principal in month 1 saves interest in months 2 through 22. That is the whole mechanism, and it runs whether you watch it or not.
