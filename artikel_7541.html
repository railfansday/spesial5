A Good Statue Is Hard To Find

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

Not long ago, I drifted through a handful of old European cities. What stayed with me was neither the cathedrals nor the cafés with their thimbles of ferocious coffee.

It was the company the bronze kept: The squares belonged to poets and playwrights, astronomers and philosophers, figures in stone cloaks gazing over bustling pigeons as if weighing one more line. Here and there, a statesman sulked on his plinth, and the courthouses guarded their jurists, but officeholders were scarce.

The exception was the kings, those equestrian gentlemen forever reining in horses that never move.

The pattern has a plausible explanation. Europe was hardly a stranger to electoral politics. Athenian citizens voted in assembly, the Roman Republic chose its consuls, Iceland’s Althing has convened since 930, and Venice elected its doges for roughly 11 centuries.

So, the scarcity of political monuments cannot be explained by the absence of voting. A better account is that a reputation lasts about as long as the work behind it remains usable.

A literary or scientific achievement keeps functioning. Dante’s “Commedia,” Shakespeare’s plays, and Newton’s “Principia” can still be met directly, without an intermediary, and they answer questions each generation asks anew.

A statesman’s achievement, by contrast, is tied to the circumstance that called it forth: a treaty, a succession crisis, a levy. Once that circumstance passes, the achievement survives chiefly as historical record. The principal lessons learned from political figures are the consequences of their decisions rather than the reasoning that produced them, which is seldom preserved and less often consulted.

Political monuments are also unusually exposed to revision. The removal of Lenin statues across the former Eastern Bloc after 1989, and the toppling of Edward Colston in Bristol in 2020, suggest that a statesman’s standing stays open to dispute in a way a poet’s rarely does. Southerners know this quite well, having watched Confederate monuments come down in town after town.

Which brings me home to Auburn, where the statuary has another temperament altogether. Ours wear shoulder pads. Heisman winners stride eternally outside Jordan-Hare, and Shug Jordan keeps his vigil.

I love a Saturday on the Plains as dearly as anyone alive. But an athlete’s fame is the briefest bloom in the garden, briefer even than a senator’s. Who toasts the champions of antiquity? The wrestlers and charioteers of Olympia survive mostly because Pindar wrote odes to them. They endure, in other words, by the grace of a poet.

So it was with a small, private gladness that I read the news: Auburn announced a $22 million investment from the state of Alabama, “championed by Gov. Kay Ivey, an Auburn alumna, to establish the Kay Ivey Institute for Civic Leadership.” At last, we may raise a monument to the kind of figure the Old World remembers.

Auburn enters a crowded and quickening field. Recently, I mentioned the civics centers popping up across many institutions and states. Ohio has committed $24 million to create civic-education centers at five public universities. The ones to beat are well known: Florida, with its Hamilton School for Classical and Civic Education; Texas, with its School of Civic Leadership and Civitas Institute; and Tennessee, whose Institute of American Civics was established in 2022 by the state’s general assembly.

These programs now count in the standings too, since City Journal’s new college-ranking system rewards institutions that host a School of Civic Thought. It’s high time, isn’t it, that Auburn’s name sat atop that list?

Of our own institute, we know very little. The university says its academic direction, programming and long-term priorities will be developed in the coming months, so my hopes go out wearing a raincoat.

Still, the opportunity is plain. Auburn could gather some of the most distinguished and exacting scholars in the humanities: historians, political theorists, legal minds, and philosophers. Those disciplines are the timbers from which civic life is built.

The town’s growing. It’s a fine place to live and think, and its research already commands respect well beyond the state line.

The old oaks at Toomer’s Corner stood since the late 1930s. They died because of football. After Auburn beat Alabama in 2010, an aggrieved rival poured herbicide into their roots.

Their replacements fared little better. One was set ablaze during a celebration after a win over Louisiana State University, and the corner has been replanted more than once since.

Passion for a game can kill the very things it gathers around. Yet something survived. Saplings grown from the original oaks’ acorns now line the walk from the corner to Samford Hall. The descendants of those trees mark a path from the place where we celebrate to the place where we study.

If the Ivey Institute is built with care, more of us may follow that path. Someday, a visitor may find, among the halfbacks, a philosopher in bronze. Better still, he may find generations of graduates who read the founders as eagerly as they read a box score.

That would be a monument no poison or match could take down.
