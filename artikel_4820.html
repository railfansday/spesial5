Is There Alcohol in Non-Alcoholic Drinks?

The popularity of “alcohol-free” beverages has grown worldwide.1 When seeing the labels “alcohol-free”, “non-alcoholic”, and “dealcoholized,” on beverages, it is important to understand what they mean to make informed decisions about whether to drink them and how much. While these low-alcohol labels suggest very low to no alcohol content, the true meaning of these labels may surprise you and change how you choose to consume these beverages.

As it turns out, the meaning of these low-alcohol labels depends on where you live.2 In most countries, the label a manufacturer is allowed to use on their product is determined by a centralized government body (e.g., Canadian Food Inspection Agency, United States (U.S.) Food and Drug Administration (FDA) or Alcohol and Tobacco Tax and Trade Bureau (TTB)). Each government body determines which labels may be used based on the alcohol by volume (ABV) of the beverage. For context, the typical ABV for beer is 5%, for wine is 12%, and for distilled spirits is 40%.

While there are regulated processes for these low-alcohol labels on beverages, the actual ABV requirements differ between (and even within) countries. In the U.S., the maximum for the “dealcoholized” label is 0.5% ABV. “Non-alcoholic beverages” can contain traces of alcohol (less than 0.5% ABV), but an “alcohol-free” beverage must have no detectable alcohol (0% ABV). In contrast, in Canada, a “dealcoholized” beverage can contain up to 1.1% ABV. Further, the labels “non-alcoholic” and “alcohol-free” are used interchangeably for products with alcohol levels up to 0.05% ABV.

When making decisions about whether to drink these beverages (and how much), these distinctions between drink labels can be vitally important. Many people wish to avoid consuming alcohol entirely. Common reasons for not drinking alcohol at all are cultural and religious reasons, cancer prevention (alcohol is a carcinogen and increases risk for cancer), and improving mental health or well-being. For instance, the distinction between having alcohol in a beverage (even small amounts) versus not can be critical for someone who has chosen to stop drinking alcohol completely to help manage their alcohol use disorder. Further, underage consumption of these beverages is a potential concern. In many cases, underage purchasing and consumption are unregulated. While it is very unlikely that the ABV of these beverages would contribute to underage intoxication, early exposure to low-alcohol beverages that taste and look like typical alcoholic beverages may encourage young people to drink alcohol more heavily and at younger ages (increasing risk of alcohol problems).

For some people, the differences in ABV between these labels may not be as important. For instance, someone who wishes to reduce how much alcohol they drink, but not stop entirely, can meet that goal by replacing their typical alcoholic drink with a drink with any of these low-alcohol labeled beverages. This is because “dealcoholized”, “alcohol-free”, and “non-alcoholic” beverages should all have much lower ABV than typical alcoholic drinks. Similarly, switching completely to drinks with these labels will likely avoid intoxication. Intoxication typically occurs after drinking two to three typical alcoholic drinks in an hour. Given that these beverages have substantially reduced levels of alcohol, it would require drinking a considerable amount of these beverages in a short amount of time to become intoxicated. There are exceptions to this idea. In countries where these labels apply to beverages with lowered ABV, but not very low ABV, someone could still become intoxicated if they drank enough of them in a relatively short period of time. For instance, drinking four to five “deacloholized” beverages within an hour (at the upper end of 1.0% ABV limit in Canada) could result in a similar intoxication level to drinking one typical alcoholic beverage during that timeframe.

Taken together, beverages labeled as low-alcohol offer a promising alternative to consuming typical alcoholic beverages. They can be a useful substitute beverage when someone wishes to reduce their alcohol use. Depending on whether you want to reduce or stop your alcohol use entirely, it may be worthwhile taking a closer look at what those labels mean where you live and being informed about how much alcohol may be in that beverage to ensure the beverage will help you meet that goal.
