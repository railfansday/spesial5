5 Ways To Prioritize Your Brain Health

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

From understanding your risk factors to adopting brain-healthy routines, here’s how to take a more proactive role in your cognitive health.

One of the body’s most vital organs—the brain—can be overlooked, even though it guides nearly everything we do.

At its core, brain health is about how well your body’s command center functions—processing emotions, coordinating movement, and interpreting what you see, hear, taste, touch, and smell. It also includes cognitive health—your ability to think, learn, and remember.

Because the brain plays such a central role in everyday life, you don’t have to wait until something feels off to start taking care of it. Brain changes associated with Alzheimer’s disease can begin about 20 years before symptoms appear, making a proactive approach to brain health an important part of caring for your overall health.

This messaging inspired actress, author, and advocate Julianne Moore to partner with Eli Lilly and Company on their Brain Health Matters campaign to encourage people to prioritize their brain health. “From my conversations with people living with Alzheimer’s disease, I’ve seen how much proactive care matters,” Moore says. “That’s why I’m helping to encourage everyone to prioritize their brain health through regular conversations with their doctors.”

Not sure where to begin? Here are five ways to make brain health a priority part in your overall health.

You might not have any concerns about cognitive decline, much less dementia. But taking a proactive approach to cognitive health doesn’t have to wait until later in life—or until something feels off. One brain change associated with Alzheimer’s disease involves amyloid, a protein the body produces naturally. Talking to your doctor can help you understand your personal risk factors and discuss whether steps such as establishing a cognitive baseline and adopting brain-healthy habits may be appropriate for you.

Add brain health to your “must-ask” list for medical appointments and come prepared with specific questions to guide the conversation. Ask your doctor whether a cognitive assessment is appropriate for you. This spoken, written, or digital evaluation can provide a starting point for monitoring your memory and thinking over time.

While a cognitive assessment cannot diagnose a condition on its own, establishing a baseline and completing follow-up assessments as recommended by your doctor may help spot early signs of memory and thinking issues and monitor changes over time.

Research shows that up to 45 percent of dementia cases, including those caused by Alzheimer’s disease, may be prevented or delayed by managing changeable risk factors. These include blood pressure, cholesterol, and diabetes; lifestyle habits around diet, exercise, alcohol consumption, and smoking; and even hearing loss, depression, traumatic brain injury (TBI), and sleep.

You can’t change everything that affects your risk—such as age, family history, or certain genetic markers. Once you understand your risk factors, establish a cognitive baseline, and identify brain-healthy habits that could work for you, the next step is bringing those pieces together. Work with your doctor to create a plan based on your individual health needs.

Your plan might include ways to manage changeable risk factors, behaviors that support your brain health, and a schedule for revisiting your cognitive health with your doctor. Regular conversations with your doctor about these topics can help you take action early and help support your brain health for the future.

Honest discussions around brain health can help go a long way toward reducing stigma and helping others feel more comfortable seeking information, support, and care. Opening up—even in small ways—can make a difference, whether you’re checking in with loved ones, sharing your own concerns, or simply normalizing the topic in everyday life.

“Brain health is something we don’t talk about often enough,” Moore says. “There’s so much fear around cognitive health and even talking to a doctor about getting an assessment. I think it needs to be normalized.”
