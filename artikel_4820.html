That's why we tapped Schaeffer, podiatrist Daniel Geller, DPM, and Women's Health fitness editors to give us the lowdown on what to look for when shopping for the best On Cloud shoes for walking: in this case, weight, cushion, stability, and heel-to-toe drop.

This shoe has a medium cushion level for that sweet spot that’s comfortable and light on your feet without being overly plush. The CloudTec midsole does a great job absorbing impact, making it a solid choice for everything from sightseeing and airport sprints to casual daily walks. The lightweight build almost disappears on your foot, which is exactly what you want when you’re packing light or spending hours exploring.

The tradeoff for all that packability and featherlight comfort is support. The flexible upper and minimalist construction can feel a bit flimsy compared to more structured walking shoes, especially if you need extra arch support or stability.

On sneakers are extremely comfortable, but Schaeffer says that's because most of its shoes have a bit of weight to them. If a shoe is too lightweight (under four ounces), it sacrifices function and stability, he says. But, don't get it twisted—these don't feel like bricks on your feet either. Most On shoes are between four and 10 ounces, which means they're the perfect medium weight that doesn’t slack on support.

A heel-to-toe drop, sometimes called a midsole drop, is the difference in height between the heel and forefoot (toe area). Schaeffer says to look for a walking shoe with a heel-to-toe drop that puts your foot in a neutral position, which will be different for everyone’s unique foot and step pattern.

Support and stability are the number one qualities you should look for in a walking shoe, says Geller. He notes that when arches are supported, you create an "excellent" platform to launch from, meaning the feet are in the ideal position for takeoff and landing. This can help avoid foot injuries down the line.

Geller also says that many good walking shoes, including motion control shoes, will have medial posting—a firm wedge in the arch that holds up the heel—and firm cushioning for more support. "Firmer cushioning will resist the inward roll of the foot [overpronation], controlling the knees and hips better," he says.

If stability is a priority for you, also consider shoes with a broader sole to minimize excess movement. The increased material along the sides and front of your foot creates a wider platform, which is naturally resistant to feet rolling or wobbling, says Geller. In other words, it's a great feature for walkers who overpronate.

"Whenever you’re looking for a good walking shoe, you want to find a great mix of comfort and stability," says Schaeffer. You want a shoe that’s comfortable on your feet, but the level of cushioning will vary depending on your specific foot needs.

Wearing running shoes for walking isn’t inherently bad, but it’s not always the best idea either. Shoes designed specifically for running tend to have significantly more cushioning to absorb the higher impact that comes with each stride. “This isn't necessary for walking and reduces your feel of the ground, which is problematic for those with any balance issues or previous falls," Milica McDowell, a doctor of physical therapy, previously told Women’s Health.

Running shoes often have a very tight fit for higher performance, a narrow toe box, or are highly supported in the arch, which are features a lifestyle walker does not need, says McDowell. That’s why, in most cases, walkers are recommended to opt for a shoe designed specifically for walking (or one labeled for walking and running).
