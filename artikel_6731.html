Sean Duffy Is a Perfect Reality-TV Politician. Too Bad for America.

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

As a fellow MTV veteran, I recognize that the transportation secretary has been practicing for this moment since his Real World days.

Yesterday, in the wake of Friday’s grounding of all flights in the continental United States and the weekend of cancellations and chaos that ensued, Secretary of Transportation Sean Duffy made the rounds of the Sunday-morning political talk shows to make this face.

It’s been a tough few days for Duffy, and Duffy has been huffy. From Friday’s press conference with Markwayne Mullin, to his own microphonically challenged solo outing on Saturday, through all five morning-show appearances, Duffy has checked all the boxes the modern Republican official must: look aggrieved, place blame elsewhere, praise the president, be white. It’s all television, after all, and there’s a role to be played.

Sean Duffy has been at this for decades. You know he was on the sixth season of The Real World, and you know he met his wife and fellow Real Worlder Rachel Campos when they participated in an early season of what would become The Real World/Road Rules Challenge. But when you really go back and look, you’ll see that they’ve both been practicing for this moment since the ’90s. Game recognizes game; as a fellow alumnus of 20th-century MTV, let me walk you through it.

The Real World was born when MTV decided to try its hand at creating a weekly soap opera, in the style of the era-defining Beverly Hills 90210 and Melrose Place. But cable budgets being what they are, a full scripted show was untenable, so producers had the bold idea to do away with actors and writers entirely. The first couple seasons of The Real World kept the focus on art by casting young artists: Julie the emerging dancer amid the model and writer and singer-songwriters in season 1, Jon the wannabe Garth Brooks in season 2. But that went out the window quick, as producers and aspiring castmates figured out the same thing at the same time: The job was to stir the pot. So by season 3, deeply unpleasant bike messenger Puck was dropped into the San Francisco house to clash with saintly AIDS educator Pedro, with whom conservative Rachel initially refused to share a bathroom for fear of sink-based HIV transmission. In 1994.

By season 6, casts seemed chosen specifically to play a predetermined part and drive each other up the wall. Real World: Boston features Stanford med student Kameelah and ladies man Syrus, Catholic Texan Elka and Manhattan-born mess on wheels Montana. And then there was Sean Duffy, Wisconsin boy, lumberjack-games participant, and future secretary of transportation. Sean occupied the Cute Boy spot in the cast that had been held by Aaron in Los Angeles and St. Louis race car driver Mike in London. But this being 1997, he was also a different cultural archetype: the knee-jerk Rush Limbaugh–era conservative. Accent on jerk. Here’s a moment when Sean makes fun of Elka’s underwear and then immediately denies it.

Just straight-up, reflexively, smoothly denying he did something we just saw him do, because he did it on camera and into a microphone. Lying with a straight face. This would become a job skill.

It’s a little like something we saw Sean’s future wife Rachel do on her season. In an early episode, she engages in the hot, safe rebellious act of 1994 by getting her navel pierced. In a later episode, she gets a telephone call from her angry mother, who has seen the belly-button bauble in a photograph (a physical photograph, sent through the U.S. Mail—again, we lived differently in the ’90s). Rachel assures her mother it’s not a piercing but instead a sticker she put there. Forget the fact that, again, all of this is on camera and the mom is going to learn the truth soon enough. Watching back then, I remember thinking two things: One, this person lies very easily, and two, I think she might be one of those people who’s conservative just so they don’t upset their parents.

Anyway. Sean and Rachel did The Challenge and got married and had three hundred and fifty children. Sean became a congressman for Wisconsin; Rachel tried out for The View but never quite nailed it and now she’s on Fox & Friends sometimes. And because it all comes back to reality television and personal branding, the Duffy family spent a few weeks of Sean’s tenure as secretary of transportation to film a show. The Great American Road Trip was supposed to have aired on Fox Nation, but instead it got dumped onto YouTube, where most episodes are hovering around 10k views.

Also, Duffy’s son-in-law Michael Alfonso, who’s 26, is set to become the youngest person in Congress, having won a seat in Wisconsin’s 7th District in this month’s elections. Alfonso’s campaign was buoyed by generous campaign donations from numerous transportation companies, executives, and lobbyists, including a $2,500 contribution from Air Space Intelligence’s PAC in March. In June, the FAA awarded the AI start-up a contract worth up to $875.95 million. The company’s SMART system went nationwide days before the November 13 ground stop, though the FAA and the company said the outage was unrelated to SMART.

I’m sure it’s nothing. But I’m even more sure Duffy will say it is.

The way that John F. Kennedy understood television in the way Richard Nixon did not, Sean Duffy understands reality television in a way few others do. He’s lived it. He understands it as a marketing exercise. Knowing your brand, hitting the beats of the scene you’re supposed to play, showing up with a yearbook picture-day haircut and lying with a smile will get you far in this world. Whether he survives this turbulence or not, he’s learned that lesson.

Sean and Rachel have been at this for decades, but as viewers so have we. We can see them as they are: dishonest children, playing a role, begging for approval, just trying not to get yelled at.
