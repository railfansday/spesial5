Why I’m Wearing Nothing But Pajamas

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Last March, the 20th to be exact, I decided I was never going to wear anything but pajamas again, day and night. That was the day my life changed forever. That was the day my husband, Tad, died.

Tad was the only thing I never wanted to return or take back. I’m impossible to please, impossible to buy a present for, but he never stopped trying. I had no doubt about him. We met nearly 40 years ago, when I interviewed him on January 27, 1987, at three o’clock in the afternoon. He sat on a sofa, a large, overstuffed pale-gray one, in his writing studio in New York. A year and a half later, I was sitting on that same sofa and his writing studio had become our apartment. After we married, we did everything together. Worked together, played together—​our work was our play—traveled together. That never stopped, until it did.

I suddenly had four children, from my age (then mid-30s) down to four years old, plus four ready-made grandchildren and two new sobriquets—belle-mère and GrandDodie. (This was Tad’s way of easing me into early onset grandmotherhood and keeping that awful word stepmother from floating into my life.) Happily, none of the dear ones have ever called me the evil S-word, exceptfor one time on our way out to Quogue on Long Island on a summer weekend. Trying to keep cool on a hot Friday night, I’d tied my long dark hair in a knot at the top of my head. “Ooooohhhh,” Tad’s five-year-old daughter, Sarah, screeched from the back seat of our tiny Fiat convertible, where she was squished comfortably on her rocking horse. “The wicked stepmother!” There it was, out in the open.

But I stray. Pajamas. Or is it pyjamas? I like the word spelled the way the Brits do it, but feel it’s worthy of two spellings, like doughnut and donut. Either is better than the humiliating nicknames—pj’s, jammies, or jam-jams.

Pajamas—I’m going to live in them, go everywhere in them, never wear anything else. If Julian Schnabel can do it, why can’t I? Pajamas are for wearing inside, but when I feel up to going out again, I’ll wear them outside too. One night, I got a glimpse of my oldest stepdaughter, Trippy, wearing a pair in navy with white piping. I like that look. I filed the thought away. But the notion to live in pajamas hit me over the head when Tad’s niece Torrie drove me up to our house in Newport a week after Tad died. As soon as we arrived, in the middle of the afternoon, Torrie changed into the most delicious, crisp pair of navy-trimmed white-poplin Matouks. That was it. It was pajamas forever for me.

I might have mentioned my pajama game plan to my Vogue editor, because a few days later, a box from Petite Plume arrived containing the perfect pair of tailored striped ones, blue and white, a long pant and a long-sleeve top, 100 percent cotton. Chloe, my six-foot-tall editor, is more of a nightgown person and says, “Pajamas are often not made for tall people. You end up with the pant leg an awkward length.” But I don’t feel sorry for Chloe—I’m envious of her glorious monumentality (I’m five feet two when I can stand up straight), and, anyway, she gets the best nightgowns from Salter House in Brooklyn, spun from the finest cotton in all of Western Christendom.

My new pajamas are size extra-extra small and they fit just right, which means with a very loose and baggy ankle-​length pant. Webster’s defines pajamas as “worn for sleeping or lounging, typically consisting of a jacket or top and matching trousers.” The word dates back to the 19th century, derived from the Hindi term (also pājāma), which in turn comes from the Persian words pā for “leg” and jāma for “garment.” The pajama pant has long been worn throughout Asia as everyday wear, well before they became the sleeping-and-lounging uniform for every law-abiding Westerner.

I have no interest in lounging (loathe the word) and I’ll never wear them to bed. And it’s definitely not cool to wear the pajama top—pajama bottom or no pajama bottom. Pajama tops are like anchovies—they get pushed to the side of the plate. No one wants to wear just the top, except for maybe Ryan Gosling, who topped off a pair of crisp white pants with a white-piped pajama shirt at the Cannes Film Festival in 2011.

But bottoms are the tops for me, and for almost everyone I know. I’ve lived in my striped Petite Plume bottoms since the day they arrived last April. And not long ago, I found myself watching Doris Day in the 1957 movie The Pajama Game, where she worked in the Sleeptite Pajama Factory. “Wear Sleeptite at all social functions,” the workers sang. “Dresses are fine but Sleeptite’s divine. Sleeptite has everything—grace, style, and comfort.” I’m with Doris Day on this one.

During this mourning period, a few close friends have been visiting me for lunch and dinner, and I haven’t gone out. One day, the artist Jamian Juliano-Villani dropped by with Kat Herriman, a young writer friend of mine. Jamian said she likes her pajamas to be “versatile, so if you need to leave the house, you don’t look like a crazy person.” She wears two things on rotation—a Matouk robe and pajamas an art dealer gave her by the Copenhagen brand Tekla, which she wears outside. “They’re very, very cool. If you throw on a pair of sunglasses, you look great. They’re not cheap but they’re worth it.” She’s one of the few people I know who likes a matching set. “You feel more together. Go to bed, wake up….”

She does. I tell Jamian that I don’t know anybody who sleeps in their pajamas. Everyone I spoke to who wears them takes them off before going to bed—to sleep in the nude. (Not I.)

And not my friend Ruth Ganesh, who brought a herd of 100 life-size elephant sculptures made from the Lantana camara weed in Southern India to America to raise awareness for wildlife conservation. She wears Indian cotton pajamas, made in Jaipur using block technique. “They make the most beautiful multicolored ones, with flowers and patterns all over them, that give me the happiest dreams,” she tells me. “It’s a rite of passage when English people visitIndia to go pajama shopping in Anokhi. We stuff our suitcases full of them.”

Funny, Cecily Brown had told me that one of her dealers and a dear friend, Stefania Bortolami, gives her artist friends pajamas from Rome for Christmas. Seems pajamas-​as-​Christmas-​presents are a thing with art dealers and their artists. John Currin tells me that his London dealer, Sadie Coles, sends him ones from Turnbull & Asser for Christmas. The artist Diamond Stingily comes by with Pucci, her artist friend Martine Syms’s best-in-show Pomeranian, and my ears perk up when she tells me that the only place to get pajamas is a store called Schostal in Rome. “All the artists go there,” she says. Turns out artists know about the place because of Gavin Brown, whose Roman gallery is in Sant’Andrea de Scaphis, a ninth-​century deconsecrated church. Gavin and his wife, artist Hope Atherton, have introduced legions to the brand. Schostal pajamas are made of the finest materials, sewn with a sky-high thread count that makes them as soft as a baby mole’s skin and slightly see-through. They come in solids and stripes in mouthwatering colors.

I have to have a pair. However, I’m hopeless when it comes to ordering anything online, even FreshDirect. Then, Hope herself drops by for afternoon tea one Monday in June—and before I know it, a delicious pair of lemon Schostals with red-and-white pinstripes are winging their way to me from Rome. They’re not cheap—nearly $300—thanks to Trump’s tariffs.

The whole idea of wearing pajamas is to trick myself into feeling better. When I tell my friend and favorite dermatologist Joel Kassimir that I’ve decided to live in pajamas from now on, he says, “Like Peter Marino’s leathers, Julian Schnabel’s pajamas, and Vinny ‘Chin’ Gigante’s bathrobe, it is a uniform, and it works.”

The artist Rachel Feinstein brings me the best sashimi lunch from Sugarfish by Sushi Nozawa and we talk pajamas. She loves hers. “They’re by Dôen, a company that Flora (my 17-year-old daughter) is into, and she’s gotten me into them. It’s not really my style. They’re very Laura Ashley, actually—new Gen Z Laura Ashley. Made out of incredibly thin fine cotton. But I love them. They have a blue delft-like pattern, and the minute I’m home, I put them on. I don’t like silk—too cloying and hot.” No silk for me either. Or satin: too 1930s starlet boudoir—Carole Lombard, Claudette Colbert, Lauren Bacall, or Katharine Hepburn in Bringing Up Baby.

I can’t possibly make a decision about pajamas without talking to Vogue’s head of fashion, Virginia Smith. She’s running to catch a flight to Los Angeles when I reach her by phone, just in time for her to tell me about For Restless Sleepers, Olivia von Halle, and what she calls “the classics”—Charvet, Brooks Brothers, and Ralph Lauren. She also tells me about Grace Coddington wearing Michael Kors blue pajamas to the 2015 Met Gala—and that she herself wore Erdem mint pj’s to the 2024 Met Gala, “Sleeping Beauties.”

Last night I saw a woman on Park Avenue in matching pajamas walking her dog.

A few very close artist friends of Tad’s and mine have been keeping me afloat—or vertical, to use Tad’s word—throughout these difficult days, weeks, months without him. In the last 5 or 10 years of Tad’s life, whenever anyone asked him how he was doing, his answer was “I’m still vertical.”

Pajamas make me feel safe—and vertical—even though you wear them in a horizontal position. Pajamas are my safety net.

One mid-June sunny evening, Maurizio Cattelan and Anna Weyant come for dinner. Over the 30 years Tad and I have known Maurizio, every time he’d come for dinner, he’d surprise us with a wildly original present, each one topping the one before in outrageousness: ​a blowfish ceiling lamp; a human-size package of Oscar Mayer wieners; a Gucci box—open the cover and out pops a two-dimensional cutout of Tad and me. This time, he brings the tennis ball Billie Jean King signed for him the day before—he did a photo shoot with her for his magazine Toiletpaper. He mumbles something about the shoot and why she signed the tennis ball, but I don’t get it.

In advance of my birthday, which is the next day, Anna Weyant brings a present too—but hers is in a traditional white box tied up with a very pretty exaggeratedly wide vanilla grosgrain ribbon. It looks just like one of her hyperrealistic paintings. Inside is the perfect gift for me—the navy blue with white trim Eberjeys I’ve been dreaming about. “You can return them if you don’t like them,” she says. “No, I don’t want to return these,” I say with delight. I’m in pajama heaven.

“Will you wear sleepers?” Maurizio asks me.

After he repeats the word three times—“sleepers, sleepers, sleepers”—I finally get it. Slippers to go with my Eberjeys.

June 17: It’s my birthday, and I’m happily (and stylishly) wearing my new Eberjeys. They fit just right, make me feel safe, at home, sleek, well-dressed. These are going-out-in-the-world pajamas. They’re not 100 percent cotton but rather 95 percent Tencel Modal, a proud member of the rayon family, descended from the noble beech tree. Tencel promises all the benefits of cotton—super soft, durable, and a friend of the environment.

At three o’clock in the afternoon, Cecily Brown, Rachel Feinstein, Anna Weyant, and Kat Herriman surprise me with a pajama party. One by one they arrive and immediately change into their pj’s. Rachel didn’t have time to go home to get hers, but Cecily happens to have an extra pair in her bag—a fetching solid peach with navy trim. Rachel wears the top and bottom. She looks perfect, but nobody else dares.

The conversation roams all over the place, from what Cecily did the night she turned 30 to a book about witches and witchcraft called Abject Eroticism in Northern Renaissance Art.

I go to the Mark Hotel for dinner with my friend Peter Gelb, general manager of the Metropolitan Opera, in my navy blue Eberjeys with pearls. All I need are open-toe little satin bedroom slippers to complete my outfit. Nobody notices I’m wearing pajamas, not even Peter. But I know, and I know how much I need them. I need them forever. Or at least for now.
