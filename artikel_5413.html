Stop Treating Media Coverage Like a Trophy. Put It to Work For Your Business.

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

You're viewing the US website. Looking for the Site edition?

Earned media shouldn’t stop working once the article is published. You get the most value from press coverage by treating it as the start.

Opinions expressed by Entrepreneur contributors are their own.

An agent lands a feature in a major publication. Maybe it’s a Wall Street Journal profile on luxury market trends or a Mansion Global piece showcasing a property they sold. The team celebrates, which they should. Someone posts it on LinkedIn. The logo goes on the website. And then, within about 72 hours, the whole thing gets filed away and everyone moves on to the next thing.

The publication reached its audience. The agent got their moment. Done.

Except they’ve only captured maybe one-third of the value that placement was capable of producing.

Most businesses, not just real estate, treat earned media as an event with a beginning and an end. The end is publication day, or maybe the day after when the LinkedIn post goes up. What they rarely think about is that a strong piece of coverage does something the publication’s audience only partially benefits from. It creates a transferable signal of credibility. And unlike the initial readership, which spikes and fades, that credibility doesn’t expire. It can be put to work in sales conversations, listing presentations, recruiting pitches, partnership discussions and investor meetings for as long as it remains relevant.

The publication date should be the beginning of a press hit’s useful life. Most companies treat it as the end.

When a company earns meaningful media coverage, there’s an obvious audience and a less obvious one. The obvious audience is whoever reads the publication that day. They saw it, absorbed it, formed an impression and maybe clicked through it. Some small percentage converted into something useful. That’s the audience most PR measurement focuses on, and it’s real.

The less obvious audience is everyone the company will encounter afterward who never saw the original piece. Future prospects. Potential recruits. Partners evaluating whether to work together. Investors doing background research. Sellers deciding between agents. These people didn’t read the article. But they will see what the company does with it.

Third-party credibility travels differently than self-promotion. A company saying they’re a market leader means almost nothing. A respected publication having said it, even months ago, means something different in a sales conversation, a pitch deck, or a follow-up email. The credibility is borrowed from the publication, and unlike borrowed money, you don’t have to return it. You can keep using it in every context where trust matters, which in most professional services businesses is nearly every context that matters at all.

Most companies return it anyway, unintentionally, by posting once and moving on.

Real estate is a particularly clear example of this dynamic because trust is so central to every major decision in the industry.

A luxury agent gets quoted in a piece about Central Coast market trends. That quote doesn’t just exist in the article. It can be included in the next seller presentation as third-party validation of the agent’s market knowledge. It can be referenced when following up with a prospect who went quiet. It can be sent to a past client alongside a brief note that the agent has been getting some attention for their market expertise lately. In each of those moments, the placement is doing trust work it was never credited for in any media measurement report.

A brokerage gets recognized by a real estate publication for its growth or market performance. That recognition matters on the day it runs. It also matters when a strong agent is evaluating which brokerage to join and finds it during their research. It matters when a corporate relocation client is deciding which firm to trust with their employees. It matters when a developer is choosing a listing partner. The recognition didn’t expire. It just wasn’t put back to work.

This isn’t a real estate insight specifically. Any expertise-driven business encounters the same dynamic. A founder gets a strong profile in a trade publication. A law firm gets recognized for a significant case outcome. A consultant gets quoted extensively in a piece about their industry. Each of those placements has an initial audience. Each of them also creates a credibility signal that can work across every subsequent business development interaction if the company chooses to use it that way.

There’s a structural problem in how most organizations handle earned media that helps explain why the follow-through is so consistently weak. The PR function, whether internal or external, tends to define success as placement. Coverage secured, relationship maintained, report submitted. The job is done.

But the job of making that coverage influence the business is a marketing and sales function. It belongs in listing presentations, pitch decks, email signatures and follow-up sequences. And when PR and marketing operate as separate efforts that hand off awkwardly or don’t communicate at all, the coverage falls into the gap between them. It gets celebrated briefly and then abandoned.

The practical shift here doesn’t require a new budget or a complicated system. It requires asking a different set of questions after meaningful coverage lands.

Where does trust matter in this business right now, and can this placement show up there? A listing presentation is a moment of trust evaluation. So is a recruiting conversation. So is the part of a sales process where a prospect is doing their own research to validate whether you’re worth engaging. Those moments have a before and after, and coverage that shows up before one of them does different work than coverage that never surfaces at all.

Who hasn’t seen this yet that should? Past clients who might refer based on a renewed sense that you’re doing well. Prospects who went quiet and might re-engage if the right reason appears. Investors or partners who would find the recognition relevant to a conversation already in progress.

How can the underlying idea travel beyond the placement itself? A quote about market conditions can become a social post with a different angle. A feature about a sold property can inspire a newsletter about what made that type of buyer willing to pay a premium. The article is one expression of an idea. The idea has more legs than the article does.

Most companies don’t need more press nearly as badly as they need to use the press they’ve already earned. One well-distributed placement that shows up in six different trust-critical moments over the next year is worth considerably more than six placements that each get posted once and forgotten.

Earn once. Distribute repeatedly. The press hit gets you in the door. What you do with it afterward determines whether it was worth earning in the first place.
