‘You said spicy, right?’ Panel takes on Washington’s economic blind spots — and what needs to change

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

by Lisa Stiffler on Nov 17, 2026 at 6:15 pmNovember 18, 2026 at 11:49 am

Washington’s biggest economic threat may be the conviction that it’s already doing everything right.

That was the pointed warning from a Technology Alliance panel Tuesday, where a conversation about talent and economic growth became a challenge to the state’s self-image as an innovation leader.

“You said spicy, right?” Rob Ogburn asked, taking up the invitation to speak bluntly.

“The economy is very strong. The economy is not competitive at all,” said Ogburn, executive director of Business and Community Services at Central Washington University.

While tech giants such as Microsoft and Amazon and sectors including aerospace and biotech are powering today’s economy, that doesn’t mean Washington is positioned to win tomorrow’s investment, develop its workforce or spread opportunity beyond its major employment centers.

Amelia Ransom, former vice president at Smartsheet and past Nordstrom leader, questioned the definition of success, arguing that future growth also depends on investing in people and businesses outside the corporate spotlight.

“Who do we mean when we say winning? Who is it? Who are we talking about, and who are we leaving out?” Ransom said, later adding, “We have to close the daylight between who we say we are and who we actually are.”

The panel was held in downtown Seattle and moderated by Jolenta Coleman-Bush, director of Microsoft Philanthropies. It also included Rachel Smith, president of Washington Roundtable.

Rather than simply calling for more talent and innovation, panelists examined how Washington develops the people already here, whose perspectives inform its decisions and how effectively its institutions and stakeholders work together.

Unification is essential. U.S. Sen. Maria Cantwell, D-Wash., led the passage of the CHIPS and Science Act in 2022 to bolster semiconductor manufacturing and innovation in AI and energy. But her home state has received just 0.3% of the funding awarded so far, Ogburn said.

He argued that other states and regions were more effective at uniting to make their case for investment, showing their collective resources and the problems they could solve.

“We showed up and said, ‘We’re competing with our neighbor, and we’re better than them.’ No investor is going to look at that and say you’re going to make the best use of my money,” Ogburn said.

Cultivate talent, don’t just recruit it. Ransom compared workforce development to a restaurant, a grocery store and a garden: recruiting ready-made workers, developing the talent already here and growing the long-term pipeline.

“The middle is where we probably need to spend more time, which is at the grocery store, which means you’ve got to learn to cook,” she said. Her point: Invest in Washington’s existing employees, students, and businesses.

Smith wants employers to identify the high-wage, high-skill jobs they need filled so educators and businesses can coordinate strategies to prepare workers for those roles.

Fill essential information gaps. Panelists called for a clearer picture of community skills and barriers to opportunity, more voices to challenge prevailing assumptions, and closer examination of how regulations affect businesses.

“We’ve gotten in this war of the rankings where everybody’s like, “No, we’re the best. No, we’re the worst.” And we we’ve got to stop doing that,” Smith said.

The region needs data-backed insights into the reasons behind the rankings, she said. Smith pointed to the recently formed Economic Development Council and its planned statewide strategy as a step toward a more coordinated approach.

Whether these efforts succeed, Ogburn said, should be judged by the communities themselves.

“And then, if you want to measure success, go back to those communities and ask them if they feel successful,” he said. “Do they have agency and self-determination?”
