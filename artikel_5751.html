AMA Steps Into the Debunking Ring

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

— The 'Health vs. Hype' podcast is promising, but a recent episode was a missed opportunity

I've spent a lot of time writing about the importance of science communication, especially for physicians. I've also spent time publicly musing about the importance of our professional societies weighing in on the dangers of health mis- and disinformation. Encouragingly, the American Medical Association (AMA) has officially entered the arena with its new podcast, "Health vs. Hype." The show has tackled the likes of creatine for muscles, protein-maxxing, detoxes, and more. The AMA's willing entry into active myth-busting and evidence-based debunking is a long-overdue and vital institutional response.

In late October 2026, Health vs. Hype posted an episode on "Sea Moss, Leaky Gut and Probiotics." In the episode, host Trace Dominguez talked with Danielle Brown, a nutrition influencer and self-identified holistic nutritionist. He then spent time speaking with gastroenterologist Mohamed Mokadem, MD, to address some of the claims made by Brown early in the episode.

Reasonable people can agree to disagree about the platforming of Brown, even if it was to debunk her claims. Personally, I'm just happy to see the AMA get into the ring of debunking health and wellness misinformation. Will there be a segment of Brown's followers -- or those sympathetic to what she does -- that simply see she was on the AMA's podcast and assume it gives her credibility without ever listening to the episode? Of course there will be. But a good argument can be made for at least attempting to debunk nutrition misinformation while platforming one of their physicians.

Yet beneath the high production value lies a critical strategic misstep: not in what the episode attempted to debunk, but in who was missing from the conversation. Registered dietitian nutritionists (RDNs) were not involved.

This oversight is glaring against the backdrop of current health policy, where the Make America Healthy Again (MAHA) movement and HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. are actively attempting to rewrite the rules of medical education and credentialing. The current political environment isn't just ignoring RDNs; it is actively seeking to supplant science-backed nutrition degrees with unaccredited health-coach certifications and algorithmic advice. RDNs and physicians are experiencing much of the same antagonism from this administration and the MAHA movement at large. And now, with Kennedy claiming that, essentially, artificial intelligence can do what physicians do but better, this alliance is becoming increasingly necessary to defend the institutions and knowledge we've worked so hard to build.

Unfortunately, when major medical platforms omit licensed dietitians from public-facing nutrition discussions, they inadvertently enable that devaluation.

The Health vs. Hype episode was an opportunity to showcase that healthcare is a team sport with physicians leading the way. A RDN should have been a part of the debunking panel for this episode. For example, while Mokadem provided sound medical oversight and explanations of clinical processes, asking a physician to field granular dietary nuances inadvertently highlights the exact disadvantage of limiting the podcast episode to only physician commentary. An RDN could have gone deeper into the claims and realities of seaweed, supplements, and beyond, while providing practical dietary guidance. This type of panel -- physician and RDN -- could have demonstrated how clinical medicine and clinical nutrition seamlessly intersect.

By platforming an RDN alongside a physician, Health vs. Hype, and therefore the AMA, could have accomplished what they set out to do (debunking health and nutrition misinformation) by providing clinical explanations (from a doctor) about where the medical science stands on some of Brown's claims while also offering expert nutrition advice (from an RDN). Physicians and RDNs are natural allies and this would have played to everyone's strengths and expertise.

It's time to start demonstrating that healthcare is a team sport and lean into the expertise of other players on the team. Physicians aren't experts in nutrition and they aren't supposed to be. RDNs aren't experts in medicine and they aren't supposed to be. They are supposed to support each other and work together. What better way to start publicly demonstrating that than through collaborating on tackling health and nutrition misinformation?

It's time for medicine to begin building strategic solidarity for better patient care and against common threats. If medicine is to withstand this wave of anti-science populism, physicians cannot fight alone on every front. Reclaiming the narrative around nutrition requires platforming the professionals who specialize in it. By elevating RDNs alongside physicians, the AMA can do more than just debunk bad science. Instead, it can model the very future of collaborative, science-based healthcare.
