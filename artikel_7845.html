En Banc Fifth Circuit Declares House COVID Proxy Voting Rule Unconstitutional

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

While purporting to offer narrow relief confined to a limited portion of the bill in Texas, the court places a bullseye on broad legislation.

Today, in Texas v. Blanche, the en banc U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit concluded that the proxy voting rule adopted by the House of Representatives during the COVID-19 pandemic violated the Quorum Clause.

The opinion, by Judge Wilson, joined by Chief Judge Elrod and Judges Jones, Smith, Willett, Ho, Duncan, Engelhardt, and Oldham begins:

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the House of Representatives adopted a rule allowing Members to record their presence by proxy for purposes of establishing a quorum. The House used this rule when passing the Consolidated Appropriations Act of 2023 (the Act), having mustered a quorum by counting hundreds of otherwise absent Members as present by proxy. The Senate then passed the bill, and the President signed it into law.

The State of Texas argues that the House's proxy-presence rule is inconsistent with the Constitution's Quorum Clause, which would mean that the House did not validly pass the Act. See U.S. Const. art. I, § 5, cl. 1. Based on that proposition, Texas seeks injunctive relief against just one portion of the Act: The Pregnant Workers Fairness Act (PWFA). The federal Government responds that the enrolled-bill rule, which instructs that a bill's passage through the legislature is conclusive proof of its authenticity, precludes judicial review of whether the Act's passage complied with the Quorum Clause. Alternatively, the Government argues that the Quorum Clause permits proxy presence.

The district court held that the enrolled-bill rule did not apply and that the House's passage of the Act—through use of the proxy-presence rule —violated the Quorum Clause. Accordingly, the district court enjoined the Government from enforcing the PWFA against Texas.

We conclude that the enrolled-bill rule does not bar our review because Texas's constitutional challenge does not implicate a dispute of fact regarding the Act's passage. We further conclude that the Act's passage was unconstitutional because the Quorum Clause does not allow proxies to be included when establishing a quorum. The Clause's text, context, original understanding, and unbroken historical application all indicate that Members of Congress must be present for quorum purposes—as the Supreme Court expressly held over 125 years ago, and as Congress has done ever since the Founding. "Proxy presence" does not count. While the chambers of the legislature have much discretion regarding their rules of procedure, the Quorum Clause's constraints on congressional power cannot be circumvented by congressional fiat. We therefore affirm the judgment of the district court.

The enrolled-bill doctrine articulated in Marshall Field does not preclude judicial review of Texas's Quorum Clause challenge. There are no disputed facts regarding the House's passage of the Act such that we would need to sift any extrinsic evidence. On the contrary, the parties agree that the House purportedly established a quorum only because it counted hundreds of proxies as "present." Texas's challenge therefore depends solely on the Quorum Clause's proper meaning.

Through that lens, the House's passage of the Consolidated Appropriations Act of 2023 contravened the Quorum Clause. The Clause's text, context, original understanding, and historical application all indicate that proxies may not be counted to establish a quorum. As the Supreme Court has held, and as Congress had previously done for over 230 years, Members must be present to satisfy the Quorum Clause. Presence-by-proxy does not suffice. An absent Member's assignment of his or her proxy cannot render that Member present for purposes of the Quorum Clause. Texas has carried its burden to show that it is entitled to a permanent injunction against the PWFA.

Judge Graves wrote the dissent, joined by Judges Stewart, Richman, Southwick, Higginson, Douglas, and Ramirez. It begins:

In this suit, the State of Texas sought a preliminary injunction preventing enforcement of one portion of the Consolidated Appropriations Act of 2023, the Pregnant Workers Fairness Act (PWFA), attempting to veto Congress's protections for pregnant workers and new parents while retaining millions in federal funding.

On May 15, 2020, in response to the COVID-19 pandemic, the House of Representatives adopted House Resolution 965, which authorized remote committee proceedings and remote voting in the House. Resolution 965 provides that:

1. [A]t any time after the Speaker . . . is notified . . . that a public health emergency due to a novel coronavirus is in effect, the Speaker . . . may designate a period . . . during which a Member who is designated by another Member as proxy in accordance with section 2 may cast the vote of such other Member or record the presence of such other Member in the House.

H.R. Res. 965 § 1(a), 116th Cong. (2020).

Section 2 described the process for remote participation through designating proxies: the designating Member had to submit a signed letter to the Clerk that specified the designated Member. Id. § 2. Section 3 outlined the process for voting during covered periods, which, absent an extension, ran for forty-five days after the Speaker, or their designee, designated such a period. Id. §§ 1(b), 3. Section 4 authorized remote Committee proceedings. Id. § 4. Section 5 mandated that the chair of the Committee on House Administration certify that it would be feasible to use technology to conduct remote voting in the House, required that the chair of the Committee on Rules submit regulations providing for the implementation of remote voting, and provided for remote voting once those regulations were in place. Id. § 5. Section 6 required that Sections 1, 2, and 3 be carried out in accordance with existing House rules to the extent practicable. Id. § 6. During the 117th Congress, the House reaffirmed this practice in House Resolution 8 and continued to apply it. H.R. Res. 8 § 3(s), 117th Cong. (2021).

At issue here: the House used these procedures as it voted on and passed the Consolidated Appropriations Act of 2023. See 168 Cong. Rec. H10,529 (daily ed. Dec. 23, 2022). Under House rules, a quorum of the 435-Member House is a simple majority: 218. See, e.g., Rules of the House of Representatives, R. XX, cl. 6(b), H.R. Doc. No. 116-177, at 873–74 (2021). The vote on the final passage of the Act was 225 yeas, 201 nays, 1 "present," and 4 non-votes. 168 Cong. Rec. H10,528 (daily ed. Dec. 23, 2022). Out of the 431 votes, 205 votes were cast by Members in person and 226 were cast remotely by proxy. See id. at H10,529.

Even though less than a majority was physically present at the time of the vote, no Member objected that a quorum was lacking. Id. Representative Chip Roy of Texas, however, noted that a physical quorum was not present and reserved the right to object. Id. In response to a series of parliamentary inquiries from Representative Roy as to how a quorum was present, the Speaker Pro Tempore, Representative G.K. Butterfield, responded that a quorum was present because "Members casting their vote or recording their presence by proxy [we]re counted for the purpose of establishing a quorum" pursuant to House Resolution 8. Id.

After the Act passed the House, presiding officers of both chambers of Congress printed and signed the bill. See 1 U.S.C. § 106. At that point, it was an "enrolled bill." Id. The enrolled bill was then sent to the President, who signed the Consolidated Appropriations Act of 2023 into law. See Pub. L. No. 117-328, 136 Stat. 4459 (2022).

The $1.7 trillion-dollar government funding bill was passed in accordance with House Rules, including House Resolution 8, which allowed members to record their presence remotely by proxy during the coronavirus pandemic. 431 votes were cast with respect to the bill. At the time, no Member objected that a quorum was lacking. Nonetheless, the State of Texas, having accepted the vast majority of the funds that Congress appropriated to it, now contends that the portion of the bill providing protections for pregnant women and workers violates the Quorum Clause.

Texas contends that the Act is invalid because the Quorum Clause required that a majority of Members be physically present on the House floor at the time of the vote. In so doing, Texas asks the courts to graft an additional requirement to the Quorum Clause of the Constitution.

I agree with the majority that the enrolled-bill rule does not bar our review of this particular constitutional challenge. However, I cannot agree with their addition of a new requirement to the Quorum Clause. Even though the majority declares that its reasoning is limited to the question of "whether the Quorum Clause allows proxy presence to be included in the quorum count," ante at 17–18, their conclusion is far more expansive. The conclusion that "[t]he Quorum Clause is concerned with physical presence" shoehorns a much larger requirement into the Constitution. Ante at 28. Because the plain text of Article I, Section 5, Clause 1, historical practice, and evidence of the framers' intent all weigh against the addition of the majority's new "physical presence" requirement, I respectfully dissent.

Judge Haynes did not join the dissent, but a footnote to the opinion reads:

Judge Haynes states that she does not think it is appropriate to throw out the Federal House of Representatives in this arena, so she dissents. She also appreciates Judge Graves's dissent.

Although the majority purports to offer a narrow holding, and only blocks enforcement of the Pregnant Workers Fairness Act in Texas, the opinions rationale would provide a basis for invalidating everything else in the omnibus bill, which included everything from the ban on using TikTok on government devices to the Electoral Count Reform and Presidential Transition Improvement Act.

I doubt this decision will stand. I am not sure the Quorum Clause is justiciable, and I cannot see the Supreme Court upending all of the affected legislation on this basis.

The petition for certiorari in this case writes itself. Another wild card is that the new Attorney General of Texas may not press its case.
