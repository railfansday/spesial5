How East Germany boosted Vietnam's coffee industry

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

When East Germany could no longer afford to import coffee, it introduced a substitute blend containing rye and chicory in 1977. A public outcry ensued. The solution to the problem lay 9,000 kilometers away — in Vietnam.

Coffee lovers, brace yourselves: Imagine taking your first sip, expecting the rich aroma of roasted coffee beans, only to discover that your cup contains a concoction made from ingredients you can barely identify.

That's exactly what millions of outraged East Germans experienced in 1977. But what led to this?

A poor harvest in Brazil saw global coffee prices skyrocketing, creating a major problem for East Germany. Unlike West Germany's Deutsche Mark, the East German Mark was a domestic currency with no value abroad.

Even under normal circumstances, the country constantly struggled to obtain the hard currency it needed to pay for imports. In 1974 and 1975, East Germany spent around 150 million Valutamarks — the term used in East Germany for the West German Deutsche Mark — on imported coffee. The following year, it had to pay almost five times as much.

However, this rate of inflation could not be passed on to consumers because, "In the GDR, there was the dogma of stable prices — set by the state through law," explains historian Stefan Wolle, a consultant at the DDR Museum in Berlin. "Prices were set by the Politburo, not by businesses. And they weren't allowed to change. What sounds wonderfully socially minded at first glance had serious negative economic consequences, because production costs were far higher than retail prices."

The government needed a solution. On August 1, 1977, it introduced "Kaffee Mix" as a substitute for popular coffee brands back then such as Kosta, Rondo and Mocca Fix. The fine print on the packaging promised "a blend of select green coffee beans and carefully balanced coffee substitutes."

In plain English, that meant 51% of the blend consisted of imported coffee beans. The rest was a mixture of rye and barley, along with chicory and either spelt or sugar beet. It tasted disgusting, Wolle recalls.

Even the coffee machines appeared to reject the concoction. The mixture expanded and clogged the filters in cafeterias and restaurants. Some machines were even said to have exploded.

Public discontent boiled over during this "coffee crisis." People responded by boycotting the product and with biting sarcasm. "The difference between a neutron bomb and coffee mix: There isn't one — the cup stays intact, but the person is destroyed.." A sharper dig? "It's pure rat poison."

More than 14,000 complaint letters poured into government offices. On September 1, the Ministry for State Security, better known as the Stasi, reported to the leadership of the ruling Socialist Unity Party of Germany or SED, that Kaffee Mix was being "rejected by broad sections of the population." (Editor's note: The SED was the only ruling party in East Germany then).

To understand the intensity of the backlash, it's important to recognize coffee's special place in East German society. "Coffee was, in a way, the social glue that held together East Germany's particular brand of conviviality and camaraderie," says Wolle. During the 1960s and 1970s, people would gather for coffee after lunch, often accompanied by homemade cakes or treats from the bakery. "It was a sacred hour in every workplace and office. It was a huge mistake for the SED leadership to slaughter this sacred cow, so to speak."

According to Wolle, real coffee was still occasionally available in the larger cities. But in smaller towns and rural areas, people often had no choice but to make do with Kaffee Mix. East Germans who had relatives or friends in West Germany and regularly received packages from them could count themselves lucky. "Our relatives and friends in the West knew what was in short supply in East Germany, so they would usually include a big bag of good West German coffee," Wolle recalls.

It is estimated that around 25% of all coffee consumed in East Germany came from the West. During the height of the coffee crisis, that share was briefly even higher. "Of course, the SED leadership had mixed feelings about people receiving so many packages from the West," Wolle says. "Police officers and full-time party officials weren't allowed to have contacts in the West. But ordinary citizens didn't care." Especially after the blend stirred up such negative reactions.

East Germany's leaders responded by looking to their socialist allies in Africa for help. They negotiated special agreements with Angola and Ethiopia. "Instead of paying in hard currency, they paid with military equipment — weapons and trucks," Wolle explains. "Weapons that were used in Ethiopia's civil war."

But the East German leadership sought a long-term solution, and eventually established a partnership with another socialist ally: Vietnam. "Coffee had already been grown there under French colonial rule," says Wolle. "People knew that coffee grew well there and that the quality was good."

Starting in the 1980s, East Germany invested heavily in developing the infrastructure needed for large-scale coffee cultivation in Vietnam, Wolle explains. It supplied machinery and equipment, built transportation routes, and provided housing for plantation workers. "There was just one catch: Coffee plants take several years before they can be harvested."

By the time the plants were finally ready to produce coffee, the Berlin Wall had fallen and East Germany was a thing of the past. "But the Vietnamese benefited enormously," says Wolle. "They converted the plantations into private enterprises fairly quickly." Today, Vietnam is one of the world's largest coffee exporters. "What's surprising is that most people don't even know that."

There’s no mention of it on the packages — and if there is, it’s in microscopic print. "The Vietnamese don’t make a big deal out of their role because they themselves probably still believe that coffee has to come from Brazil — or at least from Latin America — to be any good," says Wolle.

Passionate coffee drinkers may not care where the hot beverage comes from — as long as it doesn't taste like spelt and sugar beets, as the much-maligned East German Kaffee Mix once did.
