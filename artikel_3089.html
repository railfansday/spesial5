Defense Stocks Fell After the Midterms. Here’s the 1 Defense Name I’d Buy Anyway -- and Why It Isn’t Lockheed Martin.

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

RTX is positioned to do well regardless of the political climate.

By James Halley – Nov 21, 2026 at 5:05PM EST

The midterm election results sent a shudder through the defense industry.

President Donald Trump is proposing a $1.5 trillion defense budget for 2027, but time is running out to pass the budget in a lame-duck Congress. Next year's Congress is likely to take a more critical look at defense spending.

RTX (RTX -1.32%) is one of only two major U.S.-based defense stocks whose shares have risen since the midterm elections. There are four reasons why RTX is the one defense stock I'd buy over Lockheed Martin (LMT -1.11%).

Unlike many of its peers, the company is positioned to continue doing well because its revenue streams also include sales of parts and equipment to commercial airlines, providing a cushion if next year's Congress slashes the defense budget.

RTX has three segments, but only one of them is a pure-play defense segment: Raytheon, which makes air and missile defense systems. The other two segments, Pratt & Whitney and Collins Aerospace, make defense and commercial airline products. Pratt & Whitney makes aircraft engines for civilian and military aircraft, while its Collins Aerospace segment makes aircraft systems, including avionics, navigation systems, and flight controls for commercial and defense aircraft.

Lockheed Martin's shares, on the other hand, are down more than 3% since the elections because it has little commercial sales, with more than 69% of its revenue coming from the U.S. government.

RTX reported double-digit revenue and adjusted earnings per share (EPS) growth in the third quarter. Revenue was reported as $25 billion, up 11% year over year, and adjusted EPS grew by 33% over the prior year to $2.26. The company also raised full-year guidance. It says it expects 2026 sales of $97 billion to $97.5 billion, up from earlier estimates of between $95 billion and $96 billion. It also raised adjusted EPS from $7.10 to $7.25, up to $7.65–$7.75.

Adjusted segment operating margins expanded by 310 basis points in the third quarter, and because aircraft engine maintenance and spare parts carry higher profit margins than initial equipment sales, RTX's installed base of commercial engines and avionics acts as a compounding cash-flow machine independent of Capitol Hill.

Pratt & Whitney's commercial aftermarket sales jumped 21% year over year, while Collins Aerospace's adjusted operating profit surged 50%.

Lockheed Martin also had a strong quarter, with revenue rising 11% year over year, to $20.7 billion and EPS of $7.97, up 14% from the same period last year. However, because so much of its revenue lately is tied to Iran war spending, that could slow at any time.

RTX reported a backlog of $340 billion in the third quarter, including $164 billion in defense sales and $176 billion in commercial sales. That provides a great deal of stability for investors looking at the company's long-term potential, as it represents three years' worth of revenue.

Just in the third quarter, Raytheon booked nearly $59 billion in defense awards, including production contracts for Tomahawk missiles and air-to-air missiles. Even with Democrats soon to control Congress, global demand for air defense and munitions makes these multiyear contracts highly resilient.

Lockheed Martin's backlog is smaller at $292 billion, and all of that is tied to defense contracts.

The company raised its quarterly dividend by 7.4% this year to $0.73 per share, the 32nd consecutive year it has increased its dividend. The dividend yield is an above-average 1.42%, and while that's lower than Lockheed Martin's 2.39% yield, RTX's streak of raising dividends is eight years longer.

In addition, over the past three years, RTX has increased its dividend by more than 23%, while Lockheed has raised its dividend by just over 9%.

RTX stands out as a stronger buy than Lockheed Martin right now largely because its balanced business model insulates it from post-midterm political turbulence on Capitol Hill.

RTX's greater commercial footprint not only provides a steady buffer against congressional scrutiny, but also fuels a high-margin aftermarket engine and spare-parts business that acts as a compounding cash-flow engine independent of Pentagon appropriations.
