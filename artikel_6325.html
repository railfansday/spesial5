Earth’s magnetic poles flipped repeatedly before an ancient mass extinction

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Ancient rocks in southern China reveal that Earth’s magnetic field reversed roughly every 90,000 years before a Permian mass extinction.

Earth’s magnetic field may seem like a permanent fixture, but its north and south poles have repeatedly switched places.

About 261 million years ago it was swapping them roughly 11 times every million years, one of the fastest rates ever recorded.

The burst of flipping ran right up to one of the planet’s major mass extinctions. The geologists who measured it think the two may be connected, though they can’t yet prove it.

Min Zhang led the work with Yongxin Pan, both at the Institute of Geology and Geophysics of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, and Shu-zhong Shen of Nanjing University.

Colleagues at Utrecht University and BOKU University in Vienna took part.

Earth’s field comes from liquid iron churning in the outer core. Heat leaving the core drives that churning, so when the pattern of heat loss changes, the field changes too.

One of the ways the magnetic field changes is by flipping, north becoming south.

For about 50 million years, from roughly 318 to 265 million years ago, the field didn’t flip at all. Geologists call that stretch the Kiaman Superchron, and it is the longest run of stable polarity known.

Then the stable stretch ended, and what came next had been only patchily measured.

Few places kept laying down sediment while sea levels fell worldwide, so the rock layers that would record the field are missing in most places.

The Fengshan section, a limestone outcrop near Liuzhou in southern China, is one of the few places where those years survive without a gap.

The limestone formed on a shallow seafloor close to the equator. Its fossils are rich enough that geologists use it as a reference point for one of the Permian’s boundaries.

As the limestone formed, grains of the iron mineral hematite inside it lined up with the magnetic field of the day and then locked in place.

Zhang’s team collected 876 oriented samples from two parts of the outcrop and heated each one in steps up to 1,274°F (690°C). Heating removes the magnetic signals the rock picked up later and leaves the original one.

Only 309 of the 876 samples, about a third, gave a clean reading. The other 567 were too scrambled to use.

A flip counted only when at least three clean samples in a row showed the same new polarity.

Stacked together, the two parts of the outcrop record 29 stretches of one polarity or the other across 3.75 million years, from about 262.5 to 258.75 million years ago.

The flips were not spread evenly. In the first 750,000 years the field reversed fewer than five times per million years.

The researchers identified a period of unusually frequent reversals before the end-Guadalupian extinction, with an estimated rate of about 11 per million years.

Eleven reversals per million years is a flip roughly every 90,000 years, on a planet that had just gone 50 million years without one.

After the extinction the field flipped less often, with only three more polarity stretches in the rest of the record.

The extinction itself, about 261 million years ago, is the smaller of the Permian’s two great mass extinctions, but reef ecosystems collapsed and marine life was decimated.

The usual explanation is a huge outpouring of lava known as the Emeishan large igneous province, plus falling seas and lost habitat as Pangea finished assembling.

Why would the field go from 50 million years of stability to flipping every 90,000 years?

As the continents came together to form Pangea, slabs of old ocean floor were pushed down into the mantle.

Such slabs can take several tens of millions of years to sink to the base of the mantle.

By the late middle Permian, the authors suggested, slabs from Pangea’s assembly may have arrived there.

A cold slab reaching the bottom of the mantle would draw extra heat out of the core beneath it. The core would then lose heat faster in some places than in others.

Computer simulations of the core show that uneven heat loss like that makes the field flip more often.

The authors were careful about those simulations. All of them run under conditions far from the real Earth’s, they wrote, and their results depend heavily on the settings chosen.

The field matters to life because it deflects most of the charged particles streaming in from the Sun and deep space. A field that flips often may also be weaker.

Earlier studies did estimate the field’s strength through the Permian, and those estimates fall from the middle of the period to its end.

The researchers noted that they vary a lot in quality, though.

With a weaker field, more of those particles would reach the upper atmosphere.

The authors laid out a series of possible effects from there: ozone destroyed, oxygen lost to space, and more ultraviolet light reaching the ground.

Malformed pollen and plants with extra UV-protective compounds have been found at the end-Permian extinction, about nine million years later.

For the end-Guadalupian event, the team wrote, direct evidence of that kind is still limited.

Volcanic eruptions are a competing explanation for any ozone loss, because the lava outpourings may have released halogens that destroy ozone.

A weaker field would allow more charged particles to reach the upper atmosphere, but frequent reversals alone do not establish how weak it became.

One gap is the field’s strength. A weak field, not a flipping one, is what would let radiation through, and the authors wrote that better strength records across this same interval are needed.

Tighter dates and computer models that connect the core to the atmosphere are the other two items on their list.

For now, the Fengshan limestone records unusually frequent magnetic reversals before a major mass extinction, while leaving their possible connection unresolved.

The full study was published in the journal National Science Review.
