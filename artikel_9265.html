The Associated Press is Moving Eight US Photo Editor Jobs to India

The Associated Press is moving eight photo editor positions to India today, in a move condemned by unions.

The roles are currently carried out in the United States, but today is their last day. The offshoring follows similar moves this summer, when AP laid off 20 video production jobs in the U.S. and moved them to its hub in Noida, India.

“AP is at it again,” the AP News Guild wrote on X last week. “The News Media Guild learned Monday that AP will offshore eight photo positions after already gutting its U.S. video production team.”

In a statement, the News Media Guild president Kimberlee Krusei called AP’s move “union busting.”

“Fewer people, fewer communities being covered, and less news. That’s the impact of today’s decision,” says Krusei. “AP’s newsgathering has gone beyond ‘lean’ to leaving many coverage areas abandoned. The remaining journalists are asked to do the impossible and it shows.”

The Guild says that far from just picking photos taken by AP photographers, the editors provide direction, guide the photographers, correct essential information, and scrutinize images for alterations.

“They bring decades of local knowledge, news judgment and connections. They negotiate with outside photographers to buy the rights to photos — a now essential part of covering breaking news.”

One of the affected photo editors, Pablo Salinas, who is based in New York, says that he went above and beyond in 2019 when he obtained the rights to a shocking photo of a drowned father and daughter lying face down in the Rio Grande River.

“That was a picture that I don’t think any other photo editor would have been able to get,” says Salinas. “Because it wasn’t ours, I scoured social media and found it and got in touch with the photographer. And she reluctantly gave us permission to use it.”

The photo went viral and became part of the story about the border crisis.

“AP benefits from having photo editors that are local to where the news is being produced,” says AP photographer Matt Rourke.

“It’s a partnership,” adds Salinas. “The kind of partnership you lose when you sacrifice these jobs to save a few dollars.”

Photo editors are intimately involved in the biggest stories of the day. They might be working closely with local photographers to move hundreds of shots illustrating important election results. Or securing the rights to sensitive images that convey difficult news. An AP spokesperson tells TheWrap that the agency is respecting and following the collective bargaining agreement.

“This is part of the changes we announced over the summer to significantly scale our visual production capabilities at our new hub in Noida, India, where we have added dozens of talented journalists to our global news organization,” the spokesperson says.

The AP says that its hub in Noida will soon employ nearly 100 video producers and photo editors.
