Your own bank takes cash free on a personal account, so use its branch or one of its ATMs that accept deposits. If your bank is online-only, hand the cash to a partner store’s cashier, often for a few dollars, or deposit it at a local bank or credit union and transfer it to your online account.

Paying a few dollars to deposit your own cash adds up fast when tips or cash sales come in every week. Where you make the deposit decides whether you pay anything at all.

Your bank’s own ATMs also let you avoid ATM fees when you take cash out. If your credit union belongs to the Co-op Shared Branch network, other credit unions in it will take your deposit too, at more than 5,500 locations across the country.

Large deposits come with a federal rule. Your bank or credit union must report cash deposits of more than $10,000 in a day to the Treasury’s Financial Crimes Enforcement Network, whatever the reason for them. It will ask for your ID and Social Security number to file that report. Depositing that much is allowed, but splitting it into smaller deposits to avoid the report is a crime called structuring, even when the cash came to you legally.

Many online banks and apps take cash at a store’s register, and the store may charge for it. With the Chime® app, cash deposits are free at Walgreens and Duane Reade, but other stores can charge up to $4.95 each time1. Capital One takes cash free at partner stores such as CVS and Walgreens for its checking and savings customers.

The free workaround needs a second account. Picture a server who deposits her tips at her credit union’s ATM every week, then moves what she’s saving to an online savings account. At a store charging $4.95, those 52 deposits would cost her about $257 a year instead of nothing.

If cash comes in every week, open a no-fee checking account at a credit union or bank near work and link it to your online bank.
