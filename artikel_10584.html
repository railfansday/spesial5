I completed GTA 6's indie game darling pastiche, Tenebrae Lux, and all I got was this lousy sense of accomplishment

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

"We are but Promethean wanderers on the fringes of oblivion, clinging to a semblance of light amidst the vast abyss of our own ignorance. Voyaging uncharted waters may appear foolish to some, yet folly and heroism can often appear the same to the undiscerning eye," so says the voice of Johannes Zuch - creator, writer, director, composer, and 'perceptual architect' behind Leonida's latest favourite indie darling art game, Tenebrae Lux. It's pretentious, it's deeply up its own arse, and it's awkward and off-putting to play. A must-review for Eurogamer.

Developed, seemingly single-handedly, by an eccentric developer called Johannes Zuch - auteur, pseud, charlatan - you first get introduced to this curious little experiment in one of GTA 6's earliest missions, where protagonist Jason is forced to play it during a 'lad's night in' with buddy Cal. Jason is quick to dismiss Tenebrae Lux in company, but once Cal slinks off home - hungover and dreary - I slide the disc back into my JoyStation console and sink my teeth back into it. Jason loves this stuff, really. He only keeps Time Jackers: Rewind installed so his mates don't judge him.

The first thing I notice about Tenebrae Lux is the name. It's Latin for 'darkness and light', or something similar. Is this riffing on Clair Obscur, which is French for 'light dark'? No, it's too cerebral for that - too plodding and navel-gazey. Sandfall Interactive wouldn't be this pretentious. Maybe it's a pastiche of Dear Esther, then, drawing on the tenets and schema of the 'walking simulator' genre and using that washed-out film noir aesthetic. But then, it's too wordy for that, with too many interactive and puzzle-adjacent elements. Ah, it must be mocking The Witness, instead - there's the telltale lighthouse, the 'I'm definitely not telling you where to go and what to do' internal narration, and of course the fingerprints of Johannes Zuch are all over it. Johannes Zuch, Jonathan Blow - see how they braided them together? (Sorry).

Whilst this has been created by Rockstar as a pastiche - as much loving send-up as it is withering take down - and designed to make a quick joke in the early 'Feng Shui' mission, the game is playable from start to finish if you so desire. You boot it up by inserting a physical disc, too, which is funny given the context. And it's good! Well, not good good, but it's a proper brainteaser, and ribs 2016's The Witness with such knowledge and (I think?) respect that it's hard not to be charmed by Rockstar's game-within-a-game. There are collectibles to be found that riff on Mark Z. Danielewski's House of Leaves, too, a knowing nod to the most try-hard of all try-hard literature.

Within seconds of the title screen, too, you're presented with a game-within-a-game-within-a-game. You must clear four boards filled with puzzle pieces as the protagonist mutters something mysterious and weird in his posh voice. Do so, and you unlock the door to the next location. Head up to a bunker, and you'll see a room filled with various clues - maybe it's a flashing lamp flickering in morse code, maybe it's a malfunctioning clock presenting a maths puzzle, maybe it's a pricetag on a record that changes on every visit, maybe it's a photograph with anomalous elements, maybe it's a "Developed Using Zuchworks Engine" easter egg hidden in the environment. You need to keep your wits about you, because one of these clues changes every time you enter - and the clue corresponds to a date that changes on a calendar on the wall.

You can look up a guide if you need to, or you can spend far too long figuring out how all this relates to the telephone you can find in the room on your own, like muggins here, before opening up the path to the next puzzle. Look at the image below to see the level of record-keeping and brainteasing this bizarre little game prompted from me. I will say, the sense of achievement you get from playing around with this stuff is pretty remarkable, given - let's not forget I'm actually playing Grand Theft Auto 6 whilst I'm wandering around in the black and white murk trying to complete a puzzle game for no actual reward.

The bunker room is, by far, the hardest part of Tenebrae Lux. From here, things ease up: you need to figure out the right sequence of glyphs on a bridge, and then put some distance between yourself and a curious and rather creepy suited man that's seemingly figuring things out ahead of you in a location inspired by, I think, The Backrooms (or Portal, or Superliminal, or maybe something else entirely. Who knows what Zuch was thinking?)

"With the last enigma finally mastered," the narrator drones in his bumbling monotone, "I step forward into the void, the shadows swallow, consuming all, and then finally the sweet embrace of darkness of which there is no end." 'You have just experienced a Johannes Zuch experience', Tenebrae Lux tells me, before cutting to the best joke of the whole thing: the credits roll much akin to something Hideo Kojima would produce.

So, what do you get out of all of this? Well - just like real puzzle games in the real world - nothing, really. If you've not completed one of the other three fully playable games in GTA 6, you will get a trophy/achievement. But given the main game is all about Jason and Lucia's desperate scramble to escape the poverty trap, to try and break free of the cycle of debt, violence, and consequence that keeps them clinging to Leonida's seedy underbelly, there's no monetary reward. There's no 'progress' in the sense of the protagonist's shared story.

Even in terms of your own cold, hard, mathematical 'completion' you only need to boot each of the four games up once in order to work towards GTA 6's 100% completion. So what's the point of all this? Why did I spend the better part of 90 minutes trying to figure out how to find a sliver of light in the dark in Tenebrae Lux? The game is a wonderful pastiche in that, essentially, it's not really saying anything. You can probably project some vague meaning onto it if you wish - I'm pretty certain the protagonist literally 'finds himself' at the end - but glib little readings like that are ten-a-penny in this genre.

I think the most impressive thing about Tenebrae Lux is that it exists. You're teased with it in an early mission - Rockstar may as well say 'look, we put a game in a game, lol' - but it's more than that. This is what happens when Rockstar's 4700-plus developers are given the room to… develop. Tenebrae Lux could very easily have been a non-interactive experience, a quick, cheap joke shown over-the-shoulder taking a drive-by pop shot at Johnathan Blow, Hideo Kojima, Dan Pinchbeck, or any and all of the above. Or maybe it could be a 'vertical slice', an incomplete demo, a teaser. But it's not, because nothing about GTA 6 is quick or cheap.

GTA 6, in every single conceivable way, is a premium experience - part-sandbox, part-simulation, part-otherworld. To create not just one, but four fully playable and completable games within that framework is showboating, sure, but to make that game actually good and - perhaps controversially - even better than some of the games it's pastiching… Well, it's just rubbing salt in the wound, really.
