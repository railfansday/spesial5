Am I Done Here? — John Muir Trail Day 6

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

The night before our last hike to Sam’s car at Onion Valley I awoke and recorded the following.  It is only lightly edited:

Some thoughts in the middle of the night. It’s one a.m.  In a few hours we’ll be going over Kearsarge Pass. That’s not a walk in the park.

It’s over 2,000 feet of elevation gain. Maybe around 10 miles of hiking and then going off trail. I have to think this is probably it for me.

I am exhausted. I have been coughing and blowing my nose for six days, and for six weeks before these six days. I’m just running out of juice.

I cannot imagine coming back on trail until I’ve gone at least 24 hours without coughing once, without blowing my nose once.  And I don’t know when that will happen because it feels like it’ll never happen.  I mean, six weeks?  This thing’s been going on and on and on.

Thank God I’ve had the energy to get up and over these passes at all, but I have.  And I appreciate Sam so much.  He’s been such a great companion, so positive, so upbeat, so helpful in every way.

I really don’t think I could have made it without him. I don’t know what I would  have done, but I’ve had enough.  I just don’t see how I can come back out here without serious health improvement.

So, I have to figure out what to do.  I don’t think I’ll even talk to Sam about this until after we go over the top of Kearsarge Pass.  When we’re going downhill, I will be confident that I’ll actually make it out of here.

But no, I’m not really worried about not getting out of here, I just need time to ponder what to do.  I’d go with Sam to the airport in Las Vegas and fly home, but I don’t have a passport.  It’s with son Gabriel in Santa Rosa, California.

And my driver’s license is not a RealID driver’s license.  So, I’m hosed, I can’t fly, but that’s fine.  I could take a bus, whatever, I can figure that out.

I just gotta get better and then reappraise.  You know, if I were 30 years old, I’d say, screw it, I’ll just come back next year and try again, no biggie. But I’m not 30, I’m 69.

I’m in remission from cancer.  No one says I’m cured, but of course I hope it’s a long-term remission, naturally.

I’ve got a son with recurrent colon cancer, that came back less than two months after he finished his chemotherapy treatment.  That’s shocking and scary.

I’ve got a wife who went in for a routine appendectomy, they took out a healthy appendix, and now she is suffering.  I don’t really know what is going on there, because she can’t or won’t tell me the details.

And I’ve got to wrap up a whole bunch of different things, finish my taxes, finish my dead mother’s taxes, deal with some other stuff in my life.

The bottom line is six days of coughing and coughing, six days of blowing my nose, six days of giant, ugly, sticky mucus wads coming out of my nose and out of my chest when I cough; it’s just… it’s exhausting.  I can’t believe I made it up Mount Whitney and over Forester Pass.  It’s just astounding that I did those things given the condition I was in.

But I’ve had enough. I can’t just turn around and go back on trail.  Not the way I’m feeling.

After making that recording, I was able to get a little sleep despite peeing several more times in the night and still coughing.

The morning was cold and I said to Sam, “Let’s hold off on leaving until later,  when it’s a little bit warmer.”  That plan turned out to be necessary because our gear was pretty wet from the rain.

Sam at our camp site; you can tell it was cold by his outfit.

So we took a fairly leisurely time breaking camp, digging our cat holes, and eating breakfast, but also drying out my groundsheet which lay on a pretty wet spot overnight.

I realized in the middle of the night, checking my FarOut app, that the first mile and a half of hiking was going to entail about a thousand feet of elevation gain.  It is always better to be prepared than to be surprised by these things.  As we headed out, we were ready to sweat right away.

It was hard, but not impossibly hard, not terribly hard.  We got up those thousand feet and then the trail toward Bullfrog Lake was a series of gentle ups and downs.   It was so beautiful.  I mean, the views were stunningly, jaw-droppingly, beautiful.

At one point we saw a couple of bucks browsing by the trail.  Our presence didn’t seem to bother them too much. They didn’t run away; they kept browsing.

Bucks browsing — they kind of blend in, don’t they?

We also saw a hand-written sign warning of bears in the vicinity.

I especially like the part about “chase it like you are trying to catch and eat it!”

We continued on.  When we got to Bullfrog Lake, we actually went for a swim.

Bullfrog Lake (I think, there were a series of lakes up there…)

It was funny, because when Sam proposed the swim, I said, “Don’t you think I would have to be crazy to go for a swim in a cold mountain lake with a bad cough?”

He replied, “Oh, you mean not something sensible like climbing Mount Whitney with a cough, right?”

I had to admit; he had a point.  I stripped down and went in.

The swim actually felt really good, really fresh.  I just put on my water shoes, swam 10 yards out, swam 10 yards back, and got out.  It was a nice sunny day, so drying out was very fast, very easy.

While at Bullfrog Lake we filtered water and loaded up for the hot hike up to the pass.

We ate our lunch in that spot and then continued hiking up and up and up.

At no point was the elevation gain so difficult that I felt overwhelmed.  I just used my usual technique.  I’d count 60 paces while climbing, or I’d count 30 if it was very steep, and then I’d stop to take a series of deep breaths to slow my heartbeat and catch my breath.  But I was still coughing, still honking up a bunch of nasty mucus.

The views ascending made the climb easier…

Eventually we made it to Kearsarge Pass.  Along with a bunch of other folks going in both directions, we celebrated our accomplishment.  The views from there were glorious.  Looking across at the teeth of the mountains, the raw majesty of it was wonderful.  Those “teeth” are depicted in the feature image at the top of this post.  Even the pass itself was special.

The rocks in the pass don’t look very stable, do they?

It was four miles straight down, which meant four miles straight up if I were to return to trail.  I wondered whether I would.  We passed some beautiful lakes, such as Gilbert Lake, on the way down.  The trail also passed through a vast tumble of giant boulders.

Gilbert Lake is popular because they say it is full of fish.

As we emerged from the boulders, we finally could see the road down below, and the car park where Sam’s rental was parked.  It seemed really close when I first saw the car park.  I thought, “Oh, we’re going to be there in 15 minutes.”

See the road down there? With Independence, CA behind it.

The visual distance was deceptive; it was more like an hour before we got there.  But eventually we got to the car park.  The car was fine, everything there was fine.  Arriving was a huge relief.

As we started driving down the hillside, I told Sam, “Look, I really feel like I need to have 24 hours of no cough and no nasty mucus before I go back on this trail, because I just can’t do it.”  Fighting my health and trying to struggle up mountain passes at the same time was just too hard for me.

Repeatedly over two days on the trail we tried to book a cabin at Lauralyn’s Williamson Motel by texting her husband Doug from my Garmin.  We could not get through and were very frustrated by it.  When we drove up to the motel in Independence, I learned we had the wrong number.  It started with 760, not the 750 I was given.  To be clear, I got it second-hand, not from Lauralyn herself, so I am not suggesting we were deliberately misled.  In any case, there was “no room at the inn.”

Doug was nice enough and answered my questions.  In particular, I wanted to know where to go for medical treatment.  The South Inyo Hospital was in Lone Pine and North Inyo Hospital was in Bishop.  Doug suggested that Bishop would have a little more to offer, especially if I needed a pharmacy.  So even though it was a longer drive, we decided to go north.

The other advantage to going north was a barbeque joint Doug recommended.  It was the Copper Top BBQ in Big Pine, halfway to Bishop.  I was already salivating.

Sam spoke with his wife Mary while I was sorting this out and then I called Lee as we drove north.  I found out she had the same respiratory infection that was afflicting me.   It seemed to be affecting her even worse, mostly because of her appendectomy.  Every time she coughed it pulled at the healing adhesions within her abdomen.  So, it was really causing her a lot of pain and difficulty, and she was on a lot of medications.  Despite this, she said she was all right if I chose to stay on the trail.  She even encouraged me.

As I spoke to her, I actually shed some tears when I expressed my gratitude to Sam for his help and support.

Then we got to the Copper Top BBQ.  Oh my God.  There was a menu item that included two of everything, everything doubled.  I mean, tri-tip, pulled pork, chicken sausage, ribs, and everything!  I imagine a lot of people take some home.  Sam and I finished the whole damn thing.  It was so good.  The barbecue was great; the coleslaw was excellent; they had a chili bean dish that was fantastic.

We had the Big Pine Mega Platter (2 Person) — soooo good!

Then we made our way to Bishop.  Doug suggested either the Best Western or the Motel 6.   They were next door to each other, so we pulled into the Best Western. The woman behind the desk was on the phone; she wouldn’t even make eye contact.  We waited and waited and waited.  She was still talking, still not looking at us.  Then we heard her say, “Yeah, okay, you have a room for today for $250.”

We were like, what the hell?  No, not in Bishop, California.  So, we left Ms. No-Eye-Contact to go next door to the Motel 6.  The price was about a hundred bucks less.  We got our rooms for the night.

Even after the barbeque, we went next door for milkshakes before calling it a night.  I planned to go to North Inyo Hospital the next morning.

I actually took a bath.  In undressing I discovered my shorts had an embarrassing  tear in them in the front.  Apparently, nobody noticed, or else they were too polite to say anything.  Then I got in the tub and soaked in water that started clean but rapidly began to look like a mudbath.  I even had to shower off when I got out.

The first night off the trail in Bishop, CA I awoke and recorded the following in my hotel room.  It is only lightly edited:

Here it is now the middle of the night.  It’s actually 3:45 in the morning.  I am still coughing.  I still have mucus.

Maybe it is a little less?  Still, it does not start a 24-hour clock without symptoms.

I will go to the hospital tomorrow.  I guess it has to be the Emergency Room.  Have them look at me and assess the situation.  I am guessing the answer might be, “Well, you’ve got a bad viral infection, and you just have to wait it out.”

If that’s the case, maybe one more chance would be to try to see if there is much more improvement by tomorrow night?  If it’s clear then, maybe I can get back on the trail day after tomorrow?  Sam could drop me off before heading to Las Vegas, or I could spend another zero day, probably in Independence?

It’s not clear cut.  It really is not clear cut.  I don’t know.

I’m very confused.  I feel somewhat drained.  You know, when you embark on one of these journeys, you want to have energy and enthusiasm.  And as long as I’m still coughing and honking, I’m not sure I can conjure that up.  We will see.

Plus, I haven’t even opened my resupply box retrieved from Sam’s car.  I thought of it as too daunting.

I haven’t even thrown out my trash from the first leg of our journey.  So there are a lot of details to pick up here as well.  And I don’t even know what time the checkout is in the morning.

A lot of things to worry about.  At least I am charging my electronics.  That is great.  Now, let me go back to sleep.
