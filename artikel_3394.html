The Best Miniseries of the 2010s, Ranked Year by Year

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Serving as both the definitive peak of television’s golden era and the decade that saw the rampant rise in popularity of streaming services producing new shows, it should come as no surprise that the 2010s have an abundance of excellent miniseries. In fact, such is the quality of the miniseries of the decade; each year from 2010 to 2019 has an array of enthralling and memorable titles, with masterpieces like When They See Us, The Haunting of Hill House, and Unbelievable missing out on selection.

The shows that have made the cut consist of everything from moody historical dramas to pulsating explorations of war, and even to cutting crime thrillers that keep audiences on the edge of their seats from start to finish. Defined by their sharp writing, brilliant production value, and the enduring impact their stories have had on viewers, these miniseries represent television at its best throughout the 2010s.

A spiritual sequel to their 2001 masterpiece Band of Brothers, The Pacific sees Steven Spielberg and Tom Hanks again taking small-screen drama to the front lines of WWII with an immersion into the Pacific Theater of the war that is as grueling as it is gripping. Following the experiences of three American soldiers from different regiments, the miniseries examines the chaos and carnage of the campaign, depicting battles such as Iwo Jima, Okinawa, and Guadalcanal.

Earning universal acclaim for its visceral authenticity, The Pacific is a brutal masterpiece of war drama that soars on the back of its enormous production and the impact of its performances. Enriched by its endeavor to go beyond the ordeal of battle and explore the impact the war had on soldiers returning home, The Pacific is a contemplative and considered examination of those involved in the war. This breathtaking miniseries stands as yet another classic foray into the genre from Hanks and Spielberg.

At face value, 2011 was one of the weaker years of the decade for miniseries magnificence. However, it still contains some underappreciated gems, with Mildred Pierce serving as an overlooked drama defined by its adherence to its source material and a litany of powerful performances. The HBO-produced miniseries stars Kate Winslet as Mildred Pierce, a self-sacrificing single mother during the Great Depression who faces hardship as she tries to open a restaurant while striving to win the love and respect of her narcissistic eldest daughter.

Soaring off the back of Winslet’s Emmy Award-winning performance, Mildred Pierce finds an understated impact as a heartbreaking drama of love and desperation that is beautifully acted and delicately written. While some may argue its five-episode run is extensive and results in a number of lulls throughout the miniseries, Mildred Pierce emerges triumphant as one of 2011’s best small-screen offerings. This period piece immerses audiences in the rigors of Depression-era America and the many obstacles a single mother faced in such a time.

A three-part romantic drama exploring the impact the AIDS epidemic of the 1980s had on Stockholm’s gay community, Don’t Ever Wipe Tears Without Gloves excels with its artful presentation and delicate handling of its characters and their turmoil. Over the miniseries' three episodes, titled “Love,” “Diseases,” and “Death,” the miniseries follows 19-year-old Rasmus (Adam Pålsson) as he seeks out Stockholm’s gay community and begins a relationship with Benjamin (Adam Lundgren) after moving to the city from his rural home to attend university.

The series’ progression, including flashbacks to Rasmus’ childhood and flashforwards to his agonizing final days, is handled respectfully. The story is realized with a quiet visual beauty and powerful use of symbolism that both support the central story of Rasmus and Ben coming to terms with a damning AIDS diagnosis and illustrate the widespread fear and ignorance surrounding the disease at the time. Also incorporating how religious institutions responded to the disease and those suffering from it, Don’t Ever Wipe Tears Without Gloves is a powerful and profoundly human examination of a dark chapter in history marred by speculative terror and homophobia.

Another three-part miniseries from Europe, Generation War is a German WWII drama following the isolated experiences of five friends throughout the conflict. A tale of disillusionment, desperation, and violence, its focus on two soldiers, a nurse, an aspiring singer, and a Jewish man trying to flee the country is bolstered by its analysis of the corruption of totalitarianism. It doesn’t shirk the fact that a lot of Germans were in favor of Adolf Hitler’s vision of the world, only to be confronted with the hardship of surviving it.

While it has flaws, Generation War is defined by its striking maturity and ability to find sympathy in its main characters through their youthful naivety and brutal disenchantment. Its inherent appeals for forgiveness for those who felt helplessly complicit in the Nazi agenda may be misguided at times. However, the series showcases in unflinching fashion the harsh reality of war and the punishing final truth that, even for those who emerged from the conflict alive, there was little joy and no glory awaiting them at the end of the war.

Starring Frances McDormand and featuring an all-star supporting cast that includes Richard Jenkins, Jesse Plemons, Rachel Brosnahan, and Bill Murray, among others, Olive Kitteridge is a sorrowful, sweeping drama firmly focused on human connection. Based on Elizabeth Strout’s 2008 novel of the same name, the four-episode miniseries follows the titular Kitteridge, a retired schoolteacher with a misanthropic edge and underlying depression, and her strained familial relationships as they unfold over 25 years.

The narrative runs at a slow and measured pace. In this meticulous tempo, the performances of the actors and the impact of the writing flourish, delivering a grounded drama that makes an impression through its sincerity and tenderness. At once bleak and uplifting, Olive Kitteridge is always compelling, delving into the minutiae of life’s complexity with an enticing character who, while sympathetic, is often the source of her problems.

Adapted from Agatha Christie’s novel of the same name, And Then There Were None is something of an underrated gem of 2010s mystery television. Constantly intriguing with its notions of justice and punishment, it follows eight individuals as they converge on the isolated Soldier Island by invitation of a “Mr. and Mrs. Owen,” where they are seen by the island house’s caretakers. When one of the guests is killed, the others learn that they are ensnared in an elaborate ruse: each of them is responsible for an unjust death, and the killer—who is among them—has no intention of stopping until all of them are dead.

As dashing as it is dark, the miniseries thrives on the talent of its ensemble cast and the misanthropic lens with which it views its characters. Loyal in plot and spirit to Christie’s 1939 novel, And Then There Were None offers intoxicating suspense and intrigue as it marries its psychological murder-mystery antics with an impressive rogues' gallery of amoral and corrupt characters.

An eight-part miniseries defined by its constant and weighted sense of tension and the impact of its performances, The Night Of is one of HBO’s defining triumphs in the wheelhouse of limited series entertainment. It follows Nasir (Riz Ahmed), an American-Pakistani college student accused of murdering a girl he hooked up with at a party. Amid the apparent hopelessness of his case, Nasir forms a bond with an inmate at Rikers Island, while public defender John Stone (John Turturro) represents "Naz", believing there is more to the murder case.

Thriving not only as a gripping murder mystery, but as a dramatic observation of the legal system rife with political overtones and a commentary on racism in America as well, The Night Of excels through its timely notes of social criticism as much as it does through its captivating suspense. It's further enriched by a detailed mystery that methodically unfolds and Nasir’s surprising coming-of-age story that unfurls behind bars. The miniseries is loaded with intrigue and interest that make it one of the more noteworthy efforts of the decade.

One of the best Western stories to have graced the screen in recent years, Godless excels as a typically violent and vivacious take on the genre that challenges its male-dominated history with a brilliantly conceived story that allows for a mostly female cast. The town of La Belle, populated almost exclusively by women after a mining accident killed most of the men, becomes the center of an outlaw’s vengeance when his prodigy, Roy Goode (Jack O’Connell), seeks refuge, having split off from his gang with a stash of money.

The story takes a keen interest not only in Goode’s relationship with widowed rancher Alice Fletcher (Michelle Dockery), but also in several other figures within the town and the unique struggles they endure. Netflix's Godless is defined by its scope and ambition as well as its smart subversion of Western conventions. A confident piece of genre deconstruction that is often pulsating and quite tender when it needs to be, the Netflix original series tackles femininity in the Old West with compelling grit and refreshing insight to be a true highlight of 2010s television.

One of the most shocking series of any genre of the 2010s, Sharp Objects finds a compelling depth and striking unpredictability in its basis on Gillian Flynn’s debut novel of the same name. Amy Adams stars as Camille Preaker, a crime reporter who, after being released from a psychiatric hospital after battling alcoholism and depression, returns to her hometown to investigate the murders of two young girls. The disturbing nature of the case clashes with Camille’s strained relationships with her family, forcing her to confront her demons.

Bolstered by an array of exceptional performances, Sharp Objects is a true slow-burn tale that extracts every ounce of tension and drama from its story, entrancing viewers in the immersive allure of its bleak atmosphere rife with moments of agonizing suspense. Bereft of cat-and-mouse chaos or the tantalizing menace of a criminal mastermind, the series instead demands a typically brilliant turn from Adams to explore the full breadth of Camille’s internal struggles with deft drama and nuance. Unsurprisingly, the actress delivers in spades, compelling viewers for the entirety of the series’ eight-episode run and right up to its shocking climax.

A true masterpiece of small-screen drama, Chernobyl is comfortably among the greatest miniseries of all time and saw the 2010s end in spectacular fashion from the perspective of television entertainment. The precise, procedural historical drama transpires in the aftermath of the Chernobyl nuclear plant disaster of 1986, focusing on the immediate clean-up efforts, the fear that the radiation emitted could impact most of mainland Europe, and the eventual trial to determine culpability for the avoidable catastrophe.

Realized with near-unbearable tension, the series thrives with its imposing scope. It runs as an all-encompassing dramatization of the many sacrificial acts that ensured the disaster didn’t exacerbate, and the political conniving that tried to conceal the truth for as long as possible. It is a masterpiece of creeping dread, an eye-opening display of real-world institutional corrosion, and one of the most absorbingly horrific stories that has ever been put to screen. It will endure as an important masterwork for many years to come.
