How West Ham striker's testicular cancer tragedy is educating next generation

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Mother and brother of former West Ham and Australia youth team striker Dylan Tombides have partnered with FIFA to raise awareness of testicular cancer; Visit dt38.org to find out more about DT38 Foundation, Dylan's story and advice and guidance about testicular cancer.

Senior Football Journalist @psmithXI

Fifteen years on from West Ham striker Dylan Tombides discovering he had testicular cancer at the U17 World Cup, his mother and brother are back at the tournament delivering inspiring, emotional talks to the current generation of players to raise awareness in his honour.

Tombides passed away in April 2014 aged just 20, following a battle with the cancer he was diagnosed with while on international duty with Australia's youth team in Mexico in 2011.

A random drugs test revealed what had originally been misdiagnosed by his GP as a cyst was far more serious. Three years of "really intense chemotherapy" followed, says his mother Tracy.

The DT38 Foundation was set up in his name and, in partnership with FIFA Medical, Tracy and Dylan's brother Taylor are part of the initiative at this year's tournament in Doha to explain to players why regular self-examination matters and how to carry it out.

"Getting the call from FIFA was a massive opportunity and an amazing thing for us and for the charity," says Taylor. "Obviously, it's where Dylan last kind of played healthy before his diagnosis. So to come back to where it all started for us as a family is a grateful honour."

During two weeks of talks to 48 teams, Tracy and Taylor have presented to 1,200 players.

With testicular cancer the most common cancer in males aged 15 to 44 - but highly treatable and in most cases curable when detected early - the importance of the sessions could be life changing.

"It's the number one cause of fatality of cancer in this particular age group," explains Dr Andy Massey, FIFA's Medical Director, who helped form the partnership and has overseen the provision of free scans to players during the tournament.

"This is one of the biggest things I think I've ever done in my sports medicine career because it has such a reach."

Tracy and Taylor's accounts of Dylan's stories have had a powerful impact on their audiences.

"One of the young players stood in front of me and said, 'I'm so sorry for your loss. I'm sorry your son isn't coming home to you. We are so grateful that you are telling us all about it'," says Tracy, recounting a talk with the Jamaican team. A similarly emotional conversation with the French squad ended in hugs instead of handshakes.

The DT38 Foundation have a campaign called Check One Two, encouraging men to check themselves on the first day of every month. Dylan's nickname 'Didge' also provides an acronym and hashtag for the foundation: Delay Is Deadly, Get Educated.

"Early detection is the difference between dealing with cancer treatment that has a 96 per cent cure rate or the figures drop away very, very quickly if you have to go to round two or round three of chemotherapy," says Tracy, who also encourages men with concerns to "insist on an ultrasound" test.

Tracy and Taylor treasure "messages that we've got from people who say, 'I thought of Dylan's story when I found a lump and I knew not to delay. I had testicular cancer and now I'm cancer-free and Dylan saved my life'."

"That tells us that what we're doing is life-changing for that family, that person," says Tracy.

Taylor believes they can have an impact through generations, too. "The effect is lasting. If we can teach these kids now, we know the next generation, they'll teach their kids and they'll teach their kids and it'll be continuous," he says.

"We're not just affecting one life, we're affecting a whole generation of lives, which is the biggest part of it. Without FIFA, we couldn't touch as many people as we have."
