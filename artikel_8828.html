Clipless Pedals Can Feel Intimidating—Here Are Some Tips to Make Those First Clipless Rides Feel Smoother

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

Expert tips for cleat setup, pedal tension, and safe unclipping before your first ride.

When a rider switches from flat pedals to clipless pedals, they typically aren’t thinking about the differences of pedals like Shimano SPD or SPD-SL, Wahoo Speedplays, or egg-beater pedals. Many times, they haven’t heard of Time pedals or the different Assioma or Garmin models. The first thought many have is, “How do I get out of the pedal once I’m clipped in?”

Not all people considering the switch realize this—as contradictory as it sounds, clipless pedals refer to a pedal system where you are clipped into the pedal. If you aren’t familiar with the system, think about being clipped into a ski binding. Some pedal options offer different release settings—similar to how a DIN setting on a ski binding can be adjusted to offer different levels of force needed to release the ski boot from the binding.

Here we’ll dig into what clipless pedals are, how they can benefit you on your ride, tips for setting up your pedals and cleats, and tips for those daunting first few rides with your new pedals.

I have plenty of friends who swear by the saying, “flat pedals win medals.” However, there is a reason you’ll only see clipless pedals at world tour road races or on the majority of gravel bikes at your local gravel grinder. When you ride clipped in you end up using more of your leg musculature during each revolution of your pedal stroke.

In short, this means you’re typically more efficient when using clipless pedals compared to flat pedals. For clarity, you don’t need to ride clipless pedals to be a cyclist. But for those riders looking to take their riding to the next level, it’s worth giving them a try.

Because you’re literally attached to the pedal, you use not only your quads in the downstroke part of the revolution, but you also work your hamstrings, hip flexors, and glutes as you pull up. All of those muscles at work translate to more power while you’re riding, especially as you refine your pedaling technique. To get started, here’s your expert-backed guide to pedaling with clipless pedals—without falling over.

“Cleat placement is paramount,” says Issac Denham, owner of bike fitting studio Befitting Bicycles in Wayne, Pennsylvania. “Since incorrect cleat placement can lead to injuries, we suggest booking time with a bike fitter to place your cleats. Having a professional install your cleats also ensures that they won’t be installed improperly or even upside down.”

For those riders that want to try clipless pedals, but don’t want to jump into a precision fit, start with cleats in a neutral position. This means setting the cleats up in the middle of the slots in the shoe with the cleats relatively straight. Just make sure the left and right cleats are in roughly the same position—this will give you a good baseline moving forward.

It will take time doing the guess and check method for cleat placement, but this method gives you the chance to play around going clipless before you invest hundreds into a bike fit. If you start to develop pain after using clipless pedals, stop pedaling. This is likely due to cleat position and that is when it is time to see a local trusted shop for direction.

“Many clipless pedals have adjustable pedal tension. This is basically how hard the built-in spring holds the cleat in the pedal,” says Dan Chabonov, Bicycling test editor. “Setting this tension as low as possible means you need less force to clip in and out of the pedals and can make the learning process a little easier.”

While most clipless pedals already come set at the lowest tension setting from the manufacturer, you can also easily make the adjustment.

“Typically out of the box, pedals are set up with the least amount of release tension,” says Becky Quinn, former pro and bike fitter. “The pedal tensioner is on the rear of the pedal. There is a small 3mm bolt that requires a 3mm Allen wrench to adjust the tension. It can be turned counterclockwise to loosen the tension or clockwise to tighten it. There is a small window on the back of the pedal that has a small horizontal pin that moves up or down depending on which way you turn the tensioner.”

If you adjust the tension on your pedals, make sure you confirm that the pins are in the same place on both the left and right pedal, so the release tension is the same on both sides.

Practice does indeed make perfect when it comes to learning how to pedal with clipless pedals. The goal is to become so comfortable with the movements that they become part of your muscle memory, so you don’t have to look down at your foot or pedal for longer than a quick glance.

When you’re just starting out pedaling with clipless pedals, do it inside first. “Practicing indoors either on a trainer, or even leaned up against a wall, can be a great place to start where you don’t have to worry about falling down or looking silly,” says Denham.

When I ran a shop and was getting people set up on clipless pedals, I would suggest they get on the bike inside, ideally on a trainer or next to a wall to stabilize themselves, then put on a show or a movie. It sounds monotonous (and the people you live with might give you an odd look or two), but simply clip in, clip out, clip in, clip out, over and over. Do it looking down at your shoe, then when that’s comfortable practice doing it without looking down. Feel it out inside and you’ll be much better off when you head out the door.

To clip into your pedal, start with the pedal positioned at the bottom of the pedal stroke.

“Softly lift the front of the pedal with your toe to flip it over so that you can engage. Your toe will be slightly pointed downward. Slide your foot forward until the tongue of the cleat stops on the lip of the pedal,” Quinn explains. “Once you can’t slide your foot any further forward, continue to pedal forward and add pressure to the pedal. It will click in. Sometimes you will need to rotate your heel inward or outward at the 6 o’clock position to engage securely.”

Then it’s time to clip out: “In order to practice clipping out of your pedals, place one leg all the way down to the bottom of the pedal stroke (6 o’clock). Then, firmly rotate your heel outward. Be sure to not roll your ankle,” Quinn advises. “The motion should be similar to that of smashing a bug under the ball of your foot.”

If there is a single gem of advice we would love to pass on to every cyclist who has ever learned how to ride with clipless pedals, this would be it: Unclip the foot you intend to put down when you come to a stop before you come to a stop. “This is the most important piece of the puzzle,” says Quinn.

Once you unclip, “here is the most critical piece: Keep the unclipped foot on the pedal but continue to pedal a half stroke,” Quinn adds. “Once the foot that is still clipped in gets to the 6 o’clock position, coast and stand up. Your butt needs to get off the saddle. Continue to apply your rear brakes until you are almost at a stop. When you are about to come to a complete stop, apply your front brake and step down with the unclipped foot.”

Many cleat and pedal systems will follow a similar process with the general idea being: line up the cleat on the pedal and add downward force to clip in once the cleat is aligned.

Remember how we said switching to clipless pedals is a cycling rite of passage of sorts? Well, so is falling over as you’re learning. Practicing while riding on grass can make the falls a little easier, and falling with the proper technique (yes, there’s a technique for falling, too!) will lower your chances of serious injury.

“You will fall. We all have done it and it’s okay,” says Quinn. “Just remember that if you start to fall over, turn your buttocks so you land on it. It is the most padding we have on our bodies. Putting out an arm can break a wrist. You most likely will only get a bruise on your butt.”

The most important thing is not to get in your head, beat yourself up, or give up. Falling over at least a few times has happened to every single cyclist who rides with clipless pedals.

I’ll never forget one of my first rides with clipless pedals. I rolled over to the group in the parking lot overly confident, forgot I had to unclip my foot from the pedal, and slowly tipped over—still attached to the bike. It happens to everyone when you’re first making the switch, but some practice at home can make the transition a little less painful.
