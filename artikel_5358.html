BTR sector fears Treasury rules move the goal line on investor ban

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Treasury closed public comments on proposed ROAD act rules for build-to-rent exemptions to the institutional investor ban. Industry groups opposed the 18-month certificate-of-occupancy purchase window and sought clearer definitions for single-plat communities and rehab exceptions.  AI Summary

Earlier this year, build-to-rent projects secured some notable exemptions from the institutional investor ban included in the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act. However, while the industry still doesn’t know exactly what the final rules will look like, it just moved a step closer to finding out.

Monday marked the end of the U.S. Department of the Treasury‘s public comment period on its proposed rules for BTR exemptions from the institutional investor ban. Although ROAD explicitly exempted BTR from the ban, some of the proposed rules and language Treasury released in October have left the industry uneasy and unsure of where it stands.

BTR developers and operators say this uncertainty continues to suppress capital flowing into new projects, a trend they expect to continue until the federal government clarifies the rules.

Last month, Treasury released a notice of proposed rulemaking detailing how it would implement the institutional investor ban while protecting BTR communities. The proposed rules would exempt newly built, never-owner-occupied BTR communities if an investor buys them within 18 months of receiving a certificate of occupancy and uses them only as rentals.

Treasury would also exempt communities that include both rental and for-sale homes, allowing builders to sell the rental portion to large institutional investors while marketing the remaining homes to individual buyers. The proposals additionally call for allowing transfers of existing BTR communities between large institutional investors and keeping the renovate-to-rent exemption.

Although the rules appear to protect BTR, industry leaders say the finer details leave several concerns unresolved.

“If you read the law as a layman, you’d say, ‘all right, built-to-rent is carved out.’ But then you get this rulemaking, and it’s like you have to pat your belly and tap your head and hop on one foot for the built-to-rent to not be banned,” Sean Dobson, chairman, CEO and CIO at The Amherst Group, said in an interview with HousingWire TBD.

Over the last month, Treasury received more than 450 letters with questions and concerns concerning its proposed rulemakings. These letters detailed the outstanding concerns that the industry still has concerning how Treasury will enforce ROAD. With the institutional investor ban set to take effect on January 7, Treasury has several weeks to finalize and release the rules that BTR operators will have to follow.

Until then, the industry has to wait and see whether, and how, those concerns are addressed.

The chief concern among BTR developers, operators and advocates is the proposed requirement that institutional investors purchase BTR communities within 18 months of receiving a certificate of occupancy (CO). Under the proposed framework, if a developer fails to sell a community by that deadline, they may lose the ability to do so altogether.

That proposal matters because many BTR developers, much like multifamily developers, rely on selling their communities after completion and stabilization to make a profit and move on to their next project.

Richard Ross, CEO at Quinn Residences, argued that the 18-month window is far too limiting. A community may still be under development and lease-up after the first homes receive a CO, so 18 months will almost certainly run out before the full community is ready to sell.

“To build and stabilize a new community takes about 30 months. If I start receiving certificates of occupancy at month 15, I may not receive the final CO for the last home until month 28 or 29. I need enough time to complete and stabilize the entire community before selling it,” Ross said in an interview.

The letters that HousingWire TBD reviewed unanimously opposed the proposed 18-month window. Just like Ross, industry groups argued that the deadline does not align with how rental communities are developed, leased up, financed and sold.

The letter signed by a coalition of 12 BTR developers led by Greystar and Brookfield Residential argued that the deadline would create uncertainty for projects that may not be sold until years after construction. The letter further argued the restrictions would continue to affect financing decisions for future projects.

“A smaller potential buyer pool reduces liquidity and creates greater uncertainty regarding terminal value,” the letter read.

Additionally, the groups argued that Treasury must protect long-term institutional owners’ ability to purchase BTR communities. Otherwise, the federal government would undermine the industry.

“If large institutional investors cannot purchase horizontal multifamily communities (which individual families cannot), the values of those properties will fall and new construction and necessary maintenance and reinvestment will slow,” MBA’s letter read.

To address this, NAA and NMHC, two of the largest rental housing advocacy organizations, requested that Treasury eliminate the deadline or replace it with a provision that institutional investors can purchase BTR communities directly from the builder or developer responsible for construction.

Quinn Residences, AHV Communities and LGI Homes also requested that Treasury remove or substantially extend the deadline. LGI Homes also emphasized the need for production homebuilders to retain the option of renting homes that have not sold.

Treasury’s proposed rulemaking describes the three activities that could qualify an institutional investor as a covered entity as “purchases, constructs, or constructs and retains”. This matters because BTR developers often build and retain homes as rental properties before selling stabilized communities to finance future developments.

Treasury’s language is ambiguous, leaving open the possibility that BTR developers could be treated like institutional investors.

The National Rental Home Council made this a central argument in its letter, distinguishing between an investor buying a home from someone else and one acting as the builder. The organization urged Treasury to recognize that developers who build and retain homes as rentals operate differently from investors who purchase newly built homes from third parties.

Up for Growth Action made a similar argument, urging Treasury to differentiate between creating new rental housing and acquiring existing housing.

Several letters argued that Treasury’s proposed rule does not clearly distinguish single-plat rental communities, where multiple homes share one parcel and common ownership, from individual single-family homes. They worry the rule could inadvertently restrict the development, ownership or sale of these communities by treating them as collections of individual homes subject to the institutional investor ban.

In response, BTR advocates argued for clearer rules for single-plat communities and those mixing renters and owners, while NRHC asked whether buildings with three or more attached units could count as single-family homes.

The letters also raise concerns about the proposed exception for investors that purchase distressed homes, substantially rehabilitate them and rent them out. Treasury’s proposed rules would allow institutional investors to purchase existing homes in need of extensive repairs, as long as they spend 15% of the purchase price on improvements.

However, several of the letters argued that the 15% threshold is too high. They further argued that the rule could be hard to enforce for homes that are difficult to inspect before purchase, homes in jurisdictions without applicable local codes, and homes with problems that aren’t obvious upfront.

Rental housing advocates disagreed on how flexible the rehabilitation threshold should be. While some groups advocated for increasing the threshold, NMHC, NAA and RETTC support the 15% benchmark while advocating for more flexibility in identifying problems and applying older building standards.

The common theme is that industry leaders want the exception to work for genuinely distressed properties without requiring investors to predict every hidden problem before purchasing a home.
