AI phishing emails: how cloned voices, deepfakes and clean grammar fool readers

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Free daily briefing on global business news.

AI phishing emails no longer carry the typos and clumsy wording that once exposed them, and these 10 changes show what to watch for and which defenses still work

For years, the standard advice on phishing came down to one test: read the message closely and look for mistakes. A misspelled company name, a clumsy greeting or a sentence no native speaker would write was often enough to expose a scam. That test was never perfect, but it caught a lot of crude fraud.

Generative AI has weakened it. Large language models produce fluent, correctly spelled text in seconds. They can write in a formal register or a casual one, and in many languages. A scammer who once struggled with grammar can now send a message that reads like it came from a payroll department, a supplier or a senior executive. The errors that used to expose a fake are much easier to remove.

The change goes beyond polish. The same tools make it cheaper to tailor a lure to one person using details from a public profile. They can copy the tone of a colleague and produce many different versions of a single message. They can also help build the fake pages that sit behind a link. Voice cloning and deepfake video add another layer. A request that starts in an inbox can be followed by a phone call or a video meeting that seems to confirm it.

For anyone who handles payments, passwords or sensitive data, this shifts where attention needs to go. AI phishing emails are hard to judge by how they look or sound. They are easier to judge by what they ask the reader to do. A request to change bank details, approve an urgent transfer or sign in through an emailed link carries the same risk whether the writing is polished or clumsy.

This list covers 10 specific ways AI changes phishing, from cleaner grammar and automated spear phishing to cloned voices and convincing fake login pages. It is a selection of changes, not a complete catalogue. It includes two real fraud cases in which synthetic audio and video were used to persuade staff to send money. It also names criminal-market chatbots that security researchers found advertised on underground forums. The final slide turns to defenses that do not depend on spotting a bad sentence. These include verifying requests through a separate channel and checking the real sender domain. Passkeys and security keys also help. They will not release credentials to a lookalike site.

A phishing email with perfect spelling and polished grammar is no longer unusual. Chatbots draft clean, error-free copy on request. That leaves the old advice to hunt for clumsy wording with little value as a test.

For years, security awareness training leaned on that advice. Misspelled brand names, odd capitalization, broken idioms and stiff phrasing were treated as the giveaway. The logic was simple. Many scammers wrote in a second or third language, and few took the time to proofread.

Generative AI removes most of that friction. A rough prompt can become a finished message in seconds. The tool can tidy the punctuation, line breaks and sign-off that once looked off. It can hold a consistent tone from the subject line to the closing, whether the message poses as an invoice reminder, an IT alert or a note from HR.

Revision is cheap, too. A scammer who dislikes a draft can ask for a shorter version, a more formal one or one that sounds more rushed. Each rewrite takes seconds rather than the time a human writer would need.

The test was never perfect in the other direction either. Legitimate companies send emails with typos, and real colleagues often dash off hurried, error-filled notes. Polish was always a weak signal of trust. Now it is close to no signal at all.

The more reliable clues sit in the action a message wants. An email that pushes the reader to move money, enter a password on a linked page, open an unexpected attachment or hand over personal details deserves a second look. That is true whether the prose is clumsy or flawless.

Pressure is a second clue that AI does not remove. Phishing depends on a fast reaction before the reader stops to think. Deadlines measured in hours, warnings that an account will be locked and requests to keep a matter confidential are built to cut that pause short.

Tone can carry pressure as well. A note that flatters, scolds or invokes a senior executive's authority is trying to shift the reader from checking to complying. A language model can strike any of those tones on request.

A message can read as though a professional editor wrote it and still be a scam. Judging an email by its request and its urgency, rather than its grammar, reflects how attackers now work.

Generative AI lets a scammer turn a target's public details into a tailored phishing email in seconds. That closes the old gap between cheap mass phishing and labor-intensive spear phishing. Personalized lures can now reach far more people.

Phishing has long come in two broad forms. Mass phishing sends the same generic message to huge lists of addresses, such as a fake delivery notice or a password-reset warning. Spear phishing targets a specific person with a message built around who they are and what they do.

The trade-off used to be cost. A convincing spear-phishing email required someone to research each target by hand. That meant reading profiles, finding the right colleague's name and writing a message that fit the person's role. The effort limited how many people an attacker could reach.

Large language models remove much of that labor. A scammer can paste in a person's job title, employer and a recent public post. A model then drafts a message that references those details. A fresh version can be generated for the next name on the list.

The raw material is often easy to find. Professional networking profiles list roles, managers and past employers. Company websites publish staff directories, press releases and event schedules. Conference speaker pages and personal social media accounts fill in projects, travel plans and recent achievements.

Those details let a lure mention a real vendor, a real deadline or a real event the recipient attended. A finance employee might get a note about an invoice tied to a supplier the company uses. A new hire might get a message that appears to come from their onboarding team.

This undercuts a check many people rely on. A message that names your manager, your current project or last week's industry conference once suggested the sender knew you. Today, that knowledge may have come from a quick scrape of public pages.

The practical lesson is narrow. Familiar details in an email show only that the sender could find them. They do not show who the sender is. They do not confirm that a request is genuine.

Large language models let criminals write fluent phishing emails in languages they do not speak. That removes a clear warning sign for readers in smaller language markets. They could once dismiss a message in their own language that read as though a clumsy translator had produced it.

Europol, the European Union's law enforcement agency, flagged this risk in a 2023 report on ChatGPT and law enforcement. The report noted that language models can help criminals produce convincing text in languages they do not know. A target's native language is no longer much of a barrier to an attacker.

For years, the language gap worked in defenders' favor. Scammers targeting readers in Finnish, Hungarian, Czech or Korean often relied on older machine translation or their own limited skills. The results tended to be stiff, overly literal or simply wrong. Native speakers could often spot a fake within a sentence or two.

That gap has narrowed. A scammer can now draft a lure once and ask a model to render it in another language. The output can match the tone of a bank notice, a delivery update or a letter from a tax office. The same model can then adapt it for a second country in minutes.

Translation is only part of the shift. Many languages build politeness directly into their grammar. German distinguishes the formal Sie from the informal du. French has vous and tu. Japanese business writing depends on keigo, a layered system of honorific speech. Getting these wrong once marked a message as foreign. A model can be told to use the register a real company would choose.

Local conventions are easier to imitate as well. Date formats, currency symbols, the phrasing of a sign-off and the names of familiar national services all give a message a local feel. An attacker no longer needs a native-speaking accomplice to get those details right.

For readers outside the English-speaking world, the old shortcut no longer works. A natural-sounding message in a less widely spoken language once suggested a sender who actually lived and worked there. It no longer carries that meaning. Polished wording, in any language, now says little about who wrote the email.

A language model can copy the way a specific manager, colleague or supplier writes after seeing only a few of their emails. When an attacker controls a real mailbox, that borrowed voice can appear inside an existing reply thread. The target already trusts that conversation.

Every regular correspondent has habits. Some sign off with a single initial. Some skip greetings entirely. Others write in clipped fragments from a phone. Those quirks used to be hard for an outsider to fake. A model given a handful of past messages can reproduce them, including vocabulary, sentence length and level of formality.

The risk grows once an attacker gets inside a genuine account. Stolen passwords and earlier phishing messages are common routes into a mailbox. From there, the intruder can read months of real correspondence. That archive shows who pays whom, which invoices are pending and how each person normally phrases a request.

A hijacked thread removes several checks a careful reader would normally apply. The sender address is genuine. The subject line matches an ongoing project. The earlier messages quoted below the new reply are real. The fraudulent message only has to sound like the person who wrote the previous ones. A model can supply that voice.

Attackers also use versions of the technique without full access to the target's own account. A message can arrive from a lookalike address while continuing a thread copied from a compromised mailbox elsewhere. In a crowded inbox, it can pass as a normal reply in a familiar chain.

Mailbox intruders often add inbox rules that forward, hide or delete certain messages. Those rules can keep the real account owner from seeing replies that would expose the fraud.

The warning sign in these cases is rarely the language. It is the request itself. A new bank account for a known supplier deserves scrutiny. So does a sudden change to payment instructions, a request to keep a deal confidential or a push to skip a usual approval step. That holds even when the wording sounds exactly like the person who normally writes.

WormGPT and FraudGPT are AI chatbots that were advertised on underground forums in 2023 as tools for writing phishing and business email compromise messages. Security researchers, including the firm SlashNext, reported the listings that year.

A scammer using such a tool does not need to write a convincing lure from scratch. The scammer also does not need the skills to set up and run a language model. The forum listings pitched fraud-ready text generation as something that could simply be bought.

Business email compromise, often shortened to BEC, is the type of fraud these tools were marketed for. In a BEC scheme, the attacker poses as someone the target already deals with. That might be a chief executive, a finance director, a supplier or an outside lawyer.

The message usually asks for something specific. It might be a wire transfer, a change to the bank account on file for an invoice, or sensitive records such as payroll data. The request is framed as routine business.

Many BEC emails carry no malicious attachment and no link. They rely on wording alone. That leaves fewer technical traces for security software to catch. It also makes the writing the main tool of the scheme. Writing is exactly what a text generator produces.

The researchers' reports described the advertisements and what the sellers pitched the tools for. A sales listing on a criminal forum shows what was offered to buyers, not how well the product worked.

Mainstream chatbots such as ChatGPT run under safety rules. Those rules are designed to refuse requests for scam emails or malicious code. They can sometimes be evaded.

One method is a jailbreak prompt. It reframes a harmful request as fiction, role play or a hypothetical exercise. Another approach splits a request into harmless-looking pieces.

A BEC request can also look harmless on its own. An email asking an accountant to confirm a vendor's new bank details reads like ordinary office work. A chatbot may find little in that wording to refuse. Scammers are therefore not limited to tools sold on criminal forums.

Generative AI can rewrite one phishing lure into many distinct versions, each with different wording, structure and subject lines. That variety weakens email filters that work by matching known phrases or by spotting identical message bodies sent to many inboxes.

Many traditional spam defenses depend on repetition. When a campaign sends the same text to a long list of addresses, a filter can learn that text once and block every copy. Some systems create a fingerprint of a message body. They then compare it against fingerprints of messages already flagged as malicious. Other systems score each message on words and phrases that commonly appear in fraud.

A language model undercuts both methods. An attacker can ask for the same request — reset your password, approve this invoice, open this shared file — written dozens of different ways. The underlying instruction stays the same. The sentences, greetings, sign-offs and subject lines all change. Each version can read like an ordinary business email and share no fingerprint with the others.

Scammers tried to vary their messages long before generative AI. They swapped words in and out of templates or inserted random characters to break up matching. Those older tricks often produced awkward or garbled text. A careful reader or a well-tuned filter could catch that. Model-written variants usually keep their grammar intact. The variation itself no longer leaves an obvious trail.

Email security tools have answered by looking less at what a message says and more at where it comes from and what it does. Sender reputation weighs signals such as how long a sending domain has existed. It also considers whether that domain has a history of legitimate mail. Link analysis follows where a URL actually leads, including any redirects, instead of trusting the visible text.

Anomaly detection asks whether a message fits the normal pattern for a recipient or an organization. A first-time sender asking for a payment can raise a flag. So can a reply-to address that differs from the sender address, or a request arriving at an unusual hour. None of these signals depends on how the message is worded.

An email that reaches the inbox has not been certified safe. Getting past the filter means only that the message did not match what the filter was built to catch.

A phone call that sounds exactly like the boss no longer confirms an email request. Voice cloning lets fraudsters pair a written payment demand with a synthetic voice that matches the supposed sender. A single suspicious message then feels like a verified instruction.

The technique works by training a speech model on recordings of a real person. Executives are convenient targets. Their voices are often public. Earnings calls, conference keynotes, podcast interviews and company videos all supply usable audio.

A widely reported case predates today's consumer chatbots. In 2019, the Wall Street Journal reported that criminals had used a cloned voice to defraud a U.K. energy firm. The voice imitated the chief executive of the firm's parent company. It persuaded the head of the U.K. firm to transfer €220,000, according to the newspaper.

In a phishing scheme, the voice often plays a supporting role rather than the lead. An email arrives first. It asks for a wire transfer, a change to a supplier's bank details or a set of login credentials. A call or voice note follows, apparently from the same person, repeating the request. That second contact is designed to settle the doubts the email raised.

The sequence exploits a familiar piece of security advice. Employees are often told to confirm unusual requests by phone. If the attacker places that call, the check happens inside the scam.

A call that comes from the requester proves nothing. Caller ID can be spoofed. A familiar name or number on the screen is not evidence of who is speaking. Voice notes sent through messaging apps carry the same risk. The attacker either controls the account or chooses the recording.

Listening for robotic delivery is not a reliable test either. A short call, a poor connection or background noise can hide flaws in a synthetic voice. Attackers also tend to add urgency. That leaves less time to notice anything odd.

The safer pattern reverses the direction of contact. The recipient ends the call and rings the person back on a number taken from a company directory or an earlier trusted record — not from the email, the signature block or the incoming call. Some finance teams also require a second approver for any payment change. Under that rule, one convincing voice cannot move money on its own.

Deepfake video calls let scammers stage a whole meeting of fake colleagues to back up a payment request. In early 2024, Hong Kong police described a case in which an employee of a multinational firm sent roughly $25 million after joining a video call with deepfaked coworkers.

Arup confirmed in May 2024 that it was the company involved. Arup is a British engineering and design firm with offices in many countries.

The fraud did not begin with the video call. It began with a message that the employee initially suspected. The employee joined the call with the deepfaked colleagues after that and then sent the money.

That order of events matters. A cautious recipient who doubts an email will often look for some form of confirmation. In this case, the confirmation was itself fake.

A call with several participants works differently from a single cloned voice on the phone. Each face on screen appears to agree with the others. The request seems to come from a group rather than from one person. The employee is no longer weighing one sender's word but what looks like a room of colleagues.

Deepfake video tools build a synthetic face and voice from existing footage of a real person. Senior executives often appear in recorded presentations, interviews and conference talks. That public material gives attackers something to work from.

The Arup case shows that a familiar face on a screen does not prove who is behind it. A video call that the requester arranges is part of the scheme, not an independent check.

Companies can reduce this risk by tying large payments to fixed internal controls rather than to a conversation. One example is a rule that big transfers need sign-off from more than one person through the company's own payment system. Under that rule, the request still has to clear the usual approvals, however convincing the meeting looked.

The case also shows a pattern to watch for. A message that raises doubts, followed by a meeting meant to settle those doubts, is a reason to pause before any money moves.

Generative AI tools can produce a convincing copy of a bank, email or software login page in minutes, along with the text that surrounds it. The page behind a phishing link can now look as polished as the email that delivered it.

Fake sign-in pages are not new. Criminals have long copied logos, color schemes and layouts from real brands. They then host those copies on domains that look close to the original. AI changes the speed and finish of that work.

A scammer can ask a model to write the page's code, help text, error messages and password-reset prompts. The result has fewer of the broken layouts and odd phrases that once gave a copied page away.

The same tools can match the page to the lure. A message about a shared invoice can lead to a document-viewer login. A message about a missed delivery can lead to a form asking for card details. Each page can be written in the target's language and in the tone of the email that pointed to it.

When the page itself looks clean, the address bar carries the most reliable evidence. The part of a web address that identifies the owner sits directly before the ending, such as .com or .co.uk. In an address like "login.yourbank.com.account-check.net," the real domain is account-check.net, not the bank.

Lookalike domains rely on small substitutions. Common ones include "rn" standing in for "m," a zero in place of the letter O, or an extra word such as "secure" attached with a hyphen. Some use characters from other alphabets that look identical to Latin letters. This technique is known as a homograph attack.

The padlock icon does not settle the question. It shows that the connection is encrypted, not that the site belongs to the company it imitates. Basic security certificates are available free of charge. A phishing site can get one as easily as a legitimate business can.

On a computer, hovering over a link reveals its real destination before a click. On a phone, pressing and holding a link usually shows the address. The safer route skips the link entirely: type the company's address directly or open a saved bookmark.

Password managers add one more check. They fill in saved credentials only on the domain where those credentials were stored. A manager that offers nothing on a familiar-looking login page is a sign that the address does not match the real site.

The defenses that hold up against AI phishing do not depend on judging how a message reads. They check things an attacker cannot easily fake: a separate contact channel, the real sender domain and a sign-in method tied to the genuine website.

Out-of-band verification is the first layer. Any request to send money, change bank details or share login credentials gets confirmed through a channel the recipient already trusts. That means contact details from a company directory, a contract or a previous invoice. It never means a number or address supplied in the message itself. An organization can make this a written policy: no payment detail changes without confirmation through contact details already on file.

The second habit is reading the technical details rather than the display name. An email client shows a friendly name, such as a colleague's or a supplier's. The address behind that name can belong to anyone. The domain after the @ sign is the part that identifies the sender. A display name and the visible text of a link are both labels that the sender controls.

Phishing-resistant sign-in covers the gap that human attention leaves open. FIDO2 security keys and passkeys use public-key cryptography, and the private key stays with the user. Each credential is bound to the real website address it was created for. On a lookalike page, the browser will not offer that credential at all. A fake site has nothing to capture that it can replay on the real one.

Email authentication works at the domain level. SPF lets a domain owner publish which mail servers may send on its behalf. DKIM adds a cryptographic signature that receiving servers can check against the domain's published key. DMARC tells receiving servers what to do with messages that fail those checks. A domain owner can set that policy to none, quarantine or reject. DMARC also sends reports back to the domain owner.

Together, those three standards help block spoofing of an exact domain, such as a fake message claiming to come from a company's real address. They do not stop lookalike domains. Those are registered separately and can pass their own authentication checks. That limit is the reason the verification and sign-in habits still carry weight.

None of these measures asks anyone to spot a flaw in the writing. Each one moves the decision away from a message's tone and toward facts that a language model cannot alter.
