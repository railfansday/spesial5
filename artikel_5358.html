How real estate agents handle proof of funds and disclosures

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Proof of funds should be introduced early as a soft question but collected after the buyer relationship is established. Agents should document lender referral disclosures and use a signed authorization to share financial records only with necessary transaction parties.  AI Summary

On a recent coaching call this week, an agent asked a question that looks tactical and is actually about professional discipline. She works mostly in commercial, she has a physician buying a home and several of her clients have investors behind them, which makes gathering proof of funds a real ordeal. When do I ask for it? Does it need to be current? And how do I protect something that private? Those questions sit squarely on the line between being a salesperson and being a fiduciary, and that line is where our industry keeps getting tested.

Let me give the coaching first, because the sequence is where most agents go wrong.

Proof of funds gets requested too early and in the wrong tone. Imagine a buyer who just called on an ad. If the first thing you do is hand them a list of documents, you have converted a warm first contact into homework, and you have done it before you have given them a single reason to trust you. I do not open any client relationship by burdening the person with tasks. The order has to serve the relationship, not the file.

Here is the sequence I teach. Get them into the office first. On that opening call you can ask one soft question, do you happen to have a proof of funds ready, and if the answer is no, you keep it light. We will need it at some point, bring it when we meet if you can, but do not worry about it now. You have raised the flag without turning it into a barrier.

When they arrive, run a genuine buyer appointment and get the buyer to hire you, the same way you would work to get hired by a seller. Only then do you assign the homework, and now the identical list that would have felt like a wall on the phone lands as a plan. Here is what we need from you, and in the meantime let’s define exactly what you are looking for, let me show you what is available, and we can even see a couple so you get a feel for the market. The serious looking begins once I have the documents. The buyer never feels interrogated. They feel represented.

Powerfact: You earn the right to ask for documents by first earning the relationship.

Now to the part that matters for leaders, because it is about how we protect our businesses and our clients at the same time.

She asked whether to send buyers to their own bank or recommend someone she knows. I prefer to recommend my own lender, and I say that carefully, because we are operating in a litigation environment, and the professional move is to assume you could be sued and to run your business so that you are covered. Practically, that means disclosing any financial relationship in writing, every time. On our coaching platform I built a document I call the Transparency Conversation, a simple editable form that states who you are connected to, how you get paid, and whether anyone shares in a fee. If the lender is tied to your company and a financial arrangement exists, that gets disclosed plainly. If you are recommending someone purely because you know and trust the loan officer, you say exactly that and nothing more.

Here is how I used to present the recommendation itself, and the logic has aged well. You can use any lender you want, that is your call. The reason I like my person is this. I could refer a lot of lenders, and I choose this one, because if he ever lets you down, he does not just lose your loan, he loses every piece of business I will ever send him. So he will do whatever it takes, within the rules, to get you to the closing table. That framing respects the buyer’s freedom and explains why your recommendation carries weight. It is service, stated out loud.

Her question was about confidentiality. She had a buyer with roughly $2 million in proof of funds, and people are private about money at that level. Does it become confidential? Do we keep the figure quiet even though we hold the documentation?

Another agent said she never provides proof of funds until after an offer is accepted. That is a sound default in many cases, but it depends on the seller. On luxury homes, estates, and commercial property, the listing side will often require proof of funds before they open the door, and that is legitimate. Sellers of a multimillion dollar property do not want a stream of non-buyers touring for decorating ideas or dream-board photos. When you represent that buyer, prepare them for the requirement so a reasonable request does not feel like an insult.

For brokers and team leaders, here is the translation. Our industry has spent two years being told that our compensation and our value must be disclosed, documented, and justified. Good. The agents who will thrive under that scrutiny are the ones who already behave like fiduciaries by habit, not the ones who treat disclosure as a form to dodge. Teach your people the sequence, relationship first, documents second. Standardize the written disclosures so no agent is improvising a legal boundary on the fly. And model the confidentiality, because a buyer who sees you guard their two million dollar statement like it is your own will refer you for the rest of their life.

The whole thing fits on one page. Do not lead with the paperwork. Get them in, get them to hire you, assign the homework as part of the service. Recommend your lender, and disclose everything in writing. Treat their financial information as a fiduciary, with a signed authorization. And read the other side of the transaction, so you know when a request for proof is simply professionalism doing its job.
