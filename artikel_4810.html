Postpartum Prevention Plan: Time Outside, Exercise, Socialize

Postpartum depression and anxiety are major issues that affect new caregivers. Many new parents focus on the birth plan to prepare for this major stage of life. But what I find in my work as a clinical psychologist is that new families really benefit from a postpartum plan as well. What are the needs, relationships, and activities that will help you maintain your well-being postpartum? Knowing these will promote your child’s well-being as well. I developed the following mnemonic to capture core helpful practices that are supported by research: Time Outside, Exercise, and Socialize. These three practices can provide a daily structure that maximizes joy during a time that is challenging but also fulfilling.

1. Time Outside: Follow the mantra often told to caregivers of newborns — leave the house every day. Time spent in nature has been associated with enhanced mood, emotional regulation, optimism, resilience, and child development (Hall et al., 2023). Research on mothers and nature has attested to the benefits of the outdoors on postnatal well-being and the relief it provides from indoor stressors (Hall et al., 2023). Getting out is key. Even if being in nature isn't possible, go out for coffee, lunch, or to a museum to provide novelty and stimulation that can vary your routine and perk up the day. Physicians have dispelled myths that you shouldn’t leave home with your newborn, as fresh air and a change of scenery are beneficial to both parent and child well-being (Davis, 2015).

2. Exercise: If you are able to, getting some kind of walk in every day with a newborn is not only good for you but also for your child. Strap them into a carrier of your choosing or affix a stroller with all the things you might need to leave the house for a stretch. A meta-analysis reviewing a wide range of studies found that exercise alone (without the use of other interventions) is effective in reducing postpartum depression and anxiety (Deprato et al., 2025). A moderate reduction in the severity of symptoms was achieved through at least 80 minutes a week of rigorous exercise, which could include a brisk walk, swimming, cycling, and other activities.

3. Socialize: Invite a friend or family member for a visit or walk. Attend a local baby-friendly gathering that can take you and an infant out into the world, like a yoga, music, play, or story time group. Research suggests that social interaction is a powerful protective factor in preventing postpartum depression and anxiety (Feinberg et al., 2022). Another study found that social support is particularly important for women who are at risk of postpartum mental health problems, such as those with more than one child, pregnancy loss, negative body image, and a history of mental health problems.

It is important to note that all three of these components overlap — socializing, time outside, and exercise could all take place during a long walk with a friend. But if you are having trouble getting out with the baby, have others help you, if possible, so you don’t have to make this happen on your own. And if consistent practice of these activities doesn’t seem to help, reaching out to a medical and/or mental health professional is essential. Severe cases of postpartum depression and anxiety may not remit with daily health behaviors, but require professional treatment, which can be very effective. More support is available at the National Maternal Mental Health Hotline (1-833-852-6262) and Postpartum Support International (1-800-944-4773).
