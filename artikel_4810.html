If You and Your Child Are More Nervous These Days, Here's What to Do

These are difficult days for just about everyone. It is common for people coming to us to note that they are feeling more uneasy and that everyone they know is too. Parents are struggling with issues from financial insecurity to political unrest to safety concerns amid news reports of shootings and teen suicides. They are also seeing that the baseline level of anxiety in their children is at an all-time high, and this only exacerbates their own worrying.

How can you manage your feelings and those of your child? We have 3 guideposts to support you through these times. We suggest you talk with your child and model these as ways to work with what feels overwhelming to them. You are conveying that being upset is something we work with rather than something that's unchangeable. You can do this even with young children, varying the specific language to the child’s age.

1. Recognize what you can versus what you cannot control. There is an old saying: If you can change something, why worry? And if you can’t change it, why worry? Understanding that we cannot always change what happens to us but that we do have it within our power to change how we respond to it shifts us into more adaptive reactions. When we face challenges, we can look for what within the situation we can impact rather than just letting it loop anxiously in our minds. For example, we can become active in support of a political cause, get a second opinion about a medical problem, or, if it's a concern your child has in school, arrange a parent-teacher meeting.

2. Stop believing everything you think. We know this may seem like unexpected guidance from two professionals who focus on how people think and feel. It makes sense, however, when we recognize that anxiety is fungible–even if you have a positive impact on one thing that is troubling you, an anxious mind will look around for what else it needs to worry about. And our anxious minds are creative! There is always something nervousness can attach to in our lives, whether or not it may actually deserve significant concern. Recognizing that, we can get some perspective on automatic thoughts and that in and of itself is calming. If you name it, you tame it. With your child, you can identify the good outcomes that might be possible to widen the lens on what they foresee.

3. Learn what your body does when you are anxious. Our bodies express our feelings and if we focus on our bodies (“drop down into your body” ) rather than just our thoughts, we can actually do things to calm a nervous response. A calmer body signals to our minds that we are not as threatened as we might think we are. The first step is to identify where in your body anxiety tends to sit: Is it a churning belly or hunched shoulders or shallow breathing? Every person, adult or child, has their own characteristic body response to anxiety. If you know yours and you help your child identify theirs, you can work with it. There are many ways to do that. Deep slow breathing helps calm all areas of the body. There are relaxation techniques such as tensing all of our muscles on purpose—especially those hunched shoulders—and then unclenching all over. If you do this several times, your body learns that it doesn’t need to be so tense. In a sense, you are teaching your mind to stay with this moment, the one you are in right then, to draw your thoughts away from rehashing the past or predicting future catastrophes. Other common options to relax your body as your anxious thoughts swirl include meditation, journaling, listening to calming music, reaching out to a supportive person you trust such as a friend, family member, clergy member, or therapist. Getting out in nature is good to do as well. You and/or your child can do any or all of these things.

Some anxiety is inevitable at times for just about all of us. The goal is to recognize it, name it as such, and begin to access ways to work with it as a malleable experience. We live in an uncertain world, and constant news and social media feeds can exacerbate our sense of vulnerability and impending doom. You can’t change everything that you find disturbing but you can use these tools to help you and your child feel more grounded and calm.
