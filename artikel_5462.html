15 royal palaces you can visit, from Versailles to the Forbidden City

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

These 15 royal palaces you can visit span Europe and Asia, from Versailles and the Alhambra to Beijing's Forbidden City, with notes on history and access

A royal palace was never just a place to live. It was a statement of power, built to impress courtiers, foreign envoys and subjects who passed through its gates. The rooms, gardens and courtyards were part of how a dynasty presented itself to the world. Many of those buildings are now open to visitors, even where the dynasty that built them no longer rules.

This selection gathers 15 royal palaces you can visit across Europe and Asia. It covers sites in France, Austria, Spain, Germany, Turkey, Russia, China, Thailand and India, plus three in the U.K. Windsor Castle traces its origins to William the Conqueror, who founded it after the Norman Conquest. The Forbidden City in Beijing was completed in 1420. Mysore Palace in Karnataka, India, was completed in 1912.

Access varies widely. Some palaces now operate mainly as museums. Beijing's imperial palace houses the Palace Museum. Topkapı Palace in Istanbul, once the main residence of the Ottoman sultans, is now a museum too. Others remain working royal buildings. Buckingham Palace is still the London residence and administrative headquarters of the monarch. Its State Rooms open to visitors only for limited periods in summer. The Palace of Holyroodhouse in Edinburgh opens when the royal family is not in residence. Visiting rules also differ from site to site. Neuschwanstein Castle in Bavaria, commissioned by King Ludwig II in 1869, can be seen on guided tours only.

Visitors to the Grand Palace in Bangkok, which includes the Temple of the Emerald Buddha, must follow a dress code. Several palaces on this list, including Versailles, the Alhambra and Schönbrunn, are UNESCO World Heritage sites. Opening dates, ticketing and entry rules change from year to year. Each slide below describes access in general terms rather than listing hours or prices. The 15 palaces appear as a selection, not a ranking. Every entry covers one site on its own — who built it, what it is known for and how visitors can see it today.

The Palace of Versailles, west of Paris, was the principal residence of the French royal court from 1682 until the French Revolution. Its best-known room is the Hall of Mirrors, or Galerie des Glaces.

The site began as a hunting lodge built for Louis XIII in the 1620s. His son, Louis XIV, expanded it over several decades into a seat of government and a home for the court. The architects Louis Le Vau and later Jules Hardouin-Mansart directed much of that building work.

Once the court settled there, leading nobles were expected to live at or attend court, close to the king. Daily routines such as the king's rising and retiring became formal ceremonies with assigned roles for courtiers.

The Hall of Mirrors runs along the garden side of the central building. Hardouin-Mansart designed it, and work on the room was completed in 1684. One long wall holds arched windows that face the gardens. The opposite wall is lined with mirrors that reflect the daylight back into the room. Charles Le Brun painted the vaulted ceiling with scenes celebrating the reign of Louis XIV.

The room later hosted events far beyond court life. The German Empire was proclaimed in the Hall of Mirrors in January 1871, during the Franco-Prussian War. The Treaty of Versailles, which formally ended World War I, was signed in the same hall on June 28, 1919.

The gardens were designed by André Le Nôtre. They follow the French formal style, with geometric flower beds, long straight views and clipped hedges. Water features include the Grand Canal, which extends the main axis of the gardens away from the palace. The estate also contains the Grand Trianon and the Petit Trianon, two smaller residences set apart from the main building. Marie Antoinette used the Petit Trianon as a private retreat.

The royal family left Versailles in October 1789, when crowds forced Louis XVI and his household to move to Paris. In the 1830s, King Louis-Philippe converted the palace into a museum dedicated to French history. Versailles was added to the UNESCO World Heritage list in 1979.

Schönbrunn Palace was the summer residence of the Habsburgs, the dynasty that ruled Austria for centuries. Its Baroque form largely reflects 18th-century work under Empress Maria Theresa.

The estate sits in Vienna's Hietzing district, southwest of the city center. The name comes from "schöner Brunnen," German for "beautiful spring," after a spring on the grounds. Habsburg rulers used the land as a hunting area before the main palace took shape.

Johann Bernhard Fischer von Erlach drew up the first designs in the late 17th century for Emperor Leopold I. The building that stands today owes more to the following century. Maria Theresa made Schönbrunn a center of court life and had it remodeled. Her court architect, Nikolaus Pacassi, oversaw much of that work in the mid-1700s.

The palace rooms hold several turning points in Habsburg history. In 1762, a six-year-old Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart performed for Maria Theresa there. Emperor Franz Joseph was born at Schönbrunn in 1830. He also died there in 1916.

In 1918, Emperor Charles I signed his renunciation of state affairs at the palace. That act ended the Habsburg dynasty's rule over Austria. The building that had hosted the imperial court became the property of the new republic.

The park behind the palace climbs uphill to the Gloriette, a colonnaded structure completed in 1775. It stands on a rise above the formal gardens and faces back over the palace toward the city. The gardens between them are laid out along long axes, with clipped hedges, fountains and statuary.

The grounds also hold Tiergarten Schönbrunn, a zoo that dates to 1752. It began as an imperial menagerie and still operates inside the palace park. A glass and iron Palm House, opened in 1882, adds a botanical collection nearby.

UNESCO added the Palace and Gardens of Schönbrunn to its World Heritage list in 1996. The listing treats the palace, its park and the garden buildings as a single Baroque ensemble.

The Forbidden City in Beijing was the imperial palace of China's Ming and Qing dynasties. Today the walled complex at the center of the city is home to the Palace Museum.

Construction began in 1406 under the Yongle Emperor, the third ruler of the Ming dynasty. He moved the imperial capital from Nanjing to Beijing. The new palace was built to house his court.

The Chinese name, Zijin Cheng, translates roughly as the Purple Forbidden City. The "forbidden" part reflected the palace's status. Ordinary people could not enter without the emperor's permission.

The complex is a large rectangle enclosed by high walls and a wide moat. Its main southern entrance is the Meridian Gate. Tiananmen, the gate that gives its name to the square beyond, lies farther south. Jingshan Park rises just north of the palace.

Inside, the layout divides into two zones. The outer court held the halls used for state ceremonies. The most prominent of these is the Hall of Supreme Harmony, where events such as enthronements took place. The inner court, farther north, held the living quarters of the emperor and his household.

Most buildings are timber halls set on raised stone terraces. Their roofs are covered in yellow glazed tiles. Yellow was the color associated with the emperor.

The palace outlasted the dynasty that built it. The Qing took over the complex after the Ming dynasty fell in 1644. Qing emperors ruled from it until imperial rule ended. Puyi, the last emperor, abdicated in 1912. He was allowed to remain in the inner court until he was expelled in 1924.

The Palace Museum opened in 1925. Its collections include paintings, ceramics, bronzes and objects from imperial court life. UNESCO added the palace to its World Heritage list in 1987.

The museum sets its own entry arrangements, and they can change from season to season. Visitors generally enter from the south through the Meridian Gate. From there they walk north through the outer and inner courts.

The Alhambra is a hilltop palace and fortress complex in Granada, Spain, built by the Nasrid dynasty that ruled the Emirate of Granada. It is known for the Court of the Lions and its carved Islamic-style decoration.

The complex sits on the Sabika hill above the city, in Spain's Andalusia region. Its name comes from the Arabic for "the red one." The name refers to the reddish color of its walls.

The Nasrids held Granada from the 13th century until 1492. That year, the emirate surrendered to Ferdinand and Isabella, the Catholic Monarchs of Castile and Aragon. Granada was the last Muslim-ruled state on the Iberian Peninsula.

The Alhambra has several distinct parts. The Alcazaba, the military citadel at the western end, is the oldest section. The Nasrid Palaces hold the royal rooms. These include the Court of the Myrtles, with its long reflecting pool, and the Hall of the Ambassadors, which served as a throne room.

The Court of the Lions dates to the 14th-century reign of Muhammad V. It is a rectangular courtyard ringed by slender columns. At its center, 12 marble lions support a fountain basin. Narrow channels carry water from the fountain toward the surrounding halls.

Decoration covers most interior surfaces of the Nasrid Palaces. Carved stucco, glazed tile and muqarnas — honeycomb-like vaulting — fill the walls and ceilings. Arabic inscriptions run through the patterns. They include verses of poetry and the Nasrid motto, "There is no victor but God."

Later rulers added to the site. Charles V, Holy Roman Emperor and king of Spain, commissioned a Renaissance palace inside the walls in the 16th century. Its square exterior and circular inner courtyard stand directly beside the Nasrid rooms.

The Generalife lies on a neighboring slope. It served as a country estate for the Nasrid rulers, with gardens and orchards fed by irrigation channels. Its Court of the Water Channel, or Patio de la Acequia, is built around a long pool lined with fountains.

The American writer Washington Irving lived in the Alhambra in 1829 and published Tales of the Alhambra in 1832. The book introduced many English-language readers to the site. UNESCO listed the Alhambra and the Generalife as a World Heritage site in 1984. In 1994, it extended the listing to include the Albaicín, the old quarter facing the hill.

Topkapı Palace in Istanbul was the main residence of the Ottoman sultans for roughly 400 years, from the 1460s into the 19th century. It now operates as a museum, and its Imperial Harem and Treasury are its best-known sections.

Sultan Mehmed II ordered the palace built after the Ottoman conquest of Constantinople in 1453. He chose Seraglio Point, a headland where the Golden Horn, the Bosphorus and the Sea of Marmara meet. The site overlooked the waterways that linked the capital to the rest of the empire.

The name Topkapı means "cannon gate" in Turkish. It comes from a gate in the seaside walls near the palace.

Topkapı was never a single large building. It grew as a series of courtyards, pavilions, kitchens and gates spread across the headland. Each courtyard was more restricted than the one before it. Outsiders could reach the outer areas, while the innermost zones belonged to the sultan and his household.

The palace was also the seat of government. The Imperial Council, known as the Divan, met in a domed chamber in the second courtyard to handle state business. That layout put the administration of the empire within steps of the sultan's private quarters.

The palace kitchens lie along the same courtyard. They now display a large collection of Chinese porcelain gathered by the Ottoman court.

The Imperial Harem was the household's private world. It housed the sultan's mother, his wives and concubines, his children and the staff who served them. The sultan's mother, the valide sultan, held considerable authority inside it. Its rooms form a dense network of corridors, small courtyards and tiled chambers.

The Treasury holds objects the dynasty accumulated over centuries. Its best-known pieces include the Topkapı Dagger, set with large emeralds, and the Spoonmaker's Diamond. The palace also keeps a collection of Islamic relics.

The sultans moved their main residence to Dolmabahçe Palace on the Bosphorus in the mid-19th century. Topkapı opened as a museum in 1924, soon after the founding of the Republic of Turkey. It is part of the Historic Areas of Istanbul, a UNESCO World Heritage site.

Buckingham Palace is the London residence and administrative headquarters of the British monarch. Visitors can see its State Rooms only during limited periods in summer, and the opening dates change from year to year.

The building began as Buckingham House, a town house built for John Sheffield, Duke $DUK of Buckingham, in 1703. George III acquired it in 1761 as a private residence for Queen Charlotte.

Its conversion into a palace came under George IV. He commissioned the architect John Nash, who enlarged the house in the 1820s. Edward Blore completed the work after Nash was dismissed.

Queen Victoria was the first sovereign to use the palace as her official residence. She moved in shortly after her accession in 1837.

The facade most people recognize came later still. The east front, facing The Mall, was refaced in Portland stone in 1913 to a design by Aston Webb. Its central balcony is where the royal family appears on public occasions such as Trooping the Colour.

The State Rooms are the formal spaces where the monarch receives and entertains guests. The Ballroom hosts state banquets for visiting heads of state, as well as investiture ceremonies. The visitor route also passes through the Grand Staircase and the Throne Room.

The Picture Gallery displays paintings from the Royal Collection, which the monarch holds in trust for successors and the nation. The collection includes works by Rembrandt, Rubens and Canaletto. The Royal Collection Trust runs the public openings.

The palace remains a working royal residence, not a former palace turned museum. Public access is restricted and varies with royal use. The summer opening covers the State Rooms only, not the palace as a whole.

The Changing the Guard ceremony takes place in the palace forecourt and can be watched from outside the railings. Anyone planning a visit to the State Rooms should check the current year's opening period. A previous summer's schedule is not a reliable guide to the next one.

Windsor Castle in Berkshire is a working royal residence that is also open to visitors, with access that varies around royal use. William the Conqueror founded it after the Norman Conquest, and St George's Chapel stands within its walls.

The castle started as a Norman fortification in the years after 1066. Its site is a chalk ridge above the River Thames, west of London. Later monarchs rebuilt and enlarged it many times. The Round Tower, raised on an artificial mound near the center, still marks the outline of the early fort.

The castle remains in active royal use. The monarch uses it as a residence and as a venue for state visits and other official occasions. Opening days and the rooms shown to the public can change when the castle is needed for royal business. Anyone planning a trip should expect access to vary through the year.

A standard visit centers on the State Apartments, a series of formal rooms used for ceremony and entertaining. They display paintings, furniture and other works from the Royal Collection. Queen Mary's Dolls' House, a detailed miniature home made in the 1920s, is also on show.

Fire caused heavy damage to part of the castle in November 1992, including St George's Hall. The restoration was completed in 1997. The castle also gave the royal family its current name. In 1917, George V adopted Windsor as the name of the royal house.

St George's Chapel occupies the castle's Lower Ward. Edward IV began building it in 1475, and it is an example of Perpendicular Gothic architecture. It is the spiritual home of the Order of the Garter, the order of chivalry founded by Edward III in 1348. The banners of Garter knights hang above the stalls in the quire.

The chapel holds several royal tombs. Henry VIII and Charles I are buried there. Queen Elizabeth II was buried in 2022 in the King George VI Memorial Chapel, a small annex of the main building. The chapel also hosts royal weddings, including that of Prince Harry and Meghan Markle in 2018. It remains a working church, with regular services held alongside visits.

The Palace of Holyroodhouse is the British monarch's official residence in Scotland, standing at the foot of the Royal Mile in Edinburgh. Visitors can tour it when the royal family is not in residence. Access therefore varies through the year.

The Royal Mile is the chain of streets that runs through Edinburgh's Old Town. Edinburgh Castle stands at its opposite end, on its volcanic rock. Together, the two buildings frame the historic spine of the Scottish capital.

Holyroodhouse grew out of a religious foundation. King David I founded Holyrood Abbey on the site in 1128. Scottish kings stayed in the abbey's guest quarters long before a dedicated palace existed. James IV began building a royal residence beside the abbey in the early 16th century. The roofless ruins of the abbey church still stand next to the palace.

Mary, Queen of Scots made Holyrood her main home during her personal reign in Scotland. She arrived there after returning from France in 1561. She married Lord Darnley at Holyrood in 1565. The following year, her private secretary David Rizzio was murdered in her apartments, in front of the queen. Her rooms sit in the palace's northwest tower, which survives from that era.

Much of what visitors see today dates from a later rebuilding. Charles II ordered the palace reconstructed in the 1670s. The architect Sir William Bruce designed the classical courtyard and surrounding ranges. His plan kept the older northwest tower and balanced it with a matching tower at the other end of the front.

The palace remains a working royal building. The monarch stays there for a period each summer, known as Holyrood Week, and hosts official engagements and investitures. Those functions are the reason the palace closes to visitors at certain times.

Holyrood Park and the hill called Arthur's Seat rise directly behind the palace grounds. The Scottish Parliament building stands across the road from the main gates. Royal, religious and political history all sit within a short walk of one another at this end of the city.

The Grand Palace in Bangkok was built from 1782 as the royal residence, when the city became Thailand's capital. Its walled grounds also contain Wat Phra Kaew, the Temple of the Emerald Buddha.

King Rama I, the first ruler of the Chakri dynasty, ordered the palace built after moving the capital across the Chao Phraya River from Thonburi. The complex stands on Rattanakosin Island, the historic center of Bangkok. Later kings added throne halls, pavilions and courtyards. The grounds now mix buildings from several periods.

The Chakri Maha Prasat Throne Hall shows that mix most clearly. It was completed in 1882 under King Chulalongkorn, Rama V. Its lower floors follow a European design, while its roofline carries traditional Thai spires.

The palace no longer serves as a day-to-day royal home. Later monarchs moved their living quarters to other palaces in the city. King Bhumibol Adulyadej, Rama IX, lived at Chitralada Palace during his reign. The Grand Palace is now used for royal ceremonies, state functions and official receptions.

Wat Phra Kaew sits within the outer court of the palace. It houses the Emerald Buddha, a small seated figure carved from green stone and the most revered Buddha image in Thailand. Unlike most Thai temples, Wat Phra Kaew has no living quarters for monks. It functions as the royal chapel.

The Emerald Buddha wears gold costumes that change with the seasons. A ceremony marks each change three times a year, for the hot, rainy and cool seasons. The king traditionally performs the change himself, or a royal representative does so in his place.

The cloisters around the temple are lined with murals of the Ramakien, the Thai version of the Indian epic the Ramayana. The painted scenes run along the gallery walls and enclose the temple compound on all sides.

Access to the complex varies. The palace remains in royal use, and some areas can close for ceremonies or state events. Many interiors, including the residential halls, are not open to the public. A dress code applies to all visitors. Official palace channels list current conditions for anyone planning a visit.

Peterhof Palace, founded by Peter the Great in the early 18th century, is best known for its fountains and the Grand Cascade. The estate lies west of Saint Petersburg on the southern shore of the Gulf of Finland.

Peter built Peterhof as a seaside residence for the Russian court. The main palace stands on a natural ridge above the coast. That change in height shaped the whole design. It gave engineers the drop they needed to move water across the grounds.

The Grand Cascade descends the slope in front of the Grand Palace. Gilded statues line its steps, and water spills down them into a basin. From there, the Sea Channel carries the flow in a straight line through the Lower Park to the gulf. The layout ties the residence directly to the sea.

The Samson fountain rises from the center of that basin. It shows the biblical figure prying open a lion's jaws. A tall jet of water shoots from the lion's mouth. The sculpture commemorates Russia's victory over Sweden at the Battle of Poltava in 1709.

The fountains run without pumps. Water gathers in ponds and reservoirs on higher ground inland. It then travels through canals and pipes to the park. Gravity supplies the pressure that drives the jets.

Peter also kept a smaller retreat at the water's edge called Monplaisir. The single-story house sits in the Lower Park. It is far more modest in scale than the Grand Palace on the ridge above it.

Later rulers expanded the estate. Under Empress Elizabeth, Peter's daughter, the architect Bartolomeo Francesco Rastrelli enlarged the Grand Palace in the mid-18th century.

German forces occupied Peterhof during World War II, and the palace and grounds were badly damaged. The original Samson figure disappeared during the occupation. A recreated statue was installed in 1947. Restoration of the wider estate continued for decades afterward.

Peterhof is a UNESCO World Heritage site. It is included in the listing for the Historic Centre of Saint Petersburg and Related Groups of Monuments.

Neuschwanstein Castle is a 19th-century Bavarian castle that King Ludwig II commissioned in 1869. It was still unfinished when he died in 1886. Visitors can see its interiors only on guided tours.

The castle stands on a rocky ridge above the Pöllat Gorge, near the town of Füssen in southern Bavaria. It sits at the edge of the Alps, close to the Austrian border. Hohenschwangau Castle stands on the slope below. Ludwig's father, King Maximilian II, rebuilt it, and Ludwig spent much of his childhood there.

Neuschwanstein was never meant for defense. It went up long after castles had lost their military purpose. Ludwig wanted a retreat that recreated an idealized medieval world. The theater designer Christian Jank worked on the early drawings. The architect Eduard Riedel drew up the first plans. The result is a Romanesque Revival building of towers and turrets, faced in pale limestone.

Richard Wagner's operas shaped the project. Ludwig was Wagner's patron. The interiors draw on the legends behind works such as Lohengrin and Tannhäuser. The swan, a symbol tied to the Lohengrin story, recurs throughout the decoration. The name Neuschwanstein translates roughly as "new swan stone." That name came into use only after Ludwig's death. During construction, the building was known as New Hohenschwangau Castle.

Two rooms reflect the sources behind the plan. The Singers' Hall on the upper floor was modeled partly on a hall at Wartburg Castle in Thuringia. The Throne Hall was designed in a Byzantine style, with a mosaic floor beneath a domed ceiling. A throne was never installed there.

Ludwig lived in the castle for only a short time. In 1886, the Bavarian government declared him unfit to rule and removed him from power. He died days later in Lake Starnberg. The circumstances of his death have never been fully explained.

Building work was scaled back after Ludwig died, and many planned rooms were never completed. The castle opened to paying visitors within weeks of his death. Ludwig had financed construction from his own funds and heavy borrowing rather than from the state treasury.

The Bavarian Palace Administration now manages the site. The Marienbrücke, a footbridge across the Pöllat Gorge, is named for Ludwig's mother, Queen Marie. It looks back at the castle from the opposite side of the ravine.

The Palace of Fontainebleau, southeast of Paris, served French monarchs from the Middle Ages to the 19th century. Francis I made it a major Renaissance site in the 16th century, and Napoleon later lived there. Today the palace is a national museum open to visitors.

Fontainebleau was not built in a single campaign for a single ruler. Successive kings added wings, courtyards and interiors over several centuries. The result is a complex where medieval, Renaissance, classical and imperial layers stand side by side.

The site began as a royal hunting base. The surrounding Forest of Fontainebleau gave French kings access to game within reach of Paris. That function kept them returning long before the palace took its present shape.

Francis I changed its character in the 16th century. He brought Italian artists to France to decorate the rebuilt residence, among them Rosso Fiorentino and Francesco Primaticcio. Their interiors combined painted frescoes, stucco relief and carved wood paneling. The style blended Italian Mannerism with French decorative traditions. The Gallery of Francis I is the main surviving example of that work.

The artists and craftsmen who worked at the palace became known as the School of Fontainebleau. Their decorative approach spread through French art. It helped carry Renaissance ideas from Italy into northern Europe.

Later monarchs kept using the palace for court business. Louis XIV signed the Edict of Fontainebleau there in 1685. That decree revoked the Edict of Nantes and ended legal toleration for French Protestants.

Napoleon took up the palace after the Revolution, which had stripped many royal residences of their contents. He restored and refurnished it as an imperial residence. Pope Pius VII was held there on Napoleon's orders from 1812 to 1814.

In April 1814, Napoleon signed his first abdication at Fontainebleau. He then bid farewell to his Old Guard in the main courtyard, at the foot of a horseshoe-shaped staircase. That space, the Cour du Cheval Blanc, is also called the Cour des Adieux after the event.

UNESCO inscribed the palace and its park as a World Heritage site in 1981. The grounds extend toward the same forest that first drew French kings to the site.

The Royal Palace of Madrid is the official residence of the Spanish royal family, but its use is limited to state ceremonies. The palace is also open to the public.

That ceremonial role sets it apart from royal buildings that still serve as homes. The palace works as a stage for Spain's formal state occasions rather than as a private household. Visitors walk through the setting of those events, not through rooms where a monarch lives day to day.

That dual role shapes how the building can be seen. The palace remains an official royal residence used for state business. Public access varies as a result, and anyone planning a visit should check current arrangements before going.

The present building dates from the 18th century. It stands on the site of the old Alcázar, the earlier royal structure that occupied the same ground. The palace replaced that structure, so the site has served Spain's crown through more than one building.

Amber Fort, also called Amer Fort, is a Rajput fort-palace built from red sandstone and marble on a hillside outside Jaipur. Raja Man Singh I began construction in 1592. The complex is now part of the UNESCO World Heritage listing for the Hill Forts of Rajasthan.

The fort stands above the town of Amer, north of Jaipur's walled old city. It served as the seat of the Kachwaha rulers for generations. The dynasty later moved its capital to the newly founded city of Jaipur in 1727, under Sawai Jai Singh II.

Man Singh I was a Kachwaha ruler and one of the senior commanders at the court of the Mughal emperor Akbar. That link shows in the building. Rajput defensive planning sets the outer form, with high walls following the ridgeline. The courtyards, audience halls and formal gardens inside show clear Mughal influence.

The work did not stop with its founder. Mirza Raja Jai Singh I expanded the palace in the 17th century. Later rulers added further rooms. The complex reflects several building phases rather than a single design.

The palace rises in a sequence of courtyards, each more private than the one before. Visitors arrive in Jaleb Chowk, the large public square where troops once assembled. From there, a stairway leads up to the Diwan-i-Aam, the hall of public audience, with its rows of columns.

The Ganesh Pol, a painted gateway, marks the passage into the royal family's private quarters. Beyond it lies the Sheesh Mahal, or mirror palace. Its walls and ceilings are covered with inlaid glass and mirror work.

Maota Lake sits at the foot of the hill below the fort. A terraced garden called Kesar Kyari was laid out on a platform in the water. Higher on the same ridge stands Jaigarh Fort, joined to Amber by fortified walls.

UNESCO inscribed the Hill Forts of Rajasthan in 2013. The serial listing groups six forts: Chittorgarh, Kumbhalgarh, Ranthambore, Gagron, Jaisalmer and Amber. Together they represent Rajput military architecture adapted to hilltops, rivers, forests and desert terrain.

Mysore Palace in the southern Indian state of Karnataka was the residence of the Wadiyar dynasty, the rulers of the Kingdom of Mysore. The building that stands today was completed in 1912.

That completion date places the current structure in the 20th century. The 1912 date refers to this building specifically, not to the Wadiyar seat as an institution.

The palace takes its name from the kingdom the dynasty governed. Its role as the Wadiyar residence is the reason it was built.

The palace is best known for its lighting. On Sundays and holidays, bulbs fitted across the building are switched on after dark. They trace the outline of the palace against the night sky.

The display follows a regular schedule tied to the calendar. Visitors who want to see the palace lit should plan around a Sunday or a holiday.

Opening times, ticketing and access arrangements are not covered here. Check them with the palace before planning a trip.
