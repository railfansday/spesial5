The ADAA Fair Is Back, and Sometimes Smaller Really Is Better

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

Art fairs should do two things: let visitors see art, ideally stuff they haven’t seen before, and help dealers sell that art to people who want to buy it. Simple enough. And yet, somewhere along the way, fairs became so enormous that seeing everything on offer began to feel like an endurance sport.

That’s one reason the newly rechristened Art Dealers Association of America Fair, which opened Thursday night at the Park Avenue Armory, was such a pleasure. With just 63 exhibitors, it’s small enough to navigate without a map, manageable enough to spend time actually looking at the art, and varied enough to reward a little wandering. These may sound like modest achievements, but anyone who’s spent six hours trudging through a convention center will appreciate them. The setting helps. The Park Avenue Armory still has plenty of its old New York charm, something that’s increasingly hard to come by in an art fair. Organizers have also moved the bar to the center of the exhibition hall, improving the sightlines and making the whole affair feel more open. You can actually see what’s going on across the room, rather than navigating a maze of booths.

Of course, the fair’s return wasn’t entirely straightforward. Last July, the AADA abruptly canceled its 2025 edition, calling the decision a “strategic pause.” The organization subsequently retired the Art Show name and ended its decades-long fundraising partnership with the Henry Street Settlement, which had received more than $38 million through the fair’s benefit previews. The Whitney Museum of American Art is now the beneficiary, and the fair has returned with 12 fewer exhibitors and what seems to be emphasis on its national membership.

The reasons behind the sudden cancellation remain something of a mystery (and there were no shortage of rumors percolating about what happened with the fair’s old partner, Henry Street) but whatever the circumstances of its reinvention, the result is a fair that gets at least one of those two basic things right.

As for the selling, well, that’s up to the dealers.

Kinsey Robb, the ADAA’s executive director, was spritely and in good spirits at Thursday’s gala, quite literally shining even, thanks to her skirt, which was decorated with what she described as little chandeliers. It was a new purchase and even spent some time at the tailor before the evening’s debut. Organizing a fair, she explained, comes with plenty of highs and lows. Sometimes a little retail therapy helps.

Robb, who took over the association in September 2024, has spent much of the past year talking about making the fair more valuable to its members. But when I asked whether that meant exhibitors were actually paying less to participate, she offered a rather different definition of value.

“I think it’s sales, sure,” she said, “but the real benefit was the connections dealers make with one another and with collectors.” She described a collegial atmosphere during installation, with dealers more than pleased to see old friends and actually interested in what their neighbors were showing.

That might sound a little sentimental, but Robb makes a reasonable point. The association represents a large network of galleries, many of them small businesses whose concerns are rather different from those of the industry’s multinational behemoths. The fair, she argued, gives them somewhere to meet, exchange ideas, and share their expertise.

“It’s not my fair,” she said. “It’s the members’ fair.”

Fortunately, there was evidence that the members were doing business, too.

According to sales figures released by the ADAA following Thursday’s preview, Jonathan Boos reported two of the evening’s biggest transactions: Agnes Pelton’s The Well of Peace (1932), with an asking price of $2.4 million, and a Beauford Delaney painting priced at $1.6 million.

The action wasn’t confined to the million-dollar end of the market. AH Arts sold a Helen Frankenthaler work on paper for a reported $75,000, while James Barron Art placed works by Beverly Pepper, Kenneth Noland, and Kikuo Saito, with asking prices ranging from $40,000 to $85,000.

Contemporary art found buyers as well. Jessica Silverman reported selling eight works by Julie Buffalohead, priced between $22,000 and $50,000, while Charles Moffett sold all four of its Lily Stockman paintings at $20,000 apiece and four works by Julia Jo priced between $30,000 and $40,000.

Kraushaar Galleries, meanwhile, reported two six-figure sales alongside several works priced below $2,000. That’s a fairly remarkable spread, and perhaps a better illustration of the ADAA’s appeal than another speech about the importance of community.

Of course, these are dealer-reported sales, and asking prices don’t necessarily reflect what buyers actually paid. Still, they suggest that at least some collectors came to do more than admire the art and enjoy the open bar.

And there was plenty worth admiring. Here are five booths that caught my eye.

At Gavlak, two generations of artists offer very different takes on light, color, and the cosmos. Lita Albuquerque, a veteran of California’s Light and Space and Land Art movements, presents a series of luminous paintings in which gold-leaf discs appear to float against fields of saturated blue, black, and crimson.

In Goya’s First Breath (2025), a pale gold oval glows against a black background, looking something like a distant planet glimpsed through the darkness.

Across the booth, Jessica Cannon takes a more intricate approach. Her paintings are built from overlapping geometric forms and thousands of tiny marks that create shimmering, almost woven surfaces. In August Radii (2026), bands of peach, orange, pink, and green spiral around a central point, while Razor Moon (2026) offers a cooler composition of blues and purples.

The two artists make an excellent pairing. Albuquerque’s paintings seem to radiate light from within, while Cannon’s appear to catch and scatter it. They’re also the sort of works that reward seeing them in person, changing subtly as you move around them. Albuquerque’s paintings are priced between $90,000 and $125,000, while Cannon’s range from $25,000 to $40,000.

Alex Katz is one of those artists whose work is so instantly recognizable that it’s easy to forget how much thinking goes into making something look so effortless.

Peter Blum’s booth, devoted entirely to Katz’s works on paper, offers a particularly enjoyable reminder. Spanning roughly seven decades, the presentation brought together early cut-paper collages and later charcoal and graphite drawings.

One particularly charming little collage, Untitled (ca. 1957–58), shows four figures relaxing on a nearly deserted beach. A thin strip of dark green landscape cuts across the horizon, while the figures, rendered in tiny scraps of colored paper, occupy only a fraction of the composition. Most of it is simply sand.

It’s all very Katz, despite measuring just five by eight inches. There’s something wonderful about how little he needs to establish a scene.

The more recent drawings, including studies of flowers, reveal the same economy of line. Very little unnecessary information, yet somehow everything you need is there.

Eric Firestone, as usual, put together an excellent booth of seven women associated with the New York School, including Helen Frankenthaler, Pat Passlof, Elise Asher, Miriam Schapiro, Sari Dienes, Adaline Kent, and Jeanne Reynal.

It’s a compelling collection of artists whose contributions to postwar American art were frequently overshadowed by those of their male contemporaries. More importantly, the works themselves are terrific, and the presentation makes a convincing case for how much experimentation was happening beyond the familiar names of Abstract Expressionism.

Among the highlights is a small, previously unexhibited 1951 landscape by Frankenthaler, made with oil and pencil on a piece of palette paper. It’s a fascinating glimpse of the artist before the breakthrough that would transform her practice the following year.

There’s also Dienes’s Burnt Totem (1961), a monumental assemblage made from discarded wooden hat forms, and Passlof’s Keeping Still Mountain, a large, restless abstraction whose title comes from the I Ching.

The booth is a firm nod to the fact that there is a lot of art history that remains to be reconsidered, even when the period in question has been studied to death.

Maureen O’Leary’s paintings at Cristin Tierney are wonderfully strange. At first glance, her bright colors, simplified figures, and seemingly cheerful domestic scenes suggest something almost storybook-like. Spend another minute looking, though, and things become considerably less straightforward.

In Spring (2026), a backyard gathering unfolds around a barbecue, with children, animals, and plants crowded into a busy, brightly colored landscape. Flames leap from the grill while the surrounding figures seem to inhabit slightly different versions of reality. There are echoes of the Nabis and Post-Impressionists in the flattened perspective and improbable colors, but O’Leary’s world is distinctly her own.

What makes the paintings work is the tension between their familiar subject matter and the peculiar atmosphere she creates. These are scenes of ordinary life, but ordinary life as remembered in a particularly vivid dream.

The presentation coincides with The Linden Trees, O’Leary’s second solo exhibition at the gallery, which runs through December 19.

Sculpture can be a difficult proposition at an art fair, where space is limited and paintings tend to get most of the attention. But Piero Atchugarry’s solo presentation of Brazilian artist Túlio Pinto is worth seeking out.

Pinto works with stone, steel, and glass, arranging materials in configurations that appear to defy the laws of physics without actually doing so. A heavy stone rests on a chain made of glass. Elsewhere, an enormous steel beam appears to depend on another delicate glass chain to remain upright.

In Complicity #52, rust-colored steel beams grip enormous amber glass forms, while a large piece of rose quartz hangs precariously from one end. The whole structure looks as though it ought to collapse, which is precisely what makes it so compelling.

The four works on view span several years of Pinto’s practice, including pieces from his Complicity Vectors series and more recent Gravitas sculptures. They share an interest in balance, resistance, and the sometimes surprising strength of materials we ordinarily think of as fragile.

It’s a smart, physically engaging, and exactly the sort of thing that makes a smaller art fair worth visiting.
