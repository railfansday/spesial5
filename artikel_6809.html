“There's this idea that talking about sex beforehand somehow takes away from the spontaneity or excitement. I actually think the opposite. Having these conversations is what allows people to feel safe enough to explore,” says Sarah Kelleher, LCSW, a sex and relationship psychotherapist.

After all, the appeal of kink might involve stepping outside your comfort zone, but that doesn't mean anyone gets to decide where your boundaries are except you.

In BDSM, limits are the boundaries that determine what you're comfortable doing during sexual play, what you're not interested in, and what might require a little more discussion. They're especially important when exploring activities involving power, control, or physical sensations, where one person's idea of a good time might be another person's absolute nightmare.

Hard limits are firm, non-negotiable boundaries. They're the things you absolutely don't want to do, regardless of the circumstances. For example, someone might be comfortable with light spanking but draw the line at anything that leaves bruises. Others might rule out humiliation, certain types of penetration, or specific language during dirty talk. Whatever the reason, a hard limit means no. Full stop.

Importantly, a soft limit isn't permission to keep asking until someone changes their mind. “'Soft' doesn't mean optional, negotiable by default, or something that can be ignored,” says Lisa Finn, a sex educator at Babeland. And while limits are particularly important in BDSM, they're not exclusive to kink. Everyone has sexual boundaries, whether they're experimenting with elaborate power dynamics or sticking to their favorite tried-and-true positions.

But don't assume every maybe is secretly a yes waiting to happen. Kelleher recommends getting curious about why something makes you hesitant. Are you nervous because it's unfamiliar, or because you genuinely don't want to do it? There's a difference between feeling excited about a new experience and feeling like you should be excited about it.

It can also help to pay attention to how your body responds when you imagine certain activities. Tomchesson suggests noticing changes in your breathing, tension, or overall sense of comfort. And what if your partner's limits don't line up with yours? That doesn't mean someone needs to give in. “I actually prefer the word collaboration over compromise when we're talking about sex,” says Kelleher. Rather than trying to convince someone to participate in an activity they're uncomfortable with, look for other ways to explore the desires you share.
