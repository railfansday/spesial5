Legendary commentator MARTIN TYLER on taking the Premier League plunge in 1992, Arsene Wenger's fury, his love of Woking - and THAT Sergio Aguero moment

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

Published: 13:55 EST, 18 November 2026 | Updated: 15:04 EST, 18 November 2026

Two football images adorn the walls of Martin Tyler’s new home or at least they will when everything is in order.

Tyler has recently moved house and the large, framed photograph of Teddy Sheringham scoring for Nottingham Forest against Liverpool, the only goal of the first Premier League game broadcast live by Sky is still on the floor by the front door.

From wherever it ends up, it will project a moment in time when everything changed for Tyler, having walked away from a steady job at ITV and taken a leap of faith as lead commentator for the new satellite channel.

'A new frontier,' admits Tyler, the undisputed ‘Voice of the Premier League’ by the time he left the role at Sky, more than 30 years later.

'We didn’t know where it would take us, so that was a special day. Feeling a bit like a pioneer. Wondering whether we were all on a fool’s errand. Some people turned down the job I accepted because of the risk. My kids were four and two at the time.

'We used to travel to the games by train in those days, and we would look out of the window and count the satellite dishes as we passed through a big town. We didn’t know whether it was going to work. I wasn’t sure anybody would be watching.

'It was slow off the ground and there was a feeling within the game against live football, that people wouldn’t go to the games if they were televised live, and it ended up the opposite. Attendances have gone up, an amazing paradox. Now you can’t get a ticket.'

Televised football has come a long way since Tyler’s first commentary game, Southampton v Sheffield Wednesday in the old Division Two in 1974, shot with just four cameras for Southern Television.

'Now if you don’t have 24 cameras you can feel a bit short-changed,' he laughs. 'I worked at ITV for about 17 years, most of it commentating and you would get a crew on match day who had been doing Coronation Street or Emmerdale Farm in the week and a match director from light entertainment.'

The Premier League has come a long way, too. Some might argue too far. It has led football from its roots as the working man’s game to a place where clubs are owned by nations states and mired in the legal complications of the Manchester City case.

Tyler wants to reserve comment on City’s financial offences until sentencing is passed, aware he is inextricably bound to their success because of his iconic commentary for Sergio Aguero’s winner in the 93rd minute against Queen’s Park Rangers to snatch the Premier League title from Manchester United in 2012.

'I’ve always said you won’t see anything like it again, and I stick to that,' he says, although he the 'Aguero moment’ as it has become commonly known probably means less to him than it does to others.

He is proud to deliver the words but insists those moments belong to the players, the clubs and the fans, not to him.

Ask about his favourite game from three decades in the Premier League and Tyler instinctively pulls Liverpool’s 4-3 comeback win against Newcastle in 1996 from the thrillers he has called.

His own footballing background, meanwhile, could hardly be any further from the Premier League’s glitzy, moneyed elite.

Tyler has always supported Woking and played as an amateur for Corinthian-Casuals. 'The law was you couldn’t get paid,' he says. 'We were on expenses only and they weren’t dodgy expenses. If I went on the tube to Leytonstone, I got the tube fare repaid and no more.

'I played for five years at that level and never got a penny because that was the rule. I played my last game in 1973 and within two years’ they got rid of it because so many people were bending the rules. An opposition player told me their team were on £75 a game.'

From a cricketing family, Tyler was on the books at Surrey where he twice served as 12th man and lived for a time with Bob Willis, who became one of England’s greatest fast bowlers after moving on to Warwickshire.

His manager for a time at Casuals was Surrey legend Micky Stewart, who had also played for the famous amateur football club before turning professional at Charlton. In those days it was possible for the multi-talented to hold down a decent career in two sports.

An afternoon in Tyler’s company is just as likely to involve digressions into non-league football, where he worked as an assistant to Alan Dowson at clubs including Woking and Hampton & Richmond, as it is into the Premier League.

He might regale you with a story about Arsene Wenger and the time, on the way to the gantry at Wigan Athletic, he slipped deeper than he realised into long conversation with the then Arsenal boss about rioting in Paris and almost missed his pre-match slot.

And he might pivot from there into Wenger’s fierce rivalry with Sir Alex Ferguson. 'I have seen Arsene very, very grumpy,' says Tyler. 'When he lost the FA Cup semi-final when Dennis Bergkamp missed the penalty and Ryan Giggs scored.

'I have never seen a manager so angry because he knew he had handed over not only an FA Cup final place but also the domination they enjoyed at that time. They had done the Double in 98 and he felt that was not only about losing a match but losing their psychological edge over their greatest rivals. He was livid. Talk about winning and losing with dignity.'

But there’s every chance he will reminisce about the time when, as a schoolboy Woking supporter in the terrible winter of 1962-63 he was rewarded for volunteering to paint the stands with an invitation to travel on the team bus to an Amateur Cup tie at Lydbrook Athletic.

'We left Woking at seven on the morning of the match for a two o’clock kick-off in Gloucestershire and by nine we were digging the bus out of a snowdrift on the A4,' says Tyler. 'We got there at four, no floodlights, and they had marked the lines on the snow with blue soap powder but there was no way we could play. We turned around to go back, got stuck in Cheltenham and had to stay for the night.'

The only other footballing image on display in Tyler’s home is a photograph of him and Dowson celebrating as the final whistle confirmed their Hampton & Richmond side as Isthmian League champions in 2016.

After collecting the trophy, Tyler jumped into his car, drove north and less than 24 hours later was at the King Power Stadium to see Leicester crowned champions of England.

'The greatest fairytale story in the Premier League,' as he says. 'I had sampled that in a tiny non-league way, then taken those feelings with me to Leicester where it was multiplied a million times, but I could tell the world a tiny bit about how wonderful they must be feeling.'

The range of his experience is vast. From amateur football in the 60s, through regional television and ITV to Sky’s Premier League revolution. He commentates regularly and coaches at Woking’s academy and has written a book.

It is a wonderful compilation of 110 essays, the reflections on his lifetime in football. 'The random thoughts of a football obsessive,' as he likes to call it.
