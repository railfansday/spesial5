These are some of the appointments documented in health secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr.’s calendar. In a show of “radical transparency,” HHS released the secretary’s partial calendar, which reflects his activities from Feb. 13, 2025, to June 19, 2026.

The calendar — part of a much broader release of documents from the Department of Health and Human Services — includes significant redactions and missing information. It shows, for example, only one meeting with one of his closest aides, Stefanie Spear. What else did our reporters find in this treasure trove of 92,000 documents? Read on.

This morning, the Army plans to detonate a cache of “dangerously degraded” toxic material in Iowa. Experts warn the planned explosion could send particles of lead into the air; an Army analysis found a plume could travel up to 100 miles, impacting homes and 350 farms.

The issue is attracting the attention of any number of federal agencies. The explosion’s after-effects are being tracked by the Environmental Protection Agency; FEMA is supporting possible evacuations. And Kennedy has weighed in several times from the perspective of HHS. Last week, former Iowa gubernatorial candidate Zach Lahn posted on X that he had spoken with Kennedy about the planned detonation and that the secretary connected him with the White House to express his concerns. On Tuesday, Kennedy said on X that HHS is “working with state and local officials to assess potential lead exposure and support Iowa’s public health response,” adding that health department officials will “remain on the ground afterward to support the response and assist our partners with necessary cleanup.”

With MAHA advocates expressing concern about the planned detonation, it could be an opportunity for Kennedy to reassert the primacy of the movement. As my colleague Chelsea Cirruzzo put it in a recent story: “Even as Republicans pick up the pieces following devastating midterm election losses, spirits are high at the Department of Health and Human Services, where recent changes in the administration — namely the appointment of Stephen Miller as White House chief of staff — have put wind under the wings of health secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr.”

Even as judges have blocked visa restrictions and other limits on foreign scholars and scientists, the Trump administration is winning outside court. New data show a clear drop in the enrollment of international graduate students, a key piece of the U.S. biomedical workforce.

That paradox was on full display last week. On Nov. 16, a federal judge issued a final ruling blocking a proposed four-year cap on how long international students and postdoctoral researchers can remain in the country. The next day, a judge in a separate case issued a preliminary injunction preventing the administration from narrowing eligibility for a work authorization program widely used by foreign students.

But data released the same day as the ruling in the visa time limit case paint a different picture. A survey of more than 800 U.S. institutions conducted by the Institute of International Education found that foreign student enrollment for the current academic year has decreased 9% overall compared with 2025, with a 15% drop among newly enrolled students and graduate students. The findings, published this week in the New England Journal of Medicine, likely reflect “continuing vaccine hesitancy and confusion among parents after recent changes in recommendations,” the authors write. (In December 2025, a CDC panel remade by Kennedy recommended delaying the birth dose of the vaccine.)

A STAT piece from last year helps illuminate how the hepatitis B vaccine birth dose nearly eliminated the disease — which can cause chronic liver disease and even death — in kids and teens. “Transmission can happen in the household or at day care, in the infinitesimal-seeming blood exposure that might stem from a razor nick or shared toothbrush, a bite or scratch from another kid,” my former colleague Eric Boodman wrote, adding that “there are antivirals that can help control hepatitis B, but there is no cure. Once people have it, they have it for life.”

If you, like me, are unwilling to splurge on anything besides the most basic Hulu package, you’ve probably had to sit through countless drug commercials.

Turns out, those brightly colored spots about the benefits of GLP-1s for weight loss might not be so great for public health. In a new survey of 1,000 people, researchers found that adults exposed to higher levels of GLP-1 ads were more self-conscious about their weight and appearance. The study also showed, likely to the delight of advertisers everywhere, that people who’d seen more GLP-1 commercials were more likely to want to try GLP-1s.
