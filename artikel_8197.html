The Rules of Trail Running Etiquette, from the ATRA President

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

As the president of the American Trail Running Association (ATRA)—a title that sounds more impressive than it is—and co-author of The Ultimate Guide to Trail Running,” which is now in its third edition, I know a little about the proper behavior of trail runners. And as a six-decade trail veteran myself, I also know a little about the improper behavior of trail runners.

To me, the “rules” of trail running are quite simple and boil down to two principles: respect nature, and respect other users.

Those two tenets blossom into about 10 “strong suggestions” for sustainable, copasetic trail experiences, but, like with the Golden Rule, it all comes down to showing respect. Here are the basics of what you should know when you hit the trails:

Close any gates you open, don’t disturb the wildlife or livestock, and respect private land and get permission to enter.

Take only photos and pack out at least as much as you brought in, including gel wrappers. If needed, wear shorts, a jacket, or hydration pack with zip pockets to stow rubbish found on the trail.

Don’t disturb wildlife as frightened animals may be dangerous. Steer clear of trails that disturb sensitive habitats during nesting or mating seasons. When passing horses, use special care and follow directions from the horseback riders. Consider turning around and going another direction when faced with large herds of animals.

Don’t play your music loudly, engage in boisterous conversations with your fellow runners (or through speakerphone), or listen to headphones so loudly that you are unaware of your surroundings. Along the same lines, do your social media influencing elsewhere. Nature is not your recording studio. We don’t want to hear you, and you ought to be able to hear others.

We expect the same of cyclists. This should be obvious but, then again, all of this should be.

As trail runners, we move relatively quickly, especially when we seemingly emerge from nowhere on unsuspecting trail users. This may be alarming, so anticipate and give a courteous and audible announcement of your presence and intention to pass hikers on the trail by stating something along the lines of, “On your left,” or “Trail,” as you approach. Keep an eye out for those wearing headphones, as they won’t hear your warning. Be courteous when passing, slowing down or stopping if necessary to prevent accidental contact. Be ready to yield to all other trail users (cyclists, hikers, horses) even if you have the posted right of way.

This is likely to be controversial, but consider not bringing your dog on group runs and, when you do run with your dogs, be sure to leash them and keep them under your control when necessary to prevent any wildlife disturbances. Be sure to pack out their (and your) waste.

Be cognizant that off-leash dogs often chase wildlife and degrade the outdoor experience of other trail users.

Greet and say “Excuse me” to those you see and pass on the trail. This should be a common practice if for no other reason than to alert others to your presence and not frighten anyone. Similarly, be sure to stay in control of your speed, especially when you could cause rocks or other trail features to fall.

If you startle someone, apologize, and thank them if they’ve stepped aside. A little kindness goes a long way on the trail.

Don’t cut the switchbacks or widen trails by running around puddles,downed trees, or when it is too muddy

Don’t create your own trails by running off preexisting, well-marked trails. When multiple trails exist, run on the most worn. Stay off closed trails and obey all postings.

Run single file in the middle of a trail, even if it’s laden with a fresh blanket of snow or thick mud. Go over, not around, obstacles like puddles,rocks, or downed tree limbs, as that widens trails, damages vegetation, and causes unnecessary erosion.

In other words, participate in trail maintenance and give back to the trail community.

In a now-infamous Outside opinion piece of seven years ago that went viral, Marc Peruzzi claimed that trail runners are lazy parasites. Ultrarunner and lawyer Stephanie Case wrote a response piece, saying more could be done, agreeing with my stance that “we are guilty as charged … we need [to do] more. A lot more.” Case continued, “Indeed, as trail running continues to increase in popularity, it will become even more important that we expand our volunteer and conservation efforts.”

So, carry your weight and do trail work. Volunteer. Go plogging on your local trails.

Technically, those ascending are supposed to have the right of way but, in practice, people often yield to descenders, likely due to speed and momentum. Be polite, maintain control, and be prepared to yield to other users, including horseback riders.

Split larger groups into smaller groups as bigger numbers can intimidate other users and have a greater environmental impact on trails.

If you come across someone in distress, stop and check whether they need help.

Don’t become a liability by taking risks, getting lost, or ignoring trail closures that require dangerous rescues. Carry proper clothing, nutrition, and hydration so as not to put yourself at risk of exposure. Safety first is always a good rule.

If running alone, familiarize yourself with the area where you plan to run and let at least one other person know your intended route. Be prepared for the weather and conditions when you start your run and plan for the worst, given the likely duration of your run. Know and respect your limits and report unsafe conditions and activities to the proper authorities.

Trail running is beautifully simple. So is being a courteous trail runner. Follow these tips for proper etiquette and keep the trails safe, welcoming, and wild!
