Are LLMs Really More Empathetic Than Humans?

Posted November 6, 2026 | Reviewed by Tyler Woods

Barely a week goes by without reports of a newly uncovered impact of artificial intelligence (AI) on human psychology: “blandification” (Abdulhai et al., 2026), “cognitive debt” (Kosmyna et al., 2025), and “system 3 thinking” (Shaw & Nave, 2026) are among the terms that have been coined to describe large language model (LLM)-influenced impacts. While conducting a systematic review, I looked at 11 experiments from six different studies that have examined LLM-human engagements that focus on, or contain, a strong component related to emotions. The findings from four of these studies are worth reflecting on, with some strong claims being made about LLM capabilities in the realm of emotional support and emotional guidance.

Chen et al. (2026) investigated how effective LLMs were in offering emotional support when compared with humans. Across three studies, the authors report that LLMs were arguably more effective than humans at assisting in emotional regulation strategies; they used emotional validation 50% more often than humans, as well as affirmation and positive reframing. In their third experiment, they reported that LLMs were more effective at suggesting strategies to increase happiness and reduce anger. In their three experiments, Wenger et al. (2026) identified a pattern they termed “the AI empathy choice paradox”. On the one hand, their first experiment reported that humans largely preferred supportive responses from humans as opposed to LLMs. In their second study, however, they found that humans reported LLM responses as more empathetic.

Findings vary somewhat when examining the study by Kleinert et al. (2026). The authors do not share my skepticism as reflected in the article’s self-explanatory title: “AI outperforms humans in establishing interpersonal closeness in emotionally engaging interactions, but only when labelled as human”. Their headline speaks to the “AI empathy choice paradox” and seems to add support to it. In their discussion, the authors write: “Strikingly, AI-generated content outperformed human-generated content in establishing feelings of closeness during emotionally engaging deep-talk interactions (including topics such as the most treasured memory of your life…” Herein lies a source of deep incongruence: what exactly would “the most treasured memory” of an LLM be? The authors themselves note that “AI-generated responses showed higher levels of self-disclosure, which, in turn, also encouraged more self-disclosure by participants.” What the authors dismiss as anti-AI bias might instead be termed aversion to incongruence. There is an argument to be made here that LLMs, or certain LLMs, might be designed to prioritize appeasement over truth.

A more challenging conclusion came from Roshanei et al. (2026), who found a deficit in LLM empathy: “its empathetic responses can appear overly intense or unrealistic, often beyond what the situation or emotional context requires”. This arguably supports the position that what we are witnessing is less about empathy and more about appeasement. Further support for this is found in the previously mentioned finding in Chen et al. (2026) that LLMs offered more emotional affirmations than humans: does receiving affirmations always feel good? Probably. Are they always appropriate and best for us? Probably not.

It is worth recalling the methodology being used to assess factors like LLM capacity for empathy and emotional mastery: humans being presented with text, usually on a screen, that is composed by an LLM or a human being. Reading off a screen and assessing the value of those words accurately reflects one important component of human life, but it certainly does not reflect human life as a whole. Thinking more specifically about emotions and empathy, these psychological phenomena carry a linguistic element, but they are usually much broader than only that, carrying with them affective and behavioral components, even olfactory aspects, as well as forces like co-presence. As things stand, LLM empathy expresses itself on only one side of these multisided forces. Perhaps the fact that we are seemingly so receptive to it is an indication of how much of our lives has been flattened to words and images on a screen. Were it not for the gradual shift of increasing amounts of our lives onto screens, the idea of LLM empathy might be something bewildering. That is, of course, not to say that LLM word generation cannot be impressive, and can even carry adaptive psychological uses, but it will be some time before we really know its effects, and the effects of being appeased and placated by technology might be quietly impactful. Robust longitudinal studies are some way off yet, and until they start to emerge, self-experimentation–or at least self-awareness–might prove to be protective.
