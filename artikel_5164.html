64K Bitcoin Miners Hijacked: Why the Attack Failed to Shake BTC

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Home » Bitcoin (BTC) » 64K Bitcoin Miners Hijacked: Why the Attack Failed to Shake BTC

An AI-assisted exploit of Luxor’s software briefly diverted 1.4% of Bitcoin’s global computing power.

An AI-assisted exploit of Luxor’s fleet-management software briefly diverted 1.4% of Bitcoin’s global computing power, netting the attacker over $10,500 while leaving the blockchain itself untouched.

A coordinated weekend cyberattack hijacked approximately 64,000 Bitcoin mining machines, temporarily redirecting up to 13 exahashes per second (EH/s) of computing power to an unauthorized address on the OCEAN mining pool.

While the incident exposed a major vulnerability in third-party mining management tools, the Bitcoin protocol itself was never compromised, and the diverted hashrate fell far short of the threshold needed to disrupt the blockchain.

Bitcoin mining hosting provider CoreX Hosting was the first to flag the suspicious activity on November 14, warning on X that thousands of machines were suddenly connecting to the OCEAN mining pool under a single external payout address.

Just a heads up, there is a big miner hack going on as we speak. Miners are being pointed at @ocean_mining with BTC address bc1qphukp9rjfc4rcej7erpm9ae20xmtspw8pcddrr .

Less than an hour later, approximately 13 EH/s of computing power, equivalent to about 1.4% of the entire Bitcoin network’s global hashrate, was pointing toward the attacker’s wallet. CoreX said it was working to restore the affected machines’ original mining configurations.

By 1:40 a.m. ET on November 15, CoreX confirmed the attack had been contained. The hashrate tied to the rogue address plunged from 13 EH/s down to roughly 100 petahashes per second (PH/s), signaling that nearly all compromised rigs had been recovered or disconnected.

Initially, blockchain trackers noted that the attacker had failed to mine a single block during the brief hijack window, suggesting the heist had yielded zero profit. However, because of how OCEAN’s payout model works, the attacker still managed to walk away with a modest payday.

Under OCEAN’s reward distribution rules, valid mining shares submitted during an active window remain eligible for payouts when the pool subsequently finds blocks.

On-chain tracking later confirmed that the attacker’s address received payouts totaling between 0.162 BTC (roughly $10,500) and 0.243 BTC across two to three block rewards before the eligible work window expired.

While the attacker’s haul was relatively small compared to the massive computing power hijacked, affected mining operators lost real-time revenue during the outage while continuing to pay electricity costs.

The incident took another turn on November 15, when mining services company Luxor confirmed that its Commander mining equipment management software had been exploited to redirect hashrate.

In a post on X, Luxor said its internal monitoring detected unauthorized pool reassignments affecting “just over a dozen” client accounts. Engineers patched the vulnerability within two hours and began reverting customer rigs to their original pools. Luxor emphasized that no user data was leaked and that its core mining pool and LuxOS firmware remained unaffected.

Last night our internal monitoring alerted us to an unauthorized redirect of hashrate through Commander. Our on-call engineering team jumped on it right away.Impact: just over a dozen client accounts. Affected machines were pointed to a different pool for a limited window. No…

In a follow-up statement, Luxor CEO Nick Hansen apologized to affected clients and said the attack involved a “sophisticated AI-enabled vector.”

He warned that AI-powered cyberattacks are becoming an increasing threat to crypto infrastructure, with automated systems probing for vulnerabilities around the clock.

Luxor promised to fully reimburse affected clients for lost mining revenue, paying them as if their machines had continued mining normally.

The incident exposes a vulnerability in Bitcoin’s mining infrastructure that exists outside the blockchain itself. Still, it never posed a systemic threat to Bitcoin: controlling the network requires over 50% of global computing power.

At its peak, the hijacked hashrate accounted for just ~1.4% of the network and lasted only a few hours—nowhere near enough to alter transactions or double-spend coins.

Because no user wallets were drained and Bitcoin’s consensus rules operated normally, the breach had no direct impact on Bitcoin’s market price, which hovered near the $64,500–$65,000 range amid broader macro-driven market volatility.
