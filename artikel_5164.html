Understanding How Decentralized Finance Protocols Work

DeFi, or decentralized finance, works through smart contracts that coordinate financial activity on-chain. Instead of relying on a centralized intermediary, DeFi protocols use liquidity pools, automated market makers, collateral rules, oracles, governance systems, and composable protocol integrations to support trading, lending, yield strategies, derivatives, and other financial functions.

DeFi 201 is intended for readers who already understand crypto basics and want to understand how DeFi protocols actually work. It moves beyond beginner definitions and into the systems that keep decentralized finance running: exchanges, lending markets, yield strategies, oracle networks, derivatives, governance, and composability.

DeFi is best understood as a connected protocol system rather than a list of isolated apps. Its main components include decentralized exchanges, lending protocols, yield aggregators, oracles, decentralized derivatives, governance, and composability.

DeFi means decentralized finance. It refers to financial protocols that run through blockchains and smart contracts rather than traditional intermediaries. In DeFi, users can trade, lend, borrow, supply liquidity, manage collateral, vote on protocol changes, or build products by connecting multiple protocols together.

The important idea is not that DeFi removes every kind of intermediation. It changes where coordination happens. Rules that would usually be enforced by a platform, broker, exchange, or bank can be encoded into smart contracts, governance systems, oracle feeds, and liquidity mechanisms.

Most DeFi protocols combine a few common building blocks: users, smart contracts, liquidity providers, traders, borrowers, lenders, governance participants, and external data providers. The exact mix depends on the protocol type.

Smart contracts: Encode the rules for trading, lending, collateral, fees, voting, or settlement.

Liquidity pools: Hold assets that traders or borrowers interact with.

Liquidity providers: Supply assets to pools and take protocol-specific risks.

Oracles: Bring external data, such as prices, into smart contracts.

Governance: Coordinates upgrades, parameters, incentives, and treasury decisions.

Composability: Lets protocols plug into each other and create new products.

Decentralized exchanges allow users to trade on-chain through smart contracts. Early exchange design borrowed from order books, where buyers and sellers post bids and asks. But on-chain order books can be expensive and capital-inefficient because each order update consumes gas and liquidity may sit idle.

Automated market makers changed that model. In an AMM, traders do not need to match with another person’s order. They trade against a shared liquidity pool supplied by liquidity providers. A liquidity pool is a smart contract that holds tokens, and an algorithmic formula determines prices based on the pool’s reserves.

The constant product AMM model uses the formula x times y equals k. It keeps the product of the two token reserves constant, so prices move automatically as traders buy one asset and add another. This model is simple and resilient, but it spreads liquidity across all possible prices, which can make capital inefficient.

Liquidity providers deposit assets into pools so other users can trade. In return, LPs may earn a share of trading fees. But providing liquidity is not passive income without risk. LP returns depend on trading volume, fee tiers, price movement, pool design, and impermanent loss.

LP profitability can be expressed as trading fees earned minus impermanent loss, if any.

DeFi lending protocols let users supply assets to earn yield and borrow against collateral without a traditional bank. Smart contracts replace bilateral lending relationships with pooled markets that set rules, manage collateral, and enforce liquidations automatically.

A typical lending protocol uses overcollateralization. A borrower deposits collateral, borrows a smaller value against it, and must keep the position healthy. If collateral value falls too far, the protocol can liquidate the position to protect the lending pool.

Collateral: Assets pledged to secure a loan or leveraged position.

Utilization: How much of a lending pool is currently borrowed.

Supply APY: The rate suppliers may earn for lending assets.

Borrow APY: The rate borrowers pay to use borrowed assets.

Health factor: A measure of whether a position is safely collateralized.

Liquidation: Forced sale or closure when a position becomes unsafe.

Yield aggregators simplify DeFi yield generation by routing deposits across strategies and automating parts of the process. Instead of manually moving funds between pools or protocols, users deposit into a vault. The aggregator then follows a strategy, compounds returns, and abstracts away some of the operational work.

This does not remove risk. Aggregators add another layer of smart-contract exposure, strategy risk, fee policy, governance risk, and dependence on keepers or automation agents. Yield optimization is a mechanism to understand, not a recommendation.

DeFi protocols often need information that does not naturally live on-chain. Lending markets need asset prices to value collateral. Derivatives need reference prices. Synthetic assets need external data. Liquidations depend on timely and accurate price information.

Oracles provide this data to smart contracts. Because smart contracts can execute automatically based on oracle inputs, oracle design is a core security issue. Bad data, delayed data, manipulation, or concentrated data sources can create losses across trading, lending, derivatives, and liquidation systems.

More resilient designs combine multiple sources and aggregate observations before publishing a value on-chain. That can reduce dependence on one provider, but it does not eliminate latency, source correlation, or governance risk.

Decentralized derivatives create exposure to assets or market outcomes without necessarily requiring users to hold the underlying asset directly. Common examples include perpetual futures, options, synthetic assets, and other collateralized instruments.

The key mechanics include collateral, margin, funding rates, liquidation thresholds, and protocol risk controls. Perpetual futures have no expiry and use funding payments to help keep contract prices aligned with spot prices. Like other DeFi products, derivatives can increase capital efficiency but also introduce leverage, liquidation, oracle, and systemic-risk concerns.

Many DeFi protocols are governed through DAOs, governance tokens, proposals, voting systems, delegation, and treasuries. Governance determines how protocols update parameters, allocate incentives, manage upgrades, and respond to risk.

Proposals may need a quorum or other participation threshold before a vote can take effect. The exact rules determine how voting power is counted, how delegates act, and how quickly a protocol can respond to changing conditions.

Governance can make protocols more community-driven, but it can also create concentration and coordination problems. Token-weighted voting may give large holders outsized influence. Low voter participation can make decisions fragile. Delegation can improve expertise but may also centralize practical control.

Composability is the ability for DeFi protocols to plug into and build on top of one another. This is why DeFi is often described as money legos. A lending protocol can use an oracle, a yield aggregator can route deposits across lending markets, and a derivatives protocol can depend on multiple liquidity and data systems.

Composability accelerates innovation because developers can reuse existing liquidity, infrastructure, and protocol primitives. But it also creates dependency risk. If one widely used protocol, oracle, bridge, collateral asset, or governance decision fails, the effects can cascade across many connected applications.

DeFi replaces some intermediaries with code, liquidity systems, and governance. That does not remove risk. It changes the form of risk.

Smart contract risk: code can contain bugs or be exploited.

Oracle risk: bad or delayed data can trigger wrong pricing, lending, or liquidation outcomes.

Liquidity risk: pools can become shallow, imbalanced, or expensive to trade against.

Liquidation risk: collateralized positions can be closed when markets move quickly.

Governance risk: token voting can be captured, delayed, or dominated by large holders.

Composability risk: protocols that depend on one another can transmit failures across the system.

Regulatory risk: DeFi protocols, interfaces, and participants may face changing legal treatment.

A user wants to trade ETH for USDC through a decentralized exchange. Instead of matching with a specific seller, the user trades against an ETH/USDC liquidity pool. Liquidity providers have deposited both assets into the pool. The smart contract calculates the trade based on the pool’s pricing formula, updates the token reserves, charges a trading fee, and sends the output token to the user.

The user gets an on-chain swap. LPs earn fees. The pool’s price changes because its reserves changed. If the trade is large relative to pool liquidity, the user may experience slippage. If the pool is part of a larger aggregator or intent-based route, solvers or routers may search across multiple liquidity sources to improve execution.
