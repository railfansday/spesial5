Website accessibility for small business sites: the WCAG 2.2 fixes that take under an hour

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Short answer: The accessibility problems on small business websites are mostly the same few, and most of them are quick to fix. WebAIM's 2026 scan of the top one million home pages found low contrast text on 83.9% of them, missing image alt text on 53.1% and missing form labels on 51%. Fixing text contrast, alt text, form labels, visible focus, tap target size and the page language takes about an hour on a typical five-page site. It removes the most common barriers, but it doesn't make a site fully WCAG conformant on its own.

This post walks through those fixes with the WCAG 2.2 success criterion behind each one, the code, and how to check your work. It also covers what the law does and doesn't say, because that's the first question most owners ask.

WCAG (Web Content Accessibility Guidelines) is the W3C's standard for accessible web content, and Level AA is the usual target. WCAG 2.2 was first published as a W3C Recommendation on 5 October 2023, with the current version dated 12 December 2024. It added nine success criteria and removed one (4.1.1 Parsing, now obsolete).

Each success criterion is a testable rule with a level: A (the minimum), AA (what laws and contracts usually reference) and AAA (enhanced). Two of the new AA criteria matter on almost every small site: 2.4.11 Focus Not Obscured (Minimum) and 2.5.8 Target Size (Minimum). Both are in the fixes below.

There's no federal regulation with detailed technical web standards for private businesses, but the ADA still applies to them. The Department of Justice's web guidance says the ADA's requirements apply to all the goods and services offered by businesses open to the public, that inaccessible website features can limit access to them, and that "the Department of Justice does not have a regulation setting out detailed standards." It describes WCAG as "helpful guidance."

The rule people often hear about is the 2024 Title II rule, which adopted WCAG 2.1 Level AA for state and local governments, not private businesses. An interim final rule published in April 2026 moved its compliance dates to April 26, 2027 for larger governments and April 26, 2028 for smaller ones.

What this means for you as the developer:

Make body text at least 4.5:1 against its background, and large text at least 3:1. That's 1.4.3 Contrast (Minimum), Level AA. "Large" means at least 18 point, or 14 point bold, which works out to about 24 CSS pixels regular or about 18.7 CSS pixels bold.

Check each color pair with a contrast checker (WebAIM's is free) or the color picker in Chrome DevTools, which shows the ratio. For hero text over photos, add an overlay rather than changing the brand color:

Icons, input borders and focus outlines fall under a separate rule, 1.4.11 Non-text Contrast (AA): at least 3:1 against adjacent colors.

Give every meaningful image a short text alternative that says what it shows or does, and give decorative images an empty alt="". That's 1.1.1 Non-text Content, Level A, and missing alt text was the second most common failure in the WebAIM data.

The one we see most on local sites is worse than missing alt text: the opening hours, the menu or a price list posted only as an image or a scanned flyer. Put that information in real HTML text. It helps screen reader users, people who zoom, and search engines, and the owner can update it without opening a design app.

Every input needs a visible label tied to it with for and id. That's 3.3.2 Labels or Instructions (Level A), with 1.3.1 and 4.1.2 covering the code side. A placeholder isn't a label: it disappears when you start typing and is usually low contrast.

Page builder form widgets sometimes hide labels and rely on placeholders by default. Look for a "show label" setting before writing custom code.

At least 24 by 24 CSS pixels, or spaced so that a 24 px circle around each one doesn't touch another target. That's 2.5.8 Target Size (Minimum), Level AA. There are exceptions, including links inside a sentence and controls the browser draws itself. The AAA level, 2.5.5, asks for 44 by 44 pixels, and that's a sensible design target for a site people use on their phone.

The usual offenders are the row of social icons in the footer, the close "X" on a popup, and phone and email links squeezed together in a top bar.

When you report back to the owner, describe barriers removed, not legal status. A fictional example:

I fixed the issues that stopped some customers from using your site: the grey text on the homepage was too faint to read for many people, the quote form fields had no labels for screen readers, and the hours were only in a picture. Those are all fixed now. This isn't a full accessibility audit, but it covers the most common problems we see on small business sites.

The manual way: search a trade and a town, run a few home pages through WAVE, and note the ones with faint text and unlabeled forms. On most local sites you'll find something within a minute.

Level AA is the usual target and what most laws, contracts and policies reference. The current version is WCAG 2.2, which adds nine new criteria to WCAG 2.1 and drops the obsolete 4.1.1 Parsing.

The DOJ says the ADA applies to the goods and services of businesses open to the public, including what they offer online, but it has no regulation with detailed web standards for private businesses. For legal questions, the owner should talk to a lawyer; your job is fixing the barriers.

4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text (at least 18 point, or 14 point bold) under 1.4.3, Level AA. Icons, input borders and focus indicators need 3:1 under 1.4.11.

24 by 24 CSS pixels under 2.5.8 Target Size (Minimum), Level AA, with exceptions for spacing, inline links and browser-drawn controls. 44 by 44 pixels is the AAA level and a good design target.

Don't count on it. The FTC finalized an order in April 2025 requiring overlay vendor accessiBe to pay $1 million over claims that its plug-in could make any website WCAG compliant. Fixing the underlying HTML and CSS is the reliable route.
