FAA Ground Stop Blamed On Elon Musk—Here’s Why That’s Ridiculous

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Friday’s nationwide FAA ground stop followed a contractor’s router installation in Arkansas. Now the story is becoming about Elon Musk: a claim that DOGE cut FAA employees, oversight was loosened, and this led to the flight stoppage. There’s a real oversight question here. It long predates DOGE. In fact it goes back decades. And even there, the connection between specific oversight failures and what actually happened hasn’t been made. Anyone writing about this needs to at least know that an L3Harris contractor’s router configuration error caused a nationwide air traffic disruption in 2009, too. And federal investigators criticized the FAA’s oversight after that incident! FAA problems did not begin with DOGE. A Router Change Disrupted The National Network The November 13 nationwide departure stop began at 3:54 p.m. Eastern and lasted until 8:15 p.m. Aircraft already airborne continued landing. The FAA’s preliminary account describes excessive connection attempts flooding its telecommunications network. Instability affected 17 of 22 enroute centers and spread to approach facilities. L3Harris had installed a router at a radio site in El Dorado, Arkansas. Investigators also identified a radar site in Amarillo, Texas. Blocking those sites stabilized the network, and officials disabled the relevant function on more than 60 similar routers. FAA Oversight Has Been A Problem, Long Before DOGE One Mile at a Time links the failure to DOGE, drawing on CNN’s reporting about a March 2025 maintenance memo. According to CNN, contractor maintenance shifted from requests requiring approval to notifications that could proceed without explicit approval. Liaisons were still supposed to evaluate the work, but there was a small staff handling thousands of notices. Bedford said the contractor was monitored and a risk assessment occurred. The technicians’ union says the FAA employee merely provided access. An FAA spokesperson later admitted that the escort doesn’t remain during work on L3 equipment. (To be sure, the union saying none of its members performing a risk assessment doesn’t mean nobody performed one.) Additional FAA review might have caught a problem the contractor missed. It’s speculative to suggest that FAA supervision, rathe rthan contractor supervision would have made a difference. And CNN itself describes a chronic technician shortage stretching back years – that I’ve written about several times – as a reason for the policy change. One Mile at a Time though says Elon Musk’s DOGE laid off hundreds of FAA employees, making for the biggest single year layoffs at the organization in history.In fact, the FAA fired 11,400 controllers in 1981. Here there’s no identified fired employee or eliminated review position connected to this installation. Moreover, the Professional Aviation Safety Specialists union announced that its 132 fired probationary employees were being reinstated with back pay, with a March 20, 2025 return date. There were other departures. And it’s plausible DOGE was counterproductive here. I disagreed with the effort overall and didn’t expect success. But there’s just not been anything more than insinuation that that effort was related to Friday’s ground stop. The Transportation Department’s inspector general found that roughly 75% of network routes were blocked. Restoration took five hours. Monitoring failed, and technicians struggled to locate the source. It also found that FAA oversight should have been more effective. Investigators had already called for better equipment configuration controls in 2008. The 2009 outage primarily affected flight planning and data services. But contractor router work, a problem spreading through the network, slow fault detection, and inadequate FAA oversight were documented together in a federal report sixteen years ago with this network and this contractor. The FAA struggled long before the current administration. A July 2024 aviation industry coalition letter traced a shift toward fixing equipment after it failed to the 2013 budget sequester. As I covered in March, Bob Poole’s reporting describes decades of technician cuts. His sources put the decline at more than 6,600 technicians to roughly 4,000. Insufficient staffing and poor implementation make fixing things after they fail the general path FAA appears to have taken. Their problems aren’t generally that nobody smart enough or sufficiently determined has shown up to fix them.
