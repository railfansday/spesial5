Cashier’s Checks Are Bank-Backed Only When They’re Real – Call the Issuing Bank First

Sellers and closing agents ask for cashier’s checks on big payments because the bank’s own money stands behind them, and that trust is why scammers fake them. Buy yours at your own bank or credit union. When you’re handed one, call the issuing bank at a number you look up yourself before you hand anything over.

Deposit one with a teller, and the Federal Reserve’s Regulation CC requires your bank to release the money by the next business day, with a few exceptions, such as holding back part of a large check. Banks can take weeks to spot a fake, though, and the Federal Trade Commission warns that by then you’re the one who repays the bank.

Money you can withdraw isn’t proof the check is real. Fakes often carry the names of legitimate banks and can fool even bank employees, the FTC says. The bank named on the check is the one that can tell you whether it issued it.

Expect to be asked for one at a home closing, a private car sale, or a lease signing. Buy it at a branch, or online where your bank offers that. You pay the amount up front, plus the fee on your bank’s fee schedule, which some premium checking accounts waive. A certified check is your own check that the bank signs after setting the money aside, and a money order is prepaid too but sold only in small amounts.

You can stop payment on your own check before it clears, but not on a cashier’s check, which the bank must honor. If one goes missing, you’ll sign a sworn declaration of loss and may have to buy an indemnity bond, insurance that covers the bank if the original gets cashed. The bank can also hold off on a refund or replacement until 90 days after the check’s date.

Anyone paid by cashier’s check should get the issuing bank’s phone number from its own website, never from the check, since the number on a fake may be the scammer’s. The FDIC says the bank will likely ask for the check number, its issue date, and the amount. Turn down a check for more than your asking price, and never send money back out of one you just deposited.

If the issuing bank has no record of the check, don’t deposit it, and report it to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.
