This new scorecard shows whether your favorite retailer is using Flock cameras

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Automated license plate readers from Flock Safety and other vendors have become increasingly controversial across the country after reports of misuse by officers, high error rates that can lead to dangerous police encounters, and concerns about access by Immigration and Customs Enforcement.  

Flock, in particular, operates a nationwide camera network, with data from cameras operated in one jurisdiction frequently accessed by investigators from elsewhere, making it hard for local officials to understand and control privacy risks. As a result, while Flock and other providers emphasize that their cameras have been used to investigate legitimate crimes, rescue kidnapped kids, and track stolen cars, many municipalities have ended their contracts, in some cases even covering cameras awaiting removal by the service provider.   

Many businesses deploy their own automated license plate readers, or ALPRs, to track vehicles in their parking lots, sometimes sharing that information with law enforcement. But since private companies aren’t subject to freedom of information laws, it can be hard to know which businesses have them and what they’re doing with the data they collect.

To help people understand in time for the holiday shopping season which stores use the devices, Fight for the Future just released a scorecard listing 10 major retailers and what the organization knows about their use of the cameras.  

“Retailers also need to be brought into the conversation,” says Reem Suleiman, senior campaign director at Fight for the Future, which previously organized a campaign with home improvement influencers that called on Home Depot and Lowe’s to cut ties with Flock.

The chart is based on information the organization received from a questionnaire sent to the retailers and, in cases where they didn’t respond, third-party news reporting, crowdsourced camera spotting, and information from published privacy policies. “We got responses from just a handful of [businesses],” Suleiman says.  

The exact details vary, and many businesses don’t appear to use the technology at every location. Costco, for instance, told Fight for the Future that it uses ALPR cameras at “less than a dozen locations nationwide,” so it’s difficult to know just from the checklist whether your local branch of a national chain is equipped with the cameras.

But it’s easy to see at a glance that many major brands at least acknowledge in their privacy policies that they might be using the technology. Some stores also indicate that they may share information from the devices with law enforcement. 

The scorecard offers links to retailers’ emails, along with suggested text, to ask them to refrain from using ALPR technology, even if (or perhaps especially if) the details about how they do so may be opaque. Fight for the Future also encourages people to reach out with any tips about retailers that are using the cameras or with requests to add particular stores to the scoresheet. 

“We don’t have a FOIA request that we can do for companies or a public records act request that we can impose upon companies, but nothing’s stopping us from writing to them,” Suleiman says. “Nothing’s stopping people from letting these companies know what they want and what they expect.”
