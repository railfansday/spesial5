I voiced a character in ‘Grand Theft Auto.’ I never expected her to follow me for 17 years

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

The release of Grand Theft Auto VI generated more than $1.65 billion in preorder sales, broke internet usage records, and shattered streaming records on platforms like Twitch and YouTube. For millennials who played video games growing up, this is a cultural moment.

For me, as someone who works in digital editorial and audience engagement for a media company that covers tech and news, the data is staggering. It also reminds me of when I stumbled into the Grand Theft Auto universe in my twenties.

In 2009, I was fresh out of Tisch School of the Arts at New York University and was auditioning endlessly. I spent my days at castings surrounded by dozens of other girls who looked like me, only prettier. I spent my nights waitressing until the wee hours of the morning, or haphazardly attending parties and clubs where I admittedly hoped to be discovered. These were chaotic times: years of small successes, like a role on Law & Order or a bit part on a soap opera, separated by months upon months of fruitless auditions and absolutely no work.

So when a friend from high school reached out to me about a gig recording voice-over for The Ballad of Gay Tony, a 2009 expansion of Grand Theft Auto IV, I quickly accepted without asking many questions or seeing the script. To be frank, I wouldn’t have known what to ask about how the character interacted with the universe of GTA. My parents were strict, especially about video games. They were both educators and categorized video games as brain rot before that was even a thing, so I’d never played GTA before.

I recorded Joni’s voice track in a couple of hours at Rockstar Games in SoHo, with my high school friend in the audio booth. This was the easiest acting job—there hadn’t been any awkward audition, all of my scenes were set in a nightclub, which was an environment I knew well, and I didn’t have to memorize anything. I just had a sheet of one-liners to deliver in a sassy club manager voice. Since I was a waitress at the time, I remember using one of my managers from The Coffee Shop on Union Square as inspiration. (I won’t name her out of respect.)

I needed to separate the character from myself because I wasn’t half as bold as Joni, who spent a lot of time propositioning Luis while simultaneously mocking his, er, body part size. It seems important to note that I didn’t realize players could interact with characters beyond speaking to them—in ways that are both violent and sexually violent—but I’m not sure I would’ve turned down the job even if I had known. After all, Joni was a character, she was just an avatar, and I was an actress. I left the studio with few regrets, and even fewer when the check cleared on time.

This was 2009. The term “digital footprint” wasn’t part of the zeitgeist. Instagram didn’t exist, and I still typed websites I liked into my browser to check the news—and none of those sites covered gamer culture. Could I have anticipated that Grand Theft Auto would remain a cultural phenomenon that would follow me forever? I don’t think so.

I also didn’t run in circles where “fandoms” were a thing, so I was surprised when fans of Joni started emailing me in 2010. In hindsight, this speaks to the growing popularity of the GTA franchise, and the fact that Joni was probably a lot of people’s cup of tea. Their emails were typically short and bizarre, and a few were desperate for a response. It takes a lot of deep thinking about a character to research the voice-over actor and email her, but if there’s one skill I already possessed at age 26, it was knowing that often no response is the best response.

Not long after I recorded my voice-over for GTA, I began to suspect I had skills beyond acting. Motivated by the fact that I never felt particularly intrigued by the scripts I was given to audition for, I created my own web series, took a job managing social media and, through a combination of hard work and luck, very quickly built a media career. In 2011 or 2012, I took my acting website down and the emails stopped. When Instagram introduced direct messages, the messages returned, but at a slower pace.

During the pandemic, I managed an editorial team that included a section dedicated to gaming. I’d all but forgotten about Joni, but decided to trot out my voice-over credit as a fun fact during one of those team meetings where we were all trying to bond as humans while working from home in isolation. When we finally returned to the office, one of the producers who’d been on the call brought me a poster of Joni as a gift.

I unrolled it at my desk. There she was, a reminder of a period in my life when any job that was even moderately creative seemed preferable to serving mojitos. On the poster, Joni is happy as can be, spraying champagne into the air and grinning in her pink dress. It dawned on me that some of the people on my team had been teenagers when the game came out. I thanked the producer and tried not to imagine what sorts of interactions he might have had with Joni.

I’ve managed to scrub much of my twenties from the internet—a feat I suspect only elder millennials will be able to pull off, as the internet grows more permanent and everyone becomes increasingly obsessed with saving “receipts.” But Joni is still out there. She occasionally resurfaces in my professional life, partly because Rockstar Games remains a cultural force, and partly because the way players interact with characters—especially women—is still a conversation worth having.

I’ve spent years putting distance between myself and the person I was when I voiced her. Joni, meanwhile, is still in that pink dress, spraying champagne, having the time of her life.
