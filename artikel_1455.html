Venousization enables angiogenesis in the absence of VEGFR signalling

The vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) ligand and its main receptor VEGFR2 are considered to be essential for endothelial differentiation, proliferation, sprouting and survival1. Blood vessels cannot form in growing embryos or tissues when VEGF–VEGFR2 signalling is compromised in all cells2,3. The anti-VEGF antibody, which can block angiogenesis in cancer, wound healing or ischaemic diseases, is among the most widely used antibodies in the clinic. However, vascular resistance to anti-VEGF therapy has been reported4,5. Here we used iFlpMosaics and iSuRe–HadCre to induce and track genetic mosaics of endothelial cells (ECs) lacking VEGFR2 during the entire embryonic and postnatal development. These Vegfr2-knockout (Vegfr2KO) ECs adapted, proliferated normally and competed with wild-type cells over time, eventually forming a substantial portion of the vascular network in most organs. Vegfr2KO ECs could not sprout during the first wave of angiogenesis in most tissues, because this process is highly VEGF-dependent and favours growth and mobilization of wild-type ECs. However, owing to their inability to respond to VEGF, Vegfr2KO ECs became dominant in veins, which represented a long-term and continuous source of ECs for subsequent waves of VEGF-independent angiogenesis. Comparative single-cell RNA sequencing analysis of ECs with acute and long-term loss of VEGFR2 identified both common and organ-specific molecular mechanisms of adaptation and resistance to VEGF signalling loss. These included significant endothelial venousization and upregulation of ligands for VEGFR1 and VEGFR3. VEGFR1 only partially compensated for VEGFR2 loss in capillary ECs, whereas VEGFR3 compensated only in arterial ECs. Loss of the three VEGF receptors did not compromise venous growth. Notably, loss of the master regulator of venous development, NR2F2 (also known as COUP-TFII), significantly compromised expansion of Vegfr2KO ECs. This work has implications for our understanding of the role of VEGF receptors in angiogenesis and demonstrates venousization as a mechanism of adaptation and resistance to VEGF signalling loss during angiogenesis.

VEGF is frequently targeted in clinical settings to suppress tumour angiogenesis, metastasis and retina disease6. However, resistance to VEGF-blocking antibodies arises over time, which limits the effectiveness of such treatments4,5,7,8. By contrast, resistance to loss of VEGF signalling has not been reported during physiological vascular development.

Embryos or postnatal tissues lacking activity of the VEGF signalling pathway in all cells fail to develop vessels3. VEGFR2 (encoded by the Kdr (also known as Vegfr2) gene in mice) is the most important VEGF receptor and has been shown to be essential for angiogenesis in all vascular development contexts studied so far1,9. Germline mutants for this gene completely lack blood vessels and die very early during embryonic development2, and mouse embryos chimeras formed by wild-type and Vegfr2-knockout (Vegfr2KO) progenitor cells cannot form endothelial cells (ECs)10. Conditional deletion of Vegfr2 with Tie2–Cre after endothelial differentiation11, or in postnatal tissues with Cdh5–CreERT2, also strongly impairs angiogenesis12, without any evidence of genetic compensation or resistance.

Despite a large body of literature showing that VEGFR2 is essential for endothelial differentiation, proliferation, migration and survival, previous studies involving deletion of Vegfr2 in all ECs (Vegfr2iDEC) during embryonic or postnatal organ development could only analyse its effect on angiogenesis for a relatively short period of time. Its essential role in vascular development and hence in tissue development and organ function causes severe impairment of angiogenesis and premature lethality of Vegfr2iDEC embryos and postnatal animals; thus, such studies have been unable to determine whether ECs can adapt to loss of VEGFR2 over time or make certain organ vascular beds in the complete absence of VEGF–VEGFR2 signalling.

Here we used iFlpMosaics13 and iSuRe–HadCre14 to mosaically induce and longitudinally track Vegfr2KO ECs, enabling us to assess the role of this gene during the entire embryonic and postnatal vascular development of different organs. We found that despite its essential role during the initial wave of embryonic and postnatal angiogenesis, VEGF–VEGFR2 signalling is dispensable for many subsequent angiogenic processes originating from veins. Vegfr2KO ECs expand and out-compete wild-type ECs in venous vessels, which serve as a continuous source of these mutant cells for angiogenesis in most organs. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) showed that Vegfr2KO ECs adapt over time through upregulation of ligands for other VEGF receptors and through increased expression of venous-specific genes, particularly Nr2f2. Targeting these adaptive and resistance-associated molecular mechanisms prevents expansion of ECs following prolonged loss of VEGFR2 signalling.

Given the essential role of VEGFR2 in early angiogenesis and embryonic development, we induced mosaic loss of VEGFR2 with iFlpMosaics13 to investigate whether mutant cells could adapt and form new vessels over time. This technology enables FlpO-dependent ratiometric induction and tracking over long periods of time of cells expressing MTomato-2A-Cre (knockout for any floxed gene) or MYFP (wild type). To induce these mosaics, we used the Apln-FlpO allele, which is expressed in the first angiogenic and sprouting ECs at embryonic day 8.5 (E8.5) and throughout vascular development. In female embryos, the X-chromosome-located Apln–FlpO allele is mosaically silenced15 and expressed only in a fraction (23–50%) of ECs, generating relatively few mutant and wild-type cells, which we found to be compatible with embryonic development (Fig. 1a and Extended Data Fig. 1a). Analysis of major organs of Apln-FlpO iFlpMosaic mice showed that ECs with full loss of VEGFR2 (Vegfr2KO-E8.5–P7) were able to survive and proliferate during embryonic development and until postnatal day 7 (P7), with only a relatively minor competitive disadvantage (2–7-fold) compared with wild-type cells (Fig. 1b). This competitive disadvantage was more pronounced in the lung and kidney endothelium and less so in brain ECs (Fig. 1b,c and Extended Data Fig. 1a–c). As Apln-FlpO is constitutively active and can drive recombination at later stages of embryonic development, we also induced Vegfr2 deletion using 4-hydroxytamoxifen (4-OHT) at E10.5 and E12.5 in embryos carrying Cdh5-CreERT2, iSuRe-HadCre and an iMb2-Mosaic16 control reporter that recombines independently of Vegfr2 deletion (Extended Data Fig. 2a–c). This resulted in a maximum of 1–6% recombined ECs, as opposed to 23–50% with Apln-FlpO. However, this recapitulated the trend in which brain ECs were least affected by VEGFR2 loss, followed by those in the heart, liver, lung and kidney (Extended Data Fig. 2b,c).

VEGFR2 activity is essential for NOTCH activation and the development of arteries17,18,19. Arteries are formed by tip ECs20, which have the highest level of VEGF–VEGFR2 signalling and guide vascular growth. Our high-resolution spatial zonation data with long-term iFlpMosaics showed that Vegfr2KO-E8.5–P7 ECs were significantly enriched in veins and surrounding capillaries, and avoided angiogenic front capillaries or tip ECs, but were able to form arteries (Fig. 1d; see also Extended Data Fig. 3a for the heatmapping method).

To complement these results, we intercrossed the Vegfr2 floxed allele with Cdh5-CreERT2 and iSuRe-HadCre14 alleles to ensure fast (24 h; Extended Data Fig. 3b,c) and effective mosaic genetic deletion and full loss of VEGFR2 protein and downstream targets in ECs (Cdh5 expressing), after a single injection of 4-OHT. Vegfr2IECKO-P1–P7 ECs were located mainly in veins and surrounding capillaries, as well as in the more proximal and mature segments of retina arteries (Fig. 1e). This indicates that when Vegfr2KO ECs are induced in the initial retina sprouting plexus at P1, they can make arteries forming from P1 to P3. However, owing to their inability to continue to sprout and form tip cells at the leading edge, they cannot participate in the subsequent arterial development process occurring from P3 to P7, which in the retina is dependent on incorporation of new tip cells21.

Notably, compared with Vegfr2IECKO-P1−P7 ECs, Vegfr2KO-E8.5−P7 ECs were more frequent at the leading edge and angiogenic front capillaries (Fig. 1f,g) and were still able to make arteries. This suggests that embryonic Vegfr2KO-E8.5−P7 ECs can adapt to long-term loss of VEGFR2 and contribute to venous vascular growth, tip cells and arteries at postnatal stages.

We next investigated how Vegfr2KO ECs could compete with wild-type cells and contribute to growing vascular networks over long periods of time, given the proposed important role of this pathway in EC proliferation1,22. During angiogenesis, there are two main locations for EC proliferation: the capillary angiogenic front and veins (Fig. 2a). We found that the surviving Vegfr2KO-E8.5−P7 ECs proliferated (as determined by Ki-67 detection) as well as wild-type cells in postnatal brain, heart and lung vessels (Fig. 2b).

Venous ECs provide a continuous source of ECs for angiogenesis and these migrate against the blood flow to capillaries and then towards arterial vessels20,23,24. When we induced Vegfr2KO ECs for longer periods of time, from P1 to P20 (Vegfr2IECKO-P1–P20), we observed that mutant cells stayed in the vein stem, where they proliferated and gradually outcompeted wild-type cells, which moved outwards towards the VEGF-rich leading edge and adjacent capillaries (Fig. 2c). This preferential occupation of the venous stem niche by Vegfr2KO ECs allowed them to continue proliferating for longer periods of time and later to form most of the perivenous capillary vessels and the deeper vascular plexus that developed during a second wave of VEGF-independent angiogenesis from P7 to P20 (Extended Data Fig. 4a,b). This was consistent with previous findings showing that the tip cells that dive to form the deeper vascular plexus in the retina lack expression of the VEGFR2 signalling target gene Esm1; instead, their migration is dependent on other pathways such as Alk or TGFβ and WNT signalling25,26,27.

Together, these data show that VEGFR2 function is essential for the initial sprouting angiogenesis driven by high VEGF levels in hypoxic embryos and organs but not for the development of veins and perivenous capillaries or the subsequent steps of capillary angiogenesis and remodelling that occur after the first vascular plexus is formed in various organs. Thus, by forming veins, which are known to provide a continuous source of ECs for angiogenesis23, Vegfr2KO ECs can continue to proliferate and participate in subsequent steps of vascular development for long periods of time.

We previously observed that the distribution of Vegfr2KO ECs in the retina plexus at P7 differed between animals with long-term (Vegfr2KO-E8.5–P7) and short-term (Vegfr2IECKO-P1–P7) mosaic deletion of Vegfr2. Vegfr2KO-E8.5–P7 ECs were more frequent at the leading edge and angiogenic front than Vegfr2IECKO-P1–P7 ECs (Fig. 1f,g). Vegfr2KO-E8.5–P7 ECs also proliferated normally (Fig. 2b). Therefore, we hypothesized that Vegfr2KO-E8.5–P7 ECs might be genetically rewired and adapt over time to loss of the most important receptor for VEGF.

To assess the early response of ECs to loss of VEGFR2, we first analysed apoptosis markers in growing retina vessels, as VEGFR2 has been shown to regulate EC survival. Vegfr2IECKO-P4–P7 mice showed a very significant increase in the number of apoptotic (cleaved caspase 3 positive) ECs (Fig. 2d). This apoptosis was a reaction to the acute loss of VEGFR2, as Vegfr2KO-E8.5–P7 or even Vegfr2IECKO-P1–P7 mice did not show any apoptotic ECs (Fig. 2e), suggesting adaptation of the surviving cells over time. When the acute loss of Vegfr2 was induced in all ECs, we observed an immediate depletion of Vegfr2KO ECs from the VEGF-high angiogenic front, but not from veins or more mature vessels (Extended Data Fig. 4c). Therefore, even though many ECs in the retina plexus showed upregulation of the apoptosis marker in the first days after Vegfr2 loss (Fig. 2d), only the angiogenic ECs that were exposed to high VEGF levels at the time of Vegfr2 deletion died (Extended Data Fig. 4c).

We also found that Vegfr2IECKO-P4–P7 ECs were significantly less proliferative than control cells only in the heart and kidney (Fig. 2f), whereas the proliferation of Vegfr2KO-E8.5–P7 ECs was similar to that of controls (Fig. 2b). This suggests that Vegfr2KO ECs adapt over time and are later able to proliferate in these organs. In the newborn retina, deletion of Vegfr2 did not affect the proliferation of surviving ECs in any segment (Extended Data Fig. 4d), indicating that in this organ VEGFR2 mainly controls EC migration and survival, not proliferation.

Together, our data suggest that ECs lacking VEGF signalling during active angiogenesis suffer an acute VEGFR2 withdrawal effect, followed by a period of adaptation to the loss, after which they can form new vessels. ECs that are more distant from the VEGF source, and therefore have less VEGF signalling, adapt better to the loss and can expand over time, particularly in veins and perivenous vessels.

To further confirm the insensitivity of more mature ECs to loss of VEGFR2 signalling, we deleted Vegfr2 in several adult quiescent vascular beds for 2 weeks. Only the quiescent sinusoidal liver endothelium showed a strong sensitivity and loss of Vegfr2KO ECs, whereas only the kidney endothelium showed significantly lower proliferation (Extended Data Fig. 5), suggesting that only fenestrated or discontinuous mature endothelial vessels present in these organs remain sensitive to VEGFR2 loss.

Given the observed differences in the biology of Vegfr2IECKO-P4–P7 and Vegfr2KO-E8.5–P7 ECs, we performed scRNA-seq of these cells and respective control Tomato+ cells to identify possible differences in their transcriptional profiles and potential mechanisms of adaptation to loss of VEGFR2 signalling in distinct organ vascular beds.

Although VEGFR2 is a central regulator of angiogenesis and widely regarded as essential for EC biology, Vegfr2IECKO-P4–P7 ECs showed only modest changes in their transcriptome (Fig. 3 and Extended Data Fig. 6), even though VEGFR2 protein and downstream signalling were lost already within the first 24 h following 4-OHT administration (Extended Data Fig. 3b,c). In general, the expression of genes related to sprouting or tip cell biology was decreased, whereas that of genes related to apoptosis and vascular permeability was increased in all organs analysed (Fig. 3a,b). In the brain, clusters of proliferating (S–G2–M) ECs expressing venous capillaries markers were increased after Vegfr2 deletion for 3 days (Fig. 3c–f). This was consistent with higher clonal expansion of Vegfr2KO ECs from P1 to P20 in this organ (Extended Data Fig. 7a). Clusters comprising capillary and activated or angiogenic ECs (with high expression of Apln, Adm, Mcam, Lamb1 and Angpt2) were decreased (Fig. 3d–f). In total, only 569 genes were significantly deregulated (Fig. 3g). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed an increase in the expression of E2F and MYC target genes, as well as G2M, DNA repair and mitotic spindle genes, confirming the increase in EC proliferation (Fig. 3h and Extended Data Fig. 7a). The expression of most tip cell marker genes was decreased in Vegfr2IECKO-P4–P7 brain ECs (Fig. 3i and Extended Data Fig. 6a–f).

In contrast to those in the brain, Vegfr2IECKO-P4–P7 capillary ECs in the heart were significantly less proliferative (Fig. 3j–m and Extended Data Fig. 7b), matching the 54% decrease in Ki-67 percentage observed in sections (Fig. 2f). The proportion of mutant ECs found in bigger vessels (arteries and veins) was increased 2- to 4-fold (Fig. 3k–m), suggesting a proportional fast depletion of Vegfr2KO capillary ECs in only 3 days. Unlike in the brain, GSEA of Vegfr2KO heart ECs showed the expected decrease in expression of E2F and MYC target genes, as well as decreases in expression of G2M, mitotic spindle and tip cell genes (Fig. 3n–p), in accordance with the decrease in proliferation and sprouting of Vegfr2IECKO-P4–P7 heart ECs. Vegfr2KO heart ECs also showed upregulation of genes related to apoptosis and epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (Fig. 3n–p), as has been shown previously for ECs losing ERK signalling28.

In contrast to the results for brain and heart, we found no significant difference in the proportions of proliferating cells among control and Vegfr2IECKO-P4–P7 ECs in lung (Extended Data Fig. 6g–j), as shown by immunostaining for Ki-67 and clonal analysis (Fig. 2f and Extended Data Fig. 7c). The proportion of the vein cluster was only slightly increased, and the capillary cluster was slightly decreased. Despite the lack of significant changes in cluster proportions, GSEA showed increased TNF signalling through NF-κB, hypoxia and reactive oxygen species signalling and a decrease in the interferon response (Extended Data Fig. 6k,l). In lung, there was only a small trend of decreased expression of tip cell marker genes (Extended Data Fig. 6m), and several genes related to apoptosis were upregulated (Extended Data Fig. 6l,m).

In the kidney, similar to the heart, Vegfr2 deletion for 3 days led to an increase in the relative proportion of ECs of bigger vessels (arteries and veins), suggesting fast depletion of a fraction of capillary ECs in only 3 days (Extended Data Fig. 6n–q). Proliferation and clonal expansion of kidney capillary ECs were also reduced (Extended Data Figs. 6o,q and 7d), as shown by immunostaining for Ki-67 (Fig. 2f). GSEA showed the expected decrease in expression of E2F, G2M and mitotic spindle genes and an increase in the expression of genes related to p53, xenobiotic metabolism and apoptosis (Extended Data Fig. 6r,s), indicative of cellular stress in kidney ECs after short-term Vegfr2 deletion from P4 to P7. Similar to brain and heart ECs, the expression of tip cell marker genes was also decreased in kidney Vegfr2IECKO-P4–P7 ECs (Extended Data Fig. 6t).

In contrast to Vegfr2IECKO-P4–P7 ECs, Vegfr2KO-E8.5–P7 ECs exhibited predominantly upregulation rather than downregulation of genes, including marked upregulation of several venous-enriched genes (compare Figs. 3a,b and 4a).

In contrast to brain Vegfr2IECKO-P4–P7 ECs, brain Vegfr2KO-E8.5–P7 ECs had increased frequencies of venous capillaries (Extended Data Fig. 8b–d) and marked upregulation of the key regulator of vein specification Nr2f2 (Extended Data Fig. 8e). Similarly to Vegfr2IECKO-P4–P7 ECs, brain Vegfr2KO-E8.5–P7 ECs had decreased frequencies of activated and sprouting ECs and lower expression levels of several tip cell genes (Extended Data Fig. 8c–e). The number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs), particularly upregulated genes, was much higher (1,621 versus 569 DEGs) in Vegfr2KO-E8.5–P7 ECs; this is likely to represent a long-term adaptation response to loss of VEGFR2 function (compare Extended Data Fig. 8e with Fig. 3g). Whereas in Vegfr2IECKO-P4–P7 brain ECs, genetic pathways related to EC proliferation and cellular activity (E2F targets, MYC, oxidative phosphorylation and glycolysis) were paradoxically increased, in Vegfr2KO-E8.5–P7 ECs they were decreased (compare Extended Data Fig. 8e,f with Fig. 3g,h). This suggests that brain Vegfr2KO-E8.5–P7 ECs adapt to the long-term loss of VEGFR2 signalling through a decrease in cellular activity and upregulation of Nr2f2, which probably decreases their dependence on VEGF signalling for growth and survival.

In the heart, we confirmed previous histological analysis showing that heart Vegfr2KO-E8.5–P7 ECs proliferated at levels similar to those of control cells and markedly higher than those of Vegfr2IECKO-P4–P7 ECs (compare Fig. 3k,m with Fig. 4b–d). They also had many more DEGs (1,750 DEGs versus 242 DEGs), particularly upregulated genes (compare Fig. 3n with Fig. 4e). GSEA showed increased epithelial–mesenchymal transition, KRAS signalling and angiogenesis, and less expression of metabolic-pathway-related genes (Fig. 4e,f), as seen in the brain. Expression of tip or activated EC genes was not as compromised in Vegfr2KO-E8.5–P7 ECs compared with Vegfr2IECKO-P4–P7 ECs (Fig. 4g), suggesting some adaptation over time to the loss of VEGFR2. Notably, Vegfr2KO-E8.5–P7 ECs were four times more frequent in veins (Fig. 4c,d), similar to our observations in the retina (Fig. 1d–f), confirming that mosaic loss of Vegfr2 causes mutant ECs to expand more in veins. In comparison with Vegfr2IECKO-P4–P7 ECs, Vegfr2KO-E8.5–P7 ECs showed strong upregulation of most venous-specific genes, including Nr2f2, Fbln2, Igf1 and Vwf (Fig. 4e). This was not caused only by the increased proportion of venous ECs in these mutants; capillary and angiogenic ECs also showed much higher expression of these venous marker genes, indicating venousization of mutant Vegfr2KO-E8.5–P7 capillary ECs over time (Fig. 4h,p).

In the lung, the same adaptation and venousization of Vegfr2KO-E8.5–P7 ECs was observed. Mutant cells were four times more frequent in veins (Fig. 4i–k), and even capillary or CAR4+ ECs showed significantly upregulated expression of lung venous genes including Vegfc, Nr2f2, Vwf and Fbln2 (Fig. 4l,m and Extended Data Fig. 9a,b). Similar to findings in the other organs, lung Vegfr2KO-E8.5–P7 ECs had significantly more DEGs than lung Vegfr2IECKO-P4–P7 ECs (3,116 vs 502), particularly upregulated genes (compare Extended Data Fig. 6k with Fig. 4m). Volcano plots and GSEA revealed upregulation of many venous-enriched and epithelial–mesenchymal transition genes, as well as oestrogen response genes. Lung Vegfr2KO-E8.5–P7 ECs had lower expression of E2F targets and metabolism genes, as seen in the other organs (Fig. 4m,n).

Notably, deletion of Vegfr2 for 2 weeks in adult organ blood vessels did not induce venousization of adult Vegfr2KO ECs; instead, it decreased expression of venous genes (Extended Data Fig. 8g–u), indicating that upregulation of Nr2f2 and venousization may represent a long-term adaptative response to VEGFR2 loss that occurs only during active angiogenesis.

The scRNA-seq data indicating venousization of Vegfr2KO-E8.5–P7 ECs over time were confirmed by immunostaining for the master regulator of venous development NR2F2 (also known as COUP-TFII) (Fig. 4o,p and Extended Data Fig. 9c,d). To understand the venousization requirement for long-term adaptation of Vegfr2KO ECs, we also analysed Apln-FlpO iFlpMosaic Vegfr2Flox/Flox, Nr2f2Flox/Flox mice. When ECs lacked both VEGFR2 and NR2F2 (also known as COUP-TFII), their capacity to expand was significantly reduced, particularly in veins (Fig. 4q,r and Extended Data Fig. 9e). This suggests that endothelial venousization is a key mechanism of adaptation to long-term loss of VEGFR2 signalling during angiogenesis that enables mutant cells to expand in veins and derived vessels over time.

In addition to analysing embryonic and postnatal angiogenesis, we tested the capacity of Vegfr2KO ECs to form tumour vessels over a period of 2 weeks. When Vegfr2 deletion was induced a few days before tumour implantation, Vegfr2IECKO-day-3–adult ECs formed tumour vessels mainly at the tumour border and failed to penetrate deeper into the tumour, where they were 5.2-fold less frequent than control ECs (Extended Data Fig. 10a). By contrast, Vegfr2KO-E8.5–adult ECs, which had undergone venousization throughout embryonic and postnatal development, could better reach inside the tumour and were only 2.6-fold less frequent than control ECs (Extended Data Fig. 10b). These data show that although VEGFR2 signalling promotes tumour angiogenesis, ECs can progressively adapt to its absence and sustain tumour angiogenesis.

In addition to our findings with respect to venousization, we observed significant upregulation of expression of other VEGF receptor ligands in Vegfr2KO-E8.5–P7 ECs (Extended Data Fig. 11a), including PGF and VEGFC, which bind and activate VEGFR1 and VEGFR3, respectively1. In the absence of VEGFR2, VEGFR1 is the only other receptor that can bind VEGFA. However, all studies so far have shown that VEGFR1 kinase activity is too weak29,30, whereas its extracellular soluble form (sVEGFR1 or sFLT1) acts as a strong VEGF decoy receptor31. Therefore, when VEGFR2 is present, VEGFR1 is considered to mainly antagonize VEGFA–VEGFR2 signalling and angiogenesis. However, we found that Vegfr1 expression was much higher in ECs in the brain, the organ least sensitive to VEGFR2 loss (Extended Data Fig. 11b). We also found that during postnatal angiogenesis, sVegfr1 showed 2–5-fold lower expression than the membrane full-length Vegfr1 (flVegfr1) mRNA form, except in brain ECs (Extended Data Fig. 11c), suggesting a potential signalling role for flVEGFR1.

To evaluate whether VEGFR1 could compensate for loss of VEGFR2, we induced mosaic codeletion of Vegfr1 and Vegfr2 in ECs with iSuRe-HadCre from P1 to P7 (Vegfr1/2IECKO-P1–P7). Compared with Vegfr2KO ECs, Vegfr1/2KO ECs showed a further decrease in ability to migrate and form retina capillaries, but not veins or arteries (Fig. 5a). In veins, Vegfr1/2KO ECs could clonally expand (Extended Data Fig. 11d). Brain and heart Vegfr1/2KO ECs proliferated less than Vegfr2KO ECs at P7 (Extended Data Fig. 11e,f). Deletion of both Vegfr2 and Vegfr1 had a stronger effect on endothelial and pup survival (Extended Data Fig. 11g–i), confirming the requirement for VEGFR1 signalling when VEGFR2 is absent in capillaries. Mosaic deletion of Vegfr1 alone also led to a decrease in EC migration towards the VEGF-rich front (Extended Data Fig. 11j). In contrast to deletion of Vegfr1 in all ECs, mosaic loss of Vegfr1 in single ECs did not increase VEGF signalling (as assessed by ESM1 expression), probably owing to normal levels of sFLT1 present in the mostly wild-type microenvironment (Extended Data Fig. 11k).

In addition to VEGFR1 and VEGFR2, ECs express VEGFR3. Previous studies have shown that VEGFR3 is strongly expressed by capillary and venous ECs; however, this receptor cannot bind VEGF (VEGFA), only VEGFC and VEGFD, which are key molecules for lymphangiogenesis but less relevant to blood vessel angiogenesis. Vegfr2IECKO-E8.5–P7 ECs showed very strong upregulation of VEGFC (Extended Data Fig. 11a), which could signal to VEGFR3 in the absence of VEGFR2. Loss of VEGFR3 in growing retina ECs has been shown to induce angiogenesis, suggesting that VEGFR3 negatively regulates VEGFR2 signalling32. However, single Vegfr3IECKO-P1–P7 ECs also had decreased ability to migrate and reach the leading edge (Extended Data Fig. 11l–n). Arterial ECs and smooth muscle cells express high levels of VEGFC33,34,35 a ligand that can bind both VEGFR2 and VEGFR3. Compared with Vegfr2IECKO-P1-P7 ECs, Vegfr2/3IECKO-P1-P7 ECs had a very similar zonal distribution in capillaries and veins (Fig. 5b) but a 3.4-fold lower frequency in arteries (Fig. 5c); this suggests an important role for VEGFC to VEGFR3 signalling in arterial mobilization or survival, but only when VEGFR2 signalling is absent. Loss of both VEGFR2 and VEGFR3 did not compromise the proliferation of surviving ECs in any vascular segments (Fig. 5d).

We also observed that Vegfr2KO ECs had decreased VEGFR3 expression only at the angiogenic front, where VEGFA levels are highest. By contrast Vegfr1/2KO ECs showed strong downregulation of VEGFR3 in all vascular segments, except in arteries (Fig. 5e). Vegfr1/2/3IECKO-P1-P7 ECs behaved similarly to Vegfr1/2IECKO-P1-P7 ECs, except for a pronounced decrease in formation of arterial vessels (Fig. 5f–h). Taken together, our findings show that in the absence of VEGFR2 signalling, VEGFR1 is required to sustain VEGFR3 expression and growth and migration of capillary ECs. In arteries, VEGFR3 continues to be expressed after loss of VEGFR1 and VEGFR2 and regulates development of arteries when the other VEGFA receptors are missing. Notably, vein ECs can survive and clonally expand in the complete absence of all VEGF receptor signalling (Fig. 5i).

Our study challenges the longstanding view that VEGFR2 signalling is indispensable for endothelial proliferation, migration and sprouting during angiogenesis1,22. By using iFlpMosaics to induce ratiometric genetic mosaics of wild-type and Vegfr2KO ECs, we demonstrated that these mutant cells are capable of surviving, proliferating and competing with wild-type ECs during the entire embryonic and postnatal organ growth period (Extended Data Fig. 12). Contrary to previous studies suggesting that VEGFR2 is required for all forms of vascular differentiation and development10,12,36, our findings indicate that VEGFR2-independent mechanisms support angiogenesis in certain vascular beds, particularly the long-term development of venous and perivenous capillaries.

We identified significant organ-specific differences in adaptation to acute loss of VEGFR2 signalling (Extended Data Fig. 12a), thereby extending previous findings37,38. Whereas angiogenic retina ECs were strongly affected, brain ECs were the least affected, retaining high angiogenic potential, partially because of VEGFR1 compensation (Extended Data Fig. 12b). By contrast, postnatal heart, kidney and liver ECs were more sensitive to acute withdrawal of VEGFR2, showing partial depletion of Vegfr2KO ECs in capillaries, as well as a partial reduction in proliferation. The postnatal lung showed a distinct response, with no major changes in EC proliferation or cluster composition but instead a shift in transcriptional programs towards inflammation and metabolic adaptations. These findings highlight the heterogeneity of endothelial responses across different vascular beds and suggest that organ-specific factors influence EC resilience to perturbations in VEGF signalling.

The long-term adaptation of Vegfr2KO ECs was a notable observation. Although these cells initially had a competitive disadvantage during early embryonic and postnatal sprouting angiogenesis stages, they ultimately expanded and outcompeted wild-type cells in venous and perivenous regions, indicating that alternative pathways support their survival and proliferation in this niche. Veins are known to provide a continuous source of ECs for capillary angiogenesis and later arterialization20,23, and this venous dominance of Vegfr2KO ECs allows them to compete with wild-type cells over time. Our scRNA-seq analysis of different vascular beds further showed that ECs with prolonged loss of VEGFR2 exhibited a significant shift towards a venous transcriptional signature (venousization), marked by upregulation of many venous genes, including Nr2f2, a master regulator of veins development24,39. Vegfr2KONr2f2KO ECs were significantly less able to expand, particularly in veins, showing that venousization is a key adaptation mechanism that enables Vegfr2KO ECs to persist and contribute to vascular development (Extended Data Fig. 12c,d).

In addition to venousization, our findings demonstrate the compensatory roles of VEGFR1 and VEGFR3 in supporting survival and growth signalling when VEGFR2 is absent (Extended Data Fig. 12b). Previously, VEGFR1 has mainly been reported to negatively regulate VEGFR2 signalling during angiogenesis, because its soluble secreted version sFLT1 or VEGFR1 is a decoy receptor of VEGF1,22. In our mosaic Vegfr1KO and Vegfr1/2KO experiments, sFLT1 levels in the microenvironment were normal; this led us to find that membrane or full-length VEGFR1 acted as a positive regulator of angiogenesis and partially compensated for VEGFR2 loss, supporting both EC survival and migration. The mosaic single loss of VEGFR3 also showed that this receptor was a positive regulator of EC migration towards the leading edge. This was consistent with results obtained using VEGFR3-blocking antibodies40; however, our findings were contrary to results from pan-endothelial Vegfr3KO genetics experiments32 in which the receptor was lost from all ECs. In this case, VEGFC and/or VEGFD bioavailability in the whole body increases, thereby increasing VEGFC–VEGFR2 signalling, whereas in low-frequency Vegfr3KO mosaics this does not occur. In addition, Vegfr2/3KO ECs mobilized less to the angiogenic front and had decreased arterialization when compared with Vegfr2KO ECs. The ability of these alternative VEGF receptors to sustain EC survival, migration and proliferation at the single-cell level indicates potential functional plasticity and compensation within the VEGF signalling network, as suggested previously by studies using classical full-tissue conditional genetics12,32,37.

An important implication of our study is that EC sensitivity to VEGFR2 loss varies depending on the vascular context and the time since the loss. We found that acute loss of VEGFR2 during active angiogenesis resulted in significant EC depletion in the first 3 days, particularly depletion of tip cells and other ECs at the angiogenic front, where VEGF signalling is highest. However, Vegfr2KO ECs in VEGF-low areas and those that had undergone long-term adaptation were able to persist and even contribute to a substantial portion of capillary vessels, particularly those around veins. This suggests that ECs experiencing prolonged VEGFR2 deficiency undergo venousization in VEGF-low areas, enabling them to survive and then grow in the absence of canonical VEGF signalling. Our tumour models showed that Vegfr2KO ECs could form tumour vessels, particularly if they had previously undergone venousization during embryonic angiogenesis. The use of ratiometric and trackable iFlpMosaics was key to obtaining these findings.

Our work may also provide biomarkers and insights into resistance mechanisms to anti-VEGF therapies5,41,42,43. Adaptation of ECs to VEGFR2 loss by engaging alternative venous-related angiogenic pathways may also occur in tumours. If this is confirmed, therapeutic strategies that simultaneously block the compensatory and venous-related pathways identified here may be necessary to overcome resistance to anti-VEGF treatments.

In conclusion, our study reshapes our understanding of VEGFR2-dependent angiogenesis by demonstrating that ECs possess intrinsic mechanisms to adapt to long-term absence of VEGFR2 signalling. The discovery of endothelial venousization and compensatory VEGF receptor signalling provides a new framework for investigation of alternative modes of vascular development and resistance to anti-VEGF therapies.

Genes used for differential gene expression analysis were prefiltered using FUNCTION_FilterLowExpressionGenes_onlyTable; this removes genes with low and highly heterogeneous expression patterns across cells, which are often inconsistently detected between datasets and potentially reflect technical artefacts such as ambient RNA contamination. The function acts as an empirical, distribution-based filter to improve robustness of downstream differential expression analysis. Differential expression heatmaps were generated using a custom function (FUNCTION_Find_PlotGenesHeatmap_FC_Metadata), implemented in-house and based on the pheatmap package.

Most numerical data shown in charts were first compiled and processed with Microsoft Excel 2019 and 2024 and then analysed and plotted with GraphPad Prism v.10.1.0. All bar graphs show mean ± s.d. Experiments were repeated with independent animals, as stated in the source data file or figure legends. Comparisons between two sample groups with a Gaussian distribution were performed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests. Comparisons among more than two groups were made by one-way or two-way analysis of variance followed by multiple comparison tests. Data points were analysed and plotted with GraphPad Prism. No randomization or blinding was used, and the animals or tissues were selected for analysis on the basis of their genotype, the detected Cre- or FlpO-dependent recombination frequency and the quality of multiplex immunostaining. For mosaic analysis, only samples with less than 50% recombination were selected. The sample sizes were chosen according to the observed statistical variation and published protocols.
