Pocognoli's problems are just the tip of the iceberg when it comes to those running the show at the SFA

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

Published: 10:30 EST, 20 November 2026 | Updated: 11:25 EST, 20 November 2026

For anyone with anything resembling a retentive memory, the alarm bells were going off all over the shop on the day Sebastien Pocognoli was unveiled as the new Scotland head coach.

Scottish Football Association (SFA) chief executive Ian Maxwell and chief football officer Craig Mulholland were there to crow about an extensive search of foreign fields to rival Captain Cook’s journeys around the Pacific and how data had helped land ‘one of Europe’s most sought-after coaches’ and secure the ‘aggressive, front-foot style of play’ that was going to bring a desired freshness.

It was impossible to avoid the elephant in the room, though. Two-and-a-half months earlier, ahead of the disaster zone that was the World Cup, they’d handed Steve Clarke a fresh four-year contract.

He was the polar opposite to Poco. Aggressive and front-foot, he most certainly wasn’t. The SFA knew Clarke didn’t have it in him to go for it at a major finals – as evidenced by two separate European Championship failures – but they decided to stick with the status quo anyway. Just as they did after Euro 2024, when he should have been sacked.

When Clarke finally did go of his own accord after the summer’s events in North America, Maxwell stated he was ‘sad’ the former Kilmarnock manager had stepped down and put a big dollop of blame on ‘some fairly hysterical commentary back home’.

SFA chief football officer Craig Mulholland,  Pocognoli and SFA chief executive Ian Maxwell

It just didn’t add up. How do you go from thinking a guy like Clarke is the future to tearing everything up, drafting in a London-based analytics firm to tell you who to go for as his replacement and coming up with a 39-year-old Belgian who wants to go for broke?

‘It doesn’t matter how you get there,’ offered Maxwell. ‘Let’s not get too hung up on the whys and the what-ifs.’

Unfortunately, people are now starting to get hung up over the fact Pocognoli, for all the good on his CV, looks like he’s in the wrong movie – with his refusal to break from a failing three-at-the-back strategy suggesting a diploma from the Wilfried Nancy School of Intransigence ought to be on that resume too.

They are getting hung up on the fact captain Andy Robertson came out after the thrashing from Switzerland’s B-team to reveal the mood inside the camp has changed and warn there’s no breathing space to sort this out.

And they are getting hung up on the fact that it’s becoming considerably easier to reach the conclusion those above all of them at the very top of the SFA have never had a proper grip on things at all.

Clarke, of course, only exacerbated those concerns in September by expressing the view at a public event that Maxwell and Co ‘panicked a little bit’ in giving him that incomprehensible long-term deal because he’d threatened to announce he was quitting before the finals unless an agreement was reached.

‘I wanted to control my narrative,’ he explained. It was hard to know who emerged from those admissions looking worse.

Clarke’s off the plot now, though, and it’s Maxwell, Mulholland and the SFA president Mike Mulraney left to try and convince us all that there is some kind of rhyme or reason to it all.

How things shape up with Pocognoli is an acid test for them. The rise or fall of a Scotland manager always hogs headlines. Yet, it is only one of a number of things coming together in what is starting to feel like a bit of a perfect storm.

Not so long ago, Maxwell and Mulholland were also yakking plenty about their statement project to increase game time for Scotland-eligible Under-21s.

It’s already in tatters, by the looks of things. The plans were watered down last month, without anything like the same fanfare, to involve Under-23 players and the intended votes on the proposals were pushed back.

A number of Premiership sides haven’t warmed to it and it remains to be seen whether it goes to a vote at all. Certainly, the radical change the top brass envisioned isn’t going to happen and that can only be regarded as an indicator that these guys running the game aren’t capable of taking it with them.

The start of Sebastien Pocognoli's reign has been concerning for Scotland fans

Dundee manager Steven Pressley’s intervention over recent days hasn’t helped them either. After a long period on the outside looking in, Scotland have turned out in three major tournaments in the last five years.

That has raised many millions of pounds, but the effect on elite level is questionable. Pressley talked of the sport being ‘built on sand’ as a result of there being no kind of national facility and you’d better believe it gained traction.

‘It says a lot about our strategy, our longevity and our long-term vision and we are finally seeing the effects of that. We have shown none of that over the period and it is all horribly catching up with us,’ he stated.

The SFA do own Hampden, of course, thanks to that peculiar £5million deal from 2019 that involved Willie Haughey and Sir Tom Hunter ponying up half the money. Yet, how distant those days of Maxwell talking about taking examples from the way VfB Stuttgart revamped the Mercedes-Benz Arena and potentially implementing them in Mount Florida seem now.

Everyone appears to have given up on making the National Stadium better. Other problems have overtaken that old chestnut. Refereeing isn’t good enough. VAR isn’t up to scratch. General facilities require considerable improvement.

Should this Youth Plan fail, there may also be a need for the SFA to look deeper within when addressing the dearth of top-tier talent coming through.

It’s OK to blame clubs, but what about the level of coaching – and coach education – that exists at the national association? That’s before we even get to the ‘massive lack of expertise and education’ at the younger end of grassroots raised by St Mirren boss Craig McLeish.

The SFA set-up for producing coaches was once the envy of many. We all remember Jose Mourinho and others at the Largs finishing school. Now? Even Maxwell, after stating there were no viable Scottish rivals to Pocognoli for the Scotland job, admitted the coaching pathway needs looked at.

There is only one problem here, though. Maxwell has been the chief executive since May 2018. He’s in his ninth year in the role. What has he really achieved? What have his big ideas been?

Yes, Scotland made it back to major finals under Clarke, but Clarke was a painfully obvious choice to be made national coach in 2019. He’d worked wonders at Rugby Park and did transfer that to the international scene until it came to actually turning out in those showpiece competitions.

Now they’ve had to get creative, there is so much hanging on Pocognoli’s reign for the guys at the top.

Maxwell was a bloke who landed in the role of Partick Thistle GM almost by mistake after failing to beat Jackie McNamara to the manager’s job. Just before leaving for the SFA, the Jags got relegated.

Talk of redeveloping Hampden has gone quiet as other problems take the spotlight

At his Hampden unveiling, he had to fend off questions about only getting that position through behind-the-scenes politicking and insist he wouldn’t be a puppet for anyone.

Mulholland also seems a surprising choice as the architect of the national sport’s renovation. He spent two decades in the Rangers academy and eight years as head of it.

Has it been a conveyor belt of talent for the Ibrox first-team? Do you recall much wailing and gnashing of teeth when he left in 2023 amid a wider changing of the guard?

Then, there’s Mulraney, the former Alloa chairman and SFA director for over a decade.

You don’t see much of him these days. Maybe because people want to ask about his position as chair of FIFA’s finance committee in the wake of the most greed-infested World Cup ever and the ongoing soap opera surrounding Gianni Infantino. But maybe not.

How things work out with Pocognoli might end up being the one thing that puts all these guys under the kind of examination that cannot be bodyswerved, but it’s only the tip of the iceberg.
