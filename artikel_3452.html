Listings for Sex Work Have Infiltrated Google Maps. Could It Be the New Craigslist?

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

My elderly mom, who has been deeply unwell for a number of years, has recently started clenching her hands into balls and curling her feet inward like little hooks. Her gerontologist said that, horribly enough, her mind is no longer sending a message to her body that she needs to relax. Medicine can’t help, she explained, but massage might. So one evening, I turned to Google Maps, where I increasingly find everything from authentic Thai restaurants to the best place to buy youth soccer cleats. I guided the map app to the Upper West Side, one of the country’s most expensive neighborhoods, and put one word in the search bar: “massage.”

Then I squinted. Why was the massage table at “Ashley massage and spa” lit by dark purple light? And why did another listing have photos of the masseuse herself in skin-tight exercise wear, with directions to contact her on Telegram? But it all became clearer when I spotted a description of the type of massage services offered: “I’m available for deep tissue, Swedish, or any kind of massage appointment with a happy ending.”

Even if nothing should surprise us anymore about our tech overlords, I was still pretty shocked to find that Google Maps appeared to be the new Craigslist, famous for its battle with regulators over sex work classifieds. Somewhere between figuring out the location of the neighborhood CVS and “coffee near me,” it seems like Google branched out. I’d always assumed it adhered to local laws—sex work is illegal in New York state—but perhaps Silicon Valley’s fave “we’re just a platform not a publisher” defense applied here. Otherwise, why on earth was the algorithm retrieving sex work listings when I was just trying to help my 84-year-old mom?

I emailed Google PR to ask what, exactly, was going on, giving them a deadline of the next day to respond.

The response came from a senior publicist, quickly. “Hi Vanessa - I'm on the Google Comms team. Would you be able to provide the listings and screenshots?”

I did so, then received another response: “I’m reviewing all of this, but wondering if you have wiggle room in your deadline? Want to make sure I can get you the right info in time, and I think that timing tomorrow may be challenging.”

I responded that I could wait until a couple more days to find out what Google had to say.

In the meantime, I contacted some experts.

Mike Blumenthal has been studying Google Maps almost since it existed. A co-founder of Near Media, he began writing about the product in 2005 and says he first documented large-scale spam on Maps in New York City in 2009. When I showed him the strange massage listings I’d found scattered across New York, he wasn’t particularly surprised. Google Maps is an extraordinarily valuable way to get in front of customers, which gives gray and black market businesses a powerful incentive to game it. Once they figure out how to convince Google’s algorithm that real people should be directed to those listings, “they can make bank.”

Google removes an enormous amount of fake listings, reviews and images, and has tightened its verification procedures over the years, said Blumenthal. But when those systems fail, enforcement is largely reactive. Even Blumenthal, who has volunteered in Google’s forums for nineteen years and knows how to escalate a complaint, said getting suspicious listings removed can take upwards of a month or six weeks. When a problem attracts press attention, though, he says Google can act within hours. What Google doesn’t do, in his view, is spend enough to prevent the same problems from recurring. “They won’t put in place systemic fixes. They won’t put in place people to deal with this quickly…They don’t want the cost.”

After I talked to Blumenthal, I read the reviews of some of the massage businesses that Google Maps was serving up nationwide. Not all were positive. One reviewer of the “Asian Palace Spa” in San Francisco, writing five years ago, gave the business one star and called it a “fraud,” saying he had paid for an hour-long massage but received only 15 minutes before being asked for additional money for “extra services.” The review had received 17 likes. Another one-star reviewer from the same period said, “they don’t have spa, just pay for room and then pay for lady for whatever you do.”

But many more of the illicit massage businesses had positive reviews, about services that were above and beyond, with smiley kissy face icons attached. Were these just independent sex workers trying to make a living? Was it so wrong that they were using Google Maps to find clients? Perhaps it’s needless to say, but when I called several of the phone numbers and introduced myself as a reporter, information was not forthcoming.

I started to feel a little queasy as I reviewed a listing that was clearly written by a woman working for herself, just trying to make a listing. But then, as I looked more closely at others, I realized that the photos were so similar, and the setups of the listings so close to each other, that they must be coordinated by an organized group. Whether that was a lead-generator for escorts who needed extra clients, or a scammer, or a sex trafficker, I couldn’t tell.

I searched for illicit massage listings in different cities, and found them all over neighborhoods in Los Angeles, from Beverly Hills to Koreatown. I also began to notice that most of the massage spots seemed as though they were unlikely to actually be located in the spots they claimed to be. For example, a masseuse who appeared to be nude from breastbone up, with a butterfly tattoo decorating her clavicle, located her business on the Upper East Side’s Museum Mile, home to multimillion dollar apartments and New York’s great cultural institutions, but she also included information about a certain corner in Queens, where it seemed like she could’ve also set up shop

Now that I had more information, I tried to understand whether Google Maps bore any responsibility for these listings, calling Chelsea Binns, a professor at John Jay College of Criminal Justice who studies cybercrime. “It’s interesting from an anti-fraud perspective, because we rely on Google Maps virtually every day now,” she said, taking the call from Japan. “No one takes out a hard map anymore. And there’s a bit of borrowed credibility, because these listings are on Google. It’s Google Maps!”

Despite this, Binns says responsibility rests with each individual user to decipher the legitimacy of a Google Maps business, a caveat emptor situation. “A lot of us don’t read disclaimers, but somewhere deep within the service it says they’re not at fault,” explains Binns. “It’s ‘use at your own risk, research independently, and verify.’”

“But why don’t they care if it’s an illegal service?” I ask. “They wouldn’t have guns on there.”

“They do care,” says Binns. “They actively review their content. I suspect this slipped through the cracks. But at some point you’re just playing whack-a-mole. You take one down, and another pops up.”

This was the perspective of AI-critic Brian Merchant, creator of the Blood in the Machine podcast and newsletter. “As [Google] has moved their resources into moderating, training, and shipping AI, I would not be surprised if resources that were dedicated to moderating are falling off, and things are just staying up longer,” he says. “And maybe criminal organizations have been emboldened, realizing that they can get their links listed higher and longer, and it’ll be weeks before they’re taken down.”

But the next time I checked out the massage listings near my mom’s apartment, almost all had disappeared. And I heard back from Google with a statement: “Google Maps only displays places that people can visit or interact with in the real world, and we've removed the businesses that do not exist at these locations.”

This seemed to indicate that Google had taken action to remove listings in my mom’s neighborhood, and that I was also correct when I noticed that some of the sex workers weren’t actually in the locations they said they were. Google also directed me to their policies against adult-themed content, which explain that no one uploading listings can violate local laws, especially in the areas of “alcohol, gambling, tobacco, guns, health and medical devices, regulated pharmaceuticals, adult services, and financial services.”

A Google spokesperson further pushed back against Mike Blumenthal’s contention that the company doesn’t want to incur costs monitoring Google Maps, noting, “we placed posting restrictions on more than 782,000 policy-violating accounts and removed over 13 million fake Business Profiles, further defending genuine businesses on Maps. We blocked or removed over 292 million policy-violating reviews, while publishing more than 1 billion helpful reviews.” Google also added, “we hold bad actors accountable through account suspension and litigation. We display alerts to let you know if we've recently removed suspicious five-star reviews in certain circumstances. These warnings help you understand quickly if a place may be engaging in unfair review practices.”

They also directed me to their policies against adult-themed content, which explain that no one uploading listings can violate local laws, especially in the areas of “alcohol, gambling, tobacco, guns, health and medical devices, regulated pharmaceuticals, adult services, and financial services.”

I contacted Danielle Citron, a professor at the University of Virginia School of Law, to understand more. She said that Google’s statement made sense. “Ordering, filtering, and otherwise structuring the presentation of content” is likely to be shielded as “publishing and speaking” under Section 230, the law that protects tech platforms from liability for user-uploaded content, she said. But there could be a wrinkle. “A different question is getting paid for and designing systems in a particular way, [which] may not be immunized,” she explains, mentioning social media cases like New Mexico v. Meta, “where [a lawsuit challenging platform design] was permitted to go to trial because it was about what Meta did in their design of sites, not what users did to kids.” So a question could potentially be asked by attorneys in the future along these lines: Is the very structure of Google Maps allowing gray and black market businesses to flourish?

Within a week, some listings that Google removed when I last checked seemed to have popped up in New York again, but there were far less of them. Still, I found it confounding that in trying to find a massage for my mom, I had uncovered a network of massage spots that a less savvy consumer than myself might have imagined were legitimate. “We really need a better understanding of this landscape, because we need to look out for the consumer,” Binns told me. “Your mom is someone who is vulnerable in society, and that can take many forms. She’s perhaps incapable of doing the due diligence that’s required just to get an ordinary service.”

At the same time, I was aware that no one wants to change Google’s algorithm to the point that it screws over legitimate small businesses that rely on the visibility of Google Maps. Blumenthal explained the conundrum to me this way: if Google tightens their mapping search controls too much, “businesses’ reality becomes worse, because you can’t get into [Maps]. If they loosen it, consumers’ reality becomes worse, because shit gets in.”

After all of this back and forth, I still had to solve my initial problem: securing a massage for my mom. I contacted a few spots in her neighborhood that looked legitimate, but they said she’d have to raise herself on the massage table, which, since she’s in a wheelchair, wouldn’t work. I decided to reach out to her Medicare-supplied occupational therapist, who got back to me right away.

He said he’d been meaning to call. He needed to send me a form saying that my mom was no longer able to participate in the exercises they were performing together, and she’d likely be kicked off Medicare payment for them in the near future. That was a bummer, and made me regret calling him at all, but I figured while I had him on the line I’d ask another question: Does Medicare (original, parts A & B) cover therapeutic massage? He said no, apologetically.

I’d spent a week investigating a system that made it remarkably easy to sell massages that weren't massages, only to discover how difficult it was to obtain one that was.
