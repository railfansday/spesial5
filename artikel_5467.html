How the British royal family makes its money: 10 sources and mechanisms

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Free daily briefing on global business news.

A guide to how the royal family makes money through 10 funding sources and mechanisms, from the Sovereign Grant and Crown Estate to two duchies and voluntary taxes

The British monarchy's public funding is pegged to the profits of a property business the king does not personally own. Its private income comes from landed estates, one of which was created in 1337. Following the money means following both tracks, plus costs that other parts of government cover.

Any explanation of how the royal family makes money starts with a split between public and private funds. The public side is the Sovereign Grant. It pays for official duties, royal household staff and the upkeep of occupied royal palaces. Since the Sovereign Grant Act 2011 took effect in 2012, the grant has been calculated as a share of Crown Estate profits from two years earlier. It is not a fixed sum voted by Parliament each year. The private side includes income from the Duchy of Lancaster, which goes to the monarch. It also includes the Duchy of Cornwall, whose income goes to the heir to the throne.

A second distinction separates property held by the office from property owned by the person. The Crown Estate belongs to the reigning monarch by virtue of the throne. Its holdings include Regent Street in London and much of the seabed around England, Wales and Northern Ireland. A commercial organization runs it independently, and its profits go to the Treasury. The Royal Collection is held in trust for future sovereigns and the nation. Balmoral in Scotland and Sandringham in Norfolk are different. The monarch owns them privately, and their upkeep comes from private funds.

A third layer covers costs and obligations that never pass through royal accounts. Security for the royal family is paid from government and police budgets. Some costs of state occasions are met by government departments. Since 1993, the monarch has paid income tax and capital gains tax on private income under a voluntary arrangement. A separate 1993 agreement exempts assets passed from one sovereign to the next from inheritance tax.

In 1760, George III gave up the hereditary revenues of the Crown in exchange for a fixed payment known as the Civil List. That system lasted until 2012. The 10 slides that follow explain a selection of sources and mechanisms behind royal finances, not a complete ledger. Each one sets out how a single piece works, who receives the money and where the public and private boundaries fall.

The Sovereign Grant is the main source of public money for the British monarchy. It pays for the monarch's official duties, the staff who carry out that work and the upkeep of the royal palaces that are in use.

Parliament does not vote on a fresh sum for the grant each year. A formula sets the amount instead. That formula makes the grant a share of the profits earned by the Crown Estate, the property portfolio attached to the office of monarch.

The monarch does not collect those profits directly. They are paid to the Treasury. The grant is public money whose size is set by reference to those profits.

The calculation uses a profit figure from two years earlier. The grant paid in any year is therefore based on how the Crown Estate performed two years before. It does not track the estate's results in the year the money is spent.

The Sovereign Grant Act 2011 created this formula. It took effect in 2012.

The percentage of profits used in the formula is not fixed for all time. It has been changed since 2011. The size of the grant can therefore move for two separate reasons. One is a revision to that percentage. The other is a change in the Crown Estate's profits.

The grant's purpose is narrower than the phrase "royal money" might suggest. It is meant for the official side of the monarchy — the work the sovereign does as head of state, and the buildings and people needed to support it. It does not pay for the monarch's privately owned homes. It is also not a personal salary for the monarch or the wider family.

Costs that fall outside the grant's scope are met from other sources. These include private income held by the monarch, which pays for expenses the public grant does not cover.

The Crown Estate is a property portfolio whose profits go to the Treasury, not to the monarch. It belongs to whoever is reigning, by virtue of the office of sovereign. The king does not own it as personal property and cannot treat its earnings as private income.

The portfolio is run independently as a commercial business. The monarch does not manage it. Its legal framework is the Crown Estate Act 1961, the statute that governs how the estate operates.

Its holdings mix city property with natural resources. In London, the estate owns Regent Street, the West End shopping thoroughfare. Offshore, it holds much of the seabed around England, Wales and Northern Ireland.

That seabed produces income through offshore wind leasing. Developers who want to build wind farms in those waters lease seabed rights from the estate. The income from those leases adds to the estate's overall profits, alongside earnings from its property holdings.

The path those profits follow is what links the Crown Estate to royal funding. The money passes to the Treasury, where it becomes part of government revenue. A share then comes back to the monarchy through the Sovereign Grant, the main public payment to the royal household.

The connection is indirect. The monarchy's public funding is tied to how the Crown Estate performs. Even so, the monarch never collects the estate's profits. The amount returned depends on the share set for the Sovereign Grant, not on any personal claim to the earnings.

The arrangement also keeps the estate apart from the royal family's private wealth. The Duchy of Lancaster and the Duchy of Cornwall operate separately from it. So do Balmoral and Sandringham, which the monarch owns privately. When the throne passes to a new monarch, the Crown Estate passes with the office. It was never the outgoing sovereign's to keep.

The Duchy of Lancaster is a private estate that gives the reigning monarch an income outside the public funding system. That income, known as the Privy Purse, pays for expenses the Sovereign Grant does not cover.

The monarch holds the duchy under the title Duke $DUK of Lancaster. The estate belongs to whoever reigns, and it passes to each new sovereign with the crown.

The duchy's holdings include farmland and commercial property. The income they produce goes to the monarch rather than to the Treasury.

That flow of money separates the duchy from the Crown Estate. Crown Estate profits go to the Treasury, and the monarchy gets a share back only through the Sovereign Grant. Duchy of Lancaster income reaches the monarch directly.

The Privy Purse covers spending that falls outside the grant's categories of official duties, palace upkeep and household staff. Some of that spending supports relatives. The Privy Purse meets some costs of other working members of the royal family.

Privy Purse income counts as private income for tax purposes. It falls under the voluntary arrangement through which the monarch pays tax on private income.

The Duchy of Cornwall is a private estate whose income goes to the heir to the throne, who also holds the title Duke $DUK of Cornwall. Prince William has held that title, and received the duchy's income, since Sept. 8, 2022.

That was the day his father, Charles, became king. The dukedom passed from father to son on the same date the throne changed hands.

Edward III created the duchy in 1337 for his son, the Black Prince. The estate was tied to the heir from the start. That link still decides who benefits from it nearly seven centuries later.

The title and the income move together. Whoever holds the dukedom receives the duchy's revenue. The reigning monarch does not. A king or queen draws private income from a different estate, the Duchy of Lancaster.

The duchy's holdings reach into London. It owns the Oval cricket ground in Kennington and leases the ground to Surrey. That lease makes the duchy the landlord of the county cricket club's home ground.

The duchy also owns the land under Poundbury, a town development in Dorset. Construction there began in 1993. The development sits on duchy land, and the duchy's income goes to the heir.

The duchy is not liable for corporation tax. The heir pays income tax on the duchy's income on a voluntary basis instead.

Balmoral Castle in Scotland and Sandringham House in Norfolk are owned privately by the monarch. Their running costs come from private money, and the Sovereign Grant does not pay for their upkeep.

That status sets them apart from the best-known royal residences. Buckingham Palace and Windsor Castle are tied to the office of sovereign rather than to the person who holds it. As occupied palaces, they receive maintenance funding through the Sovereign Grant. Balmoral and Sandringham receive none.

Both estates came into the family through private purchases in the 19th century. Prince Albert bought Balmoral for Queen Victoria. The castle that stands there today was built for the couple. Sandringham was bought for Victoria's eldest son, the future Edward VII, while he was Prince of Wales. Neither property became part of the public estate.

Balmoral sits in Aberdeenshire, inside Cairngorms National Park. It has long served as the royal family's summer retreat. Queen Elizabeth II died there on Sept. 8, 2022. Sandringham lies in northwest Norfolk. It is where the royal family traditionally gathers at Christmas.

Neither house stands alone. Each sits at the center of a working rural estate with farmland, woodland and tenants. Parts of Sandringham House and its grounds open to paying visitors for part of the year. Balmoral's grounds also open to the public on a seasonal basis. Money from that land and those visitors is private estate income. It does not go to the Treasury.

Private ownership also means the two estates follow the person, not the office. When Charles became king in 2022, Balmoral and Sandringham became his personal property. The occupied palaces, by contrast, come with the throne itself.

For readers asking who pays for the royal family, Balmoral and Sandringham mark the line between public and private money. The palaces used for official work draw on public funding through the grant. These two country estates belong to the monarch personally, and the monarch covers their costs.

Ticket sales at royal sites earn money for the Royal Collection Trust, a registered charity, not for the royal family. The trust spends that income on conserving and displaying the Royal Collection.

The Royal Collection is not the monarch's personal property. The sovereign holds it in trust for future monarchs and for the nation. A king cannot treat it as a private asset that produces income for himself.

The trust charges admission at royal sites, including the State Rooms at Buckingham Palace and Windsor Castle. The money from those tickets stays inside the charity.

That income pays for two kinds of work. Conservation keeps objects in the collection in good condition. Display puts those objects in front of visitors.

Charitable status keeps the trust's finances apart from the royal household's. Admission revenue is not part of the Sovereign Grant. It does not go to members of the royal family.

The same split runs through the palaces themselves. The Sovereign Grant covers the upkeep of occupied royal palaces as working buildings. The objects shown in their State Rooms are a separate matter, and their care is funded through the trust.

For anyone asking who pays for the royal family, this arrangement shows that some money linked to royal sites never reaches the royals. A visitor who buys a ticket to the State Rooms at Windsor Castle is paying for the care and display of the collection, not for the household's running costs.

The British monarch has paid income tax and capital gains tax on private income since 1993. No law requires the payments. They are made voluntarily, and the arrangement has continued from Queen Elizabeth II's reign into King Charles III's.

Before 1993, the sovereign paid no income tax. Elizabeth II started paying that year and kept doing so for the rest of her reign, which ended with her death on Sept. 8, 2022. Charles, who became king that day, already paid income tax voluntarily on his own private income while he was heir. He has carried the practice on as monarch.

Two taxes fall under the arrangement. Income tax applies to the private money the monarch receives each year. Capital gains tax applies to profits made when private assets are sold. In both cases, the sovereign pays because of the voluntary agreement, not because a statute compels it. The payments go to HM Revenue and Customs, the U.K. tax authority.

The Privy Purse is one of the income streams the agreement reaches. The term refers to the income of the Duchy of Lancaster, a private estate the monarch holds as Duke $DUK of Lancaster. It covers spending that falls outside the Sovereign Grant, including some costs of other working members of the royal family. Privy Purse receipts are dealt with under the voluntary tax agreement.

The Sovereign Grant sits on the other side of the line. It is public money for the monarch's official role, and it is not treated as taxable income. That split mirrors the wider division in royal finances between what belongs to the office and what belongs to the person who holds it.

The heir to the throne follows a similar practice. Income from the Duchy of Cornwall goes to the heir, who pays income tax on it voluntarily. Prince William has held the duchy since his father became king in 2022.

Assets that pass from one British monarch to the next are exempt from inheritance tax. The exemption comes from a 1993 agreement and is tied to the succession, not to the royal family as a group.

Inheritance tax is the U.K. charge on what a person leaves behind at death. Under the 1993 arrangement, property moving from a monarch to the succeeding monarch does not trigger that charge. The recipient decides the outcome. The same asset can be exempt or taxable depending on whether it goes to the new sovereign or to someone else.

The scope is narrow. Gifts and bequests outside the sovereign-to-sovereign route follow the ordinary rules that apply to everyone else. A monarch who leaves assets to a child who is not the next sovereign, or to another relative, does not benefit from the exemption. Gifts made between other members of the family are treated the same way.

The most recent change of monarch came on Sept. 8, 2022, when Charles became king after the death of Queen Elizabeth II. That handover is the kind of transfer the sovereign-to-sovereign rule covers.

The exemption concerns what a monarch owns personally. Holdings tied to the office pass with the throne regardless of any tax agreement. The Crown Estate and the Royal Collection are two examples. The 1993 agreement does not govern them. It deals with assets a sovereign owns privately.

For royal finances, the effect is that a monarch's private wealth can reach the successor without the reduction an ordinary estate would face at death.

Royal security is not paid for out of the Sovereign Grant. Protection for members of the British royal family is funded from government and police budgets instead.

That puts the cost of guarding the royals on the state's books, not the royal household's. The grant covers the monarchy's official work. Protecting the people who carry out that work is treated as a separate public responsibility.

The arrangement also keeps security outside the formula that sets the grant. A change in what protection costs does not change the amount the royal household receives. A change in the grant does not change the protection budget either.

A similar split applies to parts of national ceremonial life. Government departments meet some of the costs of state occasions. The monarch stands at the center of these events, but part of the bill sits in departmental budgets rather than in the grant.

The split shapes any answer to the question of who pays for the royal family. A tally built only from the Sovereign Grant shows what the household spends from its main public payment. It leaves out security and the state occasion costs held elsewhere.

Public money linked to the monarchy therefore runs in two streams. One is the grant, paid to the monarchy and spent by the royal household. The other is spending by government departments and the police. That spending supports the monarchy's work without passing through royal accounts.

Royal funding moved from the Civil List, a fixed payment created under George III in 1760, to the Sovereign Grant in 2012. The newer system ties the monarchy's public funding to the profits of the Crown Estate rather than to a set sum.

The Civil List grew out of a trade. In 1760, George III handed the hereditary revenues of the Crown to the government. In return, he received a fixed payment to support the monarchy.

Under that deal, the money the Crown's revenues produced went to the government. The king and his successors relied on the agreed payment instead.

A fixed payment works differently from a share. Its value did not move up or down with the income from the revenues the king had surrendered. The payment and the revenues followed separate paths.

The arrangement lasted more than 250 years. The Sovereign Grant Act 2011, passed by Parliament, brought it to an end. The new law took effect in 2012. From that year, the Sovereign Grant replaced the Civil List.

The central change was the link between royal funding and the Crown Estate. Public money for the monarchy is no longer set as a fixed payment. It is calculated as a share of Crown Estate profits.

The profits themselves still do not go to the monarch. They are paid to the Treasury. A portion then returns to the monarchy in the form of the Sovereign Grant.

That portion has not stayed the same. The percentage used in the calculation has changed since the 2011 act was passed.

Both systems answer one question: how the state pays for the monarchy's official role. They differ in how the amount is set. Under the Civil List, the sum was fixed. Under the Sovereign Grant, it follows the profits of a property portfolio held by the Crown.
