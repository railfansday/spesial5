Institutional trading on Kalshi jumped 4,124% in six months. How much of it is hedging is unclear

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

Institutional trading in prediction-market contracts on Kalshi reached $26.3 billion in October, up from $622.7 million in April, according to figures the company provided to Fortune. That is a 4,124% increase in six months.

The growth is staggering. Less clear is how much of that money hedges economic risk and how much is trading, much of it on sports.

Kalshi counted 822 institutional accounts that had traded, up from 312 in April, with 242 trading more than $1 million a month. The figures exclude Kalshi’s perpetual futures business, market makers, designated liquidity providers, and internal accounts.

Nearly all of the $26.3 billion came from regular order-book trading rather than negotiated block trades, the company told Fortune.

Across the platform, sports dominate. Combo and sports contracts made up about 85% of Kalshi’s volume in the 30 days before Nov. 20, while financial and economics contracts together accounted for less than 1%, according to Ticker Tracker’s Kalshi volume data. Those figures cover all traders, not only institutions, and are based on face value.

Eric Zitzewitz, a Dartmouth College economics professor who studies prediction markets, told Fortune in an email that institutions trading on these platforms fall into three groups. Some make markets and mostly seek to balance their books, while others respond to requests for quotes on parlays and take the other side.

A third group trades single-event markets against small traders’ behavioral biases, such as overpricing longshots. His impression, he said, is that all three are becoming more involved.

Andy Ross, a former Standard Chartered executive who became Kalshi’s head of institutional business in March, pointed to the hedge funds that have joined or are preparing to join the platform. “If you added all of the AUM of those hedge funds together, they would have over a trillion dollars in AUM, which is massive,” he told Fortune.

That is their total assets under management, not the amount invested on Kalshi. Ross named AQR Capital Management as one firm “actively looking at” prediction markets. “We are exploring whether our models are suited to trading prediction markets directly,” AQR said in a written response to Fortune.

The approaches vary. Some funds are testing contracts with small trading teams, Ross said, while others use prediction-market data to build systematic strategies, and some trade event contracts actively.

Franklin Templeton is an example of the data-only approach. The asset manager does not invest directly in prediction markets or take positions on the outcomes of specific events, a company spokesperson told Fortune, but feeds their data into its investment views through a proprietary agentic analyst. “Prediction markets can provide valuable insights into investor sentiment and market expectations,” the spokesperson added.

Ross described election-related block trades, in which a fund could trade a political outcome directly rather than shorting stocks that may move for unrelated reasons. He would not identify the investors. Revealing them, he said, would mean “we wouldn’t have an exchange for very long.”

One of Kalshi’s first customized block trades came in April, when an environmental hedge fund used a contract tied to California’s carbon allowance auction. Bloomberg reported the trade, which Houston-based broker Greenlight Commodities arranged with liquidity from Jump Trading.

John Conlon, a Greenlight director who has spent 30 years in energy, mainly brokering and trading natural gas futures and options, told Fortune that the firm first pitched prediction markets to its longtime power and gas clients. He expected they “would come in and do something simple for the proof of concept,” such as a small trade on weekly natural gas storage data. Nobody did.

Instead, the first trade came from Fathom Energy, a Houston environmental hedge fund run by his friend Francisco Padua, Conlon said.

Greenlight has brokered trades on Kalshi ranging from $25,000 to $7 million, typically $100,000 to $250,000, and had about five active trading parties at the time of the interview.

“Every trade that we have brokered thus far has been a bona fide hedge,” Conlon said. “It won’t be that way forever.” But the brokerage generally does not know its clients’ underlying positions, which makes it impossible to quantify how much the trades saved.

Conlon sees event contracts as a way to hedge risks that traditional derivatives cannot isolate. “If you’re worried about an event or structure, there’s no more simple or true or more surgical hedge than an event contract,” he said.

Corporate hedges are emerging too. In November, Tomorrow Recycling, a Mecha-backed recycler, hedged cardboard prices with Kalshi contracts tied to a U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics index, with Susquehanna Predictions structuring and pricing the trade.

“Hedging prices in our industry has historically been done through privately negotiated arrangements, which were reserved for the bigger players in the industry,” chief financial officer Josh Branham said in written responses to Fortune. The company declined to disclose the size or cost of its position.

“We see this becoming a regular part of how we manage the business,” Branham said. “We’re approaching it thoughtfully because it’s still a relatively new market, and we want to understand how the contracts perform against our actual exposure before committing to anything on a larger scale.”

The examples build on hedges Fortune has covered. In October, a Los Angeles restaurant group operating 25 bars placed a $60,000 hedge on the Dodgers’ postseason, and in August, Kalshi partnered with Blanket, a tool that helps smaller companies find contracts to offset business risks.

Also in October, Susquehanna Predictions priced a Kalshi tungsten hedge for an Ohio metals distributor. Ross distinguished such specialized hedges from the strategies of the largest asset managers, saying cardboard and tungsten contracts could serve midsize companies without necessarily drawing the biggest hedge funds.

Customized block trades like Greenlight’s are a sliver of the $26.3 billion. Nearly all of it trades on Kalshi’s regular order book, where public data does not show which trades come from institutions or what they are betting on.

Karl Whelan, a University College Dublin economics professor who researches prediction markets, said Kalshi’s trade data does not identify counterparties or classify them as institutions, individuals, or market makers, so you have to “take them at their word.”

“The growth in institutional involvement appears to mainly be funds (and sportsbooks) offering parlay bets to retail takers,” he said. Parlays have historically been highly profitable for sportsbooks, he explained, which leaves room for big profits while still undercutting the sportsbooks somewhat, particularly with customers in California, Texas, and other states without legal sportsbooks.

That market is large. Prediction markets handled about 27% of U.S. legal sports-betting volume in the first month of this summer’s World Cup, according to H2 Gambling Capital estimates.

“I don’t think there is substantial economic hedging activity taking place on Kalshi,” Whelan said. “Despite occasional claims to the contrary, very little economic activity hinges on events like whether the Bills beat the Giants or whether Josh Allen scores two touchdowns.”

Kalshi’s fully collateralized structure requires participants to commit funds against potential losses, unlike the margin used in many conventional derivatives markets. That can discourage liquidity providers and widen spreads.

“The only thing we’re missing is margin,” Conlon said. Some prospective corporate clients, including energy companies, also cannot trade yet because event contracts have not cleared their internal risk and compliance reviews, he added.

For now, Kalshi is waiving fees to draw participants. Block trades generate no exchange fee revenue under an incentive program, Ross said, while brokers such as Greenlight collect commissions. The exchange expects to introduce a longer-term pricing structure.

Much of Kalshi’s trading also sits in contracts whose legal status is unsettled. States argue that sports event contracts are illegal gambling, and New Jersey has asked the Supreme Court to take up the question, with the NFL urging the justices to back state gambling rules. In October, the CFTC proposed explicitly defining such contracts as swaps under its jurisdiction.

The 4,124% jump measures how much institutions trade on Kalshi. It does not capture firms like Franklin Templeton that only use its prices, and it does not show how much of the trading is hedging.

The hedges that Kalshi and its brokers point to are real. For now, they are a small part of what institutions trade there.
