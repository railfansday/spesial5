Loose Lips Sink Chips: Silicon Valley Reacts to the (Alleged) Spy Who Shagged Jensen Huang

Tanggal: 2026-11-11

I remember asking my parents when I was a child why it was such a big deal for a politician to have an affair. They explained to me the many layers of possible compromise beyond the simple breach of morals and propriety. Extramarital affairs, my parents explained, could expose politicians to blackmail, undue influence, and the temptation to give up vital state secrets over pillow talk.

Maybe someone should have that explain-it-to-me-like-I’m-five conversation with investors like Shaun Maguire and Bill Ackman, who rushed to the defense of Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang this weekend following his shocking confession that he’d been involved in an affair with Megaspeed executive and alleged Chinese intelligence asset Alice Huang. (A Chinese Ministry of Foreign Affairs spokesperson has called the spy allegation “unsubstantiated” and “nothing but smears against China.”)

Sure, Jensen Huang is not a head of state. But he’s arguably someone even more powerful. He’s the man in command of the world’s largest company—and its most coveted technological infrastructure: high-performance GPUs required to train artificial intelligence systems.

For years, tech-world observers scratched their head when he insisted again and again that his company should have the right to supply those elite chips to China—even as Americans were told that letting our most formidable adversary gain any advantage in the AI race would spell doom for the Western world. When Huang took the number four spot on our New Establishment list this fall, I wrote that his commitment to fighting these export controls could become his Achilles’ heel. And boy, has it. Nvidia has now launched an independent investigation into his affair, while the government continues to assess the blast radius of the scandal.

There are “three big national security implications from this,” Ryan Fedasiuk, a senior fellow at the Foundation for American Innovation, told me this week. “The first question is whether Chinese intelligence has succeeded in obtaining any sensitive nonpublic information about Nvidia, about its products or—I think what’s being overlooked—its customers of massive quantities of GPUs, like large AI labs.” The second, he said, “is whether Chinese intelligence may have succeeded in indirectly obtaining information about President Trump or people close to him,” given Huang’s unusually close relationship with the president. And third is “the question of whether Mr. Huang’s personal relationship with his customer might have motivated the company’s decision to sell large numbers of computer chips to Megaspeed, a company that has been under investigation by the Commerce Department for violations of American law.” (A Megaspeed rep told The New York Times that the company was “conducting an internal investigation into allegations of chip diversion,” the subject of the US probe, and that it had “repeatedly offered to cooperate with the US government.”)

According to the Times, the US government has been aware of Huang’s relationship since at least 2025, prompting many more questions about why this knowledge was not made public until it was uncovered piece by piece by the killer investigative team at the Times—notably, right after the midterm elections.

“Someone in the national security establishment clearly decided that they were sick and tired of the president playing this game with Jensen,” said Dennis Wilder, a former senior intelligence official who now teaches at Georgetown’s School of Foreign Service.

Of course, investors have plenty of reasons to turn a blind eye to Huang’s misdeeds. His company has also played a keystone role in keeping the entire economy afloat, taking many investors’ portfolios along for the ride on its seemingly never-ending bull run since 2023. Many are wondering if dethroning him from the company he built from the ground up could also send our entire economy over the edge.

Fedasiuk thinks the decision is worthy of a bit more nuance. “I think investors and the American people have to remember that there is actually a distinction between the personal interests of Mr. Huang and the corporate interests of Nvidia, a $4 trillion company that is the backbone of the intelligence revolution,” he said.

Mentioned in this issue: Dario Amodei, Sherrod Brown, Whoop, Culper Research, Daniela Amodei, Holden Karnofsky, Brian Chesky, Ramp, Sean Parker, Tilt, Kalshi, Modal Labs, Lina Khan, Bernie Sanders, and Elizabeth Warren.

Buried in The New York Times’ excellent deep dive into the ideologies that influence Anthropic cofounder Dario Amodei was a link to the archived wedding website of his sister, Anthropic cofounder Daniela Amodei, and Anthropic staffer Holden Karnofsky. However weird you expect it to be, triple that.

I’ve always felt a kind of kinship with short sellers—would-be investigative journalists who’ve decided to use their skill set to make real money. Kudos to the investment-research firm Culper Research for publishing a brief back in May detailing its short position on Nvidia, taken, in part, due to Jensen Huang’s “uncomfortably close relationship with Megaspeed’s Alice Huang.”

Next time you log your weekend party habits, remember that Whoop sees you, and it’s judging you. “LSD and ketamine use were associated with bedtimes over an hour later and Recovery scores about 9 points lower. Psilocybin showed smaller changes: 33 minutes later and 2.9 points lower,” the company posted over the weekend. Tsk tsk.

Let the road show begin. Anthropic has proposed pricing its IPO shares at $70–$78 a pop, seeking a $1.3 trillion valuation for the AI company. The offering is expected to start trading on November 19.

Just the other day, a source was venting to me about how Silicon Valley billionaires have failed to use their wealth to improve their own surroundings. Was Airbnb cofounder and CEO Brian Chesky listening in on our call? He just announced a $100 million donation to benefit San Francisco parks—the largest single-donor gift to the city in its history.

Silicon Alley, I was not familiar with your game! New York City start-ups raised a record $4.94 billion this October, driven by giant funding rounds for Ramp, Kalshi, and Modal Labs.

One of the strangest stories to come out of the midterms: California’s Prop. 38, which reportedly granted $4.2 billion in state bonds to a three-person immunology nonprofit that counts Sean Parker as a board member. (The proposition was put on the ballot by the institute’s cofounder Gary K. Michelson.)

$140 billion+: Jensen Huang’s personal net worth. That would be one expensive divorce.
