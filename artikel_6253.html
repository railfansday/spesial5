The 8 Best Lawn Sweepers Make Clearing Leaves a Breeze

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Whether you attach one to your lawn mower or buy a dedicated push-behind model, using a lawn sweeper makes it easy to keep your lawn pristine.

For many of us, the pretty early days of colorful autumn trees have passed, and now we’re dealing with a deluge of leaves covering the ground. During my years in grounds maintenance, much of it landscaping, fall cleanup meant long days with a rake and a blower, then bagging everything we'd piled up. A lawn sweeper thankfully takes most of the bending out of that job and is much more efficient.

With this rake on wheels, it’s the device doing all the work. As it rolls, a gear in each wheel spins a shaft of brushes several times faster than the wheels turn, and the brushes flick leaves, grass clippings, and pine needles back into a fabric hopper—the collection bag stretched over a frame. There's no engine and no cord.

The range of lawn sweepers varies from 26-inch push sweepers you use like a lawn mower, suitable for a small lawn or a patio, to tow-behinds, some of them 54 inches wide, that are pulled with lawn tractors or zero-turn mowers. To find the right one for you, look at how fast a lawn sweeper’s brushes spin at a safe ground speed (the pace you drive or walk), how far the brushes adjust into the grass, and whether the hitch holds the machine level.

Here are our top recommendations spanning various categories and use cases.

With decades of experience testing and researching lawn care equipment, our Popular Mechanics test team compiled the following recommendations based on brands we know and trust. Lawn sweepers are typically suited for large properties, with the majority of our selections in this guide being the type of models you tow behind a lawn tractor.

The recommendations here are capable of clearing large amounts of grass clippings and leaves from lawns that span a half-acre or more. For quarter-acre yards, we generally recommend a leaf blower to clear leaves and a mower with a bagging attachment to collect grass clippings. That said, we still include two lawn sweeping options for smaller yards.

There are a number of important considerations to make before you purchase a lawn sweeper. Below this section, we’ll cover the following topics in further detail.

Before you shop for a lawn sweeper, walk your property. Note how big your lawn is, whether you have a riding mower or lawn tractor to tow with, how steep the slopes get, how wide your gates are, and what type of debris you’ll be picking up, from leaves and pine needles to cones and sticks.

Consider how you'll use a sweeper, towed or pushed, and if you’re using a riding mower, measure the height of the hitch plate from the ground. Then weigh sweeping width (how wide a strip it clears in one pass) against hopper capacity (how much it holds before you empty it, measured in cubic feet). A wider sweeper will obviously cover more area per pass, and if you have lots of thick, dense leaf cover, you want to make sure you aren’t having to stop and empty the hopper every five seconds.

Look hardest at ground speed and the brush-to-wheel ratio, because together they set how fast the brushes sweep. This in turn affects how much debris is pulled into the hopper in relation to the speed you are moving at. The brushes and how far they adjust come next. You want to make sure you aren’t scalping your lawn with the brushes, but also make sure you can adjust them to a height sufficient to pick up debris. The wheels and weight come last, since they mostly decide grip and the load on your tractor.

A push sweeper suits a small lawn, a patio, or a strip of driveway. A tow-behind needs a tractor of some kind, either a lawn tractor or a zero-turn mower with a rear hitch. If you don't have a riding mower or lawn tractor, check out our buying guides to the best riding lawn mowers and the best zero-turn mowers.

Keep in mind the working capacity of your riding mower. A tow-behind sweeper full of wet, heavy leaves can double its dry weight and put strain on your mower’s transmission. Before you buy, find the towing capacity in your tractor's manual and compare it with the sweeper's loaded weight. One final aspect of weight to factor in is the operator’s weight, which can affect the operating capacity of the mower.

Keep a tow-behind off steep slopes. Each sweeper's operator’s manual details slopes its own way, and there is no universal numbering system. One maker's published figure is 10 degrees, which is a rise of about 2 inches per foot, so read the slope warning for the model you're considering if your lawn has a grade. Your tractor's manual sets a slope limit too, sometimes a lower one for towing, and the lower of the two is the one to follow.

To measure a grade, lay a 2-foot level on the slope pointing downhill, lift the downhill end until the bubble centers, and measure the gap under that end in inches. Half that gap is the rise per foot.

"Fits all tractors" usually means the hitch pin (the steel pin that drops through the sweeper's hitch and the hole in your tractor's hitch plate to join them) fits. What varies is height. Hitch plates sit at different heights on different tractors, and the sweeper has to ride level at whatever height yours sits. A sweeper that tips forward or back changes the height its brushes run at, so they either dig in or skim. Tipped forward, it can also drag the front of its hopper on the ground.

The level check is one you can see. Hitched up on level ground, the hitch tubes should sit roughly level with the ground.

You don't need the sweeper in hand to check fit. Before you order one, park your tractor on level ground, measure from the ground to the bottom of the hitch plate, and compare that with the hitch heights the sweeper's maker lists, checking its manual for where it measures from and how it rounds. If your measurement falls outside every height a maker lists, look for a model whose range covers your dimensions.

Height range is varied with hitch brackets that assemble differently for different heights, a clevis with more than one set of mounting holes, or a hitch with several height positions. Some add an offset hitch, so you can mow and sweep in one pass.

Push sweepers usually cover a path of about 2 feet, and tow-behinds run wider, some to 54 inches. A 54-inch path covers more than a quarter more ground per pass than a 42-inch one, which saves more than one pass in five. On a lawn that takes 20 passes with a 42-inch sweeper, a 54-inch one finishes in about 16, which means less time on the seat and fewer turns at the ends.

Wider is harder to steer around beds and trees, and a tow-behind's frame can be close to a foot wider than its path. Makers list the assembled width in their specifications, so check it, and the height, against your gate and your shed door.

Hopper capacity is measured in cubic feet, and a 39-gallon lawn bag holds a bit over 5 cubic feet. Hoppers run from a few cubic feet on a small push sweeper to 30 on some large tow-behinds, and a bigger one means fewer trips to empty it. Treat the numbers as approximate, since retailer listings don't always match the maker's figure.

Tow-behinds usually empty from the seat, with a rope or a handle that tips the hopper. Push sweepers empty by lifting the hopper off the frame or tipping the whole machine forward. Given the choice, I prefer a dump handle. A rigid handle gives you leverage on a heavy, wet load, and there's no rope to tangle in the brushes. If you get a rope dump, fasten the rope or its pull handle to the tractor and cut off any loose tail, so nothing trails into the brushes or wheels. On a push sweeper, I like a hopper that lifts off, so I can carry it straight to the compost pile.

Because the wheels drive the brushes, your ground speed sets your sweeping power. Drive too slowly, and debris won't carry all the way to the back of the hopper. Drive too fast, and you risk damaging the wheels and gears.

Manufacturers generally cap ground speed between 4 and 6 mph. What I have found to be a smart rule of thumb is to start at a 3 mph cruising pace (covering about 44 feet in 10 seconds), and then gradually speed up, staying under the maker's limit, until the material is reliably hitting the back of the hopper. A 4-mph cap works out to about 59 feet in 10 seconds, and 6 mph to 88 feet. Stop now and then and look at the load. If it heaps up at the front of the hopper, the brushes need more speed, and if light debris sails over the top, slow down.

With your top speed limited, the real sweeping power comes down to the sweeper's gearing and wheel diameter. Manufacturers express this as a ratio, such as 4.5:1.

If you tow a sweeper with 12-inch wheels at 3 mph, the wheels turn at roughly 84 revolutions per minute (rpm). With a 4.5:1 ratio, those brushes are spinning at about 380 rpm. Bump that gearing up to 5.6:1 on the exact same wheels, and your brushes will spin at about 470 rpm.

Sweepers carry anywhere from two to six brushes on the shaft, and more brushes mean more sweeps for every turn. Some makers twist their brushes into a spiral or a double helix along the shaft, so part of each brush is always touching the grass and the sweeper runs smoother over bumps.

Brushes wear out, though, and can be costly to replace, which is why some warranties exclude them. Along with checking whether the warranty covers brush replacement, check that replacements are sold for the model you buy.

Brush height is a buying question, too. Some sweepers publish their range, about 1/4 inch to just over 3 inches, with a chart matching settings to conditions. Others give only a target of about 1/2 inch into the grass and no top height. For a deep layer of leaves, look for a range that reaches past 2 inches, which generally covers heavy leaves.

A sweeper's wheels turn its brushes, so a wheel that slides instead of rolling slows the sweep, and grip on the grass is part of pickup. Most sweeper wheels are semi-pneumatic tires (rubber tires that aren't inflated) or hard nylon or plastic, often reinforced with glass fiber, and none of these can go flat.

Diameter also matters. At the same ground speed, a smaller wheel turns more often, so it spins the brushes faster at the same gearing. At 3 mph, a 12-inch wheel geared at 5:1 turns its brushes about 420 times a minute, and a 10-inch wheel with the same gearing about 500.

On a push sweeper, weight is what you shove and lift. On a tow-behind, empty weight is only the starting point, and the loaded weight is what has to stay under your tractor's rated towing capacity.

Check the brush height first. Brushes set too low drag in the grass, and the wheels skip and slip instead of turning them. Stop the mower safely and go check the sweeper. Then raise the brushes one setting and try again. If the wheels still skid, stop again and look for something wrapped around the brush shaft or jammed in a wheel. If you have to pull a wheel to clear it, follow the owner's manual and take off one side at a time, since the left and right wheel gears often aren't interchangeable.

Some, but it isn't the best job for one. Wet leaves mat down and weigh far more than dry ones, which adds to what your tractor has to pull and stop, and dropping the brushes to dig into a wet mat only makes the wheels skid. Pushing into a wet mat of leaves loads the machine the same way, and a sweeper that already needs a flat lawn or several passes on dry leaves will struggle more with wet ones. Skip the job entirely on a muddy lawn. Sweep after the surface dries if you can, and empty the hopper right away. Damp material left in the hopper damages the bag, and a stored load of damp clippings can heat up enough to catch fire.

Keep the brushes as high as they'll still sweep, which cuts drag and wear on the brushes and wheels. Agri-Fab's 44-inch manual, for example, calls for oiling the wheel bearings once a season, lubricating the brush shaft at the start and end of the season, and greasing the wheel gears every two years. Some manuals call for white lithium grease on the wheel bearings and gears, and others call for light oil, so check the owner's manual before using anything.

Store the sweeper empty and clean with the brushes off the ground. When the brushes leave debris behind at a setting that previously worked fine, it's time to replace them.
