Nurses could play a bigger role in preparing for the next outbreak

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

Nurses already do One Health work, from vaccination drives to antibiotic advice about pets, yet rarely sit on the bodies that set the agenda.

Picture a nurse, and most people imagine someone caring for a patient in a hospital. But in Chad, joint teams have vaccinated children and livestock on the same trip.

In some North American clinics, nurses work beside vets. In antibiotic programs, they consider patients’ contact with animals.

Treating human, animal and environmental health as one system is an approach called One Health.

A new commentary says nurses already do a large share of that work under other labels. They are still mostly missing from the bodies that set its priorities.

Paulo Ferrinho of the Instituto de Higiene e Medicina Tropical (IHMT) in Lisbon led the paper. His co-authors are Rosa Ferrinho of Universidade Aberta, Anabela Coelho of the University of Évora and two colleagues.

The paper is an argument built from chosen studies, policy papers and examples, not a systematic review, and it carries no new data.

In an interview with Earth.com, Ferrinho said the problem starts with labels.

“A central finding is that nurses are already doing much One Health-related work, but it is often described under other headings – primary care, environmental health, antimicrobial stewardship, disaster response, community education, or planetary health – so its contribution remains invisible,” he said.

The phrase One Health has been in use since 2003, the authors wrote, and an agreed definition came only in 2022. It takes in plants and whole ecosystems as well as people and animals.

Nursing was not late to the idea. A 2008 report by the American Veterinary Medical Association listed nurses alongside doctors and vets, and the American Nurses Association endorsed the concept in 2010.

Yet the World Health Organization left it out of its 2021 guidelines for the profession, the authors wrote. So did the core definitions of nursing that the International Council of Nurses adopted in 2025.

Nursing groups tend to talk about environmental or planetary health instead.

Asked by Earth.com what surprised him in Florence Nightingale’s work, Ferrinho said the fit itself did.

“Her work was concerned not only with the care of individual patients, but also with the environmental conditions that shape health,” he said.

The authors distinguish three situations that usually get lumped together.

Nurses can be left out of One Health structures altogether. They can be counted in only as part of “the human health sector,” with doctors and public health specialists doing the deciding.

Or they can do the work on the ground with no say over priorities, money or results.

Each needs a different fix, the authors wrote. The gap is widest where nurses are the front line.

In many lower-income countries, they provide primary care where doctors are scarce, yet the committees above them often remain doctor-led.

The everyday list is long. Nurses monitor heat illness, warn communities about contaminated water, hand out bed nets against malaria, and provide disaster relief after wildfires and floods, the authors wrote.

Nurses are also the largest health workforce there is.

The paper asks for One Health to be taught in nursing degrees as a subject in its own right. That would cover animal-borne diseases, environmental health, antibiotic resistance, climate-related illness and systems thinking.

Nursing students would learn it alongside future doctors, vets and environmental scientists.

The paper also asks for seats. “Nurses should have seats – and an effective voice – in national and international One Health advisory bodies, with a role in setting priorities, allocating resources, and evaluating programmes,” Ferrinho told Earth.com. “This cannot be tokenistic.”

Nurses should be recognized as strategic leaders and connectors across the three fields, not only as service providers, he said.

He was just as clear about the cost. New duties, he added, have to come with training, authority, resources, pay and protected time, rather than being added to an already stretched workforce.

Some countries have started. Ferrinho pointed to national plans in Bangladesh, Kenya, Italy and the United States, as well as the Chad campaigns and clinics that combine nursing with veterinary care.

Nursing bodies in Australia and Portugal have backed the approach, he said. Progress remains uneven.

The commentary does not report how many nurses serve on One Health advisory bodies or how much of their work fits the approach.

It uses selected examples to illustrate the three forms of exclusion, without measuring how common each is.

Whether including nurses in these decisions improves a country’s preparedness for a heatwave or disease outbreak remains an open question.

Answering it will require tracking whether greater nursing involvement improves preparedness and response.

The full study was published in the journal CABI One Health.
