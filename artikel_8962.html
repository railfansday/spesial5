"We wanted to avoid the idea of tropical modernism" says curator of Geoffrey Bawa exhibition at Vitra Design Museum

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Photography is by Iwan Baan unless otherwise indicated.

As the Vitra Design Museum hosts Geoffrey Bawa: Architecture for the Senses, the first major retrospective of the Sri Lankan architect in over 20 years, co-curator Johan Deurell explains why the ‘tropical modernist’ label has confused Bawa’s legacy.

Geoffrey Bawa is one of Asia’s most prolific 20th-century architects, known for projects such as the Bentota Beach Hotel, the Kandalama Hotel and his own houses, Number 11 and Lunuganga.

Bawa’s work is often described as tropical modernist, as it is seen to blend Western modernist principles with Sri Lankan vernacular traditions and materials.

But Deurell said he and his co-curators deliberately stayed away from this label, which was coined by Western architects and scholars and is closely associated with British colonialism.

“We wanted to avoid the idea of tropical modernism,” he told Dezeen.

“It comes with so much baggage. But it’s also not the most useful tool to discuss Geoffrey Bawa’s work with.”

As one of the Vitra Design Museum’s in-house curators, Deurell oversaw the show with Shayari de Silva, chief curator for the Geoffrey Bawa Trust, and Ikko Yokoyama, a senior curator at M+ in Hong Kong.

The exhibition offers a broader understanding of Bawa’s legacy, highlighting qualities that set him apart from the modernists.

For instance, it highlights how his buildings worked with existing landscape conditions.

“He built with nature rather than against it,” Deurell said.

“Modernist site planning often involved clearing the land, rather than having buildings like Kandalama Hotel, which snakes its way around a rift.”

The show also spotlights the sustainability of Bawa’s designs, in terms of both ecology and socioeconomics.

His buildings harnessed the skills of local craftspeople and were built with locally sourced materials. They also featured climate-conscious strategies for shading and ventilation.

This approach is particularly relevant today, said Deurell, as the building industry faces the reality of a climate crisis.

“His ideas on landscape and ecology was really at the forefront,” the curator said. “He had a holistic approach to architecture and he built with nature in mind.”

The exhibition features a scenography designed by Lina Ghotmeh, who was named Dezeen Awards’ Architect of the Year 2026. It contains over 250 exhibits, including drawings, models, archive photography and furniture.

Among the highlights is a new photography series by Iwan Baan, capturing 11 of Bawa’s buildings as never seen before.

Drone photography offers a look at Bawa’s Number 11 house and University of Ruhuna from above, while other shots show people occupying the Lighthouse Hotel and Bentota Beach Hotel.

Furniture on show includes a mix of original Bawa designs and versions of well-known modernist pieces remade with local materials.

As Deurell explains, these were not intended as copies. The originals were not available in Sri Lanka at that time, so Bawa simply commissioned replicas for use in his interiors.

Standouts include a version of the Barcelona Chair by Mies van der Rohe and Lilly Reich, with a welded iron frame and stripy upholstery fabric designed by Sri Lankan designer Barbara Sansoni.

“Bawa collaborated with artists throughout his career, ” said Deurell, pointing to long-standing collaborations with Ena de Silva and Laki Senanayake as well as Sansoni.

“All of these artists ran their practices as social enterprises, so they would employ villagers and train them in a craft,” stated the curator. “It shows the social consciousness within his practice.”

Drawings bring another dimension to the exhibition. Highly detailed maps, floor plans and sectional drawings emphasise the relationship between building and landscape.

Although these drawings were mostly produced after the buildings were completed, they offer an insight into the architect’s mindset.

Deurell points to a quote by Bawa, where he talks about landscape elements as being part of “the total picture”.

“They are just as important as the architecture itself”, he said.

All archive material is courtesy of Geoffrey Bawa and Lunuganga Trusts.

Geoffrey Bawa: Architecture for the Senses is on show at the Vitra Design Museum in Weil am Rhein, Germany, from 26 September 2026 to 28 February 2027, before a run at M+ in Hong Kong from June 2027. See Dezeen Events Guide for more architecture and design exhibitions around the world.
