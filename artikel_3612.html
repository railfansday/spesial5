No Reservations: Jessica Craig-Martin Serves Up a Rowdy, Inside Account of ’80s Indochine

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

No one is famous when they are at home. In New York in 1984, all it took to be famous was to leave the house with intent to commit fame. Beautiful youths were shot through the fame cannon at a rate that made Warhol’s fifteen minutes seem like a century. A cloud of new money hovered over the city, ominously gathering dollars, dropping sudden fortunes on those who knew how to make it rain. No veneer of philanthropy was needed to whitewash the excesses of the wealthy. Conspicuous consumption was the new normal. The new designer clothes all had giant shoulder pads. So did the buildings. The thick-walleted and soft-bellied moved around the city at night in chauffeur-driven carapaces. Eurotrash was the latest import. Fast-talking cocaine replaced nodding-out heroin. The words supermodel and art star fell from heavily glossed lips. Everyone wanted to see and be seen. But where? Studio 54 was closed, disco was dead. Brian McNally’s new restaurant, Indochine, was poised to become the epicenter of ’80s decadence.

Jessica and another waitress with Rupert E at Indochine, 1986.

Waitressing seemed like the best way to make money. I’d met Brian while traveling with the Mardens in France, and he’d mentioned he was opening a restaurant. I had left New York as a hip urban waif with short dark hair and pale skin. I returned curvy and brown-skinned, with long hair that had turned blond in the sun. I wore no makeup and had no interest in fashion, feeling I’d done all that as a teen. I wore vintage ’40s dresses from flea markets, and plastic jelly sandals. When I dropped by 420 Lafayette to ask for a job, Brian looked me up and down and gave me a sly wink. “Yeah, you’ll do.” The question of waitressing experience did not come up.

On opening day, I arrived to find the staff running around frantically with hammers and paintbrushes; stenciling palm leaves on the walls; putting up wallpaper; accepting deliveries of banana trees, ducks, orchids, MSG, and Champagne. There was no way in hell we were going to be ready. I grabbed a brush.

Indochine opened that night, walls still wet, with a private party for Julian Schnabel’s opening at the Mary Boone Gallery. Art dealer Pat Hearn sang Patsy Cline songs wearing a tight 1950s gold brocade dress. Warhol superstar, writer, and genius-about-town Taylor Mead got up on a table to give a hilarious impromptu speech. The crowd danced all night. With one private event after another, opening night lasted a month.

Brian was truly brilliant with people. He made everyone feel like they were in the right place at the right time, and that they were clever, sexy, witty, charming, or all of the above. He was just as comfortable with Henry Geldzahler as he was with Madonna. He could converse on any topic and did so with an easy British charm and lack of pretension that disarmed even the worst snobs.

Moira Hodgson gave us two stars, but other critics found the food “rather ordinary” or “unspectacular,” and everyone agreed that the service was appalling. Ironically, as Brian pointed out later, this became part of the charm of the place. Brian had a strict policy of never refusing a reservation, no matter how overbooked we were. He knew the frenzy at the bar and long waits for booked tables only increased our status as the most sought-after restaurant in town.

When Brian told me my shirt was wrinkled, I said, “Brian, chill out. This is linen. It’s supposed to be wrinkled.” In fact, the shirt could have done with a quick press, but I had only one white shirt and no intention of buying an iron or paying for dry cleaning. Fortunately the waitstaff started to add increased personal touches to the black-and-white dress code until it disappeared. Brian stopped objecting when he saw that the stylish staff was becoming one of the draws of the place. Soon most of us were unrecognizable as waitstaff, leading to many a black-suited banker being called upon for drinks or the check while navigating his way to the bathroom.

Taylor Mead became a nightly fixture at the bar, offering brilliant quips while dining on Dewar’s. Larissa slept on her barstool, her lit cigarette in an elegantly raised hand until someone said, “Larissa!” causing her to reanimate and flick her ash while shouting: “Dahhhh-ling!” She was a movie star who didn’t need movies. If Larissa came to Indochine to sleep, John F. Kennedy Jr. and Daryl Hannah came to break up and reunite, using the vestibule to break up and the bathroom for make-up sex. Carol Channing brought her own silver. Dolly Parton brought her own food. She was on a raw-vegetable and booze diet: “Just gimme the most expensive bottle in the house, honey.” Bianca Jagger ordered her meals in one low, unsmiling breath: “Steamed vegetables no soy sauce no hot sauce no sugar no salt no MSG.” What she didn’t know was that in the kitchen there were three tubs of white powder that went into every single dish: salt, sugar, and MSG.

Dead-eyed debutantes danced in their Manolo’s on tables strewn with platters of spicy beef salad, celebrating an addition to the Guinness dynasty. I wouldn't start using a camera for another eight years, but my mother says Indochine is where I became a photographer.

One night Lena Horne spat her gum directly into my hand. “Gum,” she’d said, after beckoning me over, after which I’d instinctively put my hand out. I gave it to my boyfriend Nick for his birthday. A strand of Cindy Crawford’s hair was the following year’s gift. (We never did get around to cloning her.) Saturday nights were Bridge and Tunnel. I opened the bathroom door to find a woman in high-heeled white boots giving a blowjob to some guy, his soft leather pants around his ankles. Laurie Anderson and I waited to pee while they finished. After that we referred to Saturdays as “White Boot Nights.” Warren Beatty leaned casually against a column, asking a waitress, “You’re a dancer, right? I can see the dance in your body.” A few months later, there he was, leaning against the column again. He asked the same waitress, Robin, “You’re a dancer, right?” Robert De Niro would sit at the end of the bar checking out the scene. Then he’d leave and call the restaurant from his idling limo. He’d ask the maître d’ to tell a woman he found attractive that he was on the phone. Sometimes women simply left their dates mid-meal to join him. One night the Beastie Boys brought a homeless man to dinner who’d asked them if they could spare some change. Even the odd exiled dictator made an appearance. The shah of Iran booked one table for himself and his wife, and three for his bodyguards. I noted their ankle holsters.

The waitstaff was made up of ambitious, creative young New Yorkers. Among them were Fidel Márquez, a painter; Masha Calloway, a fashion designer; and Darrell Maupin, who would open his own successful restaurant, Flamingo East. They and Robin Renzi, the dancer, became my close friends. Robin would later launch the jewelry line Me&Ro with Michelle Quan, who left Indochine for a modeling career. All I had going on outside of work were drawing classes at the New York Academy of Art a few days a week. I had not yet located any particular ambition. I went for dinner with friends, danced all night, and had sex all day. I remember being furious with Brian for a remark he made when I arrived at work: “You look like you just got fucked all day.” I was outraged, but it occurred to me later that he was absolutely right.

There was an ecstasy dealer who took to dropping by the restaurant around midnight. We’d take the pills toward the end of our shifts, hoping no customers would linger too long. After work we’d head to the clubs, needing catharsis and easily spending the money we had just spent hours earning. The Sisyphean irony of this went unnoticed. I was amused to discover that working at Indochine had a certain cachet. Waiters and waitresses were swept past the velvet ropes into all the clubs. I was initially surprised by the mini celebrity status conferred by waiting tables, but it says a lot about the era, which was all about tipping status on its head. Julian Schnabel was flipping burgers ten minutes before he became famous overnight with his broken-plate paintings. With his sensational-looking wife, Jacqueline, on his arm and New York at his feet, he seemed to be starring in his own biopic as he swept into Indochine night after night. “Jessica, get yourself a glass—have some of this Cristal,” he would say with a beneficent wave of his arm. It was the only restaurant in town where you could see Madonna, Sean Penn, Chris Walken, and Andy Warhol in one booth, John Waters and Divine in the next, and Henry Kissinger and his wife having a quiet dinner in another.

A certain high-powered lawyer was easily the restaurant’s most repugnant client, a considerable achievement. On one occasion he was approached by his waitress, Demetrious, a Black, bald, and beautiful torch singer who always wore huge brass earrings in the shape of Africa.

“Can I offer you something from the bar?” she asked him.

“Yes. I would like a different waitress,” he told her without looking up. She ignored him, went away, and returned calmly.

“I already told you, I want another fucking waitress,” he spat.

She shrugged. “I’m your waitress. I guess you’ll just have to deal with it.”

“GET THE FUCK AWAY FROM ME YOU UGLY BLACK BALD BITCH!”

She leaned in. “Sitthefuckdownandshutthefuckupyoudumbwhite-muthafucka,” she said in one breath. Beaten, he sat. I begged Brian to ban him, but Demetrious just shrugged it off. She was used to fighting her own battles and had already moved on.

At first, I was shocked by the overtly sexist behavior and banter at the restaurant. In the art world men did not compare the sex appeal of women in the presence of other women—or to their faces. There was rampant sexism and misogyny in the art world, of course, but it was generally more institutional and less visible to the naked eye. Men in the art world were less likely to openly grab one’s ass, or to make saliva-drenched comments about the run in your stockings inspiring in them an urge to bend you over a table. The art world had its share of knuckle-draggers—but the likelihood of being kneed in the balls by the objects of their desire kept them somewhat held in check. The women who raised me considered that feminism had “worked.” Their very existence was proof. No one thought the work was over—the ERA had failed to pass, after all—but the essential equality of women was beyond question, and the idea of acting coyly around men was considered absurd. However, the liberation train I’d been raised on had not reached restaurant culture. I was shocked to see so many women pushing out their tits, wiggling and giggling for their power-suited dates. Ironically, I’d been sheltered by my avant-garde upbringing.

I met Huy Chi Le on my first shift at Indochine. He was my age and had just arrived in New York from Vietnam. He spoke almost no English. Huy worked six nights a week at Indochine and seven days at another restaurant, working twice the number of hours to make the same amount of money as a waiter. When Huy was promoted at both venues to waiter, he kept both jobs, still taking just a half day off a week. He sent funds to his family and saved the rest. He established credit, obtaining an Amex gold card while we waiters were living out of tip envelopes and dancing all night. He bought a house in Brooklyn and sponsored his family to come over one by one. By the time Brian was ready to sell, Huy was poised to buy. He is now the owner of several businesses, a multimillionaire, and—with former manager Michael Callahan and former waiter Jean-Marc Houmard—co-owner of the still incredibly popular, and now legendary Indochine.
