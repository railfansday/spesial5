Why Being Home for the Holidays Makes You Feel 15 Again

Every year, my clients tell me some version of the same story. I've been making so much progress, but after five minutes at my parents' house, it all goes out the window.

This topic frequently comes up in my sessions during the holiday season. They've spent months learning to better manage their emotions, or set healthier boundaries, and they’re enjoying the benefits of that work in their day-to-day lives.

Then, when they go home for the holidays, they automatically fall back into old habits. They're arguing with their siblings like teenagers, getting defensive when their parents offer unsolicited advice, and agreeing to things they don't want to do just to keep the peace.

Does slipping back into familiar family dynamics mean you haven't actually changed? That underneath all that growth, you're still the same person you were at 17?

Long story short, the answer is “no.” Sliding into old roles is a completely normal, well-documented phenomenon in personality science. Let’s talk about why this happens—and what to do about it.

Personality traits are a descriptive summary of how you typically think and behave. Do you generally experience big emotions or are you less reactive? Do you usually plan ahead or fly by the seat of your pants? Are you typically the person driving the conversation, or do you often find yourself hanging back to observe during social situations?

Importantly, personality is not black and white. Even though we often think about personality traits as categories (e.g., introvert vs. extrovert), traits actually exist on a continuum. People can fall anywhere along that spectrum (e.g., always introverted, often introverted, 50/50 depending on the situation, often extraverted, always extraverted).

Where you fall on a particular personality trait reflects how you tend to think, feel, and behave on average, across different situations. For example, you might be more extraverted when you're out with friends and much quieter in a work meeting.

You can shift that average, making it more likely that you'll think, feel, and behave in ways that align with your values. But even as your personality shifts, some situations will still bring out more or less of a particular trait. In other words, if you've become generally more confident and even-keeled in your everyday life, a few days of feeling insecure or emotionally reactive around your family doesn't mean you've lost the progress you've made.

And those personality averages do shift across our lives. Research shows that, on average, people become more emotionally stable, more conscientious, and better able to get along with others as they age.

Much of this personality change takes place between ages 20 and 40, a period when many people leave their family homes and gain greater freedom to choose who they spend time with and how they spend their days.

These new environments can encourage different patterns of behavior. Maybe growing up, you learned that raising your voice was the only way to get your point across. But in your adult friendships and romantic relationships, communicating calmly may be much more effective. As these healthier ways of interacting are reinforced over time, you may gradually become a more agreeable person.

Even if you've changed considerably over the years, your family may expect you to behave like your old self, and those expectations may pull you to fall back into old habits. For example, if you grew up trying to keep the peace by sacrificing your needs, you might find yourself agreeing to sleep in a separate room from your partner to appease your parents, even if you’ve been actively making progress toward being more assertive.

And the stress of the holidays doesn't help. When we're tired or otherwise stretched thin, we're more likely to fall back on well-practiced responses. The behaviors your family pulls from you were learned early and reinforced repeatedly, so under stress, your brain dusts them off because they require less energy than the newer patterns you might be working on.

The good news is that being around your family gives you an opportunity to practice the new ways of thinking and behaving you’ve been working on. Think of it like playing a video game on hard mode: The skills are the same, but the challenges are tougher. You can't necessarily change how your family treats you, but you can make small changes in how you respond to them. And over time, those small changes can help create a different dynamic.
