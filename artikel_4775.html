I'm a Therapist, and I Tested AI as My Therapist

I ran my own informal experiment using AI as my "therapist." I get it if you’re thinking, “Oh, she's a therapist—of course she's going to tell me AI therapy is bad.” Honestly, I was open to the experience, and I was surprised by the excellence of a few of the responses.

Here’s some background: I first used the AI chatbot as my emotional trashcan. Without providing identifying information, I vented my anger enough to settle my system so I could move to the next step: giving the AI a prompt addressing a real and personal communication issue I was working through.

Communication and boundary-setting are common topics in therapy. I shared some of the history of the interpersonal dynamics between this person and me, along with my current boundary-setting and communication goals, which included not setting off an emotional firestorm or inviting more back-and-forth. I then asked it what I could say.

The chatbot first provided emotional validation, which seemed excessive to me: “This sounds so painful!” followed by paragraphs of sycophancy, which is not surprising given the research on how AI tends to communicate. It took it a while to get to my actual task.

Importantly, the advice regarding what to say could have blown up the relationship I was seeking advice about. Granted, AI was textbook fabulous at validating my feelings. However, it aligned way too much with my emotions and not with my actual aims, a concern raised in some research on AI and relationships. For instance, it encouraged me to 1) say you-statements that could have easily been read as accusatory or provocative, and 2) provide too many details that would likely lead the person to focus on debating those details over receiving the boundary. Ummmm, no.

Fortunately, due to my training in communication and clinical skills, I could stop, reflect, and correct.

I found that the AI suggestions improved as I narrowed the prompts and got more and more specific. Examples included requests such as “The section starting with X concerns me. How can I say it in a gentler way, using I-statements?” “Please explore how a person who tends to respond defensively might respond to this statement,” and “Apply this communication to a person with narcissistic traits." I added the narcissistic traits comment to see if AI would use it as a pop-culture reference, a description, a diagnosis, or something else.

It argued against my “narcissistic traits” mention, telling me I couldn’t diagnose another person. It almost defended the other person. I decided to argue that I was describing patterns of behavior, not making a diagnosis. Apparently, that was not enough to stop it from continuing on (and on and on) about it. The exchange felt a lot like one with someone who learns something and then regurgitates that information without any openness to nuance.

While it seemed to be trying to make me feel understood, it simultaneously invalidated me.

Through my experience, I noticed some potentially helpful uses for AI. I’m appreciating something I hadn’t expected: It supports my geeking-out tendencies. I love language and precision, and I’m learning to use AI to push me in those areas.

I know this might sound counterintuitive given some research findings, but in some ways, I think AI can help people practice their critical-thinking skills. (It does for me!) For example, AI may help in building communication skills. Yet because it can be quite literal and may not always detect sarcasm, double meanings, or passive-aggressive undertones, the suggestions AI provides can need adjustment to accurately express what people are trying to say.

When emotions are high and a response is needed quickly, the step of asking AI to take some of the emotional charge out of something or rephrase it in a kinder or more direct way, for example, can also offer a thoughtful interruption—a tool—potentially saving a person from a reactive interaction they may later regret.

All that said, AI's communication suggestions needed refining to truly meet my goals—especially the primary one of minimizing potential reactivity from the other party, which meant shoring up my own language.

My requests were simply communication prompts, which might be seen as "lower stakes"—not more complex requests for mental-health advice and support. Regarding higher-stakes advice, a BBC article summarized real-life experiences that struck me like an unexpected slap: “Designed to converse with users and create content requested by them, they [chatbots] have sometimes been advising young people on suicide, sharing health misinformation, and role-playing sexual acts with children.” Especially when someone is in a vulnerable space, yikes.

Remind yourself that it's not human. It can miss nuance, and it can respond in ways that seem designed to please you or agree with you. Just because something sounds validating doesn't necessarily mean it's accurate or helpful. Some AI is designed to keep you engaged.

Tell AI what you're trying to accomplish. Rather than sharing only your feelings and asking what to do, explain your goals. For example, “I want to express my frustration without escalating this conflict. Can you help me find a way to say this?” And then, check how it feels to you. Maybe test it on yourself or ask a friend how they'd react to being told the same thing.

Ask it to challenge you, not just agree with you. You might try, “What am I overlooking?” or “How might the other person interpret this?” You can also ask it to identify possible unintended consequences of its suggestions. Of course, asking these questions doesn't guarantee better advice, but it does help you explore.

Recognize when AI isn't an appropriate source of support. I wouldn't recommend using AI in place of human peer support, and it's not a substitute for clinical judgment, assessment, or treatment. People experiencing suicidality or other mental health crises need appropriate human support, including professional assessment and intervention when indicated.

AI seems to be here to stay, so as a therapist, I’m bringing it up in sessions with transparency. For example, if clients say they checked with AI, I ask about their experience: What did the AI tell them? How did they feel about it? What worked for them about the answer, and what didn’t?

Just as it's beneficial to evaluate the information we're receiving online, we also need to evaluate what we're receiving from AI. Does it actually help us think more clearly, or is it just telling us what we want to hear?

For a few self-improvement or communication tools here and there, AI may be helpful. It also can offer advice that humans still need to sort through, evaluate, and adapt for themselves. Relying on it as a substitute for individualized mental health care may be downright dangerous.
