A 1997 Ferrari F50 showing just 106 original miles recently sold for $17 million. This transaction establishes a new public pricing record for the 1990s Italian supercar, reflecting a sustained secondary market demand for ultra-low mileage collector vehicles. As automotive investors increasingly target analog supercars from this specific era, the financial benchmark for pristine Maranello halo cars continues to shift upward.

This specific chassis is the exact vehicle we reported earlier when it first became available to the public. While initial industry estimates boldly suggested the supercar might surpass the $20 million mark, the $17 million closing price still comfortably exceeds all previous public sales figures. It secures the vehicle’s position as one of the most valuable modern Ferraris in existence, bridging the historical gap between the turbocharged F40 and the technologically advanced Enzo.

According to a recent post on the Hagerty Instagram page, this chassis stands as one of the lowest-mileage F50s in existence. The vehicle spent the last two decades secured in a climate-controlled collection, effectively preserving its factory-delivered condition and maximizing its appeal to high-end buyers. In the elite collector car market, an unblemished, 106-mile odometer reading from 1997 transforms a vehicle from a mere sports car into a heavily scrutinized blue-chip asset.

F50 valuations have climbed steadily over the last decade as collectors recognize the rarity of the analog driving experience. While earlier examples sold for significantly less, even when carrying notable celebrity ownership, current market dynamics have fundamentally changed. Today’s serious automotive investors place a substantially higher financial premium on absolute delivery-mileage preservation and mechanical originality rather than focusing on a vehicle’s specific ownership history or famous previous drivers.

Ferrari originally engineered the F50 to celebrate the company’s 50th anniversary, purposefully utilizing a naturally aspirated 4.7-liter V12 engine derived directly from its 1990 Formula 1 racing program. Pushing 512 horsepower through a traditional six-speed manual transmission, the carbon-fiber chassis famously lacked power steering, power brakes, and modern stability control. Because the automaker strictly capped total global production at just 349 units, any surviving F50 remains an exceptionally rare acquisition on the open market.

To command top market value at this elite financial level, a collector-grade F50 must include absolutely all of its original accessories. This specific sale meticulously included the factory flight cases, the removable hardtop with its dedicated stand, the soft top, and the matching customized luggage. Maintaining these rare factory gems is strictly required to fully authenticate a vehicle’s provenance, pass intensive judging standards, and successfully achieve a record-setting final sale price of this magnitude.
