The best (and weirdest) Spotify Wrapped features through the years

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Every year, Spotify Wrapped kicks off the same ritual: Instagram Stories fill up with top-five lists, friends compare how many minutes they spent listening, and someone has to explain how a song they swear they barely played ended up at No. 1.

Over the years, Spotify has gotten a lot more creative with those numbers. It's turned listening habits into personality tests, matched people with cities they've never visited, and even stacked their favorite genres into a sandwich. With another Wrapped around the corner, we're looking back at the features that made us laugh, argue with our results, or wonder what exactly Spotify was thinking.

In 2023, Spotify Wrapped could tell you where in the world people listened to music just like you. The feature, called Sound Town, matched listeners with one of more than 1,300 places, from Provo, Utah, to Cuiabá, Brazil, to Burlington, Vermont. To get a match, at least three of your most-played artists had to be especially popular in the same city.

The fun was finding out what else people in your Sound Town had on repeat. For example, SZA's "Kill Bill" was No. 1 in both Berkeley, California, and Cambridge, Massachusetts, and came in fifth in Jakarta, Indonesia. In Burlington, Vermont, Noah Kahan had four songs in the top 10. "Stick Season" was No. 1, naturally.

Once people started posting, a few cities, like Berkeley, Burlington, and Cambridge, took on a meaning of their own. Queer listeners comparing results noticed some familiar names in the mix, including boygenius, Charli XCX, and Janelle Monáe.

Spotify got in on it, tagging all three cities in a post, writing, "hey besties."

Somehow, with this feature, a city you'd never set foot in could suddenly feel like a place where you knew people, even if all you'd shared was a playlist. — Olivia Tauber

Spotify Wrapped added an aura reading in 2021. For Audio Aura, Spotify worked with aura reader Mystic Michaela to turn the two moods that showed up most in a listener's streaming history into a colorful, shareable card. The more someone listened to music in a particular mood, the darker its shade appeared.

Each color said something about what people reached for when they put on music. Blue was for listeners who wanted to sit with their feelings, orange was for those who needed a confidence boost, and pink was for the hopeless romantics. The cards also spelled out the moods behind the colors, with labels like "blissful" and "chill."

When Wrapped dropped on Dec. 1, listeners started posting their Audio Auras on Twitter. One user took their "chill and euphoric" result as confirmation that they had excellent taste and were, in fact, the hottest person ever.

By the next day, BuzzFeed had made a quiz predicting your 2022 aura. Later that month, Spotify built full-size pods based on the cards' color combinations for its Wrapped party, where Mystic Michaela gave guests in-person aura readings. Cute! — OT

One of Spotify's weirdest experiments was a podcast with an audience of one. Built with Google's NotebookLM, the Wrapped AI Podcast turned your listening stats into a conversation between two AI hosts. They addressed you by name, talked through your most-played songs and artists, and guessed what you might have been doing while you listened. After a year of hearing other people talk on Spotify, you could finally listen to an episode about you.

The guesses were where things got weird. For example, when Gizmodo's Florence Ion tried it at the time, the hosts wondered whether her longest listening day had been a road trip. (She'd actually spent it crying and playing sad music.)

When Wrapped dropped on Dec. 4, some listeners actually complained that Spotify had put too much emphasis on the AI podcast. They missed the human touch of features of past. — OT

If you've ever taken a personality test and immediately made the results your entire identity, Spotify Wrapped 2022 had something for you. That year, Spotify introduced Your Listening Personality, assigning listeners one of 16 personality types based on their relationship with music.

Much like the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI), Spotify's personality test measured four different characteristics: Familiarity vs. Exploration, Timelessness vs. Newness, Loyalty vs. Variety, and Commonality vs. Uniqueness. Each listener received a four-letter combination and a corresponding personality.

Even the artists got in on it. Charli XCX was classified as a Specialist, which she told Spotify made sense, given that her attention was hard to capture but once it had it, she was committed. (She also revealed that she'd listened to Will Smith's "Miami" roughly 200 times that year.)

The feature arrived at a time when the internet's appetite for personality tests seemed insatiable. Why stop at your zodiac sign, your MBTI, and your Enneagram when you could add a Spotify-assigned personality to your bio? For the record, I'm an ENVC. — Crystal Bell

Were you in your "Pink Pilates Princess Strut Pop" era or your "Sweater Weather Softcore Indie" era? These were actual things Spotify wanted you to consider in 2024.

That year, Spotify introduced Your Music Evolution, a feature that divided your year into as many as three distinct musical phases. Spotify considered when your interest in certain artists and genres peaked, how much time you spent listening to them, and how significantly those habits deviated from your usual tastes.

The resulting labels sounded like someone had fed a year's worth of TikTok aesthetics into a musical Mad Libs generator. There was "Surf Crush Beach Reggae" and "Wild West Slow Dance Country," among countless other combinations. April was my "Pumpkin Spice Roller Skating Pop" season, which felt like a personal assault on my taste.

In a year when seemingly everything had become an aesthetic, from the way we dressed to the way we decorated our bedrooms, it felt appropriate that our listening habits got the same treatment.

Were these legitimate musical genres? Not exactly. Did they sound suspiciously like something you'd find on a Pinterest mood board? Absolutely. — CB

In 2025, Spotify gave listeners a new reason to have an existential crisis: their Listening Age. The feature was inspired by a psychological phenomenon known as the reminiscence bump, the idea that people tend to form particularly strong attachments to music from their formative years.

To calculate your Listening Age, Spotify examined the release years of the songs you streamed, identifying a five-year period of music you listened to disproportionately often compared with other people your age. It then assumed you would have been between 16 and 21 when that music came out.

Someone with an affinity for late-'70s music, for example, could receive a Listening Age of 63, regardless of their actual age. Naturally, some people felt called out.

Of course, you don't have to have lived through a particular musical era to feel nostalgic for it. Younger generations regularly discover decades-old songs through TikTok, television soundtracks, and their parents' record collections.

It's easy to see why Listening Age was a hit with users. The opportunity to receive a completely unnecessary personal assessment and immediately make it everyone else's business is part of what makes Spotify Wrapped so irresistible. And there's something particularly funny about a 22-year-old proudly announcing that they have the music taste of a 65-year-old as if it's some kind of cultural flex. — CB

Remember the peak of the internet's main character era? In 2021, TikTok encouraged us to romanticize our lives, and Spotify gave us the soundtrack to do it.

With 2021: The Movie, Spotify assigned some of your most-played songs to scenes in a fictional movie starring you. There was a song for your opening credits, a dramatic declaration of love, a showdown with a rival dance crew, and even a battle against a supernatural villain. In my 2021 Wrapped, my opening credits were soundtracked by Rosé's "On The Ground," which suggests my movie was less of a rom-com and more of an existential coming-of-age drama.

What made this feature so charming was how it leaned into something music lovers already did: imagine elaborate cinematic scenarios while listening to their favorite songs. Anyone who's ever stared wistfully out of a train window with their headphones on knows exactly what I'm talking about.

Spotify understood the assignment with this one. Bring it back! — CB

Of all the ways Spotify could have visualized your top five music genres, it chose lunch.

In 2023, Spotify Wrapped served up a personalized genre sandwich, with each of your five most-listened-to genres represented by a colorful layer between two pieces of bread. The thickness of each layer reflected how much you listened to that genre relative to the others.

According to Spotify's global marketing team, the sandwich was chosen because it was globally recognizable and easily customizable. There were even different types of bread.

Spotify committed to the bit, too. The company teamed up with viral Brooklyn deli owner Rahim Mohamed, better known online as General Ock, to make an actual sandwich inspired by New York City's five most-streamed genres that year: pop, hip-hop, rock, indie, and Latin.

Was a sandwich the most intuitive way to visualize a year's worth of listening data? Probably not. But there was something delightfully absurd about telling your friends that your music taste consisted of a thick slab of K-pop, a generous helping of indie rock, and a thin slice of emo.

Personally, I'd like to know what sauce pairs best with existential synth-pop. — CB
