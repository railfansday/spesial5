How Ideas of Masculinity Might Affect a Country’s Well-Being

The Internet has been awash in commentary in recent years on the topic of masculinity. It’s spawned an influx of podcasts and social media influencers—often referred to as the “manosphere”—that have attempted to define modern-day masculinity. The Netflix documentary Inside the Manosphere, hosted by British journalist and documentarian Louis Theroux, further ignited conversations as he unpacked the impact of manosphere influencers and their potentially harmful ideologies on young boys and men.

In the field of psychology, one way of thinking about masculinity is the notion of precarious manhood. As researchers Joseph Vandello and Jennifer Bosson explain, precarious manhood has three basic tenets: First, that manhood is an “elusive, achieved status, or one that must be earned”; second, that once achieved, this manhood status can be easily lost; and, third, that manhood is largely validated by others and requires demonstrations of proof.

This may align with much of the messaging in the “manosphere,” and debates about it often consider the impact on individual boys and young men. But what’s the broader impact on society? New research suggests that countries with higher precarious manhood beliefs fare worse in terms of their economies, health, and well-being.

A recent study published in Social Science & Medicine used data from the 2024 World Happiness Report (collected between 2021 and 2023), as well as data from an investigation of gender beliefs (over 33,000 college students across 62 countries from 2018 to 2020). The study’s authors, Boston College professor James Mahalik and Ph.D. candidate Michael Harris, focused on the 59 countries covered by both studies.

Precarious manhood was examined by asking respondents things like how easy it is for one to lose his status as a man and whether some boys never become men no matter how old they get. Happiness in the World Happiness Report surveys is based on the Cantril ladder: Respondents imagine a ladder, numbered from zero (the worst possible life) to 10 (the best possible life), and indicate where they personally feel like they stand on the ladder.

The researchers found that people living in countries with higher average precarious manhood beliefs tend to be less happy, while countries with lower precarious manhood beliefs tend to be happier. Lebanon and Nigeria are among the countries that have high precarious manhood beliefs and therefore low happiness. A number of Nordic countries, including Denmark, Finland, and Sweden, have low beliefs in precarious manhood and therefore high levels of happiness. The U.S. is in the mid-range for both precarious manhood beliefs and national happiness.

It may not come as a surprise to read these findings about Nordic countries, which are often categorized as more egalitarian, have more progressive gender norms, and frequently fall near the top of the World Happiness Report rankings. In the 2024 World Economic Forum Global Gender Gap Index, for example, Finland ranks no. 2 for gender parity, Sweden ranks no. 5, and Denmark ranks no. 15. Nigeria ranks no. 125 and Lebanon ranks no. 133 out of 146. The U.S. ranks no. 43. Mahalik and Harris speculate that countries with higher precarious masculine beliefs tend to be less happy because men require more validation of behaviors that emphasize domination and self-reliance.

John Helliwell, one of the founding editors of the World Happiness Report, expressed a similar sentiment. “It’s in general true that happier societies are going to be ones where groups of people don’t feel under threat or challenged,” he said. He also pointed out the social trust in Nordic countries, which has often been a distinguishing characteristic of cultures with harsher living conditions, where people are incentivized to cooperate because their survival historically depended on it.

In their recent study, Mahalik and Harris also found that countries with higher levels of precarious manhood beliefs had lower gross domestic product (GDP). They propose that in countries where masculinity is thought of as precarious, men may be discouraged from participating in fields that are critical yet perceived as feminine, like caregiving and education, and that such beliefs may also restrict women’s opportunities in the workforce—relegating women to fields considered feminine or restricting them from the workforce altogether. However, they note that lower GDP may be what fosters precarious masculine beliefs in the first place, as economic challenges could heighten fears about fulfilling the traditional masculine role of being a provider.

Other research finds different consequences for believing in this elusive and hard-earned version of masculinity. In a study of 62 countries by Vandello, Bosson, and other colleagues in 2023, they found that men in countries with higher precarious manhood beliefs had lower life expectancy by several years than men in countries with lower precarious manhood beliefs.

Why? There could be many explanations, but they also found that national precarious manhood beliefs predicted risky health behaviors, such as substance abuse, extreme sports, and reckless driving.

Mahalik, who’s been studying gender socialization since the early 1990s, said there is longstanding research around the gender socialization of girls—more specifically, the messages they receive about body image, which research has linked to eating disorders. As Mahalik told me, “I’ve been interested in examining the messages that culture also gives to men, some of which are contradictory and inconsistent, and the impact of what men take in about their external valuation of worth.”

While Mahalik’s and Harris’s study ultimately doesn’t prove that beliefs in precarious manhood cause lower national happiness, it raises important questions about the link between rigid masculinity norms and well-being. As researchers continue to examine what makes a happy nation, this new study suggests that the answer may not just lie in government policy, but in how cultures interpret and frame masculinity. As Mahalik told me, “What sort of community are we creating if culturally we’re endorsing a model of masculinity in which men have to constantly guard against losing their status as men?”
