NBA Panic Meter: Can Pelicans fix this mess? Do Suns need to make a move?

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

We're about a month into the season, the perfect time to check in on some underperforming situations around the league. Are these overreactions given the small sample sizes? Or are there deeper things at play that will continue to be magnified? Let's take a look.

Stop me if you've heard this before, but New Orleans is a mess.

Players taking laps around the court in the midst of a near-60-point loss. Cryptic messages in pressers. Star players retweeting reports of a trade request. (I'm not joking, this is the craziest thing I've ever seen since covering this sport.)

ESPN broke news of Dejounte Murray's wishes to depart from the franchise, but the growing consensus among rival executives is that Murray's value has gotten to a point where he cannot be moved unless there is quality draft compensation attached to him, sources told Yahoo Sports. The whispers of growing discontent behind the scenes related to the accelerated hires of head coach Jamahl Mosley (this season) and by proxy, executive vice president of basketball operations Joe Dumars (last season), had only grown louder amid the Pelicans' recent six-game losing streak (they ended the streak Monday, defeating the Steph Curry-less Warriors), sources said, citing confusion as to why extensive searches were not conducted on either front.

(Henry Russell/Yahoo Sports Illustration)

As it pertains to Murray, according to sources, the thinking is simple. His unhappiness and hope for a new environment is not new and has been discussed privately since last season, with a growing sense that his reluctance to return earlier from his January 2025 Achilles tear was in hopes of being moved at last year's deadline. But it was rather difficult to define a market for an injured player (Murray returned later that month). The decision to draft Jeremiah Fears last summer was another clear indicator that New Orleans was open to moving in another direction, which sources said upset Murray — echoing similar sentiments to his departures from San Antonio and Atlanta.

"What a f***ing mess," one high-ranking executive from a rival team said. "No one wants to touch him."

The veteran guard is a polarizing talent when healthy and engaged, but finding the harmony of both has been quite the challenge over his decade-long career.

The Suns opting to go ZeroPG in their starting lineup (word to Matt Harmon) is going about exactly as you might have expected. Phoenix started the season losing five of their first six games and is currently a game under .500. But it hasn't been its 25th-ranked defense that has been the talk of the town. It's the utter lack of cohesion and control at the other end.

A glance at metrics would show you the Suns actually are 11th in overall (and half-court) offensive efficiency, which is juiced somewhat, thanks to some funky transitional opportunities (the Suns are third in points off turnovers because of an aggressive on-ball scheme that generates mistakes). They're just outside the bottom 10, though, in passing rate, assist points created and potential assists.

And they're losing the Jalen Green-at-point-guard minutes badly. Phoenix has been outscored by 137 points in the 346 minutes Green has been on the floor, according to PBP Stats. Quite simply, it hasn't gone to plan. When Green was initially traded as part of the blockbuster move sending Kevin Durant to Houston, sources say one of Green's reasons for optimism was the consistent word from the Suns that he would be given on-ball opportunities — an element of his game that gradually faded with the emergence of Amen Thompson. The numbers, at least in theory, backed it up; Green was a low-turnover, high-usage guy that showed promise in spots as a creator when called upon.

But part of the charm with Green's playmaking was it came within the flow of his aggression, utilizing his speed and power. Factor into some offseason roster moves, notably inserting Miles Bridges (who isn't one to turn down a drive), more reliance on Devin Booker and less on Green, and you have yourself a player being shoehorned into a role that doesn't suit him or the team. Green's drive rate is the lowest it's been in four seasons and he's converting shots and holding on to the ball at career lows. (He's also shooting around 64% from the charity stripe this month and has yet to crack 30% from deep.) And it's led league executives to believe the 24-year-old, with one year left on his deal and a $36 million player option for next year, is attainable — and that Green wouldn't be opposed to a better fit elsewhere. It's unclear at this stage what Green's current market looks like, but New Orleans, which has kept tabs on him since he was in Houston, just might be paying more attention.

I'll admit, this might feel a tad bit nitpicky.

The Heat are riding a five-game winning streak, have won seven of their last nine, and sit just one game off third in the East after a horrendous start to the season. Klay Thompson has effectively doubled his scoring output this month, Miami just dropped 137 on a top-10 defensive unit and the Giannis Antetokounmpo-Bam Adebayo tandem seems to be working out some early kinks.

The Heat are a stone's throw outside the bottom-five in defensive rating, 29th in opponent 3-point and midrange conversion rate, and 29th in half-court defense.

With any metric — especially this early — some noise is expected. We also don't typically have a decent grasp on a team until about 15-20 games. So yes, there will be some bad opponent shooting luck.

But part of Miami's selling point as a contender was the versatility of Antetokounmpo, Adebayo and Davion Mitchell, and some of those concessions meant assuming it was its offense, not defense, that would need time to jell. Given the uneven start, two central questions surrounding Miami's defensive output are: have the schemes changed since Giannis' arrival, and is this working?

For starters, the Heat are throwing out a lot less zone than normal. Last season, nearly 17% of their defensive possessions featured a zone defense, which has dropped over 10%(!), according to Synergy tracking data. (Miami was also allowing a little less than a point per possession on such instances, now sitting closer to league average.) They're switching more than normal but not getting the same results: bottom 10 in points allowed (that's not solely a Giannis/Bam issue, but they're not fully absolved).

Looking more closely, issues remain with that tandem. Opponents are shying away from the rim when both are on the floor (in their most-used lineup), but the ones that dare to attack are converting over 70% of chances. In addition, when they give up 3s, opponents are shooting 50% from deep. Hardly the recipe for success. Antetokounmpo and Adebayo are also contesting less, not getting enough ball pressure and just not looking aggressive in passing lanes (their "Big 3" on defense are hovering around 50% in steals and deflection rates).

I'm not overly worried, but I'm also cooling down on my prediction that this will end up as a top-five defense by season's end.

I understand why everyone (including this committee of one) was so high on Charlotte heading into the year. There was always going to be a risk in trading LaMelo Ball and his cerebral playmaking, undoubtedly the heartbeat of one of the NBA's premier offenses — but the Hornets seemed equipped to handle it. Charles Lee, one of the brightest young minds around, was still in charge. So were Brandon Miller and Kon Knueppel, to whom the keys were to be given, as well as Naz Reid's unicorn floor spacing and Coby White's tantalizing projections as lead ball-handler.

So how does this team find itself five games under .500 and ranked 30th in offense and half-court efficiency?

A few reasons. Knueppel has played just six minutes, re-aggravating the same left hamstring that kept him sidelined during the preseason. White just returned this month after suffering a calf strain and continues to be on a minutes restriction. Miller has shown no confidence in handling the lion's share of touches ceded by Ball and Miles Bridges, averaging the highest usage and lowest shot-making efficiency of his career thus far, making just 28 of 110 3-point attempts.

And the misuse of Reid has been puzzling to say the least. Over his last three seasons in Minnesota, Reid's 3-point volume accounted for around half of his offensive usage. That has been sliced down to less than 40%, which ranks in just the 34th percentile among forwards, with only 7% of his attempts coming from the corners (14th percentile). It's been a work in progress to say the least, which explains why Reid's frustration, combined with Lee's no-nonsense approach, led to a benching earlier in the month.

But this is as much of a problem of Lee's as it is the new pieces in place. The Hornets have become too predictable and stagnant, not enough rim presence and no real control, either. Being one of the best offensive rebounding and worst ball-security teams is like taking two steps forward and four back. Getting Knueppel back will solve quite a bit — as will simplifying Miller's role and expectations — but it doesn't look good in Charlotte right now.
