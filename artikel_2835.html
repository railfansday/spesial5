Here’s a 1,000-word analysis on whether or not the Patriots should sign Aaron Rodgers, and you can probably guess where this is going

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Last week, when the Indianapolis Colts released starting quarterback Daniel Jones, we took a deep dive into the why or the why not he should end up getting signed by the New England Patriots. Ultimately, our verdict was that Jones coming to Foxborough appeared unlikely to happen for a variety of reasons and, lo and behold, it looks like the team agreed with that assessment: it continues backing Tommy DeVito as the starting quarterback with Drake Maye sidelined for the foreseeable future with a fractured fibula.

However, a new challenger has now emerged — and it's a big one: Aaron Rodgers, a former Pro Football Hall of Famer and one of the best quarterbacks the NFL has ever seen.

Rodgers was unceremoniously cut by the Pittsburgh Steelers on Wednesday after his relationship with the team and head coach Mike McCarthy in particular had soured, to put it mildly. With the expectation being that he will go through waivers unclaimed due to the nature of his contract, the 43-year-old could be on the open market by Thursday afternoon, ready to be picked up for a fraction of the cost.

The initial assessment of Rodgers as a Patriots candidate ranged from "no" to "hell no," but there have been some vocal advocates both among local media circles, former Patriots players, and our little community here. Could it therefore be that the initial reaction was too harsh?

Let's put on our analytical hazmat suits and find out.

He is no longer the elite passer he was in the 2010s and the early 2020s, but Rodgers has still played some serviceable football during his one and a half years in Pittsburgh. This season, for example, he has completed 60.9% of his pass attempts for 2,241 yards with 16 touchdowns and six interceptions.

That second number, and his career-long ball security as a whole, might be key from a Patriots perspective.

Rodgers' 1.7% interception rate this year is 11th-best in the NFL, and would be a clear improvement over Drake Maye's 4.0% and backup Tommy DeVito's 3.1% (even though the latter's number needs to be taken with a grain of salt given the limited sample size). No, he wouldn't challenge Maye's status as QB1; at this point in their careers and despite his recent ball security issues, the third-year passer's ceiling is significantly higher than Rodgers'. That said, he could be a suitable stop-gap and help settle down a unit whose biggest issue besides injuries has been turnovers.

The real question in that regard would be whether or not the Patriots would trust Rodgers more than DeVito to lead the offense for the next few weeks, and put the team in position for a playoff run. Historically, that wouldn't even be a question; we are talking about four-time league MVP Aaron Rodgers.

The gap between him and a backup has shrunk significantly from his peak. Nonetheless, one could still make the argument that even a player ranked 29th among qualifying quarterbacks with -0.022 expected points added per play would be the best available option for a team like New England. And if that player is one with the experience of 22 NFL seasons, the argument tilts even further in his favor.

Rodgers having lost a step is something the Patriots would have to live with; they probably wouldn't be too worried about that in a vacuum. The same is true about him having to learn a new system and building chemistry with an unfamiliar supporting cast. If they believed in him, they probably would find a way to adjust accordingly and make it work for the handful of weeks he would be needed to.

The anti-Rodgers argument, however, has little to do with his play or lack of in-system experience relative to Tommy DeVito. It is all about him and his impact on the culture Mike Vrabel has been trying to build since his arrival as head coach in January 2025.

Rodgers has been a divisive figure for a while now, but what happened before his release on Wednesday was unprecedented even by his or the NFL as a whole's standards. Appearing on ESPN's The Pat McAfee Show less than 24 hours after being informed that he would be benched for backup quarterback Mason Rudolph, he aired his grievances pretty much unfiltered. A few hours later, and to nobody's surprise, he was cut.

Since then, additional details have come out, including Rodgers threatening to quit the team unless he was allowed to call Pittsburgh's offense himself. Other details revealed by him have been confirmed to be true, but him presenting them on national television is an unprecedented development.

The Patriots inviting that into their building, especially with a 5-5 record and some pressure already bubbling inside the team, would reek of desperation — desperation a team that played in the Super Bowl less than 10 months ago and has the pieces in place to remain competitive for years to come, shouldn't feel. Yes, Tommy DeVito might be a downgrade from him, but he would be no live grenade tossed into the locker room that could explode at any moment if things doesn't go its way.

Sure, there is a chance that won't happen again and that Rodgers lands somewhere between repentant and out to prove people wrong, but would taking this chance be worth it for a bottom-third starter?

The Patriots won't sign Aaron Rodgers as a free agent, nor should they. It's as simple as that, and as noted above is not about his diminished abilities as a quarterback or inexperience in a Josh McDaniels system.

It is, however, as simple as this: signing Rodgers after Wednesday's events would go against every message the supposed culture-building head coach Mike Vrabel has preached for the last year-plus. Every phrase about putting the team first or holding oneself accountable was tossed out the window by him, and while it might only have been a spur-of-the-moment event in the aftermath of significant in-team disagreements, adding him would still not be worth the gamble.

You can't build a culture in six weeks, but you sure can start tearing one down. And Rodgers, well, he is a sledgehammer.
