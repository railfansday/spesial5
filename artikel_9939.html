Dutch data protection authority advises against purchasing AI camera glasses

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Monique Verdier, Deputy Chair of the AP: “Glasses that film you without your knowledge are already a cause for concern. Glasses that also give you the wrong advice when someone’s life is at stake go a step too far. AI offers many opportunities, provided that we are able to trust that technology. In the case of the camera glasses that we examined, that trust cannot be justified at present.”

The AP investigated six AI camera glasses available on the Dutch market. Two of these were extensively tested in simulated real-life situations in which people’s safety was a key consideration.

The results are alarming. During a simulated emergency in which advice on CPR was sought, one of the glasses claimed on several occasions that an ambulance was already on its way, even though no emergency services had been called. In a real emergency, such an error could result in essential help arriving too late, with potentially life-threatening consequences.

The AI camera glasses also made errors related to the protection of minors, with potentially very serious consequences. For example, one pair of glasses gave dating advice to an adult who was interested in a minor. In another test, safety warnings on toys for young children were not followed. The glasses also made mistakes when reading medicine leaflets and interpreting warnings about extreme weather.

The tests show that AI camera glasses can sound convincing, even if the information provided is incorrect or even dangerous. As a result, users may wrongly rely on advice that is not safe.

In addition to these safety risks, the AP identified serious shortcomings in the protection of personal data. The recording lights on nearly all the camera glasses examined were barely visible and easy to cover up. Most glasses continued to film after the lights were covered, even though the manufacturer claimed this should not be possible. As a result, bystanders can hardly see whether they are being filmed.

Often, it is also unclear what happens to the footage and audio recordings. The AI functions examined process data through external servers, often located outside the European Union. Users are not provided with sufficient information about the AI models that process their data and the companies they share these data with. Furthermore, the AP discovered serious security issues with several pairs of glasses. As a result, someone nearby could connect to the glasses without permission, view recordings or even control the camera.

The AP currently considers AI camera glasses to be unsafe because of the risks they pose to users and bystanders. The AP therefore advises consumers against buying AI camera glasses at this stage.However, the AP also recognises several legitimate uses for the technology. AI camera glasses can be valuable for people who are visually impaired or have low literacy levels, or who otherwise need support to understand their surroundings. But it is precisely for these users that it is important for the technology to be reliable.

The AP calls on Dutch and European policymakers to include these new research findings in the further development of policies on AI camera glasses. “It is important that we, as a society, learn to deal with these kinds of high-risk AI products. Simply banning everything provides some breathing space for now, but we cannot keep this up indefinitely. It is essential that we work collectively to raise awareness, build resilience and foster ethical consciousness,” says Monique Verdier.

The AP recommends that owners and managers of locations prohibit the wearing and use of camera glasses in their house rules (in Dutch).
