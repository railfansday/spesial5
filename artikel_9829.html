The AI agent that lists my eBay items can group my photos, identify the item, price it from comps I hand it, and build the listing. It can’t open its own eBay developer account. eBay wants a person for that, and for a few more screens after it.

Bring a seller account, a GitHub account and an AI coding agent. Answer the adoption questions

Copies the code to a private folder, installs it, runs the tests

Give it a name, a contact and your AI service

Builds the link, trades the code for a token

Create shipping, payment and return policies

Make the picks it can’t, approve a new location

Writes the file, applies your adoption answers, runs five checks

Think of the refresh token from step 7 as your car key, and the pipeline as a valet with a rulebook. The valet parks the car: your agent builds an unpublished offer that buyers can’t see. It doesn’t drive out of the lot until you nod, because publishing needs your explicit yes. One honest catch. The key itself can do more than the rulebook allows, so it lives in .env on your disk and never in a chat.

Pink numbers in a screenshot match the step numbers under it. The labels match eBay’s screens as of November 2026. eBay renames things now and then, so if a label differs, trust the linked eBay page.

Everything here comes from ebay-listing-agent, the listing pipeline I run on my own seller account. Your agent needs the repo before step 1, so grab the link now.

eBay offers a Sandbox, a pretend marketplace for testing. The repo’s own guide starts there. This one doesn’t. Your listings live in Production, and a Sandbox test user starts with no business policies and no inventory location. Building those in the Sandbox is a side quest. So your agent connects to your real seller account from the start, and the pipeline’s rails do the protecting:

Warning: This setup connects your agent to your real seller account. Keep the Cert ID and the refresh token in .env only. Do not paste them into a chat. Do not commit .env to git.

Read ADOPTION-GUIDE.md in github.com/matbanik/ebay-listing-agent. Do steps 1 to 3 with me. We’re setting up eBay in Production, not the Sandbox. Stop before eBay API access.

Say “Production” out loud like that. The repo’s guide suggests the Sandbox first, and a diligent agent follows the repo.

Your seller account can’t call the API. eBay runs its developer program as a separate building with its own front door, so you need a second account. It can use the same email as your seller account. It’s still a different login.

eBay may ask for a business email address. Give it one if you have it.

If the Cert ID ever leaks, Rotate (Reset) Cert ID under it gives you a new one.

A keyset is your app’s ID card: three values that tell eBay which app is knocking. You need the Production one.

The App ID identifies your app. The Cert ID is its password, so treat it like one. The Dev ID isn’t used. The two boxed links under the App ID, User Tokens and Notifications, are the doors to steps 5 and 3. A fresh Production keyset also says “Your Keyset is currently disabled.” That’s normal. Step 3 is the unlock.

The reason buttons are blurred on purpose. The picture should not choose for you.

eBay won’t switch on a Production keyset until you answer one question: when an eBay user closes their account, how does your app find out? Apps that store eBay user data must listen for those deletion notices. Apps that don’t can apply for an exemption. The form sits behind the Notifications link under your Production App ID. You can also open the Production notifications page directly.

Caution: Select only an exemption reason that is true for you. If no reason is true for your app, do not apply. Use the endpoint route at the end of this step. If a confirmation box opens after the toggle, read it. Select Confirm only if its statement is true for you. The form says: “Failure to provide correct information may result in penalties or having your account disabled.”

Here’s what you’re deciding about. The pipeline runs only on your own seller account. It never signs in other eBay users, and it keeps no buyer data. It does keep files about your own listings on your disk: titles, prices, offer IDs and listing IDs. eBay’s wording, “any eBay data,” doesn’t say whether that counts. I can’t make that call for you, and neither can your agent.

The template has five short parts: what the app accesses, what it stores, who it shares with, how to delete it, and how to reach you. It also has a line for naming any AI service that analyzes your photos. Your agent reads your photos to identify items, so name the service you run it on. It doesn’t oversell itself, either: “This is a starting point, not legal advice.” The publish itself is one line for your agent:

RuName is short for “redirect URL name.” It’s eBay’s nickname for your redirect settings, and it looks like your app’s name went through a shredder: YourName-YourApp-ab12-cdefgh. The consent step needs it. Each keyset has its own RuName, so make this one from the Production keyset.

Why can the accepted URL be any HTTPS page? Nothing has to run there. After you accept in step 7, eBay sends your browser to that address with a one-time code tacked on, and you copy it straight from the address bar. eBay’s form adds: “If you leave these fields blank, the eBay default accept and reject pages will be used.” The address you copy then looks different. Step 7 shows both.

.env is a plain text file that holds your keys on your own disk. Your agent creates it. You put the secrets in, so they never pass through the chat.

When you’re done, the five lines that matter look like this, with your values in place of the brackets:

The RuName line is where most people trip. The setting is called redirect_uri, so a URL feels like the right answer. eBay wants the RuName. A URL there is the most common cause of redirect_uri errors.

If the Cert ID ever leaks, open Application Keys and select Rotate (Reset) Cert ID with a grace period of 0, so the old one stops working at once. Paste the new one into .env. Refresh tokens survive the rotation, so you don’t have to repeat step 7.

This is the handshake. You tell eBay, as the seller, that this app may manage your listings. eBay hands your browser a one-time code. Your agent trades the code for a refresh token, the long-lived key the pipeline uses from then on. Five minutes is the whole window, so read the list first, then go.

Here’s what the two addresses look like. Your agent hands you the first one. You hand back the second.

The part after code= is new every time. It works once and expires after 299 seconds, which is what expires_in=299 is telling you. Your address can carry other parts, such as state=. Copy all of it. Your agent picks out the code.

Is that safe to paste into a chat? The code works once and dies in five minutes. eBay trades it for a token only together with your Cert ID, and the Cert ID stays in .env. So the code is useless without the Cert ID. Two places do see it: the chat, and the site behind your accepted URL, which can log the address you land on. Neither one gets your Cert ID, and once your agent trades the code, it’s spent. The consent link does show your App ID and RuName. That’s normal: every OAuth consent link carries them.

A shipping policy is a business policy too. It is not under Shipping Preferences, just below.

Business policies are saved answers to the questions every listing asks: how does it ship, how does the buyer pay, and what happens on a return? Your agent attaches them to each offer by ID. Its tool can read policies but has no command to create them, so this part is yours.

Ask your agent to run policies first. It lists the policies you already have. If you already have a free-shipping policy, a payment policy and a 30-day return policy, skip to step 9. If policies says your account isn’t opted in to Business Policies, your agent can run setup --opt-in --confirm, but only after you say yes. Then ask your agent to run policies again. Once the opt-in error is gone, create your policies. The repo’s own notes put that wait anywhere from a few minutes to 24 hours.

Create three policies this way, one at a time.

With Offer free shipping on, the Buyer pays fields go grey at $0.00.

The pipeline’s pricing bakes the shipping cost into the item price, so it wants a free-shipping policy. Skip calculated shipping. The repo’s adoption questions give it as the example of a policy that can fail on package errors.

My payment policy requires immediate payment, and the repo’s default return policy is 30 days with the buyer paying return shipping. eBay’s form adds its own nudge: “Offering a minimum 30-day return policy helps your listings qualify for Top Rated Seller Benefits.”

Your policy IDs and inventory location are different from everyone else’s, so they live in account.json, a private file next to .env. Your agent writes it with setup. That command doesn’t interview anyone. It keeps choices already saved in account.json. Otherwise it takes the only candidate, such as your one location or your one 30-day return policy. When it can’t choose, it says so in its choices and waits for a flag. Your part is making the picks it can’t.

Your picks travel as flags. Your agent passes a policy ID with --fulfillment (that’s eBay’s name for shipping), --payment or --return, and a location with --location-key. That’s your eBay API setup done. From here, you drop photos in a folder and your agent runs five workflows, one after another. Your part shrinks to a few check-ins:

Paste sold comps from Terapeak when it asks

Nothing. It stops at an unpublished offer

Read listing-draft.md, then approve or reject

Why Terapeak by hand? The tool’s Browse command sees what other sellers are asking right now, not what buyers actually paid. An asking price is a ceiling, not a sale. So the Evaluator asks you for sold comps, and Terapeak in Seller Hub has them.

That’s the valet at work. It parks every car neatly in the lot as an unpublished offer, and anything above your price limit waits for you before it’s even parked. Nothing leaves the lot until you hand over the ticket, and the ticket is your yes in the Review step.

Get ebay-listing-agent on GitHub, give your agent ADOPTION-GUIDE.md, and tell it “Production, not Sandbox.” If a label in this eBay API setup guide no longer matches eBay’s screen, open an issue on the repo so the next seller doesn’t have to hunt for it.
