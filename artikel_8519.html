My first patient was already dead. She arrived zipped in a vinyl bag, smelling of formaldehyde and phenol, and she taught me more anatomy in one semester than any lecturer managed in four years.

Somewhere between the first incision and the nervous jokes about her coral nail polish, I figured out that the human body runs stranger than the textbook admits. The mind bolted to it runs stranger still.

The years since have only confirmed the suspicion. Some of what I’ve seen belongs in a haunted house. The rest belongs in a haunted house with a billing department.

For Halloween this year, I’m recalling some of the odd things about being human you learn as a physician over the years.

Retirement is optional for the dead. Donated bodies pick up second careers the day their first one ends. At Wayne State University in Detroit, cadavers have ridden impact sleds for decades so engineers could learn what a steering column does to a sternum when a sedan meets a bridge abutment.

Seatbelts, airbags, and padded dashboards all carry the fingerprints of people who signed a donor form and then absorbed the kind of blow no living volunteer would accept. Count the people who walk away from highway crashes today, and a fair share owe a thank-you note to somebody in a body bag.

My cadaver spent her retirement teaching a roomful of first-years how a hand works. Meanwhile I spend Tuesday afternoons on hold with a prior authorization line. She was more productive, and she never billed.

Nothing in residency prepared me for the cadaver head course. Severed heads rested face up in disposable roasting pans, lined down a row of tables, while surgeons calmly rehearsed facelifts. I kept my coffee down, though it was close.

Medicine has always practiced on the dead, and for a long stretch it practiced on dead people who never agreed to the arrangement.

Nineteenth-century anatomy schools bought corpses from grave robbers and asked few questions about provenance. In Edinburgh in 1828, William Burke and William Hare decided digging was too slow, murdered sixteen people, and sold the bodies to anatomist Robert Knox.

Britain answered with the Anatomy Act of 1832, which handed medical schools the unclaimed bodies of the poor. Reform, of a sort.

Physicians have spent centuries trying to pin down the second a person stops being a person. We have never once nailed it.

In 1905, a French doctor named Beaurieux stood beside the guillotine as a convict named Languille lost his head. Beaurieux called out the man’s name. By his own report, the eyelids lifted and the eyes fixed on him before closing, and the trick worked a second time before it quit for good.

Consciousness or reflex? Nobody could say then, and we can’t now.

Cleveland, 1970. Neurosurgeon Robert White transplanted the head of one rhesus monkey onto the body of another, and the head woke up and tracked people around the room. White spent the rest of his career insisting the operation could work in humans.

I met my own version of this riddle at the bedside of a brain-dead organ donor. He was warm and pink. His heart pumped, his kidneys made urine, and at one point a spinal reflex lifted his arm off the sheet, the move clinicians call the Lazarus sign.

The nurse beside me didn’t flinch. I did. Legally he had died hours earlier, and the procurement team was already en route.

Hospice taught me the rest. Dying unfolds over hours or days, a slow dimming no monitor captures cleanly, yet the death certificate demands a single timestamp. So we pick one and move on.

For several centuries, European apothecaries sold ground-up mummy as medicine. Physicians prescribed it for bruises, bleeding and a long menu of complaints, and when genuine Egyptian stock ran short, enterprising suppliers manufactured fresh “mummy” from whatever bodies they could find.

Folklore adds “mellified man,” a corpse supposedly steeped in honey for a century and sold by the chunk. Historians doubt he ever existed. I’ve seen supplement labels make bolder claims.

The dead, for all their weirdness, behave predictably. They stay where you put them. The living refuse that courtesy.

Beginning in the early 2000s, hundreds of children in Sweden withdrew into a state that looked like coma. Nearly all came from asylum-seeking families facing deportation.

They quit eating and talking, then quit opening their eyes, and some stayed that way for a year or longer while parents turned them in bed and nurses ran their feeding tubes. Swedish doctors named it uppgivenhetssyndrom. Resignation syndrome.

Scans came back clean, and so did the labs. Nobody found a virus, a toxin or a lesion. The condition clustered almost entirely inside Swedish borders, and many of the children surfaced slowly once their families won permission to stay.

That makes a lot of physicians squirm. The suffering is real, the paralysis is real, and the driver is a mind expressing despair in the shape its surroundings taught it to take.

I’ve watched a milder version play out in hospice more times than I can count. A patient holds steady for weeks, the family meeting goes sideways, and by the next morning he has stopped eating.

In the Kazakh village of Kalachi, starting around 2013, residents began dozing off mid-sentence. Some slept for days and woke with no memory of it. A shuttered Soviet uranium mine sat down the road, which made for a tidy villain.

Officials tested the air, water and soil, and the government eventually relocated much of the town. Plenty of clinicians point instead to a community watching its jobs and young people drain away, taking any sense of a future with them.

Le Roy, New York, ran a similar script in 2011. A cluster of high school girls developed tics, jerks and verbal outbursts that spread through the school and lit up the evening news.

Erin Brockovich showed up to investigate a chemical spill from a 1970 train derailment. Neurologists diagnosed conversion disorder, a label the families received with roughly the enthusiasm you’d expect.

I’ve delivered that diagnosis myself. The MRI was perfect, the legs still wouldn’t move, and the patient heard “it’s all in your head” no matter how carefully I phrased it. She left to find a doctor who would order another scan.

A toxin is a gift, because it blames no one. A functional diagnosis blames no one either, yet patients hear an accusation in it, and plenty of us deliver it like one. We fumble that conversation more often than we fumble a central line.

Wealthy countries have their own spirits. In El Carmen de Bolívar, Colombia, in 2014, schoolgirls began fainting and convulsing after HPV vaccination. Videos of their episodes ricocheted across social media, and the country’s HPV vaccination rate collapsed.

Two years later, American diplomats in Havana reported headaches, dizziness and strange sounds, and the press settled on a mysterious energy weapon. Some neurologists argue for a more mundane explanation that nobody in Washington wanted to hear.

Our culture supplies the toxins, the radio waves and the MRI that might at last catch the culprit. The Miskito get a dark stranger, while our demons come with CPT codes.

Funerals are where the haunted house collides with your financial plan. The National Funeral Directors Association put the 2023 median cost of a funeral with viewing and burial at about $8,300, and a funeral with cremation at roughly $6,280. Your aunt’s flower budget comes extra.

Whole-body donation to a medical school typically covers transport and cremation and returns the ashes to family, often at little or no cost. Programs vary, so call the school before you count on it.

Your body could spend its retirement crash-testing airbags or showing some nervous first-year where the brachial plexus hides. Mine would be in good company. I still owe that woman with the coral nail polish.

Physicians spend years making sure the 401(k) has a beneficiary. Few of us spend ten minutes deciding who gets the rest of the estate, meaning the part with the liver in it. Put it in writing this Halloween, somewhere between the will and the trust, and let your remains keep earning after you clock out.
