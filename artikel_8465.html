Storytelling Makes the Hiking Miles Fly By

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

We got an early start at 7:00 AM under a grey sky and cool temperatures hovering in the high 50s. A thick morning fog embraced us as we descended the hillside, crossing a different bridge than the one we had used to enter Portomarin yesterday.

Immediately after crossing the river, we entered into a forested trail that launched into a very steep climb. We had a lot of uphill work ahead of us today. Once again, we found ourselves walking along ancient dirt paths framed with old stone walls and rich, dark soil.

This forest was extraordinarily lush, dense, and beautiful. With mosses and ferns blanketing the ground, it felt very similar to sections of the Appalachian Trail. Pines dominated the canopy, interspersed with hardwoods.

The rest of the morning followed a steady, gradual ascent across a mix of dirt paths and paved country roads. Eventually, the trail ran parallel to a busy highway dotted with active farms. The strong smell of manure and squealing from farm buildings suggested we were passing large pig livestock operations.

We made our first stop at a busy cafe in the small hamlet of Castromajor, which was packed with fellow hikers. Although the quiet intimacy of the Camino prior to Sarria was gone, we had mentally prepared for this shift. Leaving the hamlet, the trail veered away from the highway onto a wide dirt road bounded by four-foot-tall rock walls, continuing its steady upward climb.

The walk felt somewhat nondescript, muted by the constant lines of loud hikers ahead of and behind us. However, we passed through several charming villages, including one where a volunteer group from Germany was stamping pilgrim passports and offering free coffee. Collecting at least two stamps per day is mandatory during this final 100-kilometer stretch to qualify for the Compostela certificate in Santiago.

After coffee, we reconnected with our tramily. The miles flew by as we entertained each other with stories. In fact, they flew by too fast—just as Robert was wrapping up a fascinating life story, Jill realized they had accidently passed their albergue for the night! They said their goodbyes and doubled back.

Shortly after parting ways, the throngs of hikers suddenly dissipated. For the first time all day, the trail ahead of us was completely empty, with only a handful of walkers visible behind us. Most of the crowds had evidently reached their daily destinations and turned in.

We pushed on for two more miles to Palas de Rei, a small city built on a steep hill. Our accommodation was a comfortable apartment situated directly above a local bar. After logging 16 miles, we were thoroughly exhausted.

For dinner we decided on pizza. Since we were in Galicia, I ordered mine topped with fresh seafood. We both left the restaurant with a firm conclusion: Spanish pizza is a very different experience than traditional Italian pizza!

After a quick grocery run, we returned to our room, enjoyed a glass of wine, and fell asleep by “hiker midnight” (7:30 p.m.). We have finally mastered the Spanish rolling window shutters (persianas). These heavy metal blinds block heat and provide security, allowing us to leave the glass window cracked behind them to let in the cool night air. Although noises from late-night partygoers occasionally drifted up from the street, we were far tired and drifted back to sleep.
