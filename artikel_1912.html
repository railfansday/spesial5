Aberdeen's new manager is a proven overachiever with a big personality... but can Dons get a bounce from their Super Mario?

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

By JOHN MCGARRY, SCOTTISH CHIEF SPORTS FEATURE WRITER

Published: 15:12 EST, 19 November 2026 | Updated: 15:20 EST, 19 November 2026

Stephen Robinson was probably destined to fail at Aberdeen in any event. While unquestionably a successful manager with Motherwell and St Mirren, there was always a lingering doubt about whether his skill set would work at a bigger club with different expectations in terms of style of play. And so it proved.

What didn’t help the Northern Irishman was the perception that those who employed him weren’t exactly bowled over by him in the first place, either.

Robinson would have been a hard enough sell to the Dons support had he been unveiled a few days after Jimmy Thelin departed the scene in January.

That it took them 67 days to deliver confirmation of the appointment – after the club had dalliances with Eirik Horneland and Sandro Schwarz – was hardly a ringing endorsement of their faith in him.

The contrast with the process to appoint Mario Frick is striking. Robinson was sacked two days after a four-goal home thrashing by Hibernian. Five days later, the former Liechtenstein international was named by the Pittodrie club as the new head coach.

After the saga at the start of the year which reflected well on no one, Aberdeen, to give them their dues, knew what they wanted and saw Frick as a hand-in-glove fit. So, what led them so quickly to his door?

Mario Frick is unveiled at Pittodrie and immediately vowed to transform the club's fortunes

The 52-year-old, essentially, is a head coach who fits in with the overarching strategy Aberdeen identified several years ago.

This has been lost along the way as poor results have seen a churn of individuals occupy the dug-out, many of whom were square pegs in round holes. This, the club believe, has been why the recruitment has been so random and ineffective.

There’s been little joined-up thinking, as evidenced by the summer window which saw 15 players arrive, many of whom were sub-standard or clearly not part of Robinson’s plan.

The intention always was for the head coach to work in tandem with the sporting director and chief executive rather than independently of them.

While Frick will have a say on what players arrive and depart this January, the conversations around the identity of the team and what’s required to improve it will be three-way.

At Luzern, Frick was happy to trust the judgment of director of football Remo Meyer on such matters.

His willingness to forge the same relationship with Aberdeen’s sporting director, Lutz Pfannenstiel, was the light bulb moment for chief executive Adrian Bevington and chairman Dave Cormack.

Frick earned 125 caps for Liechtenstein and scored 16 goals for the European minnows

In keeping with the much-vaunted ‘continental’ model, Pfannenstiel and head of recruitment Darren Mowbray will largely identify players to fit the system. Frick will then be tasked with making it all work.

The plan is for the squad to be closely aligned with the head coach’s needs rather than assembling the rag bag of players who’ve come in the three windows since Thelin won the Scottish Cup.

There’s much beyond Frick’s willingness to defer matters of player identification to others which caught the eye of the board, however.

Although very much a modern and forward-thinking coach, he’s vastly experienced in the game.

As a player, he turned out for Swiss clubs including St Gallen, Basel, Zurich and Grasshopper.

He became the first Liechtensteiner to play professionally in Italy with AC Arezzo before signing for Verona, Ternana and Siena.

Across 125 internationals, he became Liechtenstein’s all-time top scorer with 16 goals, including one against Scotland at Hampden in 2010, the infamous game which saw Stephen McManus ride to the rescue with a 90th-minute winner.

Aberdeen chief executive Adrian Bevington welcomes new manager Frick to Pittodrie

Being the star man for an underdog may have had something to do with his upbringing. Frick’s father didn’t exactly encourage him to make it as he started out in the game.

He once revealed in an interview: ‘He told me: ‘There are thousands of talents like you. You won’t make it.’

His coaching journey began eight years ago with Vaduz, who he succeeded in getting promoted to the Swiss Super League via a play-off.

Appointed head coach of Luzern almost five years ago, he turned the side from relegation candidates into one capable of finishing fourth inside 18 months. There followed finishes of seventh, sixth and seventh place prior to an amicable parting of the ways when his contract expired in May.

Although Frick didn’t win anything, Luzern is not a club which is accustomed to doing so, with their last league title arriving in 1989.

Its supporters still strongly identified with him, though, and recognised that he often got more out of modest resources than he was entitled to.

They enjoyed his big personality – not scared to wind up opponents in the media and not shy at publicly calling out his own players when they didn’t live up to the standards he’d set.

Frick famously scored for Liechtenstein against Scotland in a 2-1 at Hampden in 2010

When he left there at the end of last season, the supporters felt the loss of a passionate manager who wore his heart on his sleeve, had no time for egos and stopped at nothing to deliver success.

In the Granite City, he will call out anyone not giving their absolute all every moment they are in a red jersey. And unlike the diplomatic Thelin, the fireworks are likely to be seen in public.

If his time at Luzern proves prescient, those who resist or disrupt the dressing room will soon be moved on. Those who do their best by him will have nothing to fear, however.

As Italian football fan Fabio Baccarini will gladly attest, although tough and demanding, Frick is also renowned as being a loyal man with a huge heart.

Baccarini, from Reggio Emilia, became fascinated by Frick in the early 2000s while collecting Panini stickers due to the fact that he played for Liechtenstein, a tiny nation he struggled to pronounce.

He wrote so many letters to his various clubs, hoping to arrange a meeting, that Frick initially feared he might be a stalker.

When they first met in 2011 after a game between Liechtenstein and San Marino, the forward presented Baccarini with a signed shirt, gave him his email address and invited him to a match in Vaduz.

And so began an extraordinary friendship which saw Baccarini regularly drive 1,000km to support the minnows and become their de facto mascot.

Three years back, when Hibs travelled to Luzern in the Conference League, the Italian super-fan was unable to find a bed for the night. Frick ensured he got one in the team hotel at no cost.

The new Pittodrie boss is just the latest to follow in the footsteps of the great Sir Alex Ferguson

As heartwarming as such tales are, it was, of course, the metrics of on-field success which drew Aberdeen to him.

Frick’s willingness to give young players a chance – another criticism made of Robinson – was a tick against his name.

He’s handed top-flight debuts to more than 30 academy players during his coaching career. He appointed Ardon Jashari, who played against Scotland on Monday, his Luzern captain at the age of 20.

He does not appear to be wedded to a particular formation. He mostly played a 4-4-2 with a diamond at Luzern, but he’s very much a pragmatist.

When he takes his bow at Rugby Park on Saturday, he’ll select a system to suit the personnel rather than trying to shoehorn the players into a pre-ordained system.

With just two points separating their team from bottom-side Kilmarnock, how the Red Army could do with witnessing a Super Mario bounce.
