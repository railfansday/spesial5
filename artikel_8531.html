Investing - Theory, News &amp; General • Re: Do Tax Brackets Slightly Lag CPI-U Over Time?

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

I was looking at how federal tax brackets have changed over time and noticed something interesting.

The IRS indexes tax brackets using C-CPI-U (chained CPI), not regular CPI-U. Since C-CPI-U generally grows a little more slowly, tax brackets can rise slightly less than headline CPI-U over long periods.

For Married Filing Jointly:

Code: 

| Tax year | 24% bracket ends at | 37% bracket starts at | CPI-U ||---|---:|---:|---:|| 2018 | $315,000 | $600,000 | 2.1% || 2019 | $321,450 | $612,350 | 2.4% || 2020 | $326,600 | $622,050 | 1.8% || 2021 | $329,850 | $628,300 | 1.2% || 2022 | $340,100 | $647,850 | 4.7% || 2023 | $364,200 | $693,750 | 8.0% || 2024 | $383,900 | $731,200 | 4.1% || 2025 | $394,600 | $751,600 | 2.95% || 2026 | $403,550 | $768,700 | 2.70% |
From 2018 through 2026, both the 24% and 37% tax bracket thresholds increased at about 3.15% per year, while CPI-U increased at about 3.31% per year.

So if someone's income or spending rises with regular CPI-U, while tax brackets rise with the somewhat slower C-CPI-U, there can be a small amount of real bracket creep over time.

Is this a meaningful issue to consider in long-term retirement tax planning, or is the difference too small to matter in practice?
They have to lag. 2026's brackets were released later in 2025. Since 2026 hadn't happened yet, there was no way to know what its inflation rate was going to be ahead of time.
They also use C-CPI-U (chained CPI) not CPI-U to make the calculations
The measurement period for inflation is a 12 month period from September 1st of the previous year through August 31st of the current year, which is then used to update the brackets for the next year.
