Virginia lawmakers assess misuse of automated license plate readers

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

Automated license plate readers have drawn backlash after reports of police abuse of the surveillance tech.

Of those, 10% self-reported unauthorized use of the technology, which uses a network of cameras to track vehicle movement.

âThe commission, comprised of legislators, commonwealth’s attorneys and appointed citizens, discussed the need for more guardrails.

â"It seems to me that we need probably stronger consequences for law enforcement agencies that are not following the laws that we pass," State Senate Majority Leader Scott Surovell, a Democrat, said at the meeting.

Hampton Commonwealth’s Attorney Anton Bell questioned whether those in his role should have discretion to bring charges against police for misuse. The criminal penalty for law enforcement is a class 1 misdemeanor, but prosecutors have charged only seven officers despite the report outlining numerous instances of misuse and noncompliance with state law.

Virginia-based state and federal courts have upheld the technology’s use in Fourth Amendment challenges. But criminal justice advocates and concerned Virginia residents used the public comment period to push for a complete ban.

â"There is no policing the police," Rob Poggenklass, representing the ACLU of Virginia, said. “While technology can be helpful to law enforcement, we cannot continue to hand over sensitive personal information such as location data to the highest bidder, and that’s exactly what’s happening with these surveillance technologies.”

â"We do not surrender our constitutional rights when we leave our houses and go out in public," Smith said. “The best way to stop abuse is to prevent it from happening in the first place.”

Of the 178 agencies that have policies, 33% do not publicly post their policy as required by law. Seven agencies reported data retention periods longer than the 21-day maximum allowed. In addition, 27 agencies acknowledged they did not engage in public awareness before implementing the technology in their communities.

Virginia’s regulations rank fourth on the NYU School of Law’s Policing Project’s license plate reader policy scorecard, losing points only for lacking a formal data-sharing process to ensure ALPR data sharing is lawful, authorized and documented.
