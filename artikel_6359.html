Fear actually makes it harder for your brain to tell if you are safe, which may help explain PTSD

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Fear stores danger and safety as overlapping brain maps, and the researchers think that strategy may go into overdrive in PTSD.

After something frightening happens, the brain should be able to distinguish between the location where it actually occurred from every other place where it didn’t. However, a new study shows that fear tends to blur that line in the brain.

A memory center deep in the brain stored the dangerous place and the safe place as overlapping maps. The overlap seemed to help the animals recall the threat faster.

The researchers think that speed may be the point. They also think an exaggerated version of the same trade-off could help explain why a harmless situation can suddenly feel threatening to a person with PTSD.

Mark Brandon’s lab at the Douglas Research Centre, a mental health research center at McGill University, did the work with Robert Rozeske of the University of Toronto Scarborough.

It rests on 16 mice that learned to fear one room, and on recordings from the hippocampus, the brain structure that stores memories of places.

“Our findings suggest fear may blur the boundary between danger and safety rather than sharpen it,” said Brandon, an associate professor in McGill’s Department of Psychiatry. “This could reshape how scientists think about anxiety and PTSD.”

A mouse knows a room by its walls, its floor and its sounds, so the team built an arena where all three could change at once.

A mouse that never moved would find itself, as far as its senses could tell, somewhere else. One room had a light patterned wall, a dark floor and a hiss of white noise; the other had a gray wall, a light floor and a single high tone.

For two days, mice in the first room got six shocks to the feet each day, and the second room never came with a shock.

On the third and fourth days, the arena switched between the two rooms every two minutes. A tiny microscope fixed to each mouse’s head recorded large groups of neurons.

Shocked mice froze in fear far more often in the dangerous room than in the safe one, even though they hadn’t gone anywhere.

Mice that had never been shocked barely froze in either. Freezing is how mice show fear. The animals were telling the two rooms apart.

Robert Rozeske is an assistant professor of psychology at the University of Toronto Scarborough, and the study’s lead author.

“That gave us something conventional fear conditioning experiments cannot easily provide: a moment-by-moment view of the brain switching between competing maps of dangerous and safe memories,” he said.

The hippocampus builds a map of each place an animal knows out of cells that fire at particular spots. The team recorded two parts of it, in separate groups of eight shocked mice.

The dorsal hippocampus, toward the top of the structure, keeps the detailed spatial maps, and the ventral hippocampus, toward the bottom, is more closely connected to the brain’s emotion circuits.

The first shock changed the map of the dangerous room right away, in both parts. The change was bigger in the ventral hippocampus. There, the mice whose maps shifted the most were also the ones that froze the most.

Once the rooms started switching, the dorsal maps of the two rooms stayed distinct, and the fear training made the difference between them larger.

In the ventral hippocampus, the two maps grew more alike. Mice that had never been shocked kept two clearly separate ventral maps, so the fear itself was what blurred them.

The textbook account says the hippocampus keeps memories from interfering with each other by giving similar places different maps.

A shock in one room should then have made the two maps more different. However, the ventral maps moved the other way.

Overlap didn’t make the ventral hippocampus worse at telling the rooms apart. When the arena switched to the dangerous room, the ventral map of that room reached full strength sooner than the dorsal map did.

And the moments when one map was much stronger than the other were the moments that best predicted whether a mouse froze in fear.

Rozeske said the ventral hippocampus “may be part of a system that prioritizes a rapid ‘better safe than sorry’ response. In other words, the brain may sacrifice precision for speed when danger matters.”

The researchers suggest the ventral hippocampus keeps a rough, quick sketch of a place while the dorsal part keeps the detailed map. A sketch that overlaps with its neighbor is faster to switch into.

Over the third and fourth days, the dangerous room kept coming back without a shock, and the mice gradually froze less there. As the fear faded, the ventral maps separated again.

By the fourth day, the ventral maps looked like those of mice that had never been shocked. The ventral map of the dangerous room no longer arrived faster than the dorsal one, either.

The reversal is why the researchers think the blur is a strategy rather than damage. The overlap appeared with the fear, was larger in the mice that froze more, and was gone once the fear faded.

People with anxiety or PTSD can feel intense fear when a safe situation calls up a bad memory. Brain scientists call the system that keeps similar memories apart pattern separation.

“Our study suggests that’s not necessarily a broken pattern-separation system, but possibly an overactive version of a normal survival mechanism gone into overdrive,” Brandon said.

“That reframing could guide therapeutic approaches to address an overly fast transition to ‘danger mode,'” he added.

Everything here happened in mice. The two parts of the hippocampus were recorded in different animals, because no microscope can yet record both at once in one mouse.

The recording method was also too slow to show how the brain flips between the two maps, moment to moment.

Freezing also didn’t line up tightly with the strength of either map. So another region, such as the amygdala, probably reads the hippocampus’s maps and decides when the animal freezes.

The mice in this study stopped freezing as much after two days of safe visits. So the team can’t say what the ventral maps look like in an animal whose fear never fades, which is closer to PTSD.

The authors said future studies should take on how the quick sketch and the detailed map work together while a fear is learned and then lost.

The full study was published in the journal Nature Neuroscience.
