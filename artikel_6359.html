NASA’s Psyche spacecraft is heading to a metal-rich asteroid using gentle engines that push with the weight of an AA battery

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

NASA’s Psyche spacecraft now runs on nearly nonstop thrust, powered by sunlight and xenon, on its way to a metal-rich asteroid by 2029.

Standard spacecraft thrusters burn a lot of fuel in short, powerful bursts. NASA’s Psyche spacecraft does the opposite. From now until it arrives, its engines will run almost nonstop, with a push no stronger than the weight of a single AA battery in a person’s hand.

The tiny push adds up. It will carry Psyche to the metal-rich asteroid it’s named after, in the main asteroid belt, by the summer of 2029.

Psyche switched to nearly constant thrust on Sept. 28, according to a NASA mission update. Once a week, the spacecraft pauses to check in with mission controllers on Earth.

Don Han is the Psyche mission’s navigation lead at NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) in Southern California, which Caltech runs for the agency.

“It’s now ‘pedal to the metal’ to the asteroid Psyche,” Han said. “We will be running our propulsion system at every available opportunity, pausing only for necessary engineering activities. The thrusters will be operating nearly 90 percent of the time from now until we reach the asteroid.”

After the October 2023 launch, the lab’s team used the thrusters on and off to steer NASA’s Psyche toward the right spot near Mars.

This past May, the spacecraft passed within 2,864 miles (4,609 kilometers) of the Martian surface. Mars’ gravity sped it up and changed its path slightly, without costing any propellant.

Each thruster uses electromagnetic fields to push out atoms of xenon, a gas that doesn’t react easily with other substances.

The atoms carry an electric charge, which makes them ions. Pushing them out creates thrust and a bright blue glow. Engineers call this kind of engine a Hall-effect thruster.

NASA’s Psyche carries four of them and fires one at a time. Each one’s push tops out at 250 millinewtons, the force of that AA battery’s weight in one’s hand.

Because the thrusters provide a gentle but continuous acceleration over years, and there is little resistance in space, the spacecraft’s speed keeps climbing.

At its fastest during the cruise, Psyche will be moving more than 100,000 mph (160,900 kph) compared with someone watching from Earth.

That’s fast enough to circle Earth’s equator about four times in an hour.

The power comes from two cross-shaped solar arrays of five panels each. Together they collect sunlight across 800 square feet (75 square meters), roughly the floor size of a one-bedroom apartment.

The electricity charges the batteries and runs the thrusters.

Sunlight fades with distance. Steve Snyder, the mission’s lead engineer for propulsion operations at JPL, said the thrusters will run at full power for about three more months.

After that, they’ll slowly throttle down to about half power as Psyche gets farther from the Sun and its arrays make less electricity.

Psyche left Earth with nearly 2,400 pounds (1,090 kilograms) of xenon in seven tanks. In three years, it has used about a third of that supply. The rest is enough to finish the trip and to support two years of science at the asteroid.

As the xenon ions leave, the thruster’s magnetic field makes them swirl, and the swirl creates a slight twisting force called torque. Left alone, that twist would throw off the direction the spacecraft is pointing.

Psyche handles it with reaction wheels, spinning wheels inside the spacecraft. By speeding up or slowing down, they absorb the extra twist.

After several hours, too much of that motion has built up. So engineers have the spacecraft shut off its thruster and rotate.

It then runs a different thruster at low power, aimed another way, for several minutes. That clears the built-up motion out of the reaction wheels.

“For a propulsion engineer, it’s a fun thing that you can only measure this torque in space and not on Earth, as it’s so small,” Snyder said.

“In fact, this is the first time anyone has been able to measure this effect on a Hall thruster as a function of power level and of operating time, and for such a long duration in space.”

Early in the solar system’s history, planets grew from smaller bodies called planetesimals. Scientists think the asteroid Psyche, about 173 miles (280 kilometers) across at its widest, is part of the core of one of them.

Earth has a metal core too, but it can’t be seen or measured directly. If Psyche proves to be the metal core of an ancient planetesimal, it could give scientists a one-of-a-kind look inside rocky planets like ours. Understanding iron cores is one of the mission’s goals.

Lindy Elkins-Tanton of the University of California, Berkeley is the mission’s principal investigator, the scientist in charge.

“The Mars flyby was an incredible opportunity for the team to calibrate instruments and practice procedures for a planetary encounter ahead of arriving at the asteroid Psyche,” Elkins-Tanton said.

“Now we are cruising with confidence deeper into the solar system, powered by sunlight, ready to explore a mysterious new world in the asteroid belt.”

Whether the asteroid really is a leftover core is still an open question.

Answering it will have to wait for 2029. Once Psyche arrives, it will put itself into orbit and map the asteroid from a series of orbits, first dropping lower and later climbing higher.
