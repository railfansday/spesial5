You'll Change Your Mind: The Messages Asexual Women Remember

Have you ever been told something that stuck with you long after the conversation ended?

Maybe it was a piece of advice from a parent, like "money doesn't grow on trees," or encouragement from a teacher who believed in you when you didn't believe in yourself.

As communication researchers, we call these memorable messages—statements that stay with us over time, shaping how we understand ourselves, our relationships, and the world around us. Some offer reassurance or guidance. Others communicate expectations about who we should be or how our lives should unfold.

Asexuality is commonly defined as experiencing little to no sexual attraction, although it exists on a spectrum. Asexual individuals may experience romantic attraction, pursue relationships, marry, or have children. Their sexual orientation does not necessarily determine their interest in relationships, sexual activity, or parenthood.

Despite this diversity, asexuality remains widely misunderstood and is often characterized as an invisible sexuality. Asexual individuals frequently encounter assumptions that their identity is temporary, invalid, or something that needs to be fixed.

For asexual women, these misconceptions intersect with societal expectations surrounding relationships, marriage, and motherhood. When these milestones are treated as universal expectations, women whose identities or choices differ may find themselves having to explain or defend them.

To better understand these experiences, we asked asexual women across the United States to recall memorable messages they had received about childbearing. Of the 126 individuals who participated in our study, 107 recalled a memorable message—and 94% were negative.

Five themes emerged from their responses:

1. Motherhood as an obligation. Some women recalled messages framing motherhood as a biological, religious, or social responsibility rather than a personal choice.

One woman recalled being told by a male friend that she was "selfish " for 'choosing' to be asexual and not having children and... wasn't really a woman."

2. Family expectations and interpersonal pressure. Other messages emphasized the desires of parents and grandparents, particularly their expectations of having grandchildren.

Another woman recalled, "My mother insisted that she was suffering because I was depriving her of grandchildren."

3. Fear of a life without children. Participants also received warnings suggesting that choosing not to have children would inevitably lead to loneliness or regret.

One woman remembered being told, "[I]f you don't have children you'll end up forgotten as an elderly person and die alone."

4. Identity invalidation through future predictions. Some of the most familiar messages suggested that participants' identities or reproductive preferences were temporary. As one participant explained, "I remember hearing what most people used to say: 'It's a phase', 'You'll change your mind one day', 'I used to think I didn't want kids either until I met your father/mother.' Because what if I don't? What if I really don't want children?"

Rather than accepting women's understanding of their own identities and aspirations, these messages assumed their futures would eventually align with conventional expectations.

5. Social scrutiny and judgment. Participants described having their sexuality and reproductive choices questioned or dismissed. One participant recalled being told, "[A] woman can't be asexual and want kids. That is senseless...just say you're a lesbian."

These messages share a common assumption: There is a particular way women are supposed to experience sexuality, relationships, and motherhood.

One of the most striking findings from our study was what participants were told, but by whom. Nearly 70% of the memorable messages were communicated by other women (e.g., mothers, other family members, and friends). This raises an interesting question: Why do women sometimes reinforce the same expectations that have historically limited their own choices?

Gender norms are learned, communicated, and reproduced through everyday interactions, including conversations between mothers and daughters, among friends, and across generations.

Women who have been taught that motherhood is central to a fulfilling life may communicate that belief to others, sometimes without recognizing the pressure it creates.

For instance, one participant recalled, "My mom and grandma talk about it every time I see them because I'm getting older. They make fun of me for not having kids yet and me ending our bloodline."

In this example, motherhood becomes more than an individual decision. It becomes a family expectation, one tied to ideas about lineage, responsibility, and what women owe to previous generations. Of course, we cannot know whether the women sending these messages intend to cause harm. A mother expressing concern about her daughter's future may genuinely believe she is offering helpful advice. But intentions and impacts are not always the same. These interactions illustrate how cultural expectations can be passed down through relationships, even by people who have experienced those same pressures themselves.

The consequences of these memorable messages extended beyond the original conversations. Some participants described feeling ashamed, misunderstood, or uncertain about their identities. Others reported becoming more confident in their decisions, even when the messages themselves were hurtful.

One participant described how her mother's repeated comments about motherhood affected their relationship: "It has negatively affected my relationship with my mother even though she does not realize it. I am content with being asexual and my stance on being childfree."

Some participants became more outspoken about their boundaries. One woman recalled being told that her asexuality must have resulted from trauma and that therapy could "fix" her. Rather than reconsidering her identity, she explained, "It's made me more proactive about telling people children are entirely off the table for me. I don't allow others to even entertain the thought that they can convince me or somehow change my mind."

Although our research focused on asexual women, the findings invite us to reconsider how we talk about other people's futures. How often do we ask about marriage, relationships, or children without considering whether those are milestones someone actually wants? And how frequently do we respond to someone's choices by insisting they'll eventually change their mind?

Of course, asking about someone's future is not inherently harmful. These conversations can be meaningful ways of connecting with people we care about. But there is a difference between expressing curiosity about someone's life and communicating that their life should follow a particular path.

The messages we communicate can stay with people for years. Perhaps the most meaningful ones are those that make people feel understood rather than expected to become someone else.
