A Global Perspective on Youth Mental Health Decline

There is rather a lot going on at the moment, what with climate change, cost-of-living crises, geopolitical instability and so on. Not only has it been going on for quite some time, but also, the end of it does not seem to be nigh. And so, worldwide, it is affecting the mental health and wellbeing of many. However, it does seem to be hitting some a little more than others, especially those that aren’t as secure and positive about the outcomes of all these concerns or who have not yet weathered that many crises. In short, it appears to be disproportionately affecting the younger generations.

A recent survey of more than 50,000 people across 92 countries found that mental health is in decline. The word ‘languishing’ was used. In fact, authors called it a trend, and one that was disproportionately affecting younger populations, including in the wealthier countries. 1

To languish means to exist in a state of stagnation, weakness, or neglect. In psychology it often refers to a feeling of persistent emptiness and apathy, only without being clinically depressed. Basically, it means you’re really feeling really rather ‘meh’ about things.

The study, published in the journal Social Indicators Research, found that global mental health scores were being dragged down by the younger generations who lacked fundamental aspects of well-being, and reported lower rates of – among other things – vitality, emotional stability and resilience. 1

Kai Ruggeri, professor of health policy and management at Columbia University Mailman School of Public Health and lead author on the study said, “these findings are more than just a sign to policy makers that solutions are necessary to improve mental health among young people, or those struggling to make ends meet.”

In fact, he called the results a flashing red light that entire populations were already struggling, one that went beyond concerns for an uncertain future, and showed that resilience was being diminished in the here and now.

“Leaders can no longer use vague platitudes that hand-wave toward investing in non-specific mental health initiatives. They have to make specific commitments to significant actions: ensure livelihoods in the face of AI-driven job losses, address factors driving burnout in young working-age populations and take steps that will generate confidence toward future opportunities,” said Professor Ruggeri.

The survey was conducted mainly online and asked participants 10 questions about their mental health that captured data on feelings of competence, optimism, self-esteem, engagement, and vitality. It was then followed by questions and measurements concerning issues such as stress, depression, anxiety and overall life satisfaction.

The study found more positive signs amongst the older demographics. People who were employed and those who had higher incomes, perhaps unsurprisingly, also experienced better mental health and wellbeing outcomes.

Elsewhere in ‘youth’ another survey (this one national, American, and including more than 42 million participants) found that almost a fifth or adolescents and young adults aged 12 to 21 were using AI chatbots for mental health advice. 2

The study found that 42.8 percent of participants had consulted with chatbots on at least a monthly basis, and that 91.7 percent found them either ‘somewhat’ or ‘very’ helpful. And AI chatbot use was more frequent among females compared to males.

Reasons for use included accessibility, anonymity and the low cost. Another attractive feature was that they offered personalised and interactive guidance.

However, all this did come with a slightly worrying price attached as the authors noted that these exchanges occurred without oversight by adults and, especially, mental health professionals.

As AI therapy becomes more widespread amongst adults and young adults for both emotional and psychological support, the study authors highlighted a need for parents, clinicians and policymakers to understand their evolving role in youth mental health care.

When it comes to the latter study, I agree with the authors, it’s a wake-up call and things need to be done. If life (and by life, I mean society and all its constructs) is sucking the joy out of an entire generation before they’ve even got started, we seriously need to look at those constructs.

However, not everyone is as apathetic as the study suggests. It's not for nothing that Gen Z is protesting around the world right now. Just take a look at India, Serbia, and France. Some are calling the protests a global political awakening whilst others are complaining about them. Perhaps we need to listen more and move faster.

With the latter study, AI is here to stay (at least for now). The best we can do is advise caution and remind all those that use it that artificial intelligence gets it wrong. It makes mistakes. By all means use it but but cross-reference it and, when it comes to AI therapy, please cross-check with a trained professional.
