How Daylight Saving Time Changes the Real Estate Showing Playbook

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

With Daylight Saving Time already ended, showings that previously took place in daylight may now happen entirely after sunset. For agents, this creates challenges, including scheduling, affecting buyer engagement and property presentation. Here are some key challenges:

Daylight Saving Time changes showing logistics for agents managing multiple buyers and listings. The clock change hurts most in the after-work window. For example, in Austin, the sun now sets around 5:30 p.m., so the 5:30 p.m. to 7:00 p.m. slot that used to cover two or three homes in daylight now covers maybe one. Agents with several active buyers have to stop routing by geography and start sorting by need. One buyer has to see a yard, a view or the street before they can decide, and another is fine walking a condo at 7 p.m. That means moving showings to weekends and lunch hours, and having that talk with buyers at the start of the week rather than in the car at 5:45 p.m.

Traditional open house strategies may or may not remain effective during shorter fall and winter days. The traditional open house was never great at selling homes. It’s great at finding buyers for the agent sitting it. Shorter days don’t change much on their own, since most open houses already run on weekend afternoons. What changes is the margin. Fewer buyers are out in November and December, so a three-hour Sunday open with no promotion behind it will be quiet. We’d rather see agents run shorter, promoted windows and invite the buyer agents who have already shown interest. The open house should be one tool in the showing plan, not the whole marketing strategy.

Agents can protect a property’s curb appeal and first impression when buyers arrive after sunset. Our honest answer is to avoid showings after dark when you can. On the buy side, we tell clients not to tour homes at night. You can’t judge the yard, the street, the drainage or the neighbors’ houses, so it’s a poor experience and usually means a second trip anyway.

On the sell side, we discourage evening showings, and that’s easier than people expect: it’s close to dinner, buyers are tired, and walking through a stranger’s house after dark just feels odd. If a buyer insists, we take the showing, because we want as many showings as we can get. When we do, every exterior light is on, including porch, landscape and garage lights, and every interior light is on too, so the house reads as warm and lived in from the curb. We also make sure the path to the lockbox is well lit, and we include daytime photos in the listing so the buyer already knows what the dark is hiding. Then we offer a daylight second look, because a buyer who liked it at night is almost always worth bringing back during the day.

Showing schedules should be designed around individual property characteristics rather than convenience alone. Scheduling for convenience treats every house the same, and they aren’t the same. A west Austin home with a Hill Country view or a big lot has to be shown in daylight, because that’s what the buyer is paying for. The rare exception is a property whose selling point is the night, like a downtown condo with a lit skyline. The listing agent should set the best showing windows at the pricing conversation and get the seller to agree to them upfront. A buyer who sees a property in the dark and walks away rarely comes back for a second look.

Brokerages can use showing data and buyer feedback to identify seasonal obstacles to buyer engagement. Most brokerages collect showing feedback and do nothing with it. Single comments don’t tell you much; the patterns do. Look at what time of day each showing happened, whether evening showings turn into second showings or offers at the same rate as daylight ones, and whether the same complaints keep coming back: couldn’t see the yard, dark street, hard to find the house.

If a listing gets plenty of evening traffic and no second looks, the problem is presentation, not price, and better lighting, better photos or a daylight window can fix it. Sellers will cut the price long before they ask why showings aren’t turning into offers, so the brokerage has to look first.

Eric Bramlett is the broker/owner of Bramlett Partners, an independent brokerage in Central Texas.
