HPV Viral Load Trajectory May Improve Risk Assessment for Cervical Cancer Lesions

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

— Persistently elevated viral loads tied to increased risks for any and high-risk lesions

Persistently elevated viral loads of human papillomavirus (HPV) were linked to dramatically higher rates of cervical cancer lesions, a large prospective cohort study showed.

As compared with low-stable trajectories, the likelihood of any cervical lesion increased by twofold to as much as 124-fold with persistently elevated trajectories, even those classified as moderate-declining. Patients with the highest persistent elevations had a ninefold higher risk of high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions (HSILs), the most advanced cervical cancer lesion.

The study is one of the first to link longitudinal trajectories of HPV viral load to "clinically distinct risk phenotypes," reported Lina Zhang, MD, of Nanjing Medical University in Changzhou, China, and colleagues in Cancer.

"Among the 624 women with persistent infection, four distinct longitudinal viral-load trajectories were identified," the authors wrote. "The moderate-increasing and persistently high trajectories were associated with a markedly greater cervical lesion burden than the low-stable and moderate-declining trajectories, and these associations remained after multivariable adjustment. Longitudinal viral-load information also improved predictive performance beyond baseline characteristics and baseline viral load."

"Trajectory analysis further showed that persistent HPV infection is heterogeneous," they added. "The contrast between the moderate-declining and moderate-increasing groups was particularly informative. Although the moderate-declining group had a higher mean baseline viral load than the moderate-increasing group, its lesion prevalence was substantially lower. This contrast suggests that a single baseline viral-load measurement may not fully reflect subsequent disease risk."

The findings indicated that two women with similar baseline viral loads might have different clinical courses, a difference that has potential implications for clinical management, Zhang and colleagues noted. Additional studies in different populations are needed to validate the findings before implementation in clinical practice.

The results added to a large volume of evidence linking HPV infection and cervical cancer. A recent systematic review of 85 studies and 173,746 women showed that higher HPV viral load was associated with greater cervical cancer severity and worse clinical outcomes. Another recent study showed that higher viral loads had a significant association with higher-risk cervical lesions, specifically HSILs. Yet another study suggested that evaluating genotype-specific viral loads might help distinguish transient infections from persistent, transforming infections.

The authors cited multiple other studies showing associations between HPV persistence and higher viral load with high-grade cervical disease.

"Together, these findings suggest that viral load, genotype, and infection multiplicity may provide complementary information about the clinical course and risk associated with HPV infection," Zhang and colleagues wrote.

Previous longitudinal studies have relied on repeated-measures approaches to determine changes in viral load over time, which might not fully capture between-patient heterogeneity in longitudinal viral-load patterns, the authors noted. For the current study, they used latent class growth analysis (LCGA) and group-based trajectory modeling to describe heterogeneity in HPV viral-load patterns over time.

The study involved 1,170 women with HPV infection and classifiable outcomes, followed at 6-month intervals. Of these women, 624 patients had persistent infection, 471 with clearance, and 75 with reactivation. Baseline viral load differed significantly across the groups (4.75 log10 for persistent infection vs 4.07 for the clearance group and 4.41 for the reactivation group, P<0.001). Median follow-up also differed significantly (P<0.001) between the persistently high (23.2 months) and clearance (14.9 months) groups. Median follow-up in the reactivation group was 19.8 months among those patients with follow-up time data.

The investigators used LCGA to follow the patients with persistent infection, which was divided into four viral-load trajectories: low stable (39.6%), moderate declining (18.9%), moderate increasing (29.8%), and persistently high (11.7%). As compared with the low-stable group, adjusted odds ratios increased with trajectory: 1.95 for moderate declining (95% CI 1.02-3.74), 29.66 for moderate increasing (95% CI 16.75-52.51), and 124.29 for persistently high (95% CI 47.30-326.60). The persistently high subgroup had an HSIL odds ratio of 9.47 (95% CI 3.18-33.09).

"Trajectory group membership remained strongly associated with any cervical lesion after adjustment for HPV type, infection multiplicity, and socioeconomic, behavioral, and reproductive factors," Zhang and colleagues wrote. "The very large ORs observed for the moderate-increasing and persistently high groups should nevertheless be interpreted cautiously because they partly reflect the marked separation in lesion prevalence across trajectory groups, and the confidence intervals were relatively wide."
