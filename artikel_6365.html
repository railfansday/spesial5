A woman's heart issues seemed to be tied to her rapid gulping of ice-cold drinks.

The patient: A 46-year-old woman in California

The symptoms: After an uneventful 10-hour work shift, the woman was walking to her car and drinking an ice-cold beverage when a sudden, burning pain struck the middle of her chest. The pain spread to both of her forearms and lasted about 20 minutes.

About 10 minutes after this pain began, she started sweating and felt nauseated and lightheaded, as though she might pass out.

What happened next: The pain continued as the woman began driving home, so she changed course and instead drove to the nearest emergency department.

An electrocardiogram (EKG), which records the heart's electrical activity, showed subtle abnormalities. A blood test for troponin, a protein released into the bloodstream when heart muscle is damaged, was normal. However, a second troponin test taken before she left the hospital came back elevated after she had been discharged. Doctors called her about the new results and advised her to drive to the nearest emergency department.

There, a third blood test showed that her troponin level had risen even further, and doctors diagnosed a heart attack. An angiogram — a scan that shows the structure of blood vessels — revealed a small tear in a branch of her right coronary artery, which supplies blood to the heart.

About 30 hours after she was discharged, the woman developed similar chest pain after taking several gulps of ice-cold green tea and subsequently jogging up a flight of stairs. The pain began when she reached the top of the stairs and was accompanied by the same lightheaded feeling she had experienced before.

She called 911 and was again brought to an emergency department, and although an initial EKG showed no clear signs of a heart attack, subsequent recordings revealed heart-activity changes consistent with one. A second angiogram revealed a new tear in the left circumflex artery, a branch of the left coronary artery.

The woman developed tears in both her right and left coronary arteries, which supply the heart with oxygen-rich blood.

The diagnosis: Doctors diagnosed the woman with spontaneous coronary artery dissection (SCAD), a condition in which a tear develops in the wall of a coronary artery. In SCAD, blood collects within the artery wall, and this compresses the channel that blood normally flows through to reach the heart. As in the patient's case, this compression can sometimes trigger heart attacks by drastically restricting the heart's blood supply.

Doctors also found fibromuscular dysplasia (FMD) in a blood vessel in the woman's pelvis. FMD changes the structure of artery walls, making them too weak or too stiff, and it's known to occur in some people with SCAD. It may have made her arteries more prone to tearing, the doctors wrote in a report of the case.

The treatment: During her first emergency department visit, doctors gave the woman aspirin, fluids, medicine for nausea and an antacid. Her chest pain eventually went away. At the second visit, doctors gave her the blood thinner heparin to reduce blood clotting and the beta-blocker metoprolol to slow her heart rate. She was discharged the following morning with prescriptions for cholesterol- and blood-pressure-lowering drugs.

After the second episode, doctors gave her opioid painkillers and medicine for nausea to take as needed, along with aspirin and a cholesterol-lowering drug. The cholesterol-lowering drug was soon stopped because she did not have high cholesterol. Doctors eventually stopped the blood-pressure medication, too, after a follow-up heart ultrasound showed that the affected part of her heart was moving normally again.

She continued taking nebivolol, a different beta-blocker that slows the heart rate and can reduce stress on the organ, for years after these two episodes. And for several months after the second episode, the woman avoided cold drinks on her own initiative. She gradually reintroduced them, taking small sips rather than gulping. Ten years later, she remained free of symptoms, the report noted.

What makes this case unique: Years later, the woman learned that cold drinks had been linked to atrial fibrillation (AFib), an irregular heart rhythm. She told her healthcare team that she suspected cold drinks might have triggered SCAD in her case, too.

Separately, a 2017 case report described another woman who developed chest pain and a heart attack after drinking cold water. Doctors subsequently found that she had SCAD and FMD. However, that report did not investigate cold water as a possible trigger of the patient's symptoms.

The authors of the current report proposed that rapidly swallowing a cold drink may trigger a brief rise in blood pressure, known as a pressor response. This is associated with increased activation of the body's fight-or-flight system, which can then release stress hormones. These changes may make the heart contract more forcefully and cause the coronary arteries to narrow temporarily, thereby increasing the forces acting on their walls. In someone whose arteries are susceptible to tears, that added stress could contribute to rips in the tissue and the accumulation of blood within the artery wall.

However, the report authors stressed that they cannot establish that cold drinks caused the woman's dissections in this instance. They hope their findings will encourage doctors to ask patients with SCAD about potential triggers, but they said further research is needed to determine whether rapidly drinking cold beverages can really trigger the condition.
