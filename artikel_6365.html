Ammonia pollution from farms reaches beyond the air we breathe

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

A new review examines ammonia pollution, its health risks, and the potential for bacteria to help remove excess ammonia.

Farming is the biggest source of ammonia pollution, mainly from animal waste and fertilizers.

Once released, ammonia mixes with other pollutants in the air, creating tiny particles that can harm our health. It can also seep into water sources and affect drinking water.

Researchers from four Chinese universities reviewed evidence on ammonia pollution, ammonia buildup inside the body, and bacteria that might help remove it.

By the authors’ estimate, agriculture is responsible for 80% to 90% of the ammonia released worldwide by human activity.

That share takes in livestock farming, fertilizer use and aquaculture, the farming of fish and shellfish.

Ammonia is given off by manure and urine as they decompose, the authors wrote, so a portion is lost wherever animal waste is stored and handled.

They identified fertilizer applied to crops as the other major source.

China, India, the United States and Europe were named as the main hotspots for these emissions. Chemical plants, fertilizer factories, drug makers and oil refineries contribute to the total as well.

In cities, the researchers attributed ammonia to sewage and garbage that are poorly managed.

They added that some diesel vehicles also emit the gas from their exhaust systems, especially where low-quality urea is used in them.

Those emissions feed air pollution well beyond the fields themselves.

People who live or work near a farm or factory may inhale ammonia directly.

The authors described the gas as an irritant to the eyes and airways, and wrote that long exposure can damage the tissue lining the nose and airways.

They cited animal experiments in which the bacteria living in the nose and gut changed after exposure to ammonia.

What those changes might mean for human health has not been established, they wrote.

The review also describes how ammonia reacts with sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides, pollutants released when coal and fuel are burned.

That reaction yields ammonium salts, which are a component of the fine airborne particles known as PM2.5.

Such particles are no more than 2.5 micrometers across, roughly one-thirtieth the width of a human hair, and can reach deep into the lungs.

Fine-particle pollution has been found to aggravate asthma, chronic bronchitis and the lung disease known as COPD, and to raise rates of heart disease and death.

One analysis from 2015, cited by the authors, estimated that outdoor fine particles are responsible for more than 5% of premature deaths worldwide.

Nitrate, which is what soil microbes turn ammonium into, doesn’t stay put. The authors described it being carried down into groundwater by rain and irrigation, or washed off the land into streams and lakes.

The review also describes how plants take nitrate up from the soil while they are growing, and how lettuce and other leafy vegetables hold it in their leaves.

As a result, the authors wrote, people may be taking in nitrate with their food as well as with their drinking water.

A portion of the nitrate a person swallows is turned into nitrite in the body.

Nitrite changes hemoglobin, the protein red blood cells use to carry oxygen, so that the protein can no longer carry it.

Young infants are the ones most at risk, and severe cases of what is known as blue baby syndrome can prove fatal.

The danger to babies is one reason the World Health Organization set limits for nitrate in drinking water.

In the stomach, according to the authors, nitrate and nitrite can react with compounds from food to produce cancer-causing substances.

They linked long-term intake of the two to higher rates of stomach, esophageal and other digestive cancers.

Nitrate that is washed into rivers and lakes can fuel large blooms of algae and related organisms.

Some of those blooms give off toxins that contaminate the water or build up in fish and shellfish.

The toxins can then be swallowed in drinking water or food, and the authors wrote that some can damage the liver.

The nutrients help the bloom to grow, but the injury is caused by the toxin.

Environmental exposure is only part of the picture. The body also produces ammonia when it breaks down protein, and gut microbes generate some while processing food.

Normally the liver converts most of it into urea, which then leaves the body in urine.

The authors wrote that gut bacteria can use ammonia as they grow, or help supply substances the liver needs in order to process it. Several species with those abilities are described in the review.

Among them is Lactobacillus plantarum, which can incorporate ammonia into amino acids, the building blocks of proteins.

Experiments cited by the authors suggest it may help reduce ammonia in the brain.

That ability has yet to be shown safe in people with high ammonia levels, they added. A useful result in an experiment doesn’t amount to a treatment that doctors could offer.

A badly damaged liver may fail to remove enough ammonia from the blood.

Rising levels can then affect the brain, the authors wrote, and the result is confusion and problems with balance or movement.

The gut can add to the trouble in cirrhosis, the scarring caused by years of liver damage. They cited evidence that an enzyme which produces ammonia in the intestine becomes more active.

Studies in mice found that a long period of high blood ammonia changed the gut’s bacterial community as well.

Some bacterial groups became more common, according to the authors, and others declined, including beneficial ones.

Researchers are now trying to find out whether changing those bacteria might help patients.

Most of that research concerns ammonia made inside the body, the authors added, which leaves open questions about exposure through polluted air, water and food.

Lactulose and the antibiotic rifaximin are the drugs used to manage ammonia-related problems in people with liver disease.

Lactulose encourages a helpful species in the gut, the authors wrote, while rifaximin cuts back several harmful groups.

Both medicines can have side effects. Too much lactulose may result in dehydration and other complications, they wrote, and some patients develop severe diarrhea while taking rifaximin.

Dialysis may be used in some cases to take ammonia out of the blood.

For patients with severe liver disease, a transplant treats the underlying liver failure.

Sodium benzoate is used as a food preservative and in some ammonia-lowering treatments.

One experiment cited by the authors found little change in the gut microbiome even at ten times the accepted daily intake.

The effects of such drugs on gut bacteria have rarely been studied, they wrote.

Some bacteria studied in wastewater can convert ammonium directly into nitrogen gas, without producing nitrate or nitrite along the way.

One example given in the review is Zobellella taiwanensis, which uses acetone during the process.

Getting such bacteria to work in a person’s gut would present several problems, among them whether they could survive there at all.

The authors also wrote that some ammonia-processing bacteria take days or weeks to grow.

Four approaches are set out in the review. The first two are to start from a gut-adapted species instead of a laboratory strain, and to use dead bacteria that still carry working enzymes.

The third is to hold cells or enzymes in place so that they remove ammonia, in the gut or outside the body. The fourth is to edit the wider gut community instead of one strain at a time.

It has still to be established whether any of these proposed treatments safely lower ammonia in people, and which patients might benefit.

The review was published in the journal The Innovation Reviews.
