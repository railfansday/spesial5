Crystals reveal what pushed seven very different volcanoes to erupt

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Crystals from seven Andean volcanoes share one chemical fingerprint, tying each volcano eruption trigger to a pulse of hot magma from below.

A volcano that erupts once and goes quiet forever seems like a different animal from one that has blown up again and again.

A study of seven Andean volcanoes suggests that, despite their different histories, their eruptions shared a trigger.

The last crystals to grow before each eruption carry the same chemical fingerprint, and it points to a pulse of hotter magma pushing up from below.

Daniela Parra-Encalada, a doctoral student in the School of the Environment at the University of Queensland, read that fingerprint out of plagioclase.

That’s the common white mineral that makes up most of the crystals in volcanic rock.

Parra-Encalada worked with senior author Teresa Ubide and colleagues from Australia, the Universidad de Chile, Spain and Argentina.

Together they collected lava and ash from seven volcanoes along 143 miles (230 kilometers) of the Central Andes.

The work rests on 28 rock samples. Whether the same signal could ever help forecast an eruption is a question the team has only begun to test.

“Plagioclase grows in magma only a few kilometers below the surface, reacting to changes in temperature, water content and magma composition,” Parra-Encalada said.

One of those reactions is a swap of calcium for sodium, so each crystal keeps a record of the melt it grew from.

Two kinds of crystal turned up in every rock the team cut open. Large ones, up to about a twelfth of an inch (2 millimeters) long, grew slowly while the magma was stored underground.

Tiny ones, shorter than about a hundredth of an inch (300 micrometers), grew during the final rise to the surface.

The tiny crystals are the only ones that formed entirely from the magma that erupted.

Across all seven volcanoes, they held nearly the same calcium-to-sodium balance, and the outer rims of the large crystals matched them.

The team named that narrow band the plagioclase eruptibility field. The volcanoes couldn’t have been much more different.

Lascar, the most active volcano in the region, has neighbors that erupted only once, some as long as 3 million years ago.

Some lie on the main volcanic arc and others far behind it, where the crust is half as thick.

In an interview with Earth.com, Parra-Encalada was asked what surprised her most about the match.

“What surprised us most was discovering that this similarity was related to the textures and shapes of plagioclase crystals,” she said.

The cores of the large crystals told a different story. About half of all the plagioclase in these rocks didn’t grow from the erupted magma at all.

Its chemistry points to a silica-rich melt that cooled and crystallized for a long time a few miles down. Along the way it soaked up material from the surrounding crust.

Volcanologists call that kind of sluggish, crystal-packed body a mush.

In the Andean rocks, pieces of it arrived as clusters of interlocking crystals and as chunks of fully solidified rock, pulled loose and carried up when the eruption came.

The trapped crystals share their chemistry with crystals in the region’s ancient super-eruption deposits, which Parra-Encalada told Earth.com was one of the two findings that excited her most.

The same stalled magma systems may also matter for how copper and gold collect underground, she said.

The team is now comparing Andean crystals with those from large ore deposits.

To work out what the fingerprint means, the researchers ran computer simulations of magma cooling and crystallizing at different depths.

The trapped crystals formed at about 1,380°F to 1,560°F (750°C to 850°C), within 7 miles (11 kilometers) of the surface.

The eruptibility-field crystals formed hotter, at roughly 1,650°F to 1,790°F (900°C to 975°C). Their magma had just been reheated by a fresh injection from deeper down.

The fresh injection also carried more dissolved water. As the mixture rose, the water began bubbling out and building pressure in the shallow magma chamber.

Strontium isotopes told the same story: crystal cores carried the signature of melt that had digested old crust, while the rims matched the fresh carrier magma.

The team’s reading is that a volcano on this arc needs a shove from below to erupt, and the shove is what the newest crystals record.

Parra-Encalada then pulled plagioclase data from a public geochemistry database covering 26 volcanic arcs.

The average crystal composition worldwide fell inside the Andean eruptibility field. So did the averages for the Cascades and for Kamchatka.

“And this was not just in the Andes!” she said.

In the Aleutians and in Indonesia, the crust changes from continental to oceanic. There too, the crystal rims fell in the field while the cores scattered.

The result also speaks to an old puzzle. Lavas with intermediate silica levels commonly erupt along these arcs, yet the silica-rich and silica-poor magmas that mix to produce them rarely reach the surface on their own.

The crystals suggest the crust works as a filter: the thick, silica-rich mush and the dense, iron-rich mush both stay trapped, and only the reheated blend in between gets out.

Two of the samples came from an ancient deposit beneath Lascar. It was left by one of the caldera-forming explosive eruptions that buried the region about 4 million years ago.

Those crystals showed almost none of the reheating signature. In a chemical map of an entire rock slice, 97% of the crystals had grown steadily without disturbance.

So the shove that sets off an ordinary Andean eruption may not be what sets off the giant ones.

The authors suggest those huge silica-rich reservoirs can destabilize and empty on their own once enough magma has piled in.

Using crystal chemistry to identify volcanoes more likely to erupt remains an untested idea.

The idea, Parra-Encalada said, is that a volcano whose large crystals also fall near the eruptibility field may hold magma already close to erupting conditions.

Some crystal interiors at Lascar do. Lascar last erupted in the year 2022, two weeks after the team finished fieldwork there.

“If we observe a similar pattern in other polygenetic volcanoes, where most plagioclase crystals have compositions within the eruptibility field, it could indicate a volcanic system that deserves closer attention,” Parra-Encalada noted.

Polygenetic means a volcano that erupts more than once.

Nothing in this study links the fingerprint to how often a volcano erupts or how soon, and the team’s next paper takes up that question.

The samples also come from one slice of one continental arc. The global comparison relies on other researchers’ data, most of it without the crystal-by-crystal sorting this study depended on.

The team is expanding its work to Tonga to test whether the pattern also holds in an oceanic setting.

The full study was published in the journal Nature Communications.
