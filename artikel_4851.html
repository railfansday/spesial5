Are You Hanging On to a Negative Story That Keeps You Stuck?

Posted November 15, 2026 | Reviewed by Abigail Fagan

This morning I was in the dog park with Bucket, my Cocker Spaniel. Bucket’s favorite thing to do is chase sticks; he’d rather chase sticks than eat, sleep, or do almost anything else. Each stick seems to bring him unabashed and unwavering delight, as is evident in his dog smile. That’s the story I’ve written for him anyway… and I’m “sticking” with it.

During this morning’s “sticking” session, Bucket discovered a gigantic, thick, and heavy, unwieldy branch, which he managed to get between his jaws and then proudly carried around in his mouth, snubbing my attempts to offer him smaller, more navigable options. After some time, I noticed that he was starting to notice the squirrels running up a tree, and then another couple of dogs arrived, and I saw him staring at them seemingly with longing, as if he wanted to say hello and play.

Several times Bucket attempted to approach the dogs or run at the squirrels, but each time he tried, he was stymied by his big stick, which kept getting caught on everything he encountered. So too, the other dogs moved away when they saw the big stick coming. Bucket continued to grip onto it because he didn’t yet realize that he had the power to open his jaws and release it. He didn’t understand, yet, that the choice was his; he was the one grasping the very thing that was causing his limitation.

We sometimes do the same thing as humans: we hold onto our self-constructed stories long after they serve us, and long after they’re true in reality. Bucket eventually dropped his stick, but we humans often don’t, and continue living out a frustrating life, unknowingly depriving ourselves of what we really want—gripping onto and remaining loyal to our story, but not to ourselves. We stick with our stories even when our stories keep us stuck and cause us suffering.

If someone’s narrative, for example, is that they are unlovable, even in the face of someone loving them, they reject that truth, dismiss what’s happening in reality, the love they’re receiving, and continue insisting on their unlovable “me” story—the very story, paradoxically, that makes them suffer. Reality be damned, they are loyal to their story, not the facts, and not even to their own happiness.

We may stick with our story, even a negative story, simply because we don’t realize that it’s a story. We’re not aware of the fact that much of what we call “me” is a story, something we construct, and can in fact deconstruct. But we think our assumptions about ourselves, our “me” story, are fundamental truth—just who we are. The problem is that our “me” story is inside the lens through which we see everything in our life. The “me” whose identity story we’ve created is the one interacting with and experiencing reality, and reporting back to us on it. And what we experience is inherently interpreted and corrupted through the beliefs of this self-made “me” lens. In order to free ourselves and take off our “me” lenses, we need to see the seer, to get behind the lens through which we’re looking. We need to ask ourselves again and again what we believe about ourselves before anything else happens; what we are coming into the situation already assuming. We have to get behind the eyes through which we’re looking at and experiencing our life. When you can look at the looker, and question the story about the “me” you’ve constructed, then things change.

Our stories are often built around wounds that we’ve suffered. If, for example. our self-story is that we have to do everything ourselves, no one can help us, it’s not because it’s fun to believe that or feel that way. At some point, we probably needed help and didn’t receive it. Our early caretakers may have been unable to give us what we needed. Maybe they didn’t care or were limited and unable or unwilling to help us. The point is we felt alone. And so we created this story of being fundamentally un-helpable. If we’re somebody who has to do everything for ourselves, and no one can help; if we can know that for sure. If it’s set in stone, then we don’t have to mourn the fact that we experienced it; it’s just what is—our karma, if you will. Knowing that this is who we are, we feel more in control; we don’t have to hope for help or be sad or angry about the fact that we didn’t receive it. We don’t have to question why this happened to us, or continues to happen now, because we reject and dismiss the help that’s offered and any evidence that we’re not “all alone.” Furthermore, we don’t ever have to grieve how much we wanted and needed help, and suffered without it. We hold onto the past, counter-intuitively, as a way to not have to mourn and accept the past.

With this as our self-story, locked in place, we can know the kinds of things that happen to us. We don’t have to face the uncertainty of each moment, how it will go, and whether or not we will receive what we need. We don’t have to be vulnerable and ask for help when we need it, because we know we’re not going to get it. Even though our story is not what we want, even though it impedes our connection with others and prevents the opportunity to feel gratitude, even though it gets in the way of our satisfaction and happiness, nonetheless, it becomes our identity and a way to protect ourselves, avoid grief, shame, uncertainty, and the hope that things really can change. Our self-story, as awful as it is, becomes a kind of security blanket. We are secure in the knowing that we won’t get what we need.

The story we’re holding onto about who we are might not be true, that is, if we stopped proving it were true. It might be something we could drop, like Bucket and his stick. But to let it go can feel even more painful than sticking with our negative story. To let it go can feel like we’re abandoning our suffering, moving from it as if it’s no longer part of us. Paradoxically, it feels too painful to move on from our story of pain.

But getting free is not about dropping your story as if that experience never happened. It is about honoring the wound that the story triggers, grieving and acknowledging what you have experienced—but—without the assumption that this present moment is going to be the same, and without the story that this is who you are, a person whose life is always “like this.” The wound matters, the story can be dropped.
