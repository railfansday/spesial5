Bancroft blows open Sandpapergate with extraordinary statement

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Cameron Bancroft breaks silence on Sandpapergate and backs Usman Khawaja's claims entire Australia team knew about plan in South Africa in 2018; Bancroft says Pat Cummins' press conference denying any knowledge prompted him to speak out after being left feeling "angry, betrayed and alone"

Cameron Bancroft has directly accused Pat Cummins of knowledge of the use of sandpaper against South Africa in 2018.

In an explosive new 4,000-word statement in The West Australian newspaper, Bancroft has broken his silence on revelations in Usman Khawaja's new book, claiming that Cummins' press conference denying the allegations was the "final straw" for him, revealing that the entire bowling unit maintained full knowledge of the extensive use of sandpaper.

Bancroft saw his career turned upside after being suspended for his part in the incident alongside then captain Steve Smith and vice-captain David Warner. Those were the only three players to be punished.

"It was not just the three of us - Steve Smith, David Warner and I - who knew about me using sandpaper on the ball in South Africa. It was the whole team," Bancroft wrote.

"The final straw for me to write this account was Australian cricket captain Pat Cummins' press conference.

"It made me feel angry, betrayed and alone.

"What I saw, experienced and lived with - not only him, but everyone else on that tour in South Africa - is not the public account he gave in his press conference and in the years previously when he has spoken on this time."

Bancroft's statement heaps the pressure on Test captain Cummins, with Australia's next red-ball series against New Zealand beginning on December 9.

The claims come in the wake of a furore of discussion around the 2018 scandal, with Khawaja's book first bringing to light claims that the entire team knew about the use of sandpaper.

Khawaja also alleged that discussions about ball-tampering had been held "openly and lightheartedly" across the entire squad, involving not just the batters, bowlers and coaching staff but the wider team.

Those claims align with Bancroft's new statement, and with the recollection of former seamer Chadd Sayers, who also asserted that "everyone in that [2018] side" was aware of the ball-tampering plot.

Cummins called Khawaja's claims "complete and utter nonsense" in a sensational 68-minute press conference on Friday, with Starc also claiming "his recollection hadn't changed since 2021" when the bowlers released a joint statement denying knowledge.

The statement also comes just hours after Cricket Australia announced an independent forum to explore the scandal, stating that players could share their version of events without fears of reprisals - a decision which Khawaja and Warner have questioned.

Reacting to Bancroft, CA said in a statement: "We respect that Cameron Bancroft has told his story, and we will take time to consider his comments.

"We expect that there will be many dimensions to the events of 2018, which is why we have established the forum for individuals to give their version of events.

"We are finalising the terms of reference and will commence the process as soon as possible."

Cummins' management then issued its own statement, which read: "Whilst we feel for Cam and everything he's been through there's nothing in the statement that hasn't been answered by Pat in his press conference."

Darren Lehmann - who was Australia head coach at the time of the scandal and named in Bancroft's statement as involved in discussions over the use of sandpaper - gave his own response to ABC radio, saying: "I need to sit back, process it and work through each allegation.

"That's what I'll be doing over the next few days. I need to have a really good read of it. I need to make sure I'm saying what I want to say."

Bancroft's explosive statement alleges that the whole team would "laugh" about sandpaper and the game-changing effect it had on the Port Elizabeth Test match, where Australia took 4-28 in one session in the evening of day two. This was the second Test of the series, with Bancroft then being caught on camera in the third Cape Town Test.

"Not everyone was in the changing room at the time, but the conversation was not confined to Dave [Warner] and I," Bancroft continued.

"I was asked whether I could carry out the task. I felt uncomfortable and compromised. I knew it was wrong. I knew that using sandpaper was not simply pushing the boundaries of the laws of cricket.

"I recall conversations with all the fast bowlers Pat Cummins, Mitchell Starc, Josh Hazlewood and Mitchell Marsh about whether it was reverse swinging enough. I also spoke with Tim Paine, who was in a key position behind the stumps to observe how the ball was moving.

"Everyone would laugh and speak about it sporadically on the field. We were all well aware of the riskiness of my behaviour. As time elapsed in that session there was excitement about the movement we were getting from the ball.

"There was an uncomfortable sense that something we all knew was wrong had become part of our effort to win the Test match. We took 4-28 in that final session. That is game changing!

"This was not something I did just once in the playing area after returning to the field from the tea break. This was an act I did nearly every over the whole last session on that day to begin with and then for the remainder of that Test match, up until I was caught in Third Test in Cape Town."

Bancroft's lengthy statement detailed the discussions around the use of sandpaper that took place in the changing room, in which he claims all players and coaches were present when media manager Kate Hutchinson made clear her opposition the players' plan to alter the ball.

"I remember my ball tampering with the sandpaper the evening before being discussed and my role being acknowledged in front of the group.

"Much like the celebrations when we took 4-28 the evening before, I received wraps and encouragement from members of the playing and coaching staff, especially [bowling coach] David Saker."

Saker resigned from the Australia set up in the wake of the scandal in 2019, before joining up with England's coaching team for the 2022 World Cup and 2023 Ashes.

"It reinforced the sense that what I was doing was being accepted as part of our collective effort," Bancroft continued.

"Our media manager, Kate Hutchison, expressed her objection to what was happening. I recall her making it clear that she believed it was wrong, that she wanted nothing to do with it, and walking away from the discussion. I will never forget that.

"Somebody had demonstrated that being part of the team did not mean having to accept everything the team was doing.

"She deserves the opportunity to give her own account. But her reaction is important because it completely challenges the idea that nobody had an opportunity to question what was happening. We did. I did. And I chose to continue."

Bancroft candidly reflected on the impact of the sandpaper scandal on his own international career. The batter has not made an appearance for Australia in seven years, with his last Test cap coming in the second Test of the 2019 Ashes.

"For eight years, I shared the burden of this story for the whole team," Bancroft added.

"This was a burden which was heavier than my capacity and I took the bullet for everybody, and it was my choice.

"My cricket career has been destroyed and I have never been able to play for my country since Justin Langer was coach in 2019.

"But I have reached a point where continuing to protect an incomplete account of events feels more damaging than the truth.

Bancroft also recalls the fallout in the dressing room once the use of sandpaper had been exposed on the big screen in Cape Town during the third Test against South Africa in 2018.

"After the match, I remember a lengthy meeting involving players and staff on the tour. We discussed how to respond to the scandal and the information that would need to be acknowledged publicly.

"By then, it was clear that the use of sandpaper could no longer be concealed. Nobody in that meeting stood up and said they had been completely unaware of my use of sandpaper. Not one person.

"Everyone started to give me advice about what I should say and what I shouldn't say to minimise the damage on the team related with the sandpaper saga.

"I had convinced myself that I was carrying out a role for the benefit of the team.

"Now I was facing the possibility that my international career might be over. As it turns out I ended up becoming the scapegoat for the entire team.

"Cummins said when directly asked the question: 'Were you aware of any ball tampering?' He said no.

"He could hide behind semantics and whether he knew sandpaper was being taken on the field that particular day or that particular session. But he directly said no.

"If you read The West Australian article from Bancroft, he says the media manager walked out because she was so unhappy with what was being discussed in a team meeting.

"How does the media manager know what's going on but a bowling unit doesn't?

"It would be the first time a bowling unit does not know what is happening to a cricket ball."
