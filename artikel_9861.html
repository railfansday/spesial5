How to Build an AI-Powered Control Tower for Finance and Operations

Most finance and operations teams do not lack data. They lack a timely, trusted answer to three questions: what is going wrong, who should care, and what should they do about it?

I designed a Control Tower platform to answer those questions for a large operation. This article walks through the core concepts behind it and, more importantly, why each one is modelled the way it is. I'll use finance examples throughout (payments, invoices, cost centres and business units), but the same model works wherever you have many data sources, many teams, and a need to act on numbers before they become incidents.

Every behaviour should be a row of configuration, not a release of code.

A new data source, a new KPI, a new threshold for a new team, a new action the AI may suggest: each of these should be added through a setup wizard, validated, and picked up by the running system. That principle explains almost every modelling decision below. When something is configuration, it needs a clear concept, a clear boundary, and a clear contract with the next layer.

The most important definition in the whole system is the humble metric. It is tempting to jump straight to KPIs, but KPIs are where business meaning lives, and meaning changes often. A metric is deliberately boring: one number, from one source, computed one way.

Source: a query against a registered data source, or a stream of events.

Grouping: per entity (a payment, an invoice, a journal entry) and per dimension (cost centre, business unit, counterparty).

Aggregation: count, sum, average, minimum, maximum, distinct count, or latest value.

Window: none, rolling, tumbling, or session-based.

Why model it this way? Because it isolates the messiness of sources. Each source has its own schema, its own SQL dialect, its own quirks. A metric absorbs all of that and emits a generic row: metric, entity or dimension cell, value, time. Nothing downstream ever needs to know whether a number came from a lake house, a relational database or an event stream. When a source changes its table structure, you edit one metric's query. You don't touch a KPI or a rule.

A KPI is a business indicator. It is composed from metrics, and those metrics may come from different sources. A payment failure rate can divide a count of failed payments from a data lake by the total payments recorded in a ledger database.

The platform supports a small, closed set of KPI types:

Ratio: metric A divided by metric B (rates and percentages).

Composite: a formula across several metrics.

Delta: the change since the previous computation.

Moving average: a value smoothed over N periods.

Custom query: when the source can compute the KPI directly and efficiently.

The second key idea is the cell. A KPI isn't one number; it is a number per slice: per cost centre, per business unit, for the whole group, or per counterparty within a business unit. Each KPI declares the grains it is computed at. I add a crossing such as counterparty × business unit only when a team actually needs that view. Otherwise the number of cells grows multiplicatively and nobody looks at most of them.

Why? Because the people who act on numbers each own a slice. If a KPI isn't computed at the grain someone owns, no alert can ever reach the right person. The KPI also carries its target and direction ("lower is worse"), so the dashboard, the rules and the AI agent all read it the same way.

Data sources are registered per tenant under a named connection code. A metric refers to the connection code, never to a host or a password.

One detail I'm particularly glad we built: one circuit breaker per database technology. A slow cloud lakehouse warming up must not trip the breaker for the relational database that the rest of the KPIs depend on.

Why? Adding a new kind of source (we have added relational, columnar-analytics and cloud-lakehouse sources) becomes a driver plus a registry entry. No metric, KPI or rule changes.

Data turns into signals in three different ways, and each is chosen per use case. Treating them as one leads to either slow alerts or fragile streaming code.

Stateless, per message: entity state evaluation. Each event is judged on its own: is this entity now in a bad state?

Stateful, across messages: windowed aggregation. Many events are combined into a number over time.

Pull, on a schedule: query on a cadence. For most sources the simplest correct answer is to run the metric's query every N minutes.

Why separate them? Entity-level problems (this one payment is stuck) and aggregate problems (this business unit's failure rate is degrading) are different questions. An aggregate can look healthy while one important entity is failing, and a single bad entity says nothing about a trend. The platform keeps both paths and lets them share the same rules, routing and actions.

Early warning comes from two places. Soft thresholds warn when a KPI is getting close to its hard line. On top of that, a few ML models are built specifically to give early warning: they forecast that a KPI or an entity is likely to breach before it does, and that prediction raises an alert of its own, which the AI agent picks up in forecasting mode.

Two design choices matter more than they look:

A persona is a role anchored to a dimension and a level: a cost-centre owner owns the organisation at cost-centre level, a business-unit controller owns it at business-unit level, and a group finance head owns the whole group. A persona is not a list of users. Users are assigned to a persona and a member, for example "Business-unit controller — Business unit Retail".

Why? Organisations re-org; slices of the business don't. Anchoring ownership to dimension × level lets an alert on a cell find its owner automatically, find the next owner up when nobody acts, and survive people joining and leaving without touching a single rule.

The AI layer sits beside deterministic detection; it never replaces it. Rules decide that something is wrong. The agent helps decide what to do.

The recommendations are a small, explicit set: escalate, notify, monitor, no action, escalate to a human, and close. What the agent may recommend, and whether it may act alone, is configured per alert and dimension: allowed action, confidence threshold, auto-execute, risk level, approver, approval timeout and success criteria. The agent keeps a short memory note per entity, so the next run builds on the last decision instead of starting over.

Why this shape? It makes the AI auditable and reversible. Every recommendation has evidence, a reason, a confidence and an approver. Trust is earned one action type at a time, by moving it from "human approves" to "auto-execute" through configuration, not code.

A Control Tower is less about dashboards than about decisions: getting the right number to the right owner, early enough, with a sensible next step attached. Get the concepts right and the dashboards almost build themselves.
