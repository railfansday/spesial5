A tiny plant’s unusual sex life depends on water flowing uphill

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

Common liverworts carry water up their stalks without a pump, helping sperm reach elevated eggs and revealing how the plants evolved.

Water runs downhill, and that’s a problem for liverworts, small plants whose sperm can reach an egg only by swimming.

In the common liverwort, a flat green species, the female holds her eggs up on a short stalk, away from the damp ground.

A new study found that the outside of that stalk carries water upward, against gravity, to the eggs at the top.

The upward flow helps resolve a conflict between fertilization and spore dispersal.

Height makes fertilization harder. But height also helps the plant spread its spores, and the study traces how the stalk came to handle both jobs.

Bo Xu, a professor at the Institute of Botany of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, led the work, and Xiaotong Qi is the study’s first author.

The team studied Marchantia polymorpha, the best-studied liverwort in biology labs.

Liverworts, mosses and hornworts belong to a group called bryophytes. They make no flowers or seeds, and they don’t have true roots.

They also lack the complex network of water-carrying tissue that larger plants rely on.

In bryophytes, a sperm cell has to travel through water to reach an egg.

Once an egg is fertilized, it grows into an embryo. The embryo then becomes a structure that makes spores, and those spores spread out and grow into new plants.

Every bryophyte has to meet both needs at once. Fertilization depends on water, while spores spread better from a higher, drier spot.

How small plants managed that is part of the larger story of how plants colonized land.

On a female common liverwort, a stalk lifts the egg-holding organs above the ground.

Xu’s team found that the stalk and the head on top of it carry water upward along their outer surface.

The flow depends on several kinds of tiny structures, each placed in a precise position relative to the others.

The researchers used three-dimensional X-ray imaging to map how those structures fit together on the stalk.

Water moving up that surface helps sperm reach eggs held well above the wet ground.

Keeping those surface structures intact helped water-based fertilization succeed, the team found.

One protein, called MpLAS, controls how all of those surface structures develop. It belongs to a family of proteins called GRAS, which switch other genes on and off.

MpLAS works by guiding how cells divide as they grow.

A second GRAS protein, MpGRAS10, helps build the stalk itself. It boosts the activity of two genes, MpKAOL1 and MpKAOL2, which help the plant make compounds related to gibberellin.

In many plants, gibberellin is the hormone that makes stems grow longer.

So one protein helps raise the eggs, and another builds the surface that gets water up to them.

Xu’s team also compared the common liverwort with its relatives and worked backward through their family tree.

The female stalk appears to have evolved only once in this group of liverworts, and several later lineages lost it.

The stalk turned up before the surface structures that carry water.

The researchers read that order as two separate steps. The elevated stalk helped the spores produced after fertilization spread farther.

The surface network came later and dealt with that problem.

In their account, later traits may have arisen to make earlier ones work better, by fixing what those earlier traits couldn’t do.

They said competing demands like these can push a complex structure to evolve one piece at a time.

Comparisons among living liverworts suggest that the stalk evolved before its water-carrying surface structures.

That sequence supports the team’s proposed explanation, although it does not prove how those traits evolved.

The findings could also inspire engineers.

By carrying water upward without a pump, the liverwort offers a model for surfaces designed to move liquid on their own.

The full study was published in the journal Nature Plants.
