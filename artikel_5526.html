A Veterans Day reflection for building leaders and the work ahead

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

A Veterans Day editorial urging the housing and homebuilding community to pair gratitude with action. It spotlights two mortgage-free home commitments in Texas and outlines leadership traits tied to durable affordability progress.  AI Summary

Today, amid the quickened pulse of our nation’s political and cultural divisions, Veterans Day gives Americans a chance to take stock of something that transcends the moment’s disputes and divides.

We have inherited something incalculably, extraordinarily valuable.

Generations of men and women who served in the United States Armed Forces have helped preserve and protect a nation where we can live, work, worship, disagree, build businesses, raise families and decide what kind of future we want for ourselves and for those who come after us.

Those freedoms don’t exist in a vacuum. They’re neither inevitable nor self-sustaining, though we may act as if we take them for granted. They exist only because people before us accepted duties, endured pain, and made sacrifices for beneficiaries they didn’t know and ultimately never would.

Our children. Our children’s children. Their children. And on and on.

That generational commitment deserves more than our gratitude, though gratitude is where we begin today. And where we need to begin each day. A salute and a deeply felt debt of gratitude are an open invitation. It asks each of us to examine what we do with our hard-won inheritance. It asks us all what we are willing to do to preserve and improve it for generations beyond our own.

For those of us whose livelihoods center on making homes and neighborhoods for people to live in, that searching examination resonates. We work in a business community whose homes, neighborhoods, infrastructure and institutions endure. They’re a bedrock for generations. They profoundly nurture and shape the possibilities, the very dreams, available to American families and households.

Military service and homebuilding are not equivalent sacrifices. Still, much to learn from the commitment to mission, dependence on others, the discipline of preparation, and the willingness to serve a purpose larger than oneself that distinguishes the best traditions of military service.

This Veterans Day, we have a moving example of what happens when gratitude becomes a commitment to act. In Austin, Texas, this week, Lennar and nonprofit Operation Finally Home, joined by country music artist and U.S. Army Reserve soldier Craig Morgan, surprised two military veterans and their families with the promise of new, mortgage-free homes.

Marine Corps Sgt. Kevin Gallmeier served a decade in uniform, including combat deployments to Iraq, before retiring medically after sustaining service-related injuries. Army Spc. Jordan Taylor served in Baghdad and later continued serving his community as a firefighter and EMT until a serious injury forced him to retire.

For Taylor, his wife Aylynn, and their four children, financial hardship meant selling their family home and moving in with relatives. The promise of a new home offers a chance to regain stability and independence.

For Gallmeier and his family, a groundbreaking this week at Lennar’s Freedom Crossing community in Killeen, Texas, marked the start of a new chapter.

Operation Finally Home founder Dan Wallrath describes the organization’s mission as fighting with hammers, nails, and humility to restore dignity and peace of mind.

Nothing magical makes these homes a reality. Land must be secured, capital committed, plans developed, materials and products donated and skilled people coordinated through hundreds of decisions and handoffs before families receive their keys.

What makes the effort extraordinary is that people across organizations commit their capabilities and resources to a shared purpose, with the needs of two families at its core.

That is gratitude expressed through action.

And as HousingWire TBD reporter Tyler Williams chronicles in his Veterans Day reporting, this spirit of service spans the nation’s homebuilding community, from West to East and from South to North. From PulteGroup and Meritage Homes donating mortgage-free homes to regional builders offering military families financial assistance, and industry partners helping veterans transition into civilian careers, the commitment takes many forms.

Behind these named initiatives are countless unheralded builders, tradespeople, suppliers, employees, and volunteers who contribute their skills, resources, and time in communities across America. Their efforts reflect a deeply rooted conviction that building homes and communities also means honoring those whose sacrifices have made our freedom to do so possible.

Human beings have an extraordinary capacity to imagine a better future. That capacity is among our greatest strengths, enabling us to conceive of possibilities that do not yet exist and to organize our efforts to make them real.

We also tend toward magical thinking: believing that our wishes, convictions, or the triumph of our preferred outcomes will somehow deliver the future we envision without the difficult work required to bring it about.

Housing affordability offers a case in point.

For years, builders, developers, investors, policymakers, and families have wrestled with the interrelated constraints of lots, labor, lending, local approvals, and lumber — shorthand for the broader building-products supply chain. These five L’s often pull against one another. Improvement in one area does not necessarily lead to meaningful progress overall. In reality, getting one right often comes at the expense of one or more of the others.

And then there’s the sixth L: leadership. No single technological breakthrough, policy initiative, favorable interest-rate cycle, or election result will solve the interconnected problems of producing homes more Americans can afford.

There is no abracadabra. Only the sustained work of learning, adapting, cooperating, and improving remains.

The traditions of military service provide a useful standard for leaders in our industry to examine their own responsibilities. Five interdependent qualities stand out: character, capability, collaborative culture, customer centricity, and competitive fire.

Character means doing what is right even when it is difficult or costly. Capability is the accumulated knowledge, judgment, training, and practiced skill that enables people to perform under uncertain conditions. A collaborative culture recognizes that complex missions depend on people with different strengths coordinating their efforts toward shared objectives.

Customer centricity keeps the needs of those being served at the center of decisions. Competitive fire is the determination to keep improving, refusing complacency while adapting intelligently to adversity.

These qualities reinforce one another. Competitive fire without character becomes recklessness. Capability without collaboration becomes isolated expertise. Customer commitment without operating discipline leads to promises you can’t keep.

They also take time to develop. The strongest organizations invest in people, relationships, processes, and productive capacity, whose value can endure long beyond any individual leader’s tenure.

That is especially important now, as housing’s affordability, capital, and demand challenges strain the industry’s capacity to withstand adversity while preparing for the opportunities ahead.

Our country’s political divisions can make it mightily tempting to believe that progress requires defeating those whose beliefs differ from our own rather than finding ways to work with them. But the nation’s hardest problems will not yield simply because one side has won an election or one set of leaders has replaced another.

Nor can we assume that the good will inevitably prevail if we simply wait long enough.

Housing’s challenges remind us why cooperation matters. No single builder, developer, lender, investor, trade partner, or government agency can solve the affordability problem alone. People and institutions with different interests must find enough common ground to accomplish what none can achieve alone.

We may first need to slow the deterioration of some problems, then stabilize conditions, and only then make sustained progress toward better outcomes. That may not satisfy our appetite for quick solutions, but it is how durable improvements are achieved.

Generations of Americans have taken oaths of service without knowing whether they would live to see the fruits of their sacrifices. Their service has helped secure and preserve a nation in which we are free, individually and together, to decide what kind of future we want to pursue.

That freedom is a gift. What we do with it is our responsibility.

For those of us who build homes, develop communities, and invest in America’s future, that responsibility includes creating places where families put down roots, neighbors form communities, and children grow up with opportunities that may surpass those available to their parents.

We will not achieve those objectives through good intentions alone or by waiting for a more favorable economic cycle or a less divided political environment.

We must refuse to do nothing. We must not tolerate inaction. And we must recognize that a bigger tent, built on mutual respect and shared purpose, can accomplish more than one defined by animosity and division.

To the veterans of our United States Armed Forces, and to their families: Thank you for the freedoms you have helped protect, the sacrifices you have made, and the example of commitment to people you may never meet.

May we honor that inheritance through what we choose to build together – for our children, our children’s children, and all the generations to come.
