How soon your tax refund arrives depends on how you file. E-file and choose direct deposit, and the IRS typically pays within three weeks, while a mailed paper return takes six weeks or more. Double-check your routing and account numbers, since missing or rejected bank details can now put your refund on hold.

Two filers who finish error-free returns on the same February day, neither claiming a credit that by law delays the refund, can get their refunds weeks apart.

The one who e-files with direct deposit typically has the money within three weeks, according to the IRS’s Where’s My Refund page. The one who mails a paper return can’t see the refund’s status there for four weeks and should expect the refund six weeks or more after the IRS receives the return.

Even an e-filed refund can take longer or come in smaller. By law, the IRS can’t issue a refund before mid-February when the return claims the earned income tax credit or the additional child tax credit, and the hold covers the whole refund. A math error or a forgotten signature can delay a refund too. If the IRS mails you a letter asking you to verify your identity, such as Letter 5071C, it won’t finish processing your return until you do. And a past-due debt such as child support can take part or all of the refund.

The IRS began phasing out paper refund checks on Sept. 30, 2025, so the bank account you list on your return now matters as much as e-filing. Leave it off, or enter numbers the bank rejects, and the IRS will likely hold the refund and mail you a CP53E notice asking for a bank account. You have 30 days, and a single try, to add it through your IRS online account, and the IRS usually pays within seven days of that update. Let the 30 days pass, and a paper check goes out six weeks after the notice.

So e-file with direct deposit, and copy your account number and your bank’s routing number from its app or online banking.

Then check Where’s My Refund or the IRS app, which replaced IRS2Go, starting about 24 hours after you e-file. Both update once a day, usually overnight, so checking more often shows nothing new.
