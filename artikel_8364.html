Peaks of the Balkans: Through the Accursed Mountains

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

The devil is said to have created them, but to us, they are a hiker’s paradise: the “Accursed Mountains” at the crossroads of Albania, Kosovo, and Montenegro. The 118-mile Peaks of the Balkans Trail winds through rugged mountain landscapes, lush green valleys, and remote mountain villages. We extend the loop with several summit detours, bringing our total distance to 143 miles. Along the way, we encounter not only spectacular scenery and unpredictable autumn weather but also warm hospitality from the friendly people of the region and plenty of dogs eager to join us. Apparently, all it takes to rent a four-legged hiking companion here is a few belly rubs and some peanut butter.

Duration: 8–10 days Distance: 118 miles Start and end point: Flexible, as it’s a loop, e.g. Theth (Albania) or Plav (Montenegro) Total elevation gain: Approx. 32,800 feet Difficulty: Moderate to challenging (some steep, rocky, and exposed sections)

Theth → Valbona Pass → Valbona 11.8 mi / 4,230 ft elevation gain / 6 h

Theth is bustling with activity, and we’re in quite a hurry to escape the crowds. It’s gotten pretty warm by now, but fortunately, much of the climb leads through shady forest. Valbona Pass is one of the most popular hiking destinations in the region, and there are plenty of day hikers on the trail. But after the hustle and bustle of the cities, even a busy hiking trail is a welcome change.

Along the ascent, we pass several small cafés inviting hikers to stop for coffee or cold drinks. We initially ignore them, but at the last one before the pass, we can’t resist and order two Cokes. Whenever I hike in warm weather, my throat begs for sugary, carbonated drinks. An itch that Coke scratches the best.

Start of the Peaks of the Balkans Trail in Theth, climbing to Valbona Pass and passing cafés on the way

During our climb, Ryan strikes up conversations with some US-Americans. Among them is an older Mormon woman from Montana who lives near Glacier National Park. Sister Fisher is spending a year and a half on a mission in Albania, where she’s responsible for coordinating vehicles. We also meet a group from Indiana. When Ryan mentions that we live in Munich, one of the men tells us he would actually have preferred to go to Oktoberfest, which is currently taking place. However, the rest of the group outvoted him. I can understand why.

Besides the many hikers, we also encounter plenty of dogs looking for affection and presumably something to eat. We can at least offer them the former. One of the dogs even decides to join us and accompanies us partway up the mountain.

We reach Valbona Pass, where a large Albanian flag flutters in the wind. The black double-headed eagle against a blood-red background looks rather martial to me. With its outstretched wings and sharp claws, I could easily imagine this symbol on a medieval battlefield. In fact, the flag dates back to the Albanian national hero Skanderbeg, who led the resistance against the Ottoman Empire in the 15th century and used the black double-headed eagle on a red background as his coat of arms. When Albania declared independence in 1912, the historic symbol was revived. From here, we look out over the valleys of Valbona and Theth, as well as Jezerca, the highest peak in the Prokletije Mountains at 8,838 feet. Long before the first hikers arrived, the inhabitants of the two valleys used this pass to trade goods.

At the viewpoint above the pass, three dogs are sleeping right in the middle of the trail. They don’t seem the slightest bit bothered by the constant stream of people stepping over them. Even I find dogs this relaxed rather endearing.

Beyond the pass, things initially become much quieter. At last, we leave the noisy day hikers behind, some of whom have been blasting music from portable speakers. The trail leads beneath an impressive, towering rock face. I can even hear birds chirping again.

As we descend toward Valbona, however, the trail gets busier again. We stop for a break at another café, where beer and soft drinks are kept cool in troughs filled with water. We treat ourselves to another Coke before continuing our descent into the valley.

Eventually, we reach a gravel road. Most hikers cover the final four miles to Valbona by shuttle. Accordingly, numerous minibuses overtake us, some traveling far too fast and enveloping us in clouds of dust. But the scenery is spectacular. All around us, enormous, rugged peaks rise above the valley, enclosing it like a giant amphitheater. With views like these, even a dusty gravel road is bearable. The final 2.5 miles to Valbona are paved, but there’s a separate path for pedestrians and cyclists.

Only now do I realize that I’ve barely eaten anything today and am getting pretty hungry. So we make a beeline for a restaurant and sit down at a table. A cat keeps us company. We ask the waiter for a recommendation. He offers his personal favorite dish: byrek with onions and cheese. What arrives on our plates is delicious. Crispy, thin layers of pastry filled with onions and a salty, slightly crumbly cheese reminiscent of feta. The slightly tangy cheese provides the perfect contrast to the rich onions and buttery flavor of the pastry. A kind of Albanian pizza, if you will. I’m pretty basic when it comes to food. Give me something simple and savory, and I’m happy.

As soon as the sun disappears behind the mountains, it gets noticeably cold. We therefore move from the outdoor seating area into the restaurant, where a large fire is already burning in the fireplace. The waiter carries enormous wooden beams, six feet long, inside on his shoulder and feeds them into the fire.

As we warm up, we realize that the sun sets here at around 6:30 pm. That doesn’t leave us much time to cover a few more miles on the next climb. However, we’re not particularly fond of spending the night in towns, so after dinner, we shoulder our backpacks again and set off.

We hike a little farther uphill until we find a suitable spot for our tent and set up camp. It’s been a long first day on the trail, taking us from the busy streets of Theth over a popular mountain pass and into the impressive Valbona Valley. At last, we’re back outdoors, carrying everything we need, with nothing to do tomorrow but keep walking. Life is good.

Valbona → Prosllopit Pass → Borit Pass → Çeremi → Aljuci Pass 13.0 miles / 6,560 ft elevation gain / 8 h

The night was cold, and it’s still pretty chilly in the morning. We set off at 8:15 am, bundled up in several layers of clothing. But as soon as the first rays of sunshine reach us, it gets so warm that we start peeling off one layer after another.

Today, we’re taking the alternate over Prosllopit Pass, which adds some extra elevation gain. The trail climbs steeply, and we haven’t planned our water supplies particularly well. There’s no water to be found anywhere along the ascent. Even the so-called “Ice Cave,” supposedly filled with snow even in summer, is completely dry. The forest provides some welcome shade, but once we’re back in the sun, our thirst becomes increasingly noticeable.

We enter an impressive karst landscape dominated by Zla Kolata, the highest mountain in Montenegro at 8,314 feet. At Prosllopit Pass, we briefly cross the border into Montenegro for the first time. From here, it’s also possible to climb to the summit, adding around 1,640 feet of elevation gain. But with our water bottles now empty, that’s out of the question today.

The next water source is supposed to be beyond another pass, about two miles away. Between here and there, we have a short scrambling section to negotiate before climbing again on a comparatively easy trail. Numerous day hikers heading for the summit approach us from the opposite direction.

Just before the next pass, we spot a boy and his mother in the middle of nowhere, selling drinks, blueberries, and jewelry. What a sight! We’re incredibly excited to see them and buy ourselves a Coke.

We also meet a dog here who decides to join us. Together, we reach Qafa Borit Pass and cross back into Albania. Our long-awaited water source should be somewhere beyond the pass, but there’s no sign of it. According to my map, we should already have passed it. Does this spring even exist? Then we spot a small pipe with water flowing out of it. Another wonderful sight!

Crossing from a temporary visit to Montenegro back into Albania at Borit Pass

The flow is weak, and filling our bottles takes a while, but at least we can finally drink our fill. The dog is still with us. His light, wheat-colored fur reminds me of Weetabix, the British breakfast cereal, so that’s what we decide to call him.

The mountain dogs seem to have a simple strategy: join passing hikers and hope that some food comes their way. If you’re generous enough, you get several hours of four-legged company in return. Apparently, we make suitable candidates. Weetabix gets some cold cuts we brought from Germany and a spoonful of peanut butter. With that, our trail friendship seems to be sealed.

Together, we descend through the forest and stop for lunch. It soon becomes clear that Weetabix has his own ideas about how our food should be distributed. Several times, I have to call out a firm “Jo!” (the Albanian word for “no”), when he gets a little too pushy. He catches on quickly and then waits patiently for his next spoonful of peanut butter.

We continue downhill to Çeremi, where two men immediately try to persuade us to stay at their guesthouses. But it’s far too early for us to call it a day. We politely decline and keep walking.

At a river in the forest, we make a quick stop to refill our water bottles. Just then, another pair of hikers comes down the trail toward the village. Weetabix stops and seems to carefully weigh his options. Which hikers are more likely to provide him with additional treats? He makes his decision in favor of the newcomers. Without much of a farewell, he joins them and leaves us behind. I can’t blame him. We have another steep climb ahead of us. Further up, there’s a small bar where we can treat ourselves to a round of soft drinks. We try Bravo peach juice for the first time, and it’s excellent.

Weetabix in the hope of some food & losing him soon after Çeremi

Afterward, we follow a gravel road, whose much gentler gradient is a welcome change. Along the way, we cross the border into Montenegro once again. A narrow trail branches off into the forest, and the climbing gets steep again. After a while, the terrain finally levels out, and we refill our water bottles once more at a spring. We hike the final half mile to a flat spot that looks perfect for camping.

As soon as the sun disappears behind the mountains, it gets bitterly cold. After more than 6,560 feet of climbing and a long day spent crossing mountain passes, forests, and karst landscapes, we’re glad to finally crawl into our sleeping bags.

Aljuci Pass → Dobërdol → Ali Peja Pass → Gjeravica → Ali Peja Pass 14.5 miles / 4,774 ft elevation gain / 7.5 h

Last night was freezing again, and when we crawl out of our tent in the morning, the ground, and the walls of our tent are covered in a thin layer of frost. That’s the price you pay for hiking in the mountains in October. Ryan tells me that he heard a “big scary creature” during the night, sniffing and grunting as it prowled around our tent. Although there are bears in the Albanian Alps, a hungry dog seems much more likely to me.

We make good progress this morning. The first six miles are pleasant hiking, with only one steep climb just before Dobërdol. Soon, we reach the small mountain settlement, which could hardly be more idyllic. Sheep with particularly thick, shaggy coats wander between the houses, streams babble through the valley, and mountains rise all around us.

Dobërdol, a remote seasonal shepherd settlement and alpine plateau in Albania

We have something special planned for today: a side quest to Gjeravica, the highest mountain in Kosovo at 8,714 feet. Even the climb out of Dobërdol is beautiful. The mountainsides glow in rich shades of red, covered in shrubs displaying their autumn colors. With every bit of elevation gained, the views over the valley become more expansive.

The final section to Ali Peja Pass is quite steep, but at the top we’re rewarded with a fantastic view on the other side: a vast mountain landscape stretches out before us, with today’s summit objective rising above it. At the same time, we cross the border into Kosovo.

Shortly afterward, we find a suitable spot for our tent near the junction with the Via Dinarica, which we plan to follow tomorrow to rejoin the Peaks of the Balkans Trail. We set up our base camp here.

After a lunch break, we leave most of our gear in the tent. We take only our food with us, just to be on the safe side. After all, we don’t want curious dogs or even bears helping themselves to our supplies while we’re away.

The trail to the summit is stunning and definitely worth the extra effort. For the most part, it traverses the mountainsides at a moderate grade, although there’s plenty of constant ups and downs. While the sunlit slopes glow in warm autumn colors, the shaded areas are still covered in frost. Long strands of spider silk drift through the air, produced by spiders that use their silk threads to catch the wind and travel to new habitats. This phenomenon is known as ballooning and is particularly common in late summer.

We’ve barely encountered anyone today. It’s not until we reach a sparkling mountain lake that we meet a group of French hikers returning from the summit. We refill our water bottles at a nearby stream before tackling the next climb.

View of Liqeni i Zemrës on the climb to Gjeravica

A series of gentle switchbacks takes us up to the next pass. Beyond it lies another small depression before the final ascent begins. The last stretch to the summit of Gjeravica is really steep. There’s even some snow left on the slopes.

The view from the top is spectacular: the surrounding mountains stretch all the way to the horizon, and the warm autumn light gives the landscape a special atmosphere. I love hiking in the fall. There’s something about the light at this time of year that’s hard to put into words.

Summit of Gjeravica, Kosovo’s highest mountain

We meet another group of hikers on the summit. We take photos of each other against the mountain backdrop and chat briefly. They still have to hike all the way back to Dobërdol today. Given how late it is, I suspect they’ll be covering the final miles by headlamp.

We start making our way back to our base camp. At the stream below the mountain lake, we refill our water bottles again, this time for the night and the following morning. As we hike back, the sun slowly disappears behind the mountains, and as soon as the slopes fall into shadow, it gets noticeably cold again.

We reach our tent just before dark. As we settle in for the night, the group of hikers from the summit passes by. Godspeed my friends.

Ali Peja Pass → Tromeđa → Roshkodol Pass → Zavoj Pass → Jelenka Pass → Rugova 17.1 miles / 3,084 ft elevation gain / 8.5 h

Last night was the warmest we’ve had so far on our autumn hike along the Peaks of the Balkans Trail. Even so, I didn’t sleep particularly well. My hips hurt, so I spent the entire night turning from side to side like a rotisserie chicken. Occasionally, I tried sleeping on my stomach, but that just made my hands go numb. Ryan, meanwhile, had entirely different problems. He thought the ground on his side of the tent was too sloped. In the middle of the night, he’s had enough and simply moved outside, where he continued cowboycamping.

Early morning, we’re greeted by the sunrise. The first rays of sunshine reach our tent and warm us up nicely. After the first two frosty mornings, it’s a welcome change. What a pleasant way to start the day.

Sunrise on the Peaks of the Balkans Trail

First, we climb to Trekufiri, also known as Tromeđa, where the borders of Albania, Kosovo, and Montenegro meet. We willingly add some extra elevation gain for this short detour. Would we make the same decision again if we knew what was still ahead of us today?

We follow the ridge downhill and will now remain in Kosovo for the next few days. Along the way, we meet a woman hiking in the opposite direction with two mountain dogs in tow. She must be carrying a lot of peanut butter.

We pass a memorial dedicated to a poet who is said to have died during the war in this region in 1997. It’s a strange thought that such a beautiful place was the scene of so much suffering and violence not all that long ago.

As we continue hiking, we spot an impressive bird of prey that I can’t identify. We also watch a large flock of birds whose members seem to fly around chaotically, yet somehow move in perfect coordination. In a tree, we discover another striking bird with glossy black-and-white plumage, perched high on a branch and watching us.

We descend and come across another spring where water flows from a pipe. Ever since our water shortage at Prosllopit Pass two days ago, we’ve become a little paranoid about our water supply. We each drink an entire liter before filling all our bottles. We’re not making that mistake again.

After that, it’s more of the same: uphill, downhill, a snack, uphill, downhill. Bushes scratch our legs, and every so often, we have to stop to pull burrs out of our socks. We stay on the border ridge between Montenegro and Kosovo, following it over Zavoj Pass and Jelenka Pass, thereby bypassing the village of Milishevc.

During the final long descent, we overtake three women with an adorable mountain dog. He decides to join us but quickly realizes that our hiking pace apparently doesn’t meet his expectations either. He politely passes both of us and races down the mountain on his own. The trail is steep, muddy, and partially overgrown and then turns into a steep, rocky gravel track, with an alarming amount of trash scattered along its edges. The descent feels endless, and our knees are making their displeasure known. This is followed by another steep forest trail, but at least the leave-covered trail is much softer on the joints. By now, we’re fantasizing about dinner at the campground we’re heading for tonight. Then we spot a sign: only half a mile to go! At last, we reach the campground, just before nightfall.

Heart-shaped lake Liqeni i Lumbardhit before descending to Rugova

We meet Blendi and explain that we’d like to camp and take a shower. But there are no showers. Oh well. What about food? That’s when Blendi utters the two words we least want to hear after such a long day of hiking: “Kitchen closed.” A tragedy. At least we can buy two cans of Fanta from him. The drinks cost three euros, and camping is another five. Blendi shows us around: the outlet where we can charge our power banks overnight, the toilet, and the large grassy area where we can pitch our tent. Simple but functional.

Dinner is pasta with peanut sauce from our own camp kitchen, accompanied by the Fantas. And after giving my knees a bit of rest, they feel normal again, too. All’s well that ends well.

Rugova → Rekë e Allagës → Qafa e Hajles → Drelaj → Dugaivë → Pushimore Tini 14.7 miles / 4,462 ft elevation gain / 6.5 h

Today, we have mostly road walking ahead of us. We start by following the main road for about a mile before the next 2.5 miles take us uphill along a smaller road to Rekë e Allagës. At the intersection, three barking dogs come running toward us. For a moment, I get a little scared, but the three turn out to be friendly. A driver pulls up beside us and offers us a ride with a big smile, but we politely decline. The road winds through a gorge that receives very little sunlight throughout the day, and it’s pretty cold down here.

Along the way, autumn shows itself in all its different forms. Dried-up ferns stand alongside crocus-like flowers, grasshoppers jump through the grass, and the leaves glow in warm fall colors. Less pleasant is the ever-present trash. Along the roadside and in the river, we see beer and soda cans, chip bags, disposable coffee cups, fast-food packaging, plastic bottles, shoes, clothing, and even empty CD cases, paint cans, and oil containers. There seems to be almost nothing that people don’t dump in nature here.

Road walk to Rekë e Allagës with a new dog while we find all sorts of trash along the road, including those empty CD cases

We discover a short detour to a waterfall through a narrow gorge. Of course, we can’t pass that up. At the waterfall, I joke that Ryan could finally get the shower he’s been longing for. He takes me at my word, strips naked and actually stands under the ice-cold water. Our three new canine friends initially follow us, but two of them lose interest halfway and turn back. The third stays with us. He’s a pleasant companion: no barking, no begging, no pushy behavior. He keeps a respectful distance and simply walks alongside us. Even I don’t mind his company, and Ryan is already in love.

Detour to a waterfall along the road walk to Rekë e Allagës

Beyond the small village of Rekë e Allagës, we finally leave the road behind. A steep trail takes us uphill through the forest. The trees provide welcome shade, making the climb quite pleasant despite the gradient. At the edge of the forest, the landscape opens up, and the imposing rock face of Mount Hajla rises above us.

At the highest point, we take a break at a shelter and consider our options. From here, we could make another detour to the summit or continue following the trail. A more relaxed day sounds pretty good right now, so we decide for the latter.

We follow a gravel road downhill and encounter several shepherds with their sheep. We greet them, and they reply with something we don’t understand. So we simply smile, and they smile back. Along the way, we discover tiny red and yellow plums, barely bigger than marbles but incredibly delicious.

Our four-legged companion is still with us and has now been given a name: Peanut, because he shares Ryan’s love of peanut butter. Ryan generously feeds him spoonfuls of it, regularly leaving some stuck in Peanut’s beard. He’s a friendly dog with slightly scruffy fur, and about half of one of his ears is missing, so one ear always stands upright while the other flops down. Along the way, we discover that Peanut has rather different priorities when it comes to drinking water. Clean water doesn’t seem to interest him. Instead, he prefers drinking from the muddiest puddles he can find. Together, we descend through the forest toward Drelaj.

From Drelaj, there’s section that many hikers skip by taking a shuttle or hitchhiking to Hotel Guri i Kuq instead, as it only follows roads for almost eight miles. We decide to walk the whole distance anyway because we want to hike the entire trail. This section isn’t particularly appealing, though, as we’re once again surrounded by large amounts of trash. The road leads through another gorge, where warm stretches alternate with noticeably cold ones.

On the other side of the nearly dried-up riverbed beside the road, we spot a picnic table hidden among dense trees and bushes. Perfect for a lunch break. Peanut gets some peanut butter and a few spoonfuls of our lunch. That seems to satisfy him. Then it’s back to the road, but by now, we’re getting pretty tired of it.

Roadside Picnic. It reminds me of the Strugatsky brothers’ novel of the same name, in which the mysterious objects left behind by visiting aliens are compared to the trash humans might leave behind after a roadside picnic. Looking at all the garbage we’ve passed today, the analogy seems rather fitting.

Our next destination is a small settlement where there are supposed to be vending machines selling drinks at Sun Castle. After all those road miles, we’re looking forward to a soda. But when we finally arrive, we’re initially disappointed: the first machine is out of both. Then, shortly afterward, we discover a second one that’s fully stocked. Here, we also meet Andy from Austria, who’s hiking the Peaks of the Balkans Trail in the opposite direction.

Vending machine with sodas at the Sun Castle

About 0.6 miles farther along, there’s supposed to be a restaurant where camping is possible. Since much of the surrounding land is privately owned, finding a suitable wild camping spot is proving difficult. To reach uninhabited terrain again, we’d have to hike quite a bit farther and then climb uphill once more.

So we decide to call it a day here. Andy shows up again and decides to stay as well for the same reasoning. And the best part: the restaurant even serves dinner. We strike up a conversation with the owner and learn that his sister lives in New Jersey. He likes the United States, and we talk about how expensive life there has become. The owner’s cousin, who cooks for us, speaks German, which is fairly common in this region, largely due to decades of labor migration to Germany, Austria, and Switzerland. During the Yugoslav Wars, many refugees from Kosovo also came to German-speaking countries. He also mentions the Kosovo War. His gaze drifts into the distance, and I silently wonder what traumatic experiences he might associate with it.

While we’re eating, another man enters the restaurant, orders a beer, and starts what appears to be a rather heated discussion with our hosts. We have no idea what they’re arguing about. Eventually, he leaves the restaurant and gets into his car. Ryan wonders whether there are actually laws against drunk driving here. “I’m sure there are,” I reply. “But who’s going to enforce them out here?”

Our hosts offer us beer and some schnapps, but we politely decline, pay our bill, and head toward our campsite. Andy seems a little more receptive to the offer.

Outside, Peanut is already waiting for us, and when he sees us again, he’s absolutely thrilled. Perhaps he was worried we’d left him behind. We pitch our tent on the meadow across from the restaurant, and Peanut settles down on Ryan’s Tyvek groundsheet in front of our tent. Apparently, we have another roommate for the night.

Our campsite at Restaurant Pushimore Tini

Pushimore Tini → Kuqishtë → Hotel Guri i Kuq → Jelenka Pass → Babino Polje → Hridsko Jezero 18.5 miles / 6,135 ft elevation gain / 8.5 h

For the first time, I’m actually so warm in my sleeping bag that I have to take off my down jacket. Still, it’s not a particularly restful night. Peanut wakes us up three times with loud barking because he apparently hears or sees who-knows-what, and the two rather intimidating dogs kept in a kennel nearby enthusiastically join in every time. In the morning, Peanut makes up for it by being extra cuddly.

First, we descend a few more miles to the main road, where we picked up Peanut yesterday. On the other side, the next climb begins immediately, initially on pavement toward Hotel Guri i Kuq. Along the way, Peanut wanders off to explore his surroundings as usual, and this time he doesn’t come back. Looks like our time hiking together has come to an end.

At the hotel, I treat myself to a Coke I bought from the vending machine yesterday and saved for today. Two other hikers are just leaving their accommodation to start their day. A little later, we strike up a conversation on the trail. They’re from Israel and are also hiking the Peaks of the Balkans Trail. However, they started their loop in Plav, Montenegro, so they’re already nearing the end of their hike.

We talk about all sorts of things, from hiking and food to the wars in the region. What strikes me most is how casually they mention that rockets occasionally land near their home and that they have to seek shelter. I suppose even such extraordinary circumstances can become a kind of normality when you’ve lived with them long enough.

The trail climbs steeply, and I’m sweating more than on any previous day of the Peaks of the Balkans Trail. It’s not even hot today. It’s overcast, and the temperatures are rather cool, but the air is incredibly humid. Bad weather is forecast for tomorrow, possibly including thunderstorms, and it’s already clear that something is brewing.

We reach a green mountain lake and stop for a short break. I take the opportunity to wash out our cooking pot, but as soon as we stop moving, we get cold, so we soon continue hiking.

Our next destination is Jelenka Pass, which we already crossed from the opposite direction two days ago. Back then, we could admire the surrounding mountain scenery in all its glory. Today, however, the peaks disappear into thick clouds. I’m glad we at least got to enjoy the views the first time around.

As we reach the forest, it starts raining. At least our timing is good, as the trees provide some protection. I remember a shelter at Zavoj Pass and hope we can have lunch there. But when we arrive, an unpleasantly cold wind whistles through the open structure, blowing the rain straight inside. So much for shelter. We continue downhill toward Babino Polje, crossing the border into Montenegro once again.

By now, we’re engulfed in clouds and can barely see 20 yards in front of us. At a junction, we accidentally take the wrong turn but notice our mistake in time and head back. We continue descending until we finally emerge below the cloud cover and can make out the first houses of Babino Polje beneath us.

Shortly afterward, we take another wrong turn. This time, however, we don’t realize our mistake until we’ve already descended quite a way. Climbing all the way back up the steep trail doesn’t sound particularly appealing, so we decide to take a detour instead. But there’s a silver lining: along the way, we discover a fire salamander, black with bright yellow spots. I’ve never seen one before, so I’m delighted by the encounter. That alone makes the detour worthwhile.

We reach a river, which we cross before a short, steep, and rather overgrown trail takes us up to the gravel road leading into Babino Polje. At the first guesthouse we pass, a young dog comes running toward us and promptly decides to join us. This time, it’s a cream-colored female with long legs and oversized paws that she clearly still needs to grow into. Occasionally, she even trips over her own feet. Pretty adorable.

It continues to rain on and off, but we’re mostly walking beneath trees, so I don’t even have to get my rain jacket out. Our original plan is to spend the night at Samel’s Guesthouse above the small settlement. We’ve heard good things about it and want to treat ourselves given the poor weather forecast for tomorrow. Besides, we’re long overdue for a shower.

So we continue uphill along the gently climbing gravel road. Our new companion is still with us. Even when we encounter a group of people who apparently know her and greet her enthusiastically, she sticks with us. I try to explain to her that there’s nothing for her where we’re going. She’d probably have a better chance of finding food and a dry place to sleep in the village. But my arguments don’t seem to impress her.

When we finally reach the guesthouse, I’m thrilled. The view over the autumn-colored valley, with clouds hanging above it, is beautiful. On top of that, two adorable cats, one calico and one black, greet me with purring. I could happily stay here, but it turns out that the guesthouse is closed today.

View from Samel’s Guesthouse, that also has kittens

Since this is the last accommodation in Babino Polje, we have to rethink our plans. We would rather not turn around, and the weather is still holding up reasonably well, so we decide to continue uphill to a lake and camp there. Tomorrow, we could hike a shorter stage to Plav and get a room. Most of the remaining route follows forest trails and gravel roads, which should be manageable even in bad weather.

So we shoulder our backpacks again and follow the road uphill. Along the way, several rather large dogs bark at us, and our little companion doesn’t seem comfortable around them either. But she bravely sticks with us. The road turns into a forest trail that leads us to Hridsko Jezero. Here, we find a suitable spot and pitch our tent.

We’ve barely finished setting up when it starts raining heavily. We let our new companion take shelter in the vestibule. Ryan has made her a bed out of his Tyvek groundsheet. At first, she’s a little skeptical and doesn’t seem to know what to make of this unfamiliar luxury. She prefers lying directly on the ground instead. Fair enough. Ryan takes such good care of her. It’s remarkable how quickly he becomes attached to these dogs and how much he cares about their well-being. The mountain dogs’ strategy of attaching themselves to passing hikers certainly seems to work exceptionally well on him.

Throughout the night, heavy rain keeps coming and going, and our four-legged friend now gratefully accepts a nest made from Ryan’s Tyvek and rain jacket. A mild thunderstorm also passes overhead, but we’re all staying dry.

On the way to Hridsko Jezero, where our new friend stays in our vestibule

Hridsko Jezero → Katun Treskavička → Plav 9.0 miles / 525 ft elevation gain / 4 h

Our new companion has now been given a name: Tortilla. We seem to name our dogs after whatever food they remind us of. Come to think of it, that’s how quite a few hikers get their trail names, too. In any case, she behaved impeccably during the night. Apart from some occasional rustling and pattering around our tent, she spent most of the time sleeping.

Rain gently taps against our tent, wisps of fog drift across the lake, and the entire landscape is bathed in a rich, wet green. But we only have a short hike ahead of us today. First, we have a little more climbing to do. The trail takes us across some large boulders that we have to maneuver between, then through the forest to a saddle. There, the landscape opens up, and as the clouds slowly begin to clear, the surrounding peaks come into view.

From here on, it’s practically all downhill. As soon as I get cell service, I book us a place to stay in Plav. For two of us, that is.

Meanwhile, the weather is turning out better than expected. The rain stops, and the sun even makes an occasional appearance. Along the way, we pass a sheep pen with an enormous livestock guardian dog chained up beside it. He clearly takes his job very seriously and makes it perfectly clear that we should stay away from his sheep. When we take a closer look, we notice how much he resembles Tortilla. Same coat color, similar build, just considerably bigger. Apparently, we’re getting a glimpse of what our little companion might look like when she’s fully grown.

Fall Colors on the Peaks of the Balkans Trail

A little farther along, we pass a guesthouse with cheerful pop music blasting from inside. A woman spots us and enthusiastically calls out for us to come in. There’s cake! Naturally, we don’t need to be asked twice. We sit down on the terrace and are served several slices of homemade cake, along with delicious jam made from seasonal fruit. There’s also Fanta and coffee. As we eat, we enjoy the view and the few rays of sunshine that have managed to break through the clouds.

Our hostess is extremely talkative and visibly delighted by our visit. We’re her first guests of the day. She enthusiastically shows us photos of her family and tells us all sorts of things, although we only understand a fraction of what she’s saying. She barely speaks English, and we don’t speak Montenegrin, but that doesn’t seem to bother anyone.

We continue our descent, and soon we get our first glimpse of Plav and its lake. But as soon as we reach the first houses, the peace and quiet come to an abrupt end. There’s a market taking place along the main road into town today. People crowd between cars and market stalls everywhere. All kinds of goods are spread out along the sidewalks, vehicles inch through the crowds with their horns blaring, and the air is thick with exhaust fumes. And there are more dogs.

After the peaceful days in the mountains, it’s quite a contrast. Tortilla seems just as overwhelmed by the commotion as we are. I switch to my tried-and-tested strategy: get through the chaos and out of it as quickly as possible. In the process, we lose sight of Tortilla, but we wouldn’t have been able to take her into our accommodation anyway.

We find our accommodation on one of the quieter streets. As is often the case, it’s a private house with no obvious signage, but the owner spots us from his barbershop and greets us warmly.

Finally, we can take a shower, which is desperately needed after a week of hiking. Then we head out in search of food. By now, the streets have become a little quieter, although there are still plenty of cars and constant honking.

First, we resupply at the local supermarkets. Plav is the only town along the Peaks of the Balkans Trail with supermarkets. Along the way, we encounter cats and even more dogs. Afterward, we head to a restaurant that caught our attention because of its stuffed cabbage rolls. They’re excellent, and our waiter is very friendly, too.

And then we spot Tortilla again! Apparently, someone has given her some leftovers. By now, she seems perfectly at ease again and has settled back into life as a street dog. We buy her two cans of dog food, which she devours enthusiastically. Sadly, we have to leave her outside our accommodation afterward. She whines as we go inside, which doesn’t make things any easier, but we simply can’t take her with us.

We spend the rest of the afternoon at our accommodation. We have our laundry washed, hang it up to dry, and enjoy not having to walk for a change. In the evening, we head out again to pick up some ćevapi sandwiches. And who’s still sitting outside our accommodation? Tortilla. She’s absolutely thrilled to see us again and immediately accompanies us to the restaurant, where we’re once again served by very friendly staff. While we wait inside for our food, she watches us through the window from outside. Naturally, we can’t resist buying her another can of dog food.

Mosque in Plav & Tortilla waiting for us in front of the Ćevapi place

Plav → Bajrak → Vrh Bora → Katun Grlata 9.8 miles / 3,937 ft elevation gain / 4.5 h

In the morning, we’re still undecided whether to spend another night in Plav or hit the trail again. We’d originally planned to wait out the forecast bad weather in town. But when we look out the window, we see blue skies, and our desire to hike wins. We decide to set off and see how far we get. After all, we’re mountain people, not city people.

Before leaving, we look around for Tortilla one last time. But our little companion is nowhere to be seen. Hopefully, she’s found some other hikers to tag along with.

The first six miles take us gradually uphill along paved and gravel roads. It’s not particularly exciting, but we’re rewarded with beautiful views of Plav, the lake, and the surrounding mountains. There are benches along the road every so often, inviting us to take a break. We don’t need much convincing, so we sit down, and I treat myself to a few sips of the Coke I brought from Plav.

Fall colors on the way from Plav to the summit of Bajrak

Less pleasant is the smell of my freshly washed shirt. Apparently, our laundry was washed with an especially heavily scented detergent. The overpowering fragrance reaches my nose with every step and is becoming increasingly irritating.

We follow a narrow trail through the forest. The trees glow in vibrant shades of red and yellow. We reach the open pastures of Katun Paljivukaj, where there’s an empty sheep pen, a closed café, and a wooden outhouse labeled as a toilet, built directly over a water source.

Beyond the pasture, a steep climb leads to a saddle, and the subsequent ascent to the summit of Bajrak is even steeper, leaving me huffing and puffing. At the top, we look down toward Plav and its lake, while on the other, the rugged, jagged peaks of the Grbaja Mountains rise above us.

Steep climb to Bajrak, Plavsko Jezero in the back

Although the sun is shining, an unpleasantly cold wind is blowing. So we retreat a little toward some trees, where we can sit more sheltered from the wind and finish off yesterday’s leftover ćevapi sandwiches. We also brought something from a bakery in Plav that looks like a stack of small pancakes. We have no idea what exactly it is, but it tastes excellent. It would probably be even better with some Nutella or jam.

We continue along the ridge toward Vrh Bora, enjoying a view back toward the mountains we hiked through a few days ago. We can clearly make out Prosllopit Pass and Zla Kolata where we’ve been on our second day but had to skip the climb to Montenegro’s highest peak because we were running low on water. Tomorrow, we plan to give it another try, weather permitting.

By now, more and more dark clouds are gathering. A few rays of sunlight break through, bathing the rugged peaks in dramatic light. The landscape looks beautiful but also a little threatening. For now, it stays dry, but the cold wind reminds us that the weather could change at any moment. In any case, we’re glad we decided to keep hiking this morning. Even if a proper storm is still heading our way, the views up here have already made it worthwhile.

We follow the ridge downhill and soon reach the junction where the detour to Zla Kolata begins. And right next to it, we discover a perfect campsite. The ground is flat, a few rocks provide shelter from the wind, a water source is nearby, and the views are fantastic. All boxes are checked.

Directly in front of us rises the north face of Zla Kolata. If the weather gives us a long enough window tomorrow, we finally want to climb to its summit.

We settle in, and we’ve barely crawled into our sleeping bags when it starts raining, soon turning into a heavy downpour, accompanied by gusts of wind that give our tent a good shake. For evenings like this, we have an entertainment program: movie night in the tent, eating chips and sour gummy worms.

Katun Grlata → Prosllopit Pass → Zla Kolata → Prosllopit Pass → Vusanje 9.5 miles / 2,625 ft elevation gain / 5.5 h

It’s been raining all night, but in the morning, we actually spot a few patches of blue sky between the clouds. Maybe they’ll clear by the time we reach the pass.

Things still look quite promising in the morning

We leave our tent standing and take only the essentials for our summit climb to Zla Kolata. First, the trail takes us to the Ice Cave, which we take a closer look at this time. A perfect little shelter where we stop for a longer break, eat a salami and cheese sandwich, and wait for the clouds to break. We even try a little sun dance, and for a moment, it actually seems to get a bit brighter. Maybe it works?

The “Ice Cave” without ice provides perfect shelter

Unfortunately not. Instead, it starts raining. Since we’re getting cold sitting around, we decide to keep going anyway. The climb from the pass is very steep. By now, it’s raining heavily, and the clouds completely obscure the views. I seriously wonder what the point of rain gear is if everything ends up wet anyway. Even our supposedly waterproof gloves give up.

At least the rocks provide surprisingly good traction. Although they’re wet and look quite slippery in places, our shoes grip them well. The dirt sections are more problematic, having turned into slippery mud by now.

We slowly work our way uphill. The trail is varied and repeatedly demands our full attention. In good weather, this would probably be a fantastic mountain hike. Today, we can only imagine how impressive the surrounding landscape must be.

Eventually, we reach the summit of Zla Kolata, but the view consists of a 360-degree panorama of gray. I pull out the Coke I’ve been saving for this moment and drink it on the highest point in Montenegro. Ryan already finished his yesterday, before we’d even properly left Plav. Impulse control isn’t exactly one of his strong suits.

Summit of Zla Kolata, highest mountain of Montenegro, in the rain

We don’t stay on the summit for long, as it’s far too wet and uncomfortable. So we start making our way back down. We’ve achieved our goal, and perhaps this hike will be particularly memorable precisely because of the miserable weather. Type 2 fun, I’d say.

The descent goes smoothly. The rocks continue to provide good traction, and as long as we’re careful on the muddy sections, we make good progress. Shortly before reaching our tent, it even stops raining.

We encounter a group of four hikers who are preparing hot drinks on their camp stoves. We strike up a conversation and exchange stories about our experiences on the Peaks of the Balkans Trail so far. They’ve also fallen in love with some of the many mountain dogs along the way and had to say goodbye to them again. Clearly, we’re not the only ones who’ve grown attached to these dogs.

We retreat into our tent and peel off our wet clothes. We make some soup and settle into our sleeping bags while it starts raining again outside. However, the dampness is hard to ignore. Even my down jacket has gotten soaked, despite being stored in a Nylofume bag.

Now we have a decision to make: Should we stay here or descend all the way to Vusanje? A warm guesthouse room, a hot shower, and a chance to dry our gear sound tempting. But getting there means leaving our sleeping bags, putting our wet clothes back on, packing up our dripping tent, and hiking several more miles through the rain. Needless to say, our motivation is rather limited. Still, we decide to keep going. Drying our gear before the next stage is worth the effort.

Putting our wet clothes back on is every bit as unpleasant as expected. My cold, soggy socks are the worst, but I only have one dry pair, and I’m saving those. I’ve kept my damp upper layers on, hoping they’ll dry from my body heat, while my leggings have stayed mostly dry under my rain pants. Ryan isn’t so lucky: he has to pull his wet shirt back on. We stuff our wet tent into our backpacks and hope we’ll find a room in Vusanje. Having to pitch it again tonight would be even worse than staying here.

The descent is surprisingly easy because the ground barely absorbs any of the rainwater. Instead, most of it flows straight down the trail without making it slippery. But soon, the rain picks up again. We trudge downhill with water streaming off us. At first, I can pull myself together, but I’m cold, wet, and increasingly grumpy.

To make matters worse, I desperately need to pee. But that would mean stopping, taking off my backpack, pulling down my rain pants, and exposing my bare butt to the pouring rain. Sometimes, female anatomy is really inconvenient when hiking. So I keep walking and complain extensively about my situation.

We try to distract ourselves with the alphabet game. We go through different categories and come up with a word for each letter. It works well enough that we manage to complete two entire rounds before reaching Vusanje.

When we finally arrive in Vusanje, we head straight for the first guesthouse we come across. And sure enough, they have a room available. For 40 euros per person, we get a night’s accommodation, including dinner and breakfast. There’s even a private bathroom. Yes, please! A boy named Edin, who’s perhaps twelve years old and speaks good English, handles the arrangements and shows us to our room.

As soon as we’re alone, the great drying operation begins. We peel off our wet clothes and spread all our gear around the room. Clothes, shoes, tent, and even the money in our wallets – everything gets hung up wherever we can find space. Within minutes, the room looks like a crossover between a laundromat and a gear storage facility. We turn up the heat and take turns disappearing into the hot shower. What a feeling. All is right with the world again.

Spreading everything out to dry in our guesthouse room

A little later, Edin knocks on our door to let us know that dinner is ready. We head over to the main house and stand around somewhat uncertainly until his mother leads us into the living room. Practically the entire family is gathered there, several generations sitting on the couches. Two televisions are playing at the same time, while most people are looking at their phones. We sit down at the dining table, right next to all the family activity. It feels a little strange.

Then the food arrives. And there’s plenty of it. We’re served a rice dish, cucumber salad, large fluffy bread rolls, spaghetti, pieces of meat, pickled stuffed peppers, and cheese. While we’re eating, popcorn is prepared in a pot on the stove nearby, and everyone gets some. The grandfather asks us where we’re from, but communicating is a little difficult because of the language barrier.

To finish, we’re served tea in tiny glasses. There are two teapots on the table: one containing strong tea and the other hot water, so everyone can dilute their tea to taste. We thank the family and wish them “Laku noć” – good night.

Vusanje → Vusanjski Zastan → Liqenet e Jezercës → Cirque Below Jezerca 8.5 miles / 3,707 ft elevation gain / 4.5 h

We’re taking our time getting started today since we don’t have a particularly long hike ahead of us. Spending the night at the guesthouse was definitely worth it: Most of our gear is dry again.

For breakfast, we head back to the main house. This time, only the two older family members are sitting in the living room. On the table, there are scrambled eggs, noodle soup, and bread rolls with various spreads, along with tea.

Outside, a beautiful morning awaits us. Wisps of cloud swirl between the mountains, while blue skies and sunshine stretch above them. After yesterday’s constant rain, it’s a very welcome sight.

First, we want to take a look at Grla Waterfall. Although we can clearly hear it, we can’t see it. Nearby, a group of hikers is sitting in a pavilion, spreading out their wet gear to dry. I remember passing their tents in the pouring rain yesterday evening. Apparently, they spent the night there and are now enjoying a leisurely breakfast. Meanwhile, a cat is trying to get some of their food, and of course, I want to pet it. So I walk over, greet the hikers, and chat with them for a while. Normally, I’m not a big fan of small talk, but when there’s a cat involved, I’m happy to make an exception. Both the cat and the conversation turn out to be pleasant.

Afterward, we follow a gravel road out of Vusanje and into the Ropojana Valley. The valley was shaped by a massive Ice Age glacier that once stretched from the Jezerca massif all the way to Vusanje. At around 7.5 miles long, it was one of the largest glaciers in the region.

A small trail branches off toward another attraction. We follow the narrow path down to the stream and reach Oko Skakavice, a karst spring whose water shimmers in an almost unreal shade of blue in a small pool. The water is so clear that we can see all the way to the bottom. Together with the vibrant fall colors of the surrounding trees, it’s a beautiful sight.

Here, we meet a Dutch family with two young children. They’ve been traveling since mid-August, exploring the region by car and camping in a tent. Quite an adventure with two little kids, I think.

A little later, we leave the actual Peaks of the Balkans Trail. If we continued following it, we could finish our loop in Theth today. But we have something else in mind: we want to climb Jezerca (8,839 ft), the highest peak in the Albanian Alps. Having already summited Gjeravica, the highest mountain in Kosovo, and Zla Kolata, the highest mountain in Montenegro, Jezerca is supposed to complete our little collection of summits. However, it’s considered the most challenging of the three climbs, and stable weather is crucial.

First, the trail climbs more steeply through a beautiful forest. Moss-covered rocks lie scattered among the trees, whose leaves glow in vibrant fall colors. The ground is covered in fallen leaves that pleasantly soften our footsteps.

Then the forest thins out, we cross the border back into Albania, and suddenly find ourselves facing an enormous rocky amphitheater. Small mountain lakes lie below us, while the mighty Jezerca massif rises ahead. Although its summit is hidden in the clouds, the steep rock faces of Maja e Bojës and Maja e Kolajet tower to our right. Every so often, the clouds part to reveal their rugged peaks. We also spot our first chamois of the hike.

Liqenet e Jezercës on the way to the highest mountain of the Albanian Alps

The trail continues across a glacial moraine to a spring feeding a small lake. Here, we refill our water supplies. While doing so, we discover tiny alpine newts. The little creatures look like miniature versions of their adult counterparts.

The final section takes us uphill along a mountainside toward the cirque on the northern side of Jezerca. Here, too, we repeatedly encounter dark gray, marbled alpine newts with bright orange bellies. We really have to watch where we step because there are so many of these little creatures on the trail.

Animal encounters: baby alpine newt and chamois

On a rocky ledge, we find a spot flat enough for our tent. Jezerca rises above us, its summit still shrouded in clouds. Tomorrow, we plan to attempt the climb and hope the weather cooperates.

Cirque Below Jezerca → Leqeni i Pejës 4.5 miles / 1,214 ft elevation gain / 2.5 h

When we wake up in the morning, we’re engulfed in clouds. There’s absolutely nothing to see of the surrounding mountains, and it doesn’t look like that’s going to change anytime soon.

No chance to climb Jezerca on this foggy morning

What those exciting mountaineering documentaries on TV often don’t show is the amount of time spent lying around in a tent, waiting for a suitable weather window. And that’s exactly how we spend our morning today. We decide to give the weather until noon. Maybe the clouds will clear after all and allow us to climb Jezerca.

Until then, we listen to audiobooks, doze off every now and then, and eventually have lunch. But nothing changes outside. Quite the opposite: It even starts snowing lightly. That settles it. We won’t be climbing Jezerca today. Of course, that’s disappointing, but we don’t regret our detour. Yesterday’s magnificent views of the massive rock face and surrounding peaks alone were worth the extra distance. I’d even recommend this alternate to anyone who has no intention of climbing Jezerca at all.

So we pack up our gear and set off. As we climb toward the next pass, we’re greeted by an icy wind. The grass around us is covered in a thin layer of frost, and the landscape looks wintry. Brrr. We’re still completely surrounded by clouds and can barely see more than a hundred feet ahead. Only when we’ve descended to around 5,900 feet does the fog finally begin to lift, revealing views down to the valley floor and the massive rock walls on the opposite side. The wind is still blowing fiercely down here.

Along the way, we meet a Finnish hiker heading in the opposite direction. She’s planning to climb to where we’ve just come from and asks about suitable campsites and water sources. We tell her about our campsite below Jezerca. As hard as it is to believe in this wind, it was completely calm up there because the surrounding mountains provided excellent shelter.

Since she doesn’t yet know how far she’ll make it today, we also give her some of our water. Her original plan was to traverse Jezerca and then descend to Valbona. Maybe she’ll have better luck with the weather tomorrow. At least the forecast promises better visibility, although temperatures are expected to be freezing. With the wind chill, it’s supposed to feel like 14°F up there. We’re already cold enough down here.

We reach the valley floor and begin the climb toward the next and final pass on the Peaks of the Balkans Trail. Along the way, we pass several small concrete bunkers scattered among the rocks and meadows. They date back to the communist dictatorship under Enver Hoxha. Between the late 1960s and the 1980s, he had hundreds of thousands of these bunkers built across Albania. They were intended to defend the country against a possible invasion that never happened.

Shortly before the final pass, we reach Leqeni i Pejës. Near the lake, we find a reasonably sheltered spot for our tent between some large rocks. In this weather, we can hardly hope for a better campsite.

There’s another interesting summit in the area: Maja e Arapit, whose enormous south face towers above us. It drops almost vertically for around 2,625 feet, earning the mountain the nickname “the Matterhorn of Albania” among Western mountaineers. Locals also call it “the Dark One.” A fitting name for a mountain in the so-called Accursed Mountains. Beneath the impressive rock face lies Albania’s longest known cave. It extends for approximately 1.6 miles and features an impressive vertical difference of 1,000 feet at one point. If the weather cooperates tomorrow, we will climb this mountain instead of Jezerca. Plan B, then.

Leqeni i Pejës → Pejë Pass → Theth → Zorzit → Theth 12.2 miles / 3,871 ft elevation gain / 7 h

The planned summit attempt doesn’t work out either. Even as we climb toward the pass in the morning, the icy wind is so strong that I can barely walk in a straight line. The gusts repeatedly throw me off balance. Scrambling over rocks in these conditions would simply be dangerous. I’m reminded of Glacier National Park on the Continental Divide Trail, where I occasionally had to crawl on all fours in extreme winds to avoid being blown over. I really don’t need a repeat of that experience.

I’m very disappointed. I’d more or less come to terms with missing out on Jezerca because we had a promising Plan B with Arapi. But having to skip this summit as well is considerably more frustrating. Still, there’s nothing we can do. Climbing a mountain in this wind is out of the question.

Pejë Pass, last pass before closing the loop of the Peaks of the Balkans in Theth

So we begin our descent. The trail winds down into the valley in seemingly endless switchbacks, where we reach a gravel road that takes us back to Theth. Along the way, we repeatedly encounter larger groups of hikers heading toward the pass. They’re still smiling, but they’re in for a surprise when they discover just how cold and windy it is up there. We even run into Andy again, the Austrian we met a few days ago in Kosovo. With his distinctive elephant pants, he’s hard to miss.

The farther we descend, the more pleasant the weather becomes. Down in the valley, the sun is shining, it’s considerably warmer, and the wind has noticeably weakened. It’s hard to believe that just a few hours ago, we could barely walk in a straight line up on the pass.

Gravel road to Theth with a view back to Arapi

We reach the point where our Peaks of the Balkans loop comes full circle. We did it! To celebrate our successful hike, we sit down at a café and have a Coke. Behind us lie almost two weeks in the mountains of Albania, Kosovo, and Montenegro, countless feet of climbing, lots of dogs, and additional summit climbs.

Then we find a restaurant for lunch. The main course isn’t particularly impressive, but the various appetizers are excellent. I’ve practically filled up on those alone. The service is exceptionally friendly, too.

Of course, there’s animal company here as well: an adorable puppy and two cats. The cats, however, are far less interested in being petted than in our food. When I give them some meat, any remaining restraint disappears. From then on, they sit beside us, loudly demanding more.

While we eat, we discuss what to do with the rest of the day. The weather was too bad for Jezerca, and the wind was too strong for Arapi. But now the sun is shining, and we still have several hours of daylight left. So why not squeeze in another summit after all? We decide to climb Zorzit, which rises above Theth. Plan C, so to speak.

As we leave the restaurant, the little dog joins us. For a while, he happily hikes uphill with us. But then we encounter a group of hikers accompanied by another dog, and our companion spontaneously decides that their company is apparently more interesting. So he joins them instead.

Cat at Restaurant Ujevara e Vogel and a new companion on the way to Zorzit

The trail climbs steeply and seemingly endlessly, and I begin to wonder why we’re putting ourselves through this after the past twelve days. But of course, we keep climbing anyway.

Finally, we reach the summit of Zorzit. And this time, there’s actually a view! The low sun bathes the surrounding mountains in warm, golden light. After all the clouds and rain of the past few days, it’s a perfect finale to our hike.

It’s cold up here, too, but the wind is nowhere near as strong as it was on the pass this morning. We enjoy the views before starting our descent, which is supposed to be even steeper on this side than our climb. Hard to imagine, but we actually lose more than 2,950 feet of elevation in just 1.9 miles. By now, the sun is already getting quite low, and we need to hurry if we want to reach Theth before dark.

We make it back to Theth in time. However, we haven’t booked any accommodation yet. After all the decisions we’ve had to make over the past few days, I simply haven’t felt like sorting through the various guesthouses today.

So we stand around somewhat undecided and ask at the first guesthouse we come across. And sure enough, they have a room available. Unfortunately, it turns out there’s no heating, and by now, it’s gotten pretty cold outside. After a hot shower, the unheated room feels even colder.

For dinner, we walk a few houses down the now-dark street. We order sausages, cornbread in yogurt, a creamy vegetable soup, and potatoes in melted cheese. Everything is warm, hearty, and exactly what we need after such a long day. Afterward, we return to our cold room and crawl under the blankets.

We still have two days left, which we spend exploring the area around Theth. On our first day hike, we visit Grunas Waterfall and the famous Blue Eye, where turquoise water cascades into a crystal-clear pool surrounded by steep rock walls. It’s a beautiful place but also a popular tourist attraction, and we’re far from alone.

The following day, we head to Fusha e Dënellit, a remote alpine valley surrounded by towering cliffs. Along the way, an elderly woman shows us the trail and gives us some of the sweetest grapes I’ve ever tasted. Unlike at the Blue Eye, we barely encounter another soul up here. Well, except for three dogs who decide to accompany us for a while. Apparently, even after twelve days on the trail, we still haven’t run out of four-legged hiking companions.

The official Peaks of the Balkans loop is around 118 miles long, but with all our detours and summit climbs, we end up covering 143 miles. Add another 20 miles of day hikes around Theth, and our total comes to 163 miles. And that’s exactly the kind of hiking I enjoy most: not necessarily following a predetermined route, but constantly looking for interesting alternates and summits to explore along the way.

When we leave Theth by bus two days later, the road takes us high into the mountains one last time. The peaks that have become so familiar to us over the past two weeks glow in the warm light of the low sun. I look out the window and feel a little sad to be leaving this beautiful corner of the world. I could easily stay here much longer and just keep hiking.
