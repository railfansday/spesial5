Footwear plays an outsized role in your backpacking trip. It’s one of the most important decisions that you’ll make as a backpacker when selecting your equipment, and just honestly, it’s really difficult to enjoy yourself on a backpacking trip if every step hurts or feels uncomfortable.

And it doesn’t help that the industry also uses a lot of technical jargon to describe their shoes. It can be pretty overwhelming if you’re not familiar with how shoes are constructed and the different features of them.

Now, interestingly, when we talk about backpacking shoes, there is really no consideration in the industry of what the optimal application is of a backpacking shoe.

If you look at backpackers who are on the cutting edge of backpacking, what you’ll see is that they don’t wear boots. The footwear that they choose isn’t actually designed for backpacking. What I want to emphasize to you when you’re looking at shoes, first, is that you want to be looking at what the optimal application of a particular shoe is.

Before we talk about how to pick a shoe for the right conditions, let’s actually talk about the parts of the shoe.

Let’s start with the outsole. This is going to be on the bottom of the shoe. It’s where literally the rubber meets the road.

It is going to be typically made out of some kind of rubber compound. There is a trade-off between sticky rubber and durable rubber. So just be aware of that trade-off when you are looking at shoes.

The other thing to be looking at with your footwear is going to be the lugs, including the lug pattern, the lug depth, and also the lug volume.

Generally speaking, for softer surfaces like mud or sand or snow, you want lugs that are taller and that have shorter edges to them.

And then the opposite is true, too. If you’re going to be walking on flat benched trails, your lug pattern doesn’t need to be as aggressive.

The next part of the shoe I want to talk about is the midsole. This is going to be made out of foam.

And when you’re thinking about the midsole, you want to be paying attention to the height of the midsole. And you sometimes might find the specs called stack height. So you might see like 30 millimeters on the heel and, say, 22 millimeters at the front.

The taller the shoe, the more wobbly it wants to be. However, the taller the shoe, the more cushioning you’re going to have. So that itself is a little bit of a trade-off.

Another thing you want to be thinking about with the midsole is just the firmness of this midsole.

This midsole here, this is a HOKA shoe. This is very plush, forgiving, soft, versus the midsole foam that’s used in this La Sportiva.

This is pretty firm. And that results in this shoe being more comfortable to wear and absorbing more of the ground, for example, like when you step on a rock.

However, this shoe gives you better ground feel. Your feet definitely feel more connected to the ground, more sensitive when you’re wearing this.

The third part of the shoe to be looking at is going to be the upper.

The upper, more than probably any other part of the shoe, is responsible for how this shoe fits you, so particularly its shape and its patterning.

It’s going to be made out of fabric and perhaps some faux leather.

The things to be looking at with the upper of a shoe are whether it’s breathable or whether it’s described as waterproof.

For some environments, you also want to be considering whether it’s sand-resistant.

And then the last thing is you want to be thinking about what the expected durability is of the upper.

In my experience, the upper of a shoe is typically the thing that blows out first. It develops holes. Sometimes if you snag it bad enough, a whole section of it can rip. Sometimes fabrics just kind of slowly fray apart.

So this is the first part of the shoe that blows out. You really want to give some consideration to this part of the shoe if you need to be using it for a very long distance.

So now that we’ve talked about the parts of the shoe, let’s actually talk about how different design decisions with each of these shoes make them optimized for particular environments.

The shoe that you choose for, say, the Appalachian Trail may not be the best shoe for, say, hiking in southern Utah. And the shoe for hiking in Utah might not be the best shoe for, say, hiking in the High Sierra or for Alaska.

So let’s talk about specific conditions that affect footwear choice.

Are you going to be hiking on compacted dirt or, like, coarsely ground mineral sand? Are you going to be hiking on slippery roots, rocks, and mud? Are you going to be walking on sharp talus and scree? Are you going to be walking through loose blow sand?

If you were planning a trail hike that was mostly on compacted dirt or ground-up mineral sand, such as what you might find on the East Coast or in the northern woodlands or, say, in the High Sierra, a shoe like this without a particularly aggressive outsole would be just fine.

However, if you are planning a trip where there’s going to be slippery rocks, roots, mud, slippery tundra, having an outsole with a more aggressive lug pattern would be better, something like this.

Or if you’re going to be, say, hiking on a bunch of slickrock or doing scrambling, you might want to be using some kind of shoe with an outsole like this where there’s flatter sections of rubber that give you more of an approach-style shoe with a lot of surface area that’s going to give you extra traction on those surfaces.

The next condition that you should be thinking about is going to be the typical surface angle of whatever you’re walking on.

So if you are walking on, say, a really high-quality benched trail, I’m thinking of something like the John Muir Trail, which is, even though it’s going up a lot, up and down a lot, it’s often switchbacking, and they have benched the trail into the mountains, so the trail itself is actually pretty flat.

In that case, a shoe, something like this, that kind of has a wide last on it, is going to be great because this is going to translate to more comfort.

It’s going to be a looser-fitting shoe. It’s going to give your foot places to move around, and you’re not going to be as prone to getting pinched or any constrictions in your shoe.

Of course, the classic example, and I don’t have a pair with me because I don’t wear them, would be the Altra Lone Peaks.

And for me, personally, it feels like I’m wearing clown shoes, but I totally get why they’re so popular among long-distance hikers who are on those really high-quality trails.

If you go to the other side of the spectrum, imagine you’re hiking off-trail in steep mountain country. It behooves you to have a shoe with a much narrower last.

This is the La Sportiva Bushido. There are a lot of people out there who cannot wear these because their feet just aren’t narrow enough, or they’re not willing to deal with the discomfort of having their foot kind of wedged into a shoe.

This is a much more appropriate shoe for a high route or for early-season conditions when you want that lateral stability of your shoe.

To take it to an even more extreme example, this is the La Sportiva TX Guide, and this is even a narrower last than the Bushido, which limits its application for, or suitability for, longer days on the trail, or just longer days in general, which makes it really excel in terrain that tends to be highly technical.

So we’ve talked about the walking surface. We’ve talked about the surface angle.

The last consideration that affects the suitability of footwear would just be a number of different environmental factors.

If you’re going to be in an area with a lot of spring runoff, so I’m thinking of, say, anywhere, like any mountain environment in the springtime, there tends to be high runoff of creeks. In that case, you’re going to be looking for a breathable shoe, not a waterproof shoe. Another environmental factor would be spring snow.

So if you’re going to be up in the mountains, say, in the West, if you’re going to be there in May or early June, you’re going to want a shoe with an aggressive outsole that’ll give you good bite and traction on that snow.

Another environmental consideration would be loose sand.

So I’m specifically thinking right now about the Four Corners of the United States or the western U.S. and places like southern Utah, which have that really fine, powdery blow sand.

And then the last environmental factor that I would throw out there would be scratchy brush or vegetation.

This is gonna be more of an issue if you’re hiking off-trail, but anytime you’re walking through brush, so that might be willow or manzanita or chaparral, that is really hard on the uppers of the shoes.

So that’s a real argument then for going with a shoe that has a really durable, robust upper, not something that you would necessarily see on a road running shoe that has been given a few extra features to make it suitable for trail running.

So let’s rabbit hole for a few minutes about this discussion of waterproof or breathable shoes.

So-called waterproof shoes, they’re, it’s generous.

I would also argue that waterproof shoes don’t end up being waterproof long-term. Just the torsion that you put on a shoe from normal wear and tear breaks down that waterproof-breathable fabric.

The other thing that happens with waterproof shoes is that your feet get wet from the inside because of your perspiration.

So my recommendation to everyone has been my recommendation for, boy, going on like 20 years now.

Instead of trying to keep your feet dry in the backcountry, just embrace the fact that they’re going to get wet and learn how to manage wet feet.

Let me give you four ways that you can manage wet feet in the backcountry.

First, you can wear a breathable shoe. A breathable shoe will expel water faster, and it will also dry out faster because more air will be passing through the shoe, not just through the top, but also moving through the fabric in the upper.

Second, when you are taking a break during the day, so at rest stops, remove your shoes and socks. Just five minutes, 10 minutes, 15 minutes of fresh air to your feet can be really nurturing for your feet that otherwise are just squeezed into a wet shoe all day long.

Third, at night you want to have a dedicated pair of sleeping socks, and you want to make sure that your feet get warm and dry at night and that they stay that way.

Fourth, you can bring out a balm. So this is GurneyGoo. This is just one example of many.

And use this balm to recondition your feet at night. The way that you do it is you get to camp, you do all your dinner stuff, you go into your tent, you take off shoes and socks, you let your feet dry out for a while and get them nice and dry, and at that point you put the balm on.

And that’ll basically recondition your skin, and then in the morning the bottoms of your feet will feel a lot more restored than if you had just let them air out and get really dry and crispy over the course of the night.

At the top, I made the argument that your footwear needs to do two things for you.

The first is that it needs to be appropriate for the conditions and optimized for your application. And then the second thing that it needs to do for you is that it needs to fit.

Footwear fit is highly personalized. It’s really unlike any other type of backpacking clothing or anything else that you wear.

So as an example, if I took this shirt, which isn’t a backpacking shirt, but it’s going to convey the idea.

And that’s because fit is very personalized.

And there are a couple of things going on here.

Part of it is the shape of your particular foot, like whether you have, say, a narrow heel and a big toe splay, or maybe something that’s a little bit more ruler-shaped.

Also, what is the volume of your foot? Are your arches high? Are they low?

In addition to the shape of your foot, your gait plays into this as well.

What exactly is that process of lifting your foot in the air and putting it back on the ground? Are you pronating? Are you landing flat? There’s just lots of options in this.

Another consideration with fit is going to be your Achilles, the flexibility and strength of your Achilles tendon.

And that’s going to affect how much tolerance you have for this angle, whether you can do a right angle, or whether, like with a zero drop, you’re actually a little bit more than a right angle, or whether you need something with a little bit of a lift so that it’s a greater-than-90-degree angle.

That just is impacted by, again, the flexibility and strength of your Achilles.

And then finally, the last thing with fit is just personal preference.

Like my tolerance for squeezing my foot into something like that La Sportiva TX Guide might be higher than the next person who just isn’t willing to deal with that constant squeezing tightness for the performance gains that those shoes offer in particular environments.

So how should footwear fit you? What are you looking for?

The first is that when you put it on, out of the box, it should immediately be comfortable. It should feel like someone had a mold of your foot, and they created a shoe just for you. It shouldn’t pinch. It shouldn’t be applying pressure in places where you don’t want it. And you should be able to be very comfortable in the shoe, again, out of the box without aftermarket insoles.

I know a lot of people like their aftermarket insoles. I’ve just always felt that the designers design the shoe for a foot, and the aftermarket insole changes the intended user, and you probably shouldn’t do that. You should probably just go find another shoe.

And then the other criteria for determining whether a shoe fits you really well is you take a walk around the shoe store or around your house and try, if available, to go up and down some things. That way, you can replicate more of a true hiking environment.

And you want to feel like you can walk naturally in that shoe, that your heel-to-toe transition feels really natural, that it’s locking your heel down the way you want it to. It should feel like you’re walking in a slipper in an optimal situation.

So the last topic when it comes to fit is going to be the heel-to-toe drop.

If you have a zero-drop shoe, so this shoe, this is just an Adidas street shoe. This is, I believe, a zero-drop shoe.
