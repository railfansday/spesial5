Millennials want to quit corporate jobs for nonprofits. The problem is pay

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Millennials are increasingly disillusioned with corporate life and considering nonprofits for work. But the difference in pay is often prohibitive

Millennials have spent about 20 years building their careers, and for many of them, their pay has mostly plateaued. Glassdoor senior economist Chris Martin told Fortune in September that millennial pay has "for the most part peaked" and from here will grow only about as fast as inflation until they stop working. Millennials can now tell themselves "we've made it," he said, "and the long and the short of it is, it doesn't feel good." The disappointment has many of them reconsidering the work they're doing. When Glassdoor polled its community this summer, 76% of millennials said they're seriously rethinking the direction their careers have gone.

Many tech workers had no choice but to leave the corporate world this year. The decision was made for them. Andy Challenger, a workplace expert and chief revenue officer at Challenger, Gray & Christmas, an outplacement firm that tracks layoff announcements, has watched job cuts pile up all year. The pace of layoffs slowed almost everywhere else, but tech companies announced about one and a half times as many U.S. job cuts from January through September as they had over the same months of 2025, according to the firm's September report. "Tech remains the center of gravity for this year's cuts, Challenger said, "and AI is still the reason companies give."

The nonprofits that some of those workers might turn to for more meaningful work are laying off staff too. Nonprofits announced more job cuts in September than in any other month this year and about seven times as many as they had in September 2025. For a millennial who wants to trade a corporate job for one of a higher calling, the switch is harder to land and harder to afford. Millennials who want to leave the business world are mostly building backup careers on the side. The ones who can leave a full-time job tend to be the ones with family money or a spouse's paycheck to fall back on.

Millennials want their work to stand for something, and many have turned down an employer over it. In Deloitte Global's 2026 Gen Z and Millennial Survey of adults in 44 countries, taken from November 2025 to January 2026, 97% of millennials said finding purpose in their work matters to their well-being and to how satisfied they are with their jobs. Yet around 60% say their current job doesn't match what they believe. Another 40% say they turned down a potential employer, an assignment, or a project over their personal ethics, and that has held steady in every edition of the survey since 2023.

Many millennials can't afford to go without a paycheck while they look for work that means more to them. Nearly half of the Gen Z and millennial respondents in the same survey said they live paycheck to paycheck, and cost of living was their top worry for the fifth year in a row. "They feel really insecure in their jobs. Many are not in a position to be unemployed or strike out on their own," said Martin, the Glassdoor economist. A worker who can't miss a paycheck can't quit first and look for the next job later.

So the millennials who want change are mostly testing it while they keep the job they have. Glassdoor's latest Millennial Report calls the approach "stability stacking." Workers build up skills, income, contacts, and other job prospects at the same time, and if they lose one job, they still have the rest to fall back on. They learn AI skills, move sideways into a different role, or get known in the field they want to join, all while still drawing a paycheck. "Stability stacking is a response to this moment of, 'I thought it would feel better. I thought I could count on my career and feel stable,'" Martin added.

Kat Clark, a former Apple $AAPL senior manager who started a nonprofit for teachers in 2022, needed a side income and a cheaper state to make the switch work. Clark told Business Insider in 2023 that she joined Apple in 2019 owing more than $100,000 in business-school loans. She paid off all of it before she quit in July 2022, giving up what she called "a couple hundred-thousand dollars per year." The next month she launched Teachers in Their Power, which draws attention to the problems teachers face. Without the Apple salary, she could no longer afford to live in San Francisco, so she moved to Wisconsin and took on part-time work as a marketing executive for hire and as a consultant for education technology startups. "The biggest sacrifice I made was leaving the Bay Area," she said.

Nonprofit leaders say they get plenty of applicants but too few with the skills the job needs. In a fall 2025 survey by PNP Staffing Group and Careers In Nonprofits, leaders said they struggle to find people who know fund accounting — the bookkeeping nonprofits use to track restricted grants and donations — and those who offer bilingual services, work in affordable housing, or hold a clinical license. Some leaders also worry that people are using AI to make fake applications. Going through a big stack of them, those leaders have to sort out not just who's qualified but who's real.

Where a corporate worker's experience does fit — notably in finance roles — nonprofits say they can't pay what the market pays. Low pay was the most common reason hiring was hard in 2025, and it hurt most in fundraising jobs, finance jobs, and jobs that require a license, according to PNP Staffing Group. People are quitting fundraising, finance, and technology jobs at high rates, and nonprofits also take a long time to fill similar roles. At many of them, hiring runs past a month while leaders wait on shifting grant money and approvals. The firms said that's long enough for the best candidates to take other offers.

The nonprofit jobs that come close to corporate pay sit at the top of the biggest organizations, and the jobs below them pay a fraction of that. At the biggest nonprofits in Washington, D.C., those with budgets over $50 million, chief financial officers earned more than four times what bookkeepers did in fall 2025, according to PNP Staffing Group's salary tables. Staff accountants and database managers at nonprofits in New York and D.C. mostly earned well under six figures. Each nonprofit has only one chief financial officer, so most corporate workers who make the switch will land in the jobs below that one, at the pay those jobs offer.

The millennials who make the switch will mostly be the ones who can afford to earn less, as Clark, the former Apple manager, could after she moved to Wisconsin and took on consulting work. When nonprofits that are helping more people with fewer workers hire from the corporate world, those few millennials are who they'll get. Millennials without paid-off loans, a cheaper place to live, or a second income will keep trying out new work on the side while they hold on to corporate jobs they've already started to question.
