From interim CEO to Form 8-K: 10 steps boards take after a sudden CEO exit

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Free daily briefing on global business news.

How companies replace a CEO after a sudden exit, from naming an interim chief executive to running a board search and filing the required disclosures

A sudden CEO exit tests a board's preparation more than its judgment. When a chief executive resigns without warning, dies, falls seriously ill or is dismissed, directors rarely have time to deliberate from scratch. The decisions they make in the first days are shaped by work they did, or failed to do, long before.

The stakes reach well beyond the boardroom. Employees want to know who is in charge and whether their projects will survive. Investors want evidence that the company has a plan and that strategy will not drift. Customers and suppliers watch for signs of disruption. For a U.S. public company, regulators also expect prompt disclosure of the change.

Understanding how companies replace a CEO after a sudden exit means looking at several moving parts at once. Boards must put an accountable leader in place quickly. They must decide whether that person is a placeholder or a contender for the permanent job. They also need a process for finding a successor that staff and shareholders find credible.

The tools available to directors are well established. Many boards keep an emergency succession plan alongside their longer-term CEO succession planning. They can promote a sitting executive or ask a director to step into an executive role. They can also bring in an outside search firm to build a slate of candidates. Each route carries tradeoffs between continuity and change.

The legal and financial side matters too. A departing chief executive usually leaves behind an employment agreement, equity awards and possibly severance terms that the board must review. The interim leader and the eventual successor each need a pay package. Public companies must also manage what they say, when they say it and to whom.

The 10 slides that follow cover a selection of common steps boards take during an emergency CEO succession. They are not a complete or ranked list, and the order is not fixed. In practice, many of these steps run in parallel during the first weeks of a chief executive transition. Each slide explains one step, why boards use it and what it involves.

When a chief executive leaves without warning, the board's first move is usually to name an interim CEO. That appointment gives employees, investors and customers one accountable leader while the search for a permanent successor begins.

A company cannot run for long without someone holding the chief executive's authority. Contracts need signatures and budgets need approval. Managers need a person who can settle disputes and make final calls. Payroll, regulatory obligations and customer commitments do not pause for a leadership change. Naming an interim closes that gap and makes clear who is in charge.

Speed matters outside the company as well. Investors, lenders, customers and suppliers watch a leadership change closely. An announcement that pairs the departure with a named replacement shows that the board has acted. A long silence between the two can invite speculation about why the previous CEO left.

Interim chief executives usually come from one of two places: the board or the senior management team. A director tends to know the company's strategy, finances and governance history. A senior executive brings day-to-day knowledge of operations, staff and customers. The board weighs which of those strengths the business needs most in the moment.

The interim holds the CEO's authority, but the board usually sets the terms of the role through a formal resolution. Those terms cover whom the interim reports to and how long the arrangement is expected to last. Some boards also require their own approval for transactions above a set size.

Some interims state from the start that they will not seek the permanent role. That clarity can let other executives focus on the transition instead of on competing for the top job. Other interims stay open to the position. In those cases, the board may evaluate the interim alongside outside and internal candidates in the formal search.

The title varies from company to company. Some boards use "interim CEO," while others prefer "acting CEO." A few split the job temporarily between two senior executives as co-CEOs. That structure requires the board to spell out which parts of the business each executive oversees and how they resolve disagreements.

Many boards keep a confidential emergency succession plan that names who takes charge if the chief executive leaves without notice. Putting that plan into action lets directors carry out a decision they made in calm conditions, rather than improvising one in a crisis.

An emergency plan usually answers a short list of practical questions. It names the person or people who can step in on short notice. That is often a senior executive or a sitting director. The plan may also say who convenes the board, who speaks for the company and which outside advisers get called first.

This document differs from long-term CEO succession planning. A long-term plan develops candidates over years and prepares for an orderly retirement. An emergency plan deals with a narrower problem: who runs the company tomorrow.

The person named for an emergency is not always the board's preferred permanent successor. Keeping the two roles separate lets the board fill the gap without locking in a long-term choice. It also leaves directors room to run a fuller search later.

Confidentiality is part of the design. Boards generally keep the plan private to avoid signaling who is in line for the top job. A leaked name can unsettle other executives or create expectations the board never meant to set.

A plan works only if it stays current. Executives change roles, get promoted or leave the company. A name on the plan can go stale as a result. Boards review and update these plans periodically, often through the full board or its governance or nominating committee. Directors may also confirm that the named stand-in is still willing and able to serve.

The absence of a plan carries its own cost. Without one, directors must debate who leads while employees, customers and lenders wait for answers. That debate can stretch from hours into days. It also leaves room for internal rivalries to surface during the transition.

After a sudden CEO exit, boards commonly set up a small committee of directors to run the search for a permanent successor. The committee manages the process day to day and brings a recommendation back to the full board, which keeps the final vote.

A full board is too large, and meets too rarely, to run a search on its own. A smaller group can schedule interviews quickly and review candidates in detail. It can also keep sensitive information within a tight circle. Some boards hand the job to an existing nominating and governance committee. Others create an ad hoc committee for this search alone and dissolve it once a new chief executive is in place.

Independent directors usually make up the committee. These are directors who are not company employees and have no material business ties to the company. A lead independent director or a non-executive chair often leads the group. That person typically serves as the main contact for candidates, advisers and the rest of the board.

Membership comes with a conflict rule. A director who wants to be considered for the CEO job generally steps off the committee. The same applies to an interim chief executive who may compete for the permanent role. Keeping candidates off the committee separates the people doing the judging from the people being judged. It also strengthens the board's ability to defend its choice later.

One of the committee's first tasks is to write a candidate profile. This document sets out the experience, skills and leadership qualities the next CEO needs. It reflects the company's strategy, its current challenges and the circumstances of the departure. A profile agreed early gives the committee a fixed standard for comparing candidates.

The committee then sets the process and the timetable. It decides how candidates will be sourced, who interviews them and in what order. It also oversees reference and background checks on finalists. Those checks carry extra weight after an abrupt exit tied to misconduct.

Confidentiality shapes the committee's work. Candidate names, interview notes and deliberations stay inside the committee and its advisers. A leak can cost the company a candidate who is employed elsewhere.

The committee reports to the full board at regular intervals throughout the search. Its decision rights stop short of the appointment itself. It recommends a finalist, and the full board votes to name the new CEO.

Promoting a sitting executive, such as the chief operating officer or chief financial officer, is a common way for a board to fill a sudden CEO vacancy. An insider already knows the business, the people and the numbers. That shortens the time before the company has a working leader.

The appeal starts with familiarity. A COO typically oversees day-to-day operations and already manages many of the teams that report up to the chief executive. A CFO knows the balance sheet, the lenders and the investor base. Either one can step into the top job without a long orientation period.

Existing relationships also count. Customers, suppliers, lenders and major shareholders often already deal with the internal candidate. That prior contact can make the leadership change easier to explain to each of them. Employees may read an internal appointment as a sign that the company's direction is not about to be overturned.

An insider also brings a track record the board has watched directly. Directors have usually seen the executive present at board meetings, defend budgets and respond to problems. That direct view removes some of the guesswork that comes with a candidate the board has met only in interviews.

The tradeoff is change. An executive who helped build the current strategy may be less inclined to question it. If the exit followed poor results or misconduct, a board may judge that an insider is too close to the problem.

Promotion also creates a second vacancy. If the COO becomes chief executive, someone still has to run operations. Companies often fill that seat with an acting appointment from the next management layer. Others split the duties among existing leaders for a period. A company that promotes its CFO must keep financial reporting and internal controls running without a gap. A public company also faces periodic filing deadlines that do not pause for a leadership change.

Readiness is the last test. Running one function is different from running the whole company. A COO may have limited experience facing investors. A CFO may have little exposure to product development or sales. Boards weigh those gaps before deciding. Some pair the new chief executive with an experienced chair or outside adviser. Others set out a defined development plan covering the areas where the executive has less experience.

A board sometimes asks its chair or another director to take on a temporary executive role after a sudden CEO exit. The appeal is speed and familiarity. The cost is that the person meant to supervise management now helps run it.

Directors who step in already know the company's strategy, finances and open risks from board meetings. They have usually worked with the senior team and the largest investors. That background lets them take charge without the orientation an outside hire would need. It also gives the board a leader whose judgment it has already seen up close.

The arrangement takes two main forms. In one, the chair becomes executive chair and takes on operating duties alongside the remaining executives. The CEO seat then stays vacant or goes to an internal officer. In the other, the chair or another director is named interim CEO outright. Both forms place a board member inside management, and both are meant to be temporary.

The tension is built into the board's role. Directors hire, oversee, evaluate and, when necessary, remove the chief executive. A director serving as interim leader ends up overseeing their own work. Questions about performance, pay and strategy become harder for the board to examine at arm's length.

Boards use several safeguards to manage that conflict. A common step is naming a lead independent director, or strengthening the role if one exists. That director runs executive sessions of the non-management directors and acts as a check on the interim leader. Under NYSE and Nasdaq $NDAQ listing standards, audit committee members must be independent. A director who takes on paid executive duties generally steps off the audit committee and often the compensation committee as well.

Time limits are another safeguard. Boards often set a defined term or tie the role to the end of the permanent search. A stated end point tells employees and investors that the arrangement is a bridge, not a new leadership structure. It also keeps a split role from becoming the default.

The board also has to settle whether the director in the executive seat is a candidate for the permanent job. Boards often decide that early. A director who is a contender generally steps away from the search committee's deliberations. A director who is not can say so publicly. That statement tells internal candidates the search is open.

Once a permanent CEO arrives, the director typically returns to a non-executive role. Some boards keep the director on as executive chair for a set period to support the handoff. The board then revisits committee assignments and independence determinations. The specifics depend on the company's bylaws, governance guidelines and listing requirements.

Boards often retain an executive search firm to find and vet candidates for a permanent chief executive. Spencer Stuart, Russell Reynolds Associates, Heidrick & Struggles and Korn Ferry are among the firms that take on this kind of board-level assignment.

The firm works for the board, not for management. It typically reports to the committee of directors that oversees the search. That reporting line matters after a sudden exit, when an interim leader may also be a contender for the permanent job.

A search usually begins with a role specification. The firm and the directors agree on what the next CEO needs to deliver. That brief draws on the company's strategy, its financial position and the circumstances of the previous leader's departure. The resulting document shapes which industries, functions and backgrounds the firm targets.

The firm then maps the market. Consultants identify executives who fit the profile, including sitting CEOs, division heads at larger companies and leaders at competitors. Many of those people are not looking to move. The first approach is therefore typically discreet and framed as a conversation rather than a job offer.

Confidentiality runs in both directions. Candidates employed elsewhere often cannot afford to have their interest become public. The hiring company, for its part, may not want rivals or its own employees to learn who is under consideration before the board decides.

Assessment goes beyond the résumé. Search firms conduct structured interviews and check references. They also examine how a candidate performed in situations similar to the one the company now faces. Some boards ask the firm to evaluate internal candidates as well. That gives directors a common yardstick for comparing insiders and outsiders.

CEO searches are generally run on a retained basis. Under that model, the firm is paid for conducting the search rather than only when a candidate is hired. Fee terms are set in each engagement letter and vary by firm and assignment.

Boards also ask about conflicts. Search firms commonly observe off-limits arrangements that restrict them from recruiting executives out of their own current clients. A large firm's client list can therefore narrow the pool of executives it is able to approach.

The firm's output is a short list of finalists, not a decision. Directors interview the candidates, often over several rounds, and the full board votes on the appointment. The board, not the search firm, remains accountable for the final choice.

The choice between an internal and an external CEO candidate turns on one question: does the company need continuity or a change in direction? No fixed rule settles it. Boards weigh each sudden exit on its own circumstances.

The reason for the departure shapes the answer. A CEO who leaves because of illness or death may leave behind a strategy the board still supports. In that case, a leader who already knows the plan can carry it forward with less disruption.

A dismissal tied to misconduct or weak results points the other way. If the board has lost confidence in the direction of the business, an insider may look too close to the decisions that caused the problem. Directors then have to judge whether a familiar face can credibly signal a break with the past.

Internal candidates come with a known record. Directors have usually watched them present to the board, manage budgets and lead teams. They already understand the company's products, customers and culture. That familiarity lowers the risk of a poor fit. It can also limit fresh thinking.

External candidates offer a different set of tradeoffs. An outsider arrives without loyalty to past strategy or to particular executives. That independence can make it easier to restructure, exit a business line or reset relationships with investors.

The cost is a learning curve. A chief executive hired from outside has to learn the organization, its people and its informal ways of working. Until that happens, acting with confidence on major decisions is harder.

Culture adds another layer to the comparison. A company with a strong internal culture may resist a leader who does not share it. A company whose culture contributed to the exit may need someone willing to challenge it.

The depth of the internal bench also narrows or widens the field. If no executive is ready for the top role, the debate moves quickly toward outside candidates. If several insiders are credible, the board can compare them directly against an external slate rather than choosing one pool at the outset.

Timing is another variable. An insider can usually step into the permanent role faster. A wider external search can take longer. That extends the period the company spends under interim leadership.

A U.S. public company generally must report a CEO departure, and the appointment of an interim or permanent replacement, on SEC Form 8-K under Item 5.02. The standard filing deadline is four business days after the triggering event.

Form 8-K is the Securities and Exchange Commission's current report. Companies file it to tell investors about significant events that occur between quarterly and annual reports. Item 5.02 governs the departure and appointment of directors and certain senior officers. The principal executive officer, usually the CEO, falls within it.

A typical filing states who is leaving, when the change takes effect and who is taking charge. For a newly appointed officer, the disclosure includes background such as age and business experience. It also describes any material compensation arrangement tied to the appointment.

The four-day window is a deadline, not a waiting period. Companies often announce the news by press release on the same day and attach that release to the 8-K as an exhibit. A quick announcement shortens the stretch in which rumors circulate without an official account.

Regulation Fair Disclosure, known as Reg FD, shapes how the news goes out. The rule bars a public company from selectively sharing material nonpublic information with analysts or certain investors ahead of the general public. A sudden change at the top can be material. That pushes companies toward one broad public announcement rather than a round of private calls.

Foreign companies listed in the U.S. as foreign private issuers follow a different route. They generally furnish reports on Form 6-K rather than Form 8-K. Companies without SEC reporting obligations file no 8-K at all. Their boards still decide how and when to inform lenders, investors and staff.

Inside the company, timing is a practical problem. Employees who learn about a leadership change from a headline may question whether anyone is in control. Boards and communications teams therefore tend to schedule an all-staff message close to the public announcement.

That message usually names the person now in charge and explains what happens next. It often confirms that the board has started a search, without committing to a date. Companies generally avoid speculating about the reasons for an exit, especially while lawyers are still reviewing the circumstances.

Customers, suppliers and lenders form a third audience. Sales teams and account managers may need prepared language for their contacts. The board may also need to check whether any financing or commercial agreements require formal notice of a leadership change.

After a sudden CEO exit, the board reviews the departing executive's employment agreement to settle severance, equity awards, clawback rights and any non-compete. It also sets pay for the interim leader and, later, for the permanent successor.

The compensation committee usually leads this work, often with outside legal counsel. Its first task is to establish what the contract actually says. Employment agreements differ from company to company. The signed documents set the baseline, and departing from them usually means negotiating new terms with the former executive.

The reason for the departure shapes almost every term. Chief executive agreements commonly treat a termination "for cause" differently from one "without cause." A resignation for "good reason," as the contract defines it, often carries the same benefits as a dismissal without cause. Death and disability usually have their own provisions. A board that labels an exit incorrectly can invite a legal dispute with the former executive.

Equity awards are often the largest piece of value at stake. Unvested stock options and restricted shares may be forfeited, continue to vest or vest immediately. The outcome depends on the equity plan and the individual award agreements. Performance-based awards can carry separate rules tied to whether targets are met after the executive leaves.

Clawback provisions let a company recover pay that should not have been awarded. In the U.S., stock exchange listing standards adopted under SEC Rule 10D-1 require a clawback policy. That policy must cover erroneously awarded incentive pay after an accounting restatement. Some company policies reach further and cover misconduct.

Restrictive covenants come next. These can include non-compete, non-solicitation and confidentiality clauses. Whether a non-compete can be enforced depends on the jurisdiction. The board's lawyers assess that question for each case.

Many departures end in a negotiated separation agreement. In exchange for severance, the former CEO typically signs a release of legal claims against the company. The agreement may also cover a short transition period, a consulting arrangement or a mutual non-disparagement clause.

The committee then turns to the interim leader. An executive who steps up temporarily may receive added cash pay, a one-time equity grant or both. Those terms reflect the extra duties and the uncertainty of a temporary role. The permanent successor's package is negotiated later, as part of the search itself.

Public companies also face scrutiny of the payout. U.S. proxy statements describe the potential payments named executive officers would receive on termination. Shareholders can compare that disclosure with what the departing CEO actually receives.

During a CEO transition, an interim leader usually keeps strategy steady, holds the senior team together and leaves major long-term bets for the permanent chief executive. Retention arrangements for key executives are a common tool for limiting departures while the search runs.

A sudden exit leaves a gap at the top, and the people just below it often feel the uncertainty first. Senior executives may wonder whether the next CEO will keep them, reshape their roles or bring in a new team. Some may hear from recruiters or competitors during this period. Losing several leaders at once would add to the disruption the board is trying to contain.

The interim CEO's job is partly to answer that uncertainty. An interim leader often meets early with each member of the executive team to clarify reporting lines and decision rights. Those conversations confirm which projects continue and who owns them. That clarity lets managers keep running their units rather than wait for direction.

Retention pay is another lever. A board may offer selected executives cash bonuses or additional equity. These awards pay out only if the person stays through the transition or for a set period after it. The terms vary by company and by individual. The aim is to give critical leaders a concrete reason to stay until the new CEO is in place.

On strategy, interim leaders typically focus on carrying out the existing plan. They tend to defer decisions that would bind the next CEO for years. Examples include large acquisitions, major restructurings or a shift in the company's core direction. Routine operations, approved budgets and customer commitments continue. Postponing the biggest choices preserves options for whoever takes the permanent role.

That caution has limits. A business facing a crisis, a regulatory deadline or a fast-moving competitor cannot simply pause. In those cases the board and the interim leader decide which time-sensitive matters must move forward. The board often stays more involved than usual while those decisions are made.

The final stage is the handoff. Once the permanent CEO is named, the interim leader typically briefs the successor on open issues, pending decisions and the state of the executive team. An interim drawn from management may return to a previous role or leave the company. An interim drawn from the board may go back to serving as a director. The new chief executive then sets the longer-term direction. That includes any changes to the leadership team or strategy that the transition period left open.
