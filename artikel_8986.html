can le corbusier’s last building be disassembled and shipped?

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

some 22,000 screws hold together the pavillon le corbusier's prefabricated steel frames in zurich, yet its concrete ramp, staircase, and legacy make relocation near-impossible.

annalise kamegawa November 24, 2026 9 min

The Pavillon Le Corbusier on Lake Zurich is the last completed building by the late French-Swiss architect. Made of glass and steel, it is his only building to have been constructed in this format, and it has a tricky history of ownership, construction, and maintenance. Having been approached by the gallerist Heidi Weber, who went on to represent his non-architectural projects, the project was made possible by her tenacity, his experimentation, and a fifty-year land lease from the city.

After its opening in 1967, the pavilion enjoyed decades on that grassy plot. When the half-century period came to a close, Weber, who had a contract stating that she could take everything that was allowed to be moved, packed up the lighting, art, seating, and tables. In conversation with designboom, Christian Brändle, director of the Museum für Gestaltung Zürich, which now manages the Pavilion, mentions a popular rumor in the city: ‘There was always a bit of a difficult relationship between Heidi Weber and the city,’ he begins, before elaborating on the gossip that claims ‘she tried to dismantle the building, which is technically not really possible because of [the Pavilion’s] ramp and the staircase is made of concrete.’

In order to claim the building as part of the dispute, it has been said that she tried to take the Pavillon Le Corbusier apart and bring it with her alongside the other interior furnishings. The idea that this was possible comes from the fact that, as Brändle mentions, ‘basically, the building pretends to be prefabricated. Of course, all the frames were screwed together with around 22,000 screws on the building site… but it would be absolutely impossible to keep it as it is and to unscrew it and to rebuild it in another place.’

Here, in this pavilion, Weber gave the architect permission to experiment. Although it is a kind of house with a kitchen, bunker, and unrealized bedroom, it is ultimately a museum for his ideas. ‘He really assembled almost every idea he had as an architect in the pavilion,’ reflects Brändle on what would be Le Corbusier’s final project. ‘And a real key power of modernism has always been that design and architecture should come out of the machine,’ he says, referencing the popular idea of ‘houses as machines for living,’ or ‘une maison est une machine à habiter,’ in French, from his popular 1923 manifesto, Vers une architecture.

Brändle cites the fallout of the First World War and how prefabricated homes became a counterpoint to the overly decorated standards of the time. ‘You would divide your architecture into elements that could be produced somewhere and then you bring it to the building site and assemble it over there.’ He goes on to say that ‘Le Corbusier was, of course, always upfront when it came to innovation,’ and wanted to implement these processes in his architecture, but had passed away before truly realizing the potential of this method. Nevertheless, he got to experiment with some of these techniques in the Pavilion, which becomes evident in the aforementioned thousands of screws and the 226 × 226 × 226 centimeter units used to construct the entire building.

The building opened on July 15, 1967, as the Centre Le Corbusier – Heidi Weber Museum. Brändle, who frequently liaises between the city of Zurich and Weber herself, who is nearly a hundred years old, speaks fondly of her wit and foresight. ‘When she visited an exhibition at the Kunsthaus Zürich, the art museum, in 1957 about Le Corbusier and his oeuvre, she decided that she will devote the rest of her professional life to the spread the ideas of Le Corbusier’s ideas.’ Although it was a grand idea, he was already regarded as a kind of ‘star-chitect’ of the period, making him a tricky man to get hold of.

Brändle recounts her cleverness, saying that she finally got hold of him when a property next to Le Corbusier’s quiet, retreat-like home, Le Cabanon, went up for sale. He remarks that ‘Corbusier was a bit nervous to get a neighbor he wouldn’t like because he was there all day. If he had some clothes, it was just a swimming suit, but nothing else because it was hot and he wanted to paint, sometimes even naked!’ To avoid having new neighbors who could disturb his peace, he went about trying to find a buyer himself. He was connected to Heidi Weber, who had already become a successful gallerist, with the idea that perhaps her clients would be interested in the villa.

In the end, Weber went to visit this Mediterranean property under the guise of helping to find a buyer and instead knocked on the architect’s door and proposed the idea of becoming his future gallerist. Though he was shocked, as he had never sold a painting before, Le Corbusier eventually agreed. Unfortunately, she sold almost none of them. ‘She decided to sell her little car, her Fiat Topolino, and she went back to Paris with all the money she had from selling the car and gave it to Corbusier, pretending she sold everything. And Corbusier was absolutely thrilled and said, well, she is a genius,’ Brändle recounts of the moment that she became one of his biggest advocates in both art and architecture.

‘That the pavilion is the only building that is turned by 45 degrees,’ says Brändle of its orientation relative to all the other homes in the neighborhood that surrounds it. ‘[The other homes] are in parallel to the street. Le Corbusier always wanted to build a house that is like the Parthenon in Athens.’ The building was constructed at an angle, but a compromise had to be made. Although the architect wanted direct access from the street, a winding path had to be built instead. ‘And Heidi was very upset [about this] because she said, you are betraying not only me, but also the ideas of Le Corbusier.’ This, alongside the land dispute and other particularities, adds to the tricky history of the architect’s last structure. Since 2019, the building has been considered the third part of the Zurich Design Museum, with the institution embarking on a major restoration of the space, which involved refinishing its sloped roof.

There is an amusing contradiction in the Pavilion’s history. A building conceived around the principles of prefabrication, assembled from modular elements and thousands of screws, has proved remarkably difficult to move. Despite its appearance as a structure that could be taken apart and reassembled elsewhere, its concrete staircase and ramp firmly anchor it to its original site. The story of Heidi Weber’s dispute with the city adds another layer to this curious history, raising questions about who gets to claim a building and what it means to preserve it. In a way, the Pavilion embodies two competing ideas of architecture: one that embraces movement, experimentation, and the possibility of relocation, and another that understands a building as inseparable from its place. Today, under the care of the Museum für Gestaltung Zürich, it remains on the lakeside plot where it was first conceived. Perhaps its greatest achievement is not that it can be moved, but that it continues to make us question why architecture has to stay put.
