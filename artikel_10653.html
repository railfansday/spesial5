A designer who once spent an afternoon searching stock libraries can now describe the picture they want and have four versions in under a minute. AI image generators have moved from curiosity to everyday tool for blog headers, product mockups, ad concepts, game art and slide decks. The quality gap between tools has narrowed, but the differences that remain matter a lot: how well each follows a detailed prompt, whether it can render readable text, how it handles edits, and what you are allowed to do with the result.

This guide compares six of the best-known options, what each does well and where each falls short. Models, plans and limits change often, so check each vendor's own pages before you commit to a workflow.

OpenArt’s AI image generator puts several image models behind one interface. Its generator page lists models including GPT Image 2.5, Grok Imagine, Qwen Image 3, Nano Banana 2 and Seedream 5.0 Pro, and lets you create images from text prompts or reference photos. It also lists tools for camera angle control, upscaling, collages and a Character Builder for keeping characters consistent across scenes, with 4K export.

Best for: Creators who want to compare several models on the same prompt without juggling separate subscriptions and accounts.

Limitations: The page offers a free start but does not state credit amounts or plan details, and it does not address commercial use, so check the pricing and licensing pages before using results for client work. Output quality depends on which model you pick.

OpenAI's image generation in ChatGPT works inside the chat interface, which makes iteration natural: generate an image, then ask for changes in plain language. OpenAI highlights accurate text rendering, the ability to follow prompts with many distinct objects, and consistent characters across multi-turn edits. You can also upload reference images and have the model transform them. It handles both photorealistic and stylized looks.

Best for: Marketers and writers who want to describe, refine and re-describe an image without learning a new tool.

Limitations: OpenAI notes that tall images such as posters can be cropped too tightly, and that dense small text, multilingual text and precise graphs can still go wrong. Images can take up to a minute to render. All generated images carry C2PA metadata identifying their source.

Gemini's image generation runs on Google's Nano Banana models. Google highlights character consistency, text rendering in many languages, combining several photos, precise editing and 2K resolution. A free tier covers image generation and editing, while the most advanced model requires a Google AI plan. You start from the "Create images" option in the Gemini app, then prompt or upload a picture to edit.

Best for: People who need to keep the same subject across several images, or who want to edit existing photos quickly.

Limitations: Usage limits apply, and the strongest model is behind a paid plan. Images include an invisible SynthID watermark and a visible AI label, which is good for transparency but may not suit every use.

Midjourney is an independent research lab known for images with a strong, painterly look. Its official site points users to a sign-up flow and to a Discord community and help center for support. Many creators choose it for concept art, mood boards and illustration where style matters more than literal accuracy.

Best for: Artists, art directors and game designers who want rich, stylized results and enjoy exploring variations.

Limitations: I could not confirm current pricing, trial terms or feature details from Midjourney's public pages, so check them yourself before subscribing. The workflow can feel less like a normal app than the others on this list, and it is not the easiest route for precise, text-heavy graphics.

Adobe Firefly is built for professionals who already work in Creative Cloud. Adobe states that its Firefly models are commercially safe, trained on licensed Adobe Stock and public domain content, and outputs carry Content Credentials that show AI involvement. It offers text-to-image, image-to-image, sketch-to-image and style transfer, plus editing tools such as background removal, object removal, upscaling and generative expand. It connects with Photoshop, Illustrator, Lightroom and Adobe Express, and also lets you use partner models from other companies.

Best for: Designers and brand teams who need clear licensing and want AI inside the apps they already use.

Limitations: Plans work on a credit system, so heavy use adds up. Adobe lists a free tier with limited daily generations and paid plans starting at about $9.99 a month, but confirm current terms on its site.

Ideogram built its reputation on a problem that trips up many generators: readable text. Its current models are presented as producing clean typography in different layouts, with attention to prompt fidelity and reliable editing. It also offers an API with access to a catalog of models, and has released open weights for one earlier model for local use and customization under its license.

Best for: Posters, social graphics, logo concepts and anything where words are part of the image.

Limitations: The pages I checked did not describe a free plan, so look at current plans before relying on it. Like every generator, it still needs a human to proofread the text it produces.

Start with the job. If you want to compare several models side by side, try OpenArt. If you want to talk your way to a finished image, try ChatGPT or Gemini. If you need images that will sit inside a branded design workflow, Firefly is the safer fit. If you are chasing a distinctive look, Midjourney deserves a trial. If text must be perfect, test Ideogram.

Run the same prompt in two or three tools before paying. Differences in composition, hands, faces and text show up quickly, and they matter more than any feature list.

Reviews only go so far, because your own use case is what counts. Pick one realistic job, for example a 16:9 header image for a blog post about web performance, and run the same prompt through two or three tools. Write the prompt once and paste it unchanged, so the comparison is fair:

Then score each result on five questions: Did it follow every instruction? Are there odd details in hands, edges or objects? Is any text spelled correctly? Does it still look good when cropped to a square? Could you license it for your project? Ten minutes of this teaches more than any feature list, and it shows how much editing each tool needs before you can publish.

Most readers of this guide will put these images on web pages, where a few habits matter more than the generator you choose.

There is no single best AI image generator. The right pick depends on whether you value conversation, style, licensing, control or typography. Test two or three on a real project, compare the output, and keep a human in charge of the final choice. Whatever you generate, review it for errors and label AI-made images clearly where your audience would expect it.

Are AI-generated images free to use commercially? It depends on the provider and your plan. Read the licensing terms for the tool you use, and consider vendors that publish how their models were trained if you work for clients.

Which AI image generator is best for text in images? Ideogram and ChatGPT both highlight text rendering, and Gemini lists it as a feature. Test each with your own wording and proofread the result.

Do AI images have watermarks? Some do. OpenAI adds C2PA metadata, Google adds an invisible SynthID watermark and a visible label, and Adobe attaches Content Credentials.

Can I use more than one image model without separate accounts? Yes. Platforms such as OpenArt put several models in one interface, which makes side-by-side testing easier. Check the licensing terms for each model you use.

Should I add alt text to AI-generated images? Yes, when the image carries meaning. Describe what it shows or why it is there. Purely decorative images can use an empty alt attribute, following the W3C guidance.
