The U.S. Just Lost Its Measles Elimination Status. Here's How to Get It Back.

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

— Vaccine access and faster testing are essential

The only one of us who has seen a patient with measles is California's State Public Health Officer (Pan).

For the rest of us (Rubino, Soni, and Shete), there isn't a patient case to tell you about. Three doctors, practicing across different states, different hospitals, between medical school and residency and fellowship, and for us, there's only been measles in textbooks or on test questions.

But this is about to change, and in many places across the U.S., it already has. The deaths of an unvaccinated 7-month-old in New York state and a child in Wisconsin are the most recent to make national headlines.

The U.S. has had over 5,400 cases so far this year -- over double the total in 2025. And now, the U.S. has lost its measles elimination status.

Though this announcement may not bring new risks from one day to the next for most Americans, it signals a seismic shift in the infectious disease landscape and the entire healthcare system.

Since 2000, measles had been deemed "eliminated" from the U.S.

The Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) has an independent group of experts that monitors and verifies each member country's measles transmission status. While elimination did not mean "zero cases," it meant that the same measles strain had not been spreading for at least 12 months. This is no longer the case.

For the better part of the last two and a half decades, measles had been introduced from unvaccinated travelers and originated outside the U.S. And when small outbreaks cropped up, they were limited and addressed quickly. But the last 2 years have been different, with outbreaks spreading via transmission within the U.S.

For those who are unvaccinated -- whether by choice, due to young age, or for medical reasons -- a measles exposure brings up to 90% risk of infection and a 20% chance of hospitalization. For people who are vaccinated, who share the same healthcare system, hospitals, and ICU beds, their own healthcare access may be impacted as measles cases may siphon limited, critical healthcare resources.

The reason we lost our elimination status is simple: declining vaccination rates. The number of children entering kindergarten with the measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR) vaccine has been declining since the COVID pandemic, with only 10 states, including California, at the target of 95% in 2025-2026.

The Majority of Americans Support Vaccination

Other countries across Asia and Africa have shown that it's very possible to catch up and achieve population-level immunity with coordinated public health campaigns.

Over 80% of all U.S. adults and 76% of parents believe the benefits of childhood vaccines for MMR outweigh the risks. Trust exists across the political and ideological spectrum, with 77% of Republicans and 91% of Democrats somewhat or very confident the MMR vaccine is safe for children.

Still, many families cite confusion around vaccine recommendations, insurance coverage, and overall difficulty accessing care as the barriers. These logistical issues are cited more often by families with lower incomes, despite their desire to access and receive vaccines.

What Will it Take to Regain Our Elimination Status?

To again eliminate measles in the U.S., the first step is to make it easy and affordable for children to get the MMR vaccine.

CDC director Erica Schwartz, MD, MPH, JD, said that regardless of the U.S. measles' elimination status, she plans to stay the course and center education, awareness, and access to the MMR vaccine. She plans to continue working with states to make the vaccine available and to educate families on its role in prevention.

But the current approach may not be enough. And getting the MMR vaccine may not be as simple as scheduling a visit with the pediatrician. The portion of children with a "usual source of care," like a trusted pediatrician or health clinic, has been declining since 2014; more than 30% of children lack age-appropriate preventive care visits each year.

While systemic issues around access to care will take time to address, there are steps our healthcare and public health systems can take now.

Public health, clinic systems, and community-based organizations must make it easier to access immunization by bringing vaccines to where people live and spend time. Places like local pharmacies, health fairs, schools, and community centers should be prioritized for funding and resources.

To increase the number of pharmacies available as vaccine access points, health department partnerships with state pharmacy boards and pharmacy chains are critical. Policies that expand pharmacists' ability to vaccinate young children can improve access too.

Increasing outreach, education, and sharing information with community leaders to ensure that families know where and how to get vaccines is critical.

For those who get sick, quality testing must be available and easily accessible, especially for those who may be facing cuts to their insurance coverage. Though a new measles test has been developed and can diagnose hundreds of cases within 8 hours, the CDC failed to validate it last month. As of late October, it still remained unavailable for states to use, though nearly every U.S. state has had at least one measles case so far in 2026.

Though the implications for our healthcare system may be daunting and complex, solutions to reverse the loss of our measles elimination status are simple.

Bringing the MMR vaccine out of the clinic and into community spaces and making it faster to test for and diagnose cases when they arise can help the U.S. regain its measles elimination status. These steps can relegate measles back to the pages of textbooks and save lives.
