Can ctDNA Help Guide Treatment Decisions in Muscle-Invasive Bladder Cancer?

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

— Experts discuss new ESMO findings and the limits of risk stratification

In this exclusive roundtable video from MedPage Today, three expert leaders in the field of bladder cancer discuss the latest research presented at the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) congress.

Moderator Matthew Galsky, MD, of the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai in New York City, is joined by David Aggen, MD, PhD, of Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center in New York City, and Stephanie Berg, DO, of the Dana-Farber Cancer Institute in Boston.

In this third of four episodes, they examine new findings about circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) from KEYNOTE-B15, as well as its potential for predicting recurrence and the unanswered questions about using ctDNA to guide treatment decisions.

Following is a transcript of their remarks:

Galsky: So let's talk a little bit about the next topic, which is ctDNA. And we saw ctDNA all over the place at ESMO this year, including in some high-profile presentations related to large randomized studies. So we're really starting to get, one, more data in the context of randomized studies, and two, we're beginning to see some data with different strategies for testing ctDNA after having had data really limited to what I'll call the standard Signatera assay for a while.

So let's talk about some of that data. So we saw data from KEYNOTE-B15, and remember KEYNOTE-B15 enrolled patients who were cisplatin-ineligible* with muscle-invasive bladder cancer, and patients were randomized to receive perioperative EV [enfortumab vedotin (Padcev)], pembro [pembrolizumab (Keytruda)], and cystectomy versus neoadjuvant gem-cis [gemcitabine and cisplatin] and cystectomy; ctDNA testing was done with the Signatera assay using the more standard approach in that study.And on the EV-pembro arm, there was an 82% rate of clearance of ctDNA from baseline to pre-cystectomy versus 66.7% with neoadjuvant gem-cis. [*Editor's note: KEYNOTE-B15 enrolled cisplatin-eligible patients.]

And presurgical ctDNA negativity did not absolutely predict a path CR [pathological complete response], but it was associated with a higher path CR rate and certainly was associated with more favorable event-free survival. We saw that in the post-cystectomy setting, the likelihood of having detectable ctDNA with modern treatment regimens and cystectomy was really pretty minuscule, in about the 5% range post-cystectomy. So Dr. Aggen, what do you make of that data and how do you use it in clinic?

Aggen: So I think it's exciting now that with a more sensitive whole-genome-based assay, 83% of patients pre-cystectomy are clearing their ctDNA. But the takeaway for me actually relates more to the other group that was positive pre-cystectomy. Even if they clear with surgery, that group is probably at a much higher risk of metastatic recurrence.

And so it does inform, for me, a patient with a positive ctDNA test before surgery is a patient who probably should be getting more adjuvant therapy. And so I think largely ctDNA in this setting is prognostic. We're trying to do the trials through Alliance to figure out if we can use that as a risk stratifier for those who have persistent positivity.

I think the other takeaway for me though, is that a negative test also doesn't mean that you're totally out of the woods. In the breakdown they showed by ctDNA status, the event-free survival at 2 years in the ctDNA-negative group was about 85 or 86%. So there's still a 15% chance that those patients recur.

So I think it's nice that we have this tool now for prognostication. I don't think it's quite there yet for how we risk stratify and it's not necessarily helping me to make a decision at this moment to deescalate therapy for patients.

Galsky: How about you, Dr. Berg? How do you use it? Is baseline ctDNA testing in muscle-invasive bladder cancer helpful? Does it help you decide who should get neoadjuvant treatment at all?

Berg: I do think so in the sense that when I think about ctDNA, and at least for perioperative therapy, you have your baseline, and then you have your post-treatment before surgery, and then you have your after treatment, and then you think about adjuvant treatment.

So a lot of the data, like Dr. Aggen was saying about being prognostic, is the patients that are just negative in the beginning at that first baseline test, they are doing very, very, very well. Going through the therapy, staying negative, having a good, probably, response, and then hopefully staying negative after that. Those patients we know we do well.

And I would emphasize, like Dr. Aggen was saying, if you're going into surgery still positive, that is something I think we can educate our patients really importantly about what that could mean, not to sway them away from potentially a curative surgery, but can have that discussion and then really follow it afterwards.

So I think it's really adding this extra layer for our conversations, probably can cause some anxiety and a lot more questions, but I think it's something that we are really building into our paradigm when we see our patients next to us and follow this real-time tracker with them. And it's exciting in a way, but also again, can make everyone feel very anxious. So I think when trials are coming in, we're going to really use that more.

And I know for your study, Dr. Galsky, how do we determine afterwards what we're doing with it? And do you need more if you're positive afterwards or can you go and have deescalate when you're negative? And what does that mean to be negative and stay negative, or positive to negative or negative to positive? It's a lot. It's a lot, but it's great to see all the data coming out and all the work being done in our large studies really adding that to the data. It really is, I think, so helpful for all of us because we all are using it so often in clinic.

Galsky: And we're just starting to scratch the surface regarding kinetics of change. So pre-cystectomy ctDNA detectability, like Dr. Aggen pointed out, it's probably not all created equally. And there are patients who are detectable prior to surgery who have a rising value. There are patients who are detectable prior to surgery having a declining value. And all of that probably matters somewhat, but we don't know how yet.
