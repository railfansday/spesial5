15 Active Jobs for People Who Hate Sitting – Weigh Pay Against Physical Toll

Plenty of jobs pay you to walk, lift, and climb instead of sitting, and some pay well above the typical U.S. wage. Before you switch, compare each job’s pay and training with what it asks of your body, since the active jobs you can start fastest tend to pay the least.

Picture an office worker who trades her desk for a mail route. She now spends most of her shift on her feet, and the exercise she used to pay a gym for comes with the job.

The difference shows up in calories. The Compendium of Physical Activities, a research list rating how much energy activities take, scores each one against sitting quietly, which burns about 1 calorie an hour for every 2.2 pounds you weigh. For a 160-pound person, that puts an hour of office work at about 110 calories, delivering mail on foot at about 170, general yard work at about 290, and firefighting at about 580.

Carriers on walking routes cover about 4 to 12 miles a day and spend up to about six hours on foot, the National Association of Letter Carriers says. Over those six hours, a 160-pound carrier burns roughly 350 more calories than she would at a desk. The 15 jobs below run from that kind of steady walking to heavy lifting, and their pay ranges just as widely.

Pay below is each job’s May 2025 median from the Occupational Outlook Handbook of the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS), meaning half of workers in the job earn more and half less. For comparison, the median for all U.S. workers was $50,980.

The quickest jobs to start mostly pay the least. Animal care, grounds work, material moving, construction labor, and fitness training take little or no schooling, and all five pay under the $50,980 national median. Mail carriers are the exception. The other better-paid jobs here typically start with a training program, a degree, or an apprenticeship.

Physical work also brings more injuries than a desk job. BLS counts construction laborers, material movers, and flight attendants among the occupations with the highest injury and illness rates, and nurses and therapist assistants who lift patients are prone to back injuries.

The strain adds up with age. A University of Michigan Retirement and Disability Research Center study found that workers in more physically demanding jobs are more likely to be retired at any given age. The effect showed up almost entirely in men and was larger among those without a college degree. That makes saving early matter more in a heavy job, since your working years may be fewer.

To earn while you train, start with the trades. Electricians and elevator installers learn through apprenticeships that pay for their hours on the job, and carpenters can train the same way. The Labor Department’s Apprenticeship Finder lets you search openings by career and location.
