In The AI Era, Keep Hiring New College Grads — And Make Them Managers From Day One

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

A few months ago, I was sitting lakeside with a neighbor. He is the head of AI for a pharmaceutical company, and he mentioned the truly incredible productivity his senior team is receiving from AI.

Then he paused and turned to me and said: “You know, I may never hire another junior engineer again. Which is great for the business right now, but who’s the next generation that is going to run this when we retire?”

The concern over what will happen to young people just entering the workforce is one many of us are grappling with right now, and it has a human dimension. Not just what happens to employment rates, but what happens to my students or my children?

College graduates typically do the individual contributor knowledge work that AI is evermore capable of delivering. To cite one statistic, a recent Stanford study found employment of 22 to 25 year olds in occupations most exposed to AI is running 19% below rates for their peers in less AI-impacted sectors, and that gap has been widening.

What role will new graduates play in the age of AI? And how will we develop the generation of managers who will supervise AI? Fortunately, we have thriving examples with 200 years of history on how to teach 22 year olds to do exactly that.

Our claim is that the reason senior folks get more out of AI today is not primarily because they have greater subject knowledge, but rather because they are good managers. They have to trust folks who work for them without completely trusting them, they have to divide tasks, delegate clearly, check results and patiently correct, coach, and sometimes shift personnel on their teams.

The future for new graduates is to become great managers now, at the very beginning of their career, and not after a long period of individual contribution.

The nature of the innovation of AI is that it delivers knowledge work. We know a lot about human knowledge workers and the analogy to AI knowledge workers is strong.

Knowledge workers know much more about their individual sphere of expertise than their managers do. Good managers routinely organize and lead knowledge workers who are just flat-out much smarter than they are. They do this through the craft of management.

These same qualities, as AI behaves more like AI executives, naturally give good managers the skills to manage AI agents, who become the individual contributor knowledge workers.

It is no secret that the investment industry culture values most highly the brilliance of the individual contributor who delivers the incisive observation, makes the correct investment call, and closes the one-on-one negotiation with a win.

I have lived this culture for a long time. Calling an investor a “great people manager” is a backhanded compliment at best. In the investment industry, the jump from individual contributor to manager usually arrives midcareer or later, and typically with little to no formal training in the craft of management.

The analyst, associate and vice president are primarily acting as individual contributors (though of course they may mentor a few junior folks), and are not shifting to acting primarily as managers of other knowledge workers until principal or perhaps partner.

When I was an analyst at Morgan Stanley many years ago, the hierarchy ran from analyst to managing director. Analysts were the ultimate individual contributors, and whenever an analyst would attempt to delegate some work to others, we had a name for that: “managing analyst.” This clever put-down reflected the cultural belief that real credit goes to those who do, not those who deliver through the work of those they manage.

This blind spot on the importance of management is not limited to the investment industry. As another salient recent example, let’s look at the OpenAI-Hugging Face incident. Much has been written about this event, where OpenAI agents hacked into Hugging Face.

In OpenAI’s own report on the incident, its proposed long list of fixes, including stronger security, alerts on breaches and post-incident remediation, does not address directly training its employees to be better managers of these agents. That’s even as the company acknowledges that members of an internal team at OpenAI knew of many of the concerning behaviors (including message board and unauthorized internet access) in late May, and did not act until July.

This may be an unfair analogy, but imagine if we hired 1,200 workers to operate a warehouse complex, gave each of them detailed written instructions, and then had all the line managers and senior managers leave for a week. If we came back to find that someone had stolen a delivery van, the problem wouldn’t be that these weren’t the right workers or that the instructions weren’t clear enough. The problem is lack of management.

As I was wrestling with this problem, I talked with my business school classmate Michael Quoia, who attended the U.S. Naval Academy and served as an officer, and then built a career in executive leadership development. He recalled:

“When I met my division of 25 sailors in Yokosuka, Japan at the young age of 22 years old, I was greeted by my senior chief petty officer, who already served for nearly 20 years. Behind him, we had several organizational levels including the first line sailors who conducted maintenance on the fire control systems. This was actually a harder problem than we are facing with AI. This was about supervising at least three levels without experience. I was expected to provide character, judgment, problem solving and leadership, despite not having come up through the ranks. How did the Navy develop these qualities in me so that I could bypass time in the seat as a frontline worker?”

Like companies facing the AI dilemma we have been exploring, the military needs to develop capable leaders who lack entry-level experience. And while the military is a very relevant example, it is not the only one. The Home Depot’s store management program and General Electric’s Crotonville are two examples of corporations who recruit high-potential talent and put them through an accelerated leadership development program to prepare them to lead people in jobs they have not done.

Let’s look, though, at how the Naval Academy provided Michael’s training, and what that may mean whether you are 22 years old or hiring 22-year-olds.

First, the foundation of developing future officers is the development of moral leadership. The mission of the United States Naval Academy begins with the phrase “To develop midshipmen morally, mentally, and physically … .” In an age of AI, first-line managers of AI agents will need integrity and a moral compass, because AI does not come with that reliably pre-installed. It is no overstatement to suggest that moral leadership of AI could have societal implications and needs to be developed.

Second, the service academies are intentionally designed as leadership laboratories. From plebe summer to commissioning, midshipmen progress from learning how to be a good subordinate to true manager. What is most relevant to the age of AI is that simulated leadership learning is possible and proven if designed with care.

Third, the service academies build high-pressure situations into their leadership laboratory. While most companies are not placing their first-line managers in life or death situations, the ability to lead in high stakes situations, for instance to take personal responsibility for an AI agent swarm you launched which is now “going rogue,” requires composure and decisiveness under stress and pressure.

If a 22-year-old ensign can manage 25 sailors on a Yokosuka destroyer, albeit with support from senior officers and enlisted, our 22-year-old college graduate can certainly be trained to manage 25 super-intelligent agents. The moment for practicing the craft of management will shift from 32 years old to 22 years old, across the businesses of knowledge work.

In short: We need to bring back the “managing analyst.” We need to recognize that AI agents behave like knowledge workers and therefore need to be managed. We should reset the culture in knowledge work industries that management is a valued skill, beginning at the start of one’s career and not midcareer.

These entry-level workers are smart, motivated, learn quickly and work hard. There is a huge unmet need for the craft of management in corralling this new generation of AI agents. We have the proof points across nearly 200 years from our service academies that these skills can be taught to the young, and the young can deliver them with distinction. The future for college graduates is a redefinition of their jobs away from being individual-contributor knowledge workers and toward being effective managers and leaders from the very beginning of their careers.
