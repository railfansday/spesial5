The End (for now)

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

We head out of White Pass in the morning. The mood is somber but we’re trying to keep it upbeat. Both of our bodies have decided that since the trail is ending soon, there’s no reason to hold it together anymore. Sarah’s occasional flare ups of achilles tendonitis become daily. She can feel the swelling increase throughout the day. We try to manage it with tape, anti-inflammatories, and taking breaks, but she still limps into camp each night of the next few days. I don’t have a specific ailment but my body hurts. My hip flexors feel so tight that it takes me a full 2 minutes to fully straighten up after sitting and my feet hurt daily.

We reach what will be our final national park of the trail, Mount Rainier. On clear days at home we can see the mountain while driving each day, so this is a big deal. We’re so close to home and we walked all this way. We hike into the park and start encountering many more backpackers and day hikers. There’s a fire somewhere nearby and the smoke moves in during the afternoon. We reach a small lake and set up camp with a few other hikers. Its clear we’re closer to civilization – there is trash on the beach and the birds (camp robbers) are relentless at dinner. We can’t leave a bag unguarded or they’ll swoop down.

The next morning we wake to elk bugles and the sounds of them crashing through the water nearby. We get glimpses of the herd as we pack up. These are the first elk we’ve seen the entire trail! Its an exciting morning. We take an alternate and get great views of Rainier and Tipsoo Lake. The side trail takes us onto the road for a short time, and its weird to be surrounded by cars and tourists at the mountain. Last summer we car camped and drove around here, now we’re just walking through. It’s the same place but we’re in a totally different world.

We reach Chinook Pass and head up and out of the parking lot. We’d hoped for trail magic there but its early, and we settle for throwing out our trash and using a pit toilet. Its the little things at this point. A few miles past Chinook Pass and we’re on our own again. The day settles into a typical trail day, plus views up close to Mount Rainier. We can see Crystal Mountain ski resort from the trail and the lifts are running with mountain bikers. I miss my bike, and am reminded i’ll be home shortly to reunite with her.

We pass through another burn zone, and turn to our audiobooks to get us through. It’s strange to be on our last few days on trail. I feel like it should be different somehow. Like the end should feel less somber and I should feel lighter. But my body still hurts and the miles between here and Snoqualmie won’t walk themselves. It doesn’t help that these miles are not the most scenic. They pass through burn scars, logging areas, and forested paths. I want to be done hiking but this isn’t how I want my hike to end.

The next day is our final full day on trail. We get our first view of the massive smoke plumes of the Kings and Three Queens fires. They’re huge. We stop and stare for a while in awe and sadness. The sheer scale of the smoke is hard to take in. We know we’re making the right choice in getting off trail.

We stop to filter water for the last time. It’s a 1/4 mile off trail and Sarah’s achilles hurts so much that I offer to get the water on my own. When I bring it back up the steep side trail we sit and filter, then just stay sitting on a log for a long time. We’re not in a rush. When we start hiking again I decide to talk through each day, as much as I can remember them. I start with the first day; what we felt, how hard the first 15 miles in the desert heat were. Then we keep reminiscing on each day, from the desert to the Sierra, Northern California and Oregon. I surprise myself with my recollection of each day, and only need to check my phone for dates twice. We stop for lunch halfway through the journey down memory lane and again linger much longer than usual. When we start hiking again we pick up the days of the trail until we get all the way up to the present. This ending has been slowly approaching for 137 days now. It seems to have rushed right up to me at the end and I don’t quite understand how. Talking through all the previous days on trail helps it make a bit more sense.

Sarah’s limp is much worse and she’s in a lot of pain. It seems so silly to be so close to the end and to be in such rough shape. We decide to take the gentler graded road down to Snoqualmie instead of the trail with 3x the elevation gain and loss. It’s not how we want to end the trail but we’re already well past how we want this to end. As we walk down the road the mushroom cloud of smoke from the fires looms over us. It seems apocalyptic. Sarah limps to camp as we get our first views of I-90 down below us.

My Dad, Aunt, and Uncle are on their way to pick us up. We’re excited to see them and to get home tomorrow. The weather has turned windy and foggy, but at least we can’t see the smoke plume anymore. We set off on the final day of our continuous journey. The day would be boring and unremarkable if it wasn’t our last. I try to feel something more profound as I eat my last poptart but it’s tough. I walked here, all the way from Mexico! We’re almost to the highway, where I drove through with my Dad 4 years ago when we moved out here. It should feel more fitting, more impactful! But all I can hear is the roar of the cars on I-90 and I can feel is my feet and hips hurting.

We finally make it through the small town and road walk up to the trailhead. We approach the sign and take off our packs. Here it is, 2396 miles from where we started. We’re done walking. It doesn’t feel real at all. We hug and cry and I try to set up my tripod for a picture. Eventually someone comes by and takes one for us. Then we sit in silence for a few moments and try to let it all sink in. We’re here. We walked here. We’re done walking.

After we officially stop hiking at Snoqualmie Pass it’s a whirlwind of seeing family, getting home, and re-acclimating to society. We hold out hope for one more bit of hiking this season to wrap up our journey from Mexico to Canada. We do get our wish – there’s a tiny bit left to this story.
