32 Years Later, Disney's Most Notorious Cash-Grab Sequel Is Better Than You Remember

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

For Turkey Day, I’m standing up for my favorite parrot

For Turkey Day, I’m standing up for my favorite parrot

Ever since former Disney CEO Bob Chapek released his autobiography last September, a rather unexpected Disney movie has been a topic for discussion: the direct-to-VHS sequel to Aladdin titled The Return of Jafar. Released in 1994, The Return of Jafar was the first in a long line of direct-to-VHS (and later direct-to-DVD) sequels to theatrical Disney films. Besides Aladdin, Disney made sequels to The Lion King, The Little Mermaid, Pocahontas, Mulan, and several other then-modern Disney animated features, as well as some much older classics like Cinderella and Bambi. These movies used cheaper animation produced by Disney’s TV division​​​​​​​ and were developed in far less time than Disney spends developing animated feature films​​​​​​​.

While these sequels — especially The Return of Jafar — were highly profitable for Disney, some felt that they cheapened the work of Disney’s theatrical division. Their chief critic was Pixar head John Lasseter, and when Disney acquired Pixar in 2006, Lasseter convinced CEO Bob Iger to shut down the operation.

Since then, most Disney fans have come to agree that stopping these movies was for the best, but Chapek, who was in charge of that division for Disney, is still bitter about it. In a chapter Chapek titled “Jafar Returns, and I Emerge,” he writes: “Why was there such vocal criticism aimed at the movie? Likely because a bunch of upstart TV animators in their twenties had created something that generated five times the profits of a typical theatrical release … It was Bob from Flyover Indiana, educated at a state school, who disrupted the business and kept the studio afloat with an endeavor others had dismissed as creative heresy.”

While Chapek’s bitterness is palpable, he’s right about the profitability of The Return of Jafar​​​​​​​, which cost just $5 million to produce and raked in more than $300 million. And while I personally agree that these poorly made sequels​​​​​​​ cheapened the integrity of the originals, I do have a soft spot specifically for The Return of Jafar — but not because it made so much money.

I was seven years old when Aladdin came out and it quickly became my favorite Disney movie (and it still is). I loved everything about it and even collected the Aladdin action figures by Mattel (including the Genie, which included a cool feature where you could pull out his head and swap his expression with another head stored inside his body). The Return of Jafar came out about 18 months after Aladdin, and I loved the story about Jafar as a genie seeking revenge on Aladdin and the other characters. Sure, Genie seemed a bit different in the sequel (he was played by Homer Simpson voice actor Dan Castellaneta instead of Robin Williams), but he was still my favorite character and still very funny.

Back then, I was too young and dumb to notice the difference between Aladdin and The Return of Jafar from a quality standpoint, but over time — after parting with my Aladdin action figures and gaining a greater appreciation for the process behind Disney's animated films — I grew to understand why Return of Jafar isn’t so good.

However, I’ve since come to fall in love with it all over again for another reason: Gilbert Gottfried.

While Williams didn’t return for The Return of Jafar, the rest of the Aladdin cast did, including comedian Gilbert Gottfried as Iago​​​​​​​, the scheming parrot who sat on Jafar’s shoulders in the first film.

Iago was no longer Jafar’s sidekick in the sequel. Instead, he abandons Jafar in the beginning of the movie, as he’s fed up with getting bossed around and hates living inside of a lamp (where Jafar is imprisoned). Iago pulls himself out of the lamp and comes up with a scheme to ingratiate himself with Aladdin, Jasmine, and the others so he can live a cushy life in the palace. And while his plan is entirely cynical at first, he does find himself caring for the humans he opposed in the first film, especially Aladdin, who seems​​​​​​​ most willing to give him a second chance.

Eventually, Jafar finds a new stooge in the bumbling thief Abis Mal, who finds Jafar’s lamp and begins making hasty wishes. His first wish is for the sunken treasure ship Coeur du Mer, and Jafar teleports him deep into the ocean. Abis Mal then has to use his second wish so he doesn’t drown. As Jafar tells Abis Mal to take some time on his third wish, Jafar plots his takeover of Agrabah and even, temporarily, lures Iago back into his ranks.

As surprising as it might sound, The Return of Jafar is an Iago story first and foremost. He has a more developed character arc than anyone else in the entire movie, and the writers managed to evolve the character without undermining his established self-interest or making him any less funny. He also has two songs that Gottfried scream-sings, and they’re both still hilarious and more catchy than you might think. (“I’m Lookin’ Out for Me”​​​​​​​ is especially great.)

My renewed affection for The Return of Jafar came about in the late 2010s​​​​​​​ when I began listening to Gilbert Gottfried's Amazing Colossal Podcast, where Gottfried and his co-host Frank Santopadre interviewed showbiz legends every week. Up until that podcast, I had only a vague awareness of Gottfried’s career, mostly as a voice actor, but within a few years he became one of my all-time favorite comedians. As a result, I sought out​​​​​​​ many of his best film and TV roles and, while there were many, Gottfried almost never starred in something. His big personality and raging voice were mostly reserved for small, hilarious cameos. He was never the leading man — except in The Return of Jafar.

(I also encourage everyone to watch the 2017 documentary Gilbert, which is a touching portrait of the man.)

And since Gottfried died in 2022 of Myotonic Dystrophy Type 2, my defense of The Return of Jafar has turned downright personal. Yes, on an objective level, I can recognize the rushed narrative and inferior animation. More broadly, I understand and agree with the idea that Disney’s direct-to-video era hurt Disney’s​​​​​​​ overall artistic legacy, and that The Return of Jafar was the start of all that. But over the past few months, as Disney fans come out against Chapek’s profit-first, art-second defense by bashing this movie all over again, I can't help but think of Iago’s best song lyric from The Return of Jafar, where the aggressive parrot​​​​​​​ shouts, “I'm through with that! I'm flappin' free! From here on in, I'm lookin' out for me!”

The Return of Jafar is streaming on Disney Plus.
