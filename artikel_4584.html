When Life Becomes Frantic, We Slip into Autopilot

The world moves faster now than ever before. Rapidly accelerating technology and AI development build on each other exponentially, compressing the time it takes for major shifts to occur. Digital tools and instant communication are efficient but can also foment a work culture that embraces around-the-clock demands. Nonstop access to news, social media, and other content floods our brains with more input than we can process. This is all compounded by the overwhelming experience of poly-crisis, that is, multiple global challenges that are continuous, interconnected, and amplify one another as they collide. The bombardment of these factors can keep our brains in a low-grade fight-or-flight state.

When demands become constant, unrelenting, and urgent, your brain likely shifts from deliberate decision-making mode to automatic routines to save mental energy (Wirz, Bogdanov, and Schwabe, 2018). Research shows that approximately two-thirds of our daily actions are driven by habit rather than conscious decision-making (Rebar and colleagues, 2026). When your days fly by on autopilot, they become indistinguishable and leave you feeling spent, overwhelmed, and exhausted. Living a more intentional life and staying grounded in your body and the present moment means stepping away from autopilot and bringing conscious choice back to your days.

Learning to live with more intention starts by cultivating an awareness that your days have become blurred and indistinct, filled with routines and unexamined habits, and dominated by pressured reactions rather than intentional responses. Stop for a moment and ask: Where is all my urgent energy taking me? Is there a clear finish line? Do I just push through, day after day?

Becoming more intentional means knowing the direction you are aiming for, with the sense that your goals are worth pursuing. A strong sense of purpose has been linked to better physical health and emotional well-being, including better sleep, lower cortisol/stress, greater mental clarity, a stronger immune system, and enhanced resilience (Kim and colleagues, 2022).

Intention means taking the time to notice a moment with your full attention. When your mind is busy replaying events from the past or is perseverating on what might happen in the future, you are no longer present. You become disconnected and are likely stuck on high alert. When this happens, your brain’s ability to encode new information becomes impaired, making it harder to take in what is happening around you. Living with intention means paying attention and staying present in the moment, with mindfulness and without judgment. You can practice this with simple behaviors aimed at increasing brief moments of noticing. For example, pause for a few deep breaths before entering a meeting and ask yourself what matters most as you arrive. You can simplify this further. Whenever you switch tasks or enter a new setting, take a deep, mindful breath and say to yourself, “Arriving.”

Another way to enhance your awareness is to turn routine tasks, such as washing the dishes or taking a shower, into anchors for staying present. We tend to rush through these without really taking notice. These tasks offer perfect opportunities to slow down and notice what the water feels like and listen to the sounds around you. Adding a moment of gratitude, where you acknowledge small joys or blessings, helps shift your perspective from what is missing to what you have in the moment.

You do not need to retool your entire schedule to feel present. Small, low-effort shifts can interrupt automatic habits. Any little thing that introduces novelty can move your brain from automatic processing into active awareness—use your non-dominant hand while brushing your teeth or take an unfamiliar route home. When your brain encounters something unexpected, your internal predictions fail, and you are rewarded with a dopamine surge as your focus sharpens and awareness is enhanced as your brain moves out of autopilot.

Setting clear boundaries with yourself is something else to consider. Rather than a cluttered mind and desk, intention means actively choosing where your energy goes. Multi-tasking can take you away from intentionality. Instead, train yourself to be present with each activity. When you are eating, just eat. If you are driving, just focus on the drive. Your decisions, big and small, can be honed to better reflect your genuine priorities, such as health or creativity.

Finally, think about how you respond to messages and questions throughout your day. Rather than urgently reacting to each communication, you might pause for a reflective breath before responding. Slow down so that your nervous system remains balanced and calm. If you notice tension building or you rush through messages, step away for a brief time-out to bring yourself back into the present. Ask yourself whether the speed is actually necessary or just an ingrained habit.

Living with intention is not a single decision. It is an ongoing practice. At the end of the day, take a moment to look back and ask, “Where did my time go? What reflected my values? What did not?” These small practices add up so that the gap between the life you are living and the life you intend to live begins to narrow.
