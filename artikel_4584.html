Think Someone Envies You? Here’s Why You Might Be Wrong

As the holidays approach, you're likely to meet new people and socialize with colleagues and family you don't normally see the rest of the year.

In such settings, you're also more likely to come into situations where you sense a bit of "healthy competition." Turkey Trot, anyone? You may also interact with people playing the "one-up" game, where someone shares a more intense version of the story or situation you just shared.

Whenever people gather, we're assessing and comparing. Everyone does it. It's natural. It helps us understand how we fit in and get cues on how to behave.

Envy not only can occur, but it can also help you navigate these times...though not in the way most people expect.

When some people discover I've written a book on envy, but they are not yet aware of its contents, they will tell me, "I don't feel envy, but I know a lot of people who do." They will then proceed to tell me about the people who envy them.

They don't know that I believe that everyone envies. It is a universal emotion. Unpleasant, yes, but not necessarily a harmful one.

I get it. In this culture where envy is often seen as a deadly sin, it's hard to admit feeling envy.

But not acknowledging one's own envy is unfortunate because you miss out on all that envy can reveal to you. However, that isn't the mistake I want to focus on here. I believe the bigger mistake is to assume that you know when someone else is feeling envy.

First, we can't be certain it is envy they are feeling. Not only is it impossible to read minds, but we also can't reliably detect envy from their faces. As envy researcher Jens Lange told me, "If there were [a unique envy facial expression], we would likely suppress it."

Second, when we ascribe envy to someone else, we create an artificial barrier. We make a negative judgment about their character and lower their status to elevate ourselves. Not even conscious of this, we begin to operate on the false assumption and the distance we've created. It subtly impacts our interactions and our behaviors.

First, adopt a curious mindset. It's harder to stop your mind from thinking of something than it is to suggest something new to consider. So, instead of saying, "Don't judge or make assumptions," try, "I don't know their story." Remind yourself that you don't know what happened in their past, what just happened moments ago, what their circumstances are, or what their mood is.

Second, dial up the warmth. Amy Cuddy, Susan Fiske, and Peter Glick's research revealed that when people encounter one another, they ask two questions: "Does this person want to harm or help me?" and "Is this person capable of following through with their threat or assistance?" They found that people tend to be envied when they are not warm but competent. So, if it is true that you're envied, more reason to approach with warmth.

During your year-end holiday festivities, try combining these two concepts and adopt a "warm curiosity."

You may walk away learning unexpected reasons why you like someone you didn't before.
