Sweeping permitting overhaul passes Senate, on to House

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

From left, Sens. Mike Lee, R-Utah., Shelley Moore Capito, R-W.Va., Martin Heinrich, D-N.M., and Sheldon Whitehouse, D-R.I., hold a news conference on the measure in the Capitol on Sept. 30. (Bill Clark/CQ Roll Call)

The Senate passed a bill Thursday evening that would overhaul the federal permitting system for electric power lines, power lines wind and solar farms, hard rock mining and oil drilling projects and more.

It's the culmination of a yearslong effort intended to address industry concerns that it is too difficult to build any major infrastructure project in the United States. Supporters overcame opposition from more than half the Democratic Caucus, which will be a factor when the bill moves to the House after the Thanksgiving recess.

The bill passed on a 62-33 vote, with 31 Democrats joining two Republicans, Josh Hawley of Missouri and Mike Rounds of South Dakota, in opposition.

Shortly after the post-cloture time expired late Thursday, the Senate voted on passage of the legislation, including a substitute amendment consisting of revised text of the original permitting bill that the bipartisan authors unveiled before the midterm elections.

However, the chamber did not consider any additional amendments to the bill, as initially planned by the sponsors, including Environment and Public Works Chairman Shelley Moore Capito, R-W.Va., panel ranking member Sheldon Whitehouse, D-R.I., Energy and Natural Resources Chairman Mike Lee, R-Utah, and panel ranking member Martin Heinrich, D-N.M.

The bill includes changes to how reviews are conducted and permits are issued under the National Environmental Policy Act, National Historic Preservation Act, Endangered Species Act, Federal Power Act and Clean Water Act.

It includes provisions intended to ensure electricity ratepayers are not saddled with the costs of bringing power to data centers, and would give the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission new authority over interstate transmission lines.

It also would provide legal recourse when administrations discriminate against certain forms of energy or seek to revisit permits on ideological grounds. That's intended in part to address the Trump administration’s actions to delay or block solar and wind energy projects.

Democrats walked away from negotiations last year due to Trump administration actions to block offshore wind construction. However they ultimately said they received sufficient assurances that the administration would let stalled wind and solar projects move forward.

The "big four" senators on the permitting bill solicited feedback and changes sought by various lawmakers on both sides of the aisle, and were open to votes on additional amendments — so long as they didn't undo the measure's core tenets.

"This bill has threaded a needle the likes of which this chamber has not seen in a while, and we’ve got to take advantage of this opportunity," Capito said before passage.

Ultimately, Oregon Democratic Sens. Jeff Merkley and Ron Wyden would not consent to a time agreement allowing consideration of a finite number of amendments because Republicans wouldn't allow up-or-down votes at a simple-majority threshold on some of theirs. Merkley then objected to a unanimous consent request to consider amendments on the floor.

Wyden was pushing for a vote on an amendment that would prohibit construction of data centers on federal public lands. Merkley was pushing for amendments that included barring data centers from benefiting from “project certainty” protections against future presidents revoking their permits, and limiting presidential power to impose tariffs on clean energy project components, like wind turbines.

The Senate had already adopted the substitute amendment introduced by the bill’s sponsors during a procedural vote on Wednesday. But the lack of a unanimous consent agreement to take up more amendments meant that an additional manager's package of changes to the substitute fell by the wayside.

That amendment would have narrowed the instances where exemptions from National Historic Preservation Act reviews would be allowed, increased the annual authorization for the Historic Preservation Fund by 50 percent, and addressed concerns about the use of eminent domain when permitting transmission projects raised by Hawley and Rounds.

The sponsors introduced an updated version of their manager’s amendment Thursday afternoon that would refine exemptions from the ratepayer projection provisions so that artificial intelligence "hyperscalers" can't escape through a loophole.

The original manager's amendment sought to exempt internet and telecommunications providers' facilities, but the language was broad enough that cloud storage and other "information services" exclusions could potentially benefit data centers.

None of those changes made it into the bill as a result of the stalemate over amendments, and Merkley objected on the floor to incorporating just the manager's package.

The bill has been endorsed by a wide range of industry groups, who argue that the current federal permitting regime makes it too difficult to finance, plan and construct projects domestically. This includes the American Petroleum Institute, the American Clean Power Association, the U.S. Chamber of Commerce and the National Association of Manufacturers.

The White House expressed its support for the bill in a statement of administration policy this month.

The bill now moves on to the House, where it seemingly has the votes to pass.

Speaker Mike Johnson, R-La., said last week that the bill can pass in that chamber, while a number of Democrats including the centrist Blue Dog Coalition and Reps. Scott Peters, D-Calif., co-chair of the bipartisan Climate Solutions Caucus, and Nikki Budzinski, D-Ill., have expressed support.

Budzinski was elected chair of the 112-member New Democrat Coalition last week for the next Congress, and that group issued a statement speaking favorably of permitting while stopping short of endorsing the bill as currently drafted.

However, attendance on the House GOP side after Thanksgiving could be an issue: more than 30 lawmakers regularly skipped votes this week, the majority of them Republicans.

And many Democrats have called on Heinrich and Whitehouse to punt consideration of the bill to the new Congress, when their party will have large majorities in both chambers.

Merkley, who is in line to chair the Environment and Public Works Committee, and House Natural Resources ranking member Jared Huffman, D-Calif. — the expected chairman of that panel — both said the bill included too many provisions Democrats opposed in order to garner Republican support.

Some of the nation’s largest environmental groups including the Sierra Club and Natural Resources Defense Council oppose the bill. A coalition of 17 Democratic states also expressed their opposition. And some Native American tribes and organizations, including the National Congress of American Indians, also expressed their opposition.

"I’m hoping to have a full opportunity to address the many legitimate concerns that stakeholders have raised with this bill," Merkley said before the vote Thursday night.

But Heinrich and Whitehouse resisted calls to delay the bill, arguing that President Donald Trump is in office for two more years and that any bill needs his support to be signed into law. They added that they will be able to address environmental issues under their majority, and that this bill would represent the “floor” of their ambitions.

While Republicans have called for changes to the federal permitting system for decades, the effort gained steam with Democrats after the passage of their 2022 reconciliation law, which they dubbed the Inflation Reduction Act, or IRA.

As part of the deal to get then-Senate Energy and Natural Resources Chairman Joe Manchin III, D-W.Va., on board with the IRA, the Biden administration agreed to support a bill overhauling the federal permitting system. Manchin introduced a bill that included changes to NEPA and transmission permitting, and would approve all remaining permits needed for the Mountain Valley Pipeline in his home state.

However, his effort ultimately fell short on a 47-47 procedural vote to attach the bill to that year’s defense authorization measure.

In the 118th Congress Manchin tried again, and in 2024 he and then-Energy and Natural Resources ranking member John Barrasso, R-Wyo., introduced what Manchin called a “perfectly crafted balance.” The committee approved that bill in July 2024, but it was never voted on by the full Senate.

Capito, Whitehouse, Lee and Heinrich made a collective commitment to address permitting overhaul when they stepped into their current roles in January 2025, and spent nearly 21 months working on the legislation.

Since Manchin’s first bill, the political calculus changed.

After decades of stagnant energy demand, electricity prices began rising in part due to the expansion of data centers. Consumers also became increasingly frustrated with their local utilities over affordability concerns, which in turn spurred more lawmakers across the country, including on Capitol Hill, to critically examine their practices.

Lee also pushed for language streamlining NHPA reviews, which had not been a focus of earlier permitting discussion. He's argued that the law has expanded beyond its originally intended scope and become a major barrier to infrastructure projects in Western states like Utah.
