The male footballers best associated with each shirt number – the readers’ edition

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

Last week we selected 50 players who are synonymous with their squad numbers. Our readers spotted notable absentees so came up with an alternative list, which does not start at No 1 or end at No 50

Which footballers are inextricably linked to their shirt numbers? We picked out 50 players last week who have come to embody the numbers on their backs. It was not an exhaustive list – as our readers have proved. We couldn’t include some legends of the game – Pelé and Lionel Messi for a start – and we missed a few brilliant stories. Thankfully our readers have provided an alternative list. And, creative bunch that they are, they have begun with No 0 and ended with No 51 …

An honourable mention should go to Aberdeen’s No 0. Zerouali is one of the very few to have worn the number (before rules were changed in Scotland). He was a cult hero during a poor time at the club, who died tragically young in a car crash. Lewis

The Manchester City No 1 in the 1940s, 1950s and 1960s. Your article said that Lev Yashin was an automatic choice for No 1. While mention was made of other goalkeepers, there was no nod in the direction of the great Bert Trautmann, the German prisoner of war who became a Manchester City legend. Yashin himself said “my only equal is the German boy at Manchester City”.

Bert’s goalkeeping achievements were many. He only started playing as a keeper in a prisoner of war team before signing for St Helens in the Lancashire Combination League on release from prison. A year later he signed for City, where he made more than 500 appearances as well as playing the last 17 minutes of the 1956 FA Cup final with a broken neck in the days before substitutes were allowed, making important saves towards the end to ensure a City victory. He was also the first goalkeeper voted Footballer of the Year.

Perhaps most remarkable of all, he saved 60% of penalties he faced. Unfortunately, he was not given any international recognition. As a German citizen he could not play for England and could only play for Germany if playing in the country. He would have been a certain starter in the Germany team that won the World Cup in 1954.

He also did as much as anyone to repair British-German relations after the second world war. He went from being booed by 20,000 people on his City debut to being feted by more than 50,000 fans at his 1964 testimonial. He was awarded an honorary OBE in 2004. David Cronin

The greatest living Englishman; the best left-back to have played the game; the best player Nottingham Forest have ever had; and my hero (and Paolo Maldini’s). Will Kitchen

The captain who drove Leeds forwards for well over a decade. Captain of club and country, Bremner was the human personification of Don Revie’s team of the 1960s and 1970s. He had skill, drive, aggression, camaraderie and he played close to (and over?) the line. Thirty years after his early death, he is still the player who Leeds fans hold up as representing the qualities they value in players. It is fitting that the only player to come close to him while wearing the white No 4 was another diminutive, copper-haired Scotsman, Gordon Strachan. As the wee Scotsman with the statue outside Elland Road said: “Side before self, every time.” Nick Ramage

Stan Cullis, the Wolves manager at the time, converted Wright from wing-half to centre-half. He was little more than 5ft 8in, which suggests he would not be a good fit in the position, but he could jump and time it right. He captained England and was the first footballer in the world to gain 100 caps. He also captained Wolves and led them to some headline victories, most notably against Honved. Most importantly of all: he represents football before 1960, when football was more open, more exciting and more rapid in the transition. Kevin R Cox

He was the greatest ever No 6. If Pelé described him as the best defender he ever played against, how can the Guardian not choose him? He excelled at timing, passing and the ability to read a game. He also inspired West Ham and England with his presence. Tim Evans

There are still a few of us left who had the great pleasure to see the greatest No 7 of all time play, for Blackpool and Stoke City. His return to Stoke in the 1960s rejuvenated a team that had been stuck in the Second Division for a long time and helped put them back where they belonged, in the top flight. Matthews was the greatest No 7 of his day and, I would suggest, of all time. John Heath

I never saw him play but my father extolled his virtues to me. In this day and age when players are well paid, maybe overpaid, there should be a remembrance of those who went before them and created this wonderful game we call football. Nick Johnson

So neither of the two greatest players to play the game, at least in my lifetime, is included. If we are talking about the beautiful game, then either Pelé (my preference) or Messi has to be the No 10. Richard Hirst

Simply one of the best Premier League players this decade. His number 11 was often seen from behind, with the three defenders he was about to bamboozle looking worried in the background. Joe

Keane was the most iconic and influential No 16. In the era of omnipresent Manchester United pundits, it’s easy to focus on the contemporary theatrical Keane playing the disgruntled, unimpressed one in the studio so well. But don’t lose sight of Keane winning duels, covering every blade of grass and spraying un-Hollywood but massively effective balls forward. Think Stadio delle Alpi, the scene where he tamed Zinedine Zidane and mounted a comeback against Juventus that enabled the Treble and all that. Think Lansdowne Road in 2001 when he essentially knocked out the Netherlands from the 2002 World Cup. Does Rodri have a movie with Steve Coogan in it? Michael Craig

While I like Iván Zamorano and his little plus sign, Klinsmann wore 18 for much longer. He won the World Cup and European Championship (as captain) in it. He also decided to continue wearing it when No 9 was available, which Zamorano never did. Jochen

The number was initially forced upon him at Eibar, but he kept it at Valencia because it meant following in the footsteps of Pablo Aimar. He disproved the received wisdom that shorter, “weaker”, technical players who sparkled in the Italian and Spanish leagues would not cope with the physicality of football in England. His tactical intelligence and impeccable technique were emblematic of the change in European football that was becoming impossible for English teams to ignore.

He was one of the key components of Manchester City’s all-conquering team, but he represents more than that: the move to a positional game; conserving and recycling possession as a means of placing pressure on the opponents; building up from the back, enabled by an offensive technical player dropping deep. These innovations would have reached the English game anyway, but his presence provided some initial familiarity with them.

He made 125 appearances for Spain, scoring 35 goals and providing 29 assists. He also guided Real Sociedad into a successful spell in his mid-30s. Toby

His shirt number was retired when he moved to Borussia Dortmund and became, at the time, the most expensive 17-year-old in history. I’ve had the privilege of seeing two of Birmingham City’s greats – Trevor Francis will always be my No 8 shirt – putting seasoned defenders on their backsides or scoring brilliant goals. Francis and Bellingham were, and are, Blues legends. Ribs

The raumdeuter won so much with Bayern Munich. For Germans and younger people, if you think of 25, you think of him. He is a legendary player and great person who deserved a mention. Fionn

It has to be Doncaster Rovers legend James Coppinger. “Copps” has been associated with Rovers for more than 20 years. He joined in 2004 and dazzled on the wing for many years before retiring aged 40 in 2021 after a club-record 695 appearances. He wore the No 26 for the entirety of his career, having originally worn it as an academy graduate at Newcastle United, for whom he came on as a substitute to partner Alan Shearer up front in one Premier League game against Tottenham.

Coppinger is not only famed for his longevity. He scored some crucial goals in the club’s rise up the EFL to become an established Championship side, including a sensational hat-trick in a 5-1 win over Southend that sent the club to Wembley in 2008, when they beat Leeds to earn promotion to the second tier. He also scored the dramatic last-second winner that took the club back up as champions of League One in 2013 away at Brentford.

Coppinger is now a first-team coach at the club under Grant McCann, so continues his strong association with Doncaster, having been a central figure in all of their successes across the past 22 years. Adam

AIK are not my club but the death of Ivan Turina – who died in his sleep at the age of 32 in 2013 – shocked all of Swedish football. He wore No 27 and, for 10 straight years, AIK fans chanted his name in the 27th minute of every game. Even though he was only with AIK for four seasons, the Croatian goalkeeper seemed as if he had been there forever, the way he embodied the AIK attitude: completely unapologetic, fearlessly loyal to his own, giving no quarter to his opponents.

It was Dinamo Zagreb supporters who gave him the chant “Ivan Hooligan” but it was seamlessly taken over by AIK fans. His death came at a very sensitive time as AIK had just abandoned their beloved and historic Råsunda Stadium. He might not be well known internationally but, for those of us who follow the Allsvenskan, No 27 always belongs to Turina. David

Christian Vieri for 32! I can’t believe he wasn’t even mentioned! Unforgivable! Vieri is my favourite player of all time! Domingo

A tragic story of a world-class prodigy being struck down in his prime. The amount of former teammates who continue to wear his shirt number is a tribute to his impact. Jamie

No 37 should really have been Rob Lee. He did not wear the shirt for that long – he was No 7 at Newcastle until Ruud Gullit stripped him of that number – but it was long enough to name his autobiography after it. Josh Brown

Maeda, who is relentlessly wearing the No 38 for Ipswich, had his doubters during his time in Glasgow but his epic finish to last season was a fitting farewell for one of Celtic’s recent greats. A regular for Japan, I hope he can make a success of his Premier League move and warrant a place on your next list. Terence Brown

Anelka will always be the iconic No 39 for me, as someone who grew up watching the Premier League in the 2000s. Goals, sulks, titles; he had the lot. Sorry, Bruno Guimarães. Oliver Hatfield

There is a famous children’s song in Italy that talks about 44 cats (gatti) walking in a line. So the shirt number is a play on words with the player’s surname. He wore it at Perugia and Vicenza. PG

Forrest has spent 16 years at Celtic, where he has played more than 500 games, scored more than 100 goals and won 24 trophies. He has done way more to deserve the No 49 than a good young player at Arsenal. Gerry Stuart

While I understand you had to stop somewhere, and 50 is a nice round number, it’s a shame you couldn’t extend your list to 51, where Willian Pacho has staked a claim for being the best to ever wear it. He’s probably the only notable footballer to opt for that number, but that doesn’t diminish his achievement.

While Ousmane Dembélé is the poster boy for the new, all-conquering Paris Saint-Germain, Pacho was the final piece in the defensive puzzle that allowed that world-class attack to roll through everyone. He somehow manages to combine the positional intelligence and range of passing befitting an old-school libero with the speed and athleticism expected of modern centre-backs. That he’s also a graduate of Independiente del Valle in Ecuador – one of the great, unexpected flowerings of talents in today’s football – makes him even more worthy of note. Kári Tulinius
