Do Our Choices Reflect Our Values?

We often hear about the importance of living according to our values. Yet asserting our values is not the same as examining how they shape our decisions. When we lose sight of them or face competing commitments and pressures, we can sometimes fail to act on them. On the other hand, understanding how to incorporate them into our decision-making can help us act on them more consistently.

According to Lilach Sagiv and colleagues, personal values are “broad desirable goals that motivate people’s action and serve as guiding principles in their lives.”

As goals, values are not directed towards specific achievements but rather guide decisions across various aspects of our lives. For example, people who value honesty will try to tell the truth and seek out honest people wherever they may be, whether at work, at home, or elsewhere.

Values are desirable, and we think they are worth pursuing, and we want to pursue them consistently, even when other desires compete with them. However, competing desires may capture our attention. For example, ordering a second dessert at a celebratory meal does not necessarily mean I no longer value my long-term health. In this particular instance, I may simply be making an exception to how I usually act on that commitment.

As guiding principles, values serve not only as the lens through which we perceive our experiences, but they also become strong factors in how we choose to act. For example, people who value autonomy over security may see their job as stifling and look for other opportunities; those who value security over autonomy see the same job as stable and dependable.

When I talk about values, I like to say that values are things that we value, things we care about. This simple sentence shifts our attention from abstract concepts to the things we hold close and see as part of who we are. It also lets us see the difference between ordering that second dessert and working to be trustworthy. The former satisfies a momentary desire; the latter speaks to an enduring concern.

Comparing what we say matters to us with how we consistently act can reveal a gap between our values and behavior. Imagine someone who says they value their personal relationships, but they repeatedly choose work over spending time with friends and family. This discrepancy between what a person says and what they do should bring reflection. It may be that the person does value personal relationships, but their competing value of financial security or personal ambition has a stronger influence on their decision-making. Alternatively, they may truly prioritize personal relationships but lose sight of values when responding to workplace demands. Sometimes, however, someone may recognize their priorities clearly and still have limited freedom to act because their job is insecure or their family depends on their income. Importantly, we can examine whether our repeated behaviors reflect what we value and consider what choices would help us act more consistently on what we care about.

Bas Verplanken and Rob Holland found that values influence people’s choices and behavior when they are brought into focus and when those values are central to the person’s self-concept.
