‘La Bola Negra’ Defied Hollywood’s Expectations—and Made Movie Stars out of Miguel Bernardeau and Guitarricadelafuente

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

In 1936, Federico García Lorca, the Spanish poet and playwright, was working on a new play, La Bola Negra, which was never completed. Only four pages of it were found. Later that year, the 38-year-old homosexual anti-fascist sympathizer was executed during the Spanish Civil War on the orders of right-wing military authorities. Ninety years later, Spanish directors Javier Calvo and Javier Ambrossi have crafted a new, ambitious film named after these scant remnants. Their glamorous but gritty film stars Spaniards Miguel Bernardeau, of Elite fame, along with Álvaro Lafuente Calvo, the singer known as Guitarricadelafuente, in his first major acting role. La Bola Negra is an epic that spans close to a century; it follows several gay men whose lives are bound together by their identities, love and loss, and the looming presence of the Spanish Civil War, all under the shadow of García Lorca’s work.

There are works of art that escape definitions, pieces of media that transcend. La Bola Negra, both the film and the play, is a biography and a document of war; a love story and a tale of generational trauma; an examination of queerness in Spanish history. The film contains stories that speak of fear, of desire, of guilt, and of shame. And it is a work that defies the easy summary of a logline.

“So you would like us to craft that sentence for you,” Bernardeau, 29, says, laughing while sitting next to Guitarricadelafuente at a restaurant in Manhattan. He’s athletic and handsome. He comes across as a classic macho onscreen, perhaps a residue of the characters he’s been cast as, but in person he is softer and warm, thoughtful. His hair is cut in a shaggy semi-mullet, and he’s wearing a Dolce & Gabbana black T-shirt featuring a young Monica Bellucci, a vintage find.

“It’s a movie that when you finish it, it makes you want to live,” Guitarricadelafuente, 29, says. His curly hair spills over his forehead onto his wire-frame eyeglasses. As Guitarrica—a diminutive for guitar in Aragonese (his family has roots in Aragon) and his stage-name shorthand—he sings about romance and sex, mixing the sounds of Spanish tradition with the synths and reverberations of pop. Like Bernardeau, he’s disarmingly attractive, but his persona is more open. Not unlike their characters, Bernardeau appears more stoic, as if he’s standing on firmer ground; Guitarrica is lighter on his feet and bashful, perhaps settling into the newness of being a movie star.

“Not to be dramatic,” Guitarrica continues, “but people, friends that have seen the movie with us, it takes them a moment to compose themselves after,” he says. “Like, fuck, it’s an intense film. I think it makes you want to live and not do anything half-hearted.” Bernardeau looks at his costar with admiration and utters a line from the film: “Nadie desaparece del todo” (No one disappears completely). “It’s a phrase that Rafael says that keeps coming back to me,” he says.

With the enormity of approaching García Lorca, an idol for Spaniards, a paragon for writers, and a hero for queer men, Calvo and Ambrossi (both modern versions of all these things) were not bold enough to attempt to complete La Bola Negra. Instead they built an affecting universe around it, drawing inspiration from it and other works by García Lorca, as well as co-writer Alberto Conejero’s La Piedra Oscura. The latter was itself loosely inspired by Rafael Rodríguez Rapún, a companion of García Lorca and a member of his theater troupe, who died as a soldier during the civil war.

The film tells three stories: that of Sebastián (Guitarrica) in 1937, a young trumpeter taken into the Nationalist army who encounters Rafael (Bernardeau) as a new prisoner put under his watch; of Carlos (Milo Quifes) in 1932, the lead character in García Lorca’s original play and a young gay man who is denied membership to a casino club because of his sexuality; and in 2017, of Alberto (Carlos González), a modern-day historian who, following the death of his grandfather and an excavation into his past, finds himself indelibly connected to the other men. In Carlos’s vignette, club members vote on his admission using black and white balls, where a black ball (la bola negra) signifies a negative. When Carlos receives several, he has to confront his father. The tension between shame and confession becomes the heart of the film.

Sebastián is a character who is, as Guitarrica puts it, “like a custom-made suit” for him. He ran into Ambrossi and Calvo after performing at the Primavera Sound festival in Barcelona two years ago. “It was one of those nights where, after a few drinks, you kind of forget what was said,” he says with a laugh. But he heard from Calvo after that, and soon he was in Madrid meeting with the directors. “I thought they’d ask me to make a song for the soundtrack,” he says. Instead they told him they were writing a film with Conejero, and offered him the role of Sebastián. “I wasn’t confident at all, nothing was clear,” Guitarrica says, “but I saw the faith they had in me.”

Bernardeau, who was invited to audition for Rafael, also had his doubts. “I didn’t think I did well,” Bernardeau says of his audition. “I was like damn, I missed the opportunity of a lifetime.” But after a week and a half of self-inflicted torture, he was asked to read with Guitarrica and was then offered the role. He was sure about his costar: “The choice of Álvaro made perfect sense,” he says.

Bernardeau and Guitarrica were familiar with each other’s work. “But we met as Sebastián and Rafael,” Bernardeau says. These days they appear to have the kind of friendship, of reverence for each other, of two men who’ve gone to war together—even if just on camera.

Despite its hefty emotional punch and astonishing staging, La Bola Negra is a film about war. Human stories are its spoils, and the unrealized potential of lost lives is buried on the battlefields. The Spanish Civil War still looms darkly over the country. Their dead, from unknown soldiers to acclaimed names like García Lorca, have become Spain’s ghosts.

“I felt a ton of responsibility,” Bernardeau says of playing Rafael, with his connection to a real person. “There is a responsibility in telling the story of someone who is no longer here,” he says. “By making this film, I found that he is important to many people, and I didn’t know that.” Bernardeau knew of García Lorca much like anyone who learns about their home country’s foremost literary giants in school, but the life of Rafael perhaps eluded the textbooks. (Ambrossi and Calvo gave him a reading list, and Bernardeau got to work.)

For Guitarrica, the process was more internal. The character of Sebastián was crafted for him, which afforded him the license to find himself in the role, he says, and consider history by imagining what it would have been like to have lived it.

“There was a lot of introspection,” he says. Guitarrica was also learning how to arrive at certain emotions “and unearth my own experiences to be able to give nuance to my character,” he says. Back in Madrid, during that first meeting with the directors, they asked him if he had any emotional traumas. “I said no, and they said, we’ll see,” he says, laughing. Acting, Guitarrica has found, means excavating old wounds. Did he encounter any turmoil? “Well, yes,” he laughs, “but I’m not telling you about that.”

Since the film premiered at the Cannes Film Festival in May and received a 20-minute standing ovation, with Ambrossi and Calvo winning the award for best direction (alongside Fatherland director Pawel Pawlikowski), Bernardeau and Guitarrica have covered magazines in Spain and beyond and become two of the internet’s latest fixations. La Bola Negra will also represent Spain in the best international feature film category at the Academy Awards, should it be nominated.

“I love that it’s a story that we feel is so ours, that is such a fundamental part of our society and our collective imagination,” Bernardeau says. “The war has impacted us through generations, and it is part of us in different ways.”

He says he doesn’t want to romanticize, but the global reception of such a regional story has given him some faith in humanity. “Because, in the end, it shows that we’re all different, but all the same; that the most personal is also the most creative,” he says, making sure I know that he’s quoting a phrase attributed to Martin Scorsese.

“I think that this film is largely about an idea that is pretty straightforward,” Guitarrica says. “Simply, it’s about the drive to live. Of not leaving anything on the table or unfinished. And that is a very universal story.”
