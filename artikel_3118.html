What the EU Quantum Act changes for quantum companies?

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

The full text of the EU’s Quantum Act, published by the European Commission on Wednesday, confirms what drafts had suggested for weeks. Brussels wants to turn Europe’s strong quantum research into an industry by steering public money towards European companies.

Indeed, something that Henna Virkkunen mentioned in the press conference this morning is that we don’t lack the bright minds, actually, we might be sitting on a mine, yet we still lack the capital and the context to be able to transform quantum research into commercial products.

We attract 5% of global private funding, against 50% in the US.

The 94-page proposal, however, sets aside no money for research or companies, leaving that to the EU’s next long-term budget.

According to the proposal, Europe is home to around a third of the world’s quantum companies, and its governments have also announced €8.6bn in public quantum funding, more than the €5.3bn announced in the US.

Yet, private investors have been far more cautious in Europe, with US quantum startups raising about €7bn in 2025, against €1.1bn for their EU rivals.

At the heart of the Act sits the Quantum Europe Framework Initiative, which pulls national programmes under one EU strategy. Member states have largely run their own races until now, as France showed when it placed a €500M bet on homegrown champions.

The initiative will be run by the EuroHPC Joint Undertaking, the body that already manages Europe’s public supercomputers. It will fund grand challenges, contests aimed at the engineering problems that still hold the technology back. And it will also build shared testing and communication infrastructure inside the EU.

For companies, the Act creates a small number of innovation hubs offering engineering help, equipment, and training, plus at least one acceleration centre in every member state.

Projects that are ready for the market can be named strategic projects, which gives them easier access to EU funding for scaling up production.

The procurement rule has the sharpest edges, because public bodies may only buy quantum technology from EU-controlled companies. The one exception is partners the EU has approved through agreements with their home countries. The rule reaches down to subcontractors, and the Commission can require certain critical parts to come only from EU-controlled suppliers.

The bar for counting as EU-controlled is high, as a company qualifies only if no government, company, or citizen from outside the EU controls it.

Under that test, the European subsidiaries of US and Chinese groups would not qualify, and the same test decides who can use the new hubs and shared facilities, and who can take part in strategic projects.

The Act also tackles the security risk that has made quantum a priority for governments, since a powerful enough quantum computer could break today’s encryption. Six months after the law takes effect, central government bodies will have to buy quantum-safe products for their critical systems.

Countries must report their progress every year, and the Commission can impose binding deadlines on those that fall behind. An EU roadmap from 2025 already asks governments to protect their most sensitive systems by the end of 2030.

And here we reach the most important part: we have the minds, we have the network, but do we have money?

The Act’s own budget comes to about €71M between 2028 and 2034, and it only pays for running the new system. It covers Commission staff, IT, and support for the new hubs and centres, rather than research or companies.

The money for research, infrastructure, and companies would come from the EU’s next long-term budget, which starts in 2028 and is still being negotiated. The text proposal names the successor to Horizon Europe and the planned European Competitiveness Fund as the likely sources, but states that the Act creates no automatic right to that funding.

Moreover, the Commission estimates that Europe’s quantum projects will need €12.9bn to €18.1bn between 2028 and 2034, to be met by the EU budget, national governments, and private investors. Officials have long described the law as a framework rather than a pot of money for exactly this reason.

The biggest financial idea in the package, the Sovereign Tech Equity Fund, is only an option in the text. The Act says the EU may set it up but names no size, so the €50bn figure rests.

The fund is meant to back the EU’s chips and cloud laws as well, with large cheques for companies that have outgrown the EU’s scale-up funds.

Another aspect that the proposal also does is set no target for how powerful Europe’s quantum computers should become. The gap stands out, given that the EU’s 2025 quantum strategy aims to make Europe the world leader by 2030.

And probably the most asked question is what should we do with this Act, and how will this help Europe become less dependent on the US and China?

The proposal now goes to the European Parliament and member states, which will debate and amend it, a process that usually takes around two years. And, if it survives in the actual form, the new structures would start in 2028, with the full programme running from 2030.
