If I Had to Start Investing Again With $10,000, These 3 Dividend Stocks Would Come First

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Dollar General, Ralph Lauren, and Tyson Foods all sell things that people keep buying in good times and bad, and they pay their shareholders to be patient through the bad times.

By Micah Zimmerman – Nov 24, 2026 at 8:48AM EST

Picture a blank brokerage account and a fresh $10,000. The temptation might be to invest in whatever trend or industry is loudest this week, but tactics like those are how many beginners get burned. I'd do the opposite and look for investment themes that benefit from more basic ideas — what people spend money on when they're stressed, celebrating, or hungry.

I'd also lean on dividend payers in those areas. Checks that show up reliably whether a stock is up or down make it easier to sit still, and if you use a dividend reinvestment plan, you can use those payouts to boost the compound growth of your assets.

Dollar General (DG +3.38%) is where cash-strapped families shop when budgets get tight, and CEO Todd Vasos is blunt about that.

"Our core customers continue to be financially constrained with a variety of factors impacting their budget," he said on the August earnings call. Yet traffic grew for a fifth straight quarter, and "we were especially pleased to see our share gains accelerate."

The company says about 75% of Americans live within five miles of one of its more than 21,000 stores. That’s a footprint and level of accessibility that would be tough to replicate.

What I like most is that shoppers outside of its core demographics are broadening their horizons. Dollar General says more customers with household incomes above $100,000 are switching to the chain for value. It's also leaning on more than 2,000 items priced at or below $1, and it plans to restart share buybacks this quarter.

Here's the part to be honest about. Management has held the dividend steady at $0.59 per quarter for more than three years, and at the current share price, it yields about 1.8%, so this is a stability pick, not an income pick. High fuel prices are also squeezing its shoppers, and the tariff refunds the company got back after the Supreme Court struck down President Donald Trump’s tariffs helped this quarter's profits. But those refunds will not buoy its numbers for long.

I know, a polo shirt company on an income-stock list sounds odd. Ralph Lauren (RL +1.47%) has spent years making its brand harder to discount, and it's working.

On Nov. 5, it raised its full-year outlook again, with revenue in its fiscal 2027 Q2 up 10% in constant currency, sales in Asia up 28%, and 1.7 million new direct-to-consumer customers. "We delivered strong second-quarter performance, enabling us to raise our fiscal 2027 outlook once again," CEO Patrice Louvet said.

"We have, I believe we can say, the most loyal customer in the industry," Louvet told analysts. Shoppers are paying full price too, with average unit retail up 9%. That's a sign of a company with real economic moat power, not just one attracting customers with discounts.

The trade-off is that this is the least dividend-heavy name of the three, with a yield of about 1%. Sales in Europe are soft, with comparable store sales down 1%, and management is planning for U.S. tariff rates to climb back into the high teens in its fiscal fourth quarter.

Tyson Foods (TSN +1.47%) is the messy one, and that's why it's useful. Its beef business is losing money amid tight cattle supply, and Tyson expects fiscal 2027 beef operating income to be between a loss of $300 million and a profit of $100 million. Its chief operating officer called it plainly: "The challenges in our Beef segment are a cattle cycle issue, not a broad deterioration in the business."

Meanwhile, the rest of the company is carrying its weight. Chicken earned $1.9 billion in a year when prices fell, and the prepared foods segment, home of Jimmy Dean and Hillshire Farm, grew operating income by 5.5% to $1.3 billion. New CEO Jeff Schomburger has an easy pitch: "Selling more protein is fun." He also admits, "Our innovation success rate is not where it needs to be."

The board still raised the quarterly dividend to $0.52 per Class A share, the company’s 15th straight annual increase. That works out to about a 3.1% dividend yield at the current share price, the highest of my three. Adjusted earnings per share fell 17% in fiscal 2026 to $3.44, and the CFO warned that chicken and pork headwinds "could outweigh the controllable improvements."

If I were starting over, I wouldn't try to be clever. I'd begin building my portfolio around a store people need, a brand people love, and a food company people can't stop eating, and I'd let the dividends keep arriving while I learned. A good buy-and-hold strategy is mostly patience, and each of these investments gives you something to be patient about: a growing store base, a pricing-power brand, and a dividend streak that has survived a rough beef cycle.
