Dr. Linehan, You Changed the World

Posted November 11, 2026 | Reviewed by Monica Vilhauer Ph.D.

Back in the early 1990s, I did my undergrad psychology degree. My first-year psychology textbook had a whole chapter on personality disorders. It basically described personality disorders as unchanging, difficult ways of being; this was even how they were classified in the DSM-IV. The textbook didn’t say it directly, but the implication was that if patients didn’t get better as quickly as the psychiatrist wanted them to or didn’t follow the clinician’s instructions, then it was clearly the patient’s fault and they should be labelled as having borderline personality disorder. Patients were considered to be difficult, untreatable, and neurotic. They blamed the patient, even though they also knew that the majority of people diagnosed as BPD had a history of childhood sexual abuse.

In the corner of my first-year textbook chapter, in a little box, there were three paragraphs about a radical alternative theory of borderline personality disorder, developed by US psychologist Dr. Marsha Linehan. She dared to question the mainstream view by suggesting that borderline personality disorder is due to a combination of temperament and an invalidating environment, rather than being the patient's fault. She then even more radically suggested that BPD is treatable, and people who had previously been written off by psychiatrists as being broken for life can go on to live safe, healthy, and meaningful lives.

Dr. Linehan developed a specific therapy called dialectical behavior therapy (DBT), which helps people learn a number of skills to live a life worth living. There are distress tolerance skills, which help you safely and calmly get through a rough few minutes despite strong urges to self-harm, pick a fight, or make things worse. There are emotion regulation skills, which are the things that you do long term to help you live on a more even keel and be less likely to need to use your distress tolerance skills. Interpersonal effectiveness skills help you get your needs met and feel heard in ways that don’t inadvertently lead to rejection or more harm. Woven throughout these skills are also mindfulness skills, because if you can’t bring yourself back to where you are right now and can’t recognise how you’re feeling in this moment, then you won’t be able to choose the right skills to help yourself.

I am a DBT-trained therapist, though I’m not currently working in a DBT service. Yet there are therapy skills developed by Dr. Linehan within DBT that I use every day in my practice, and I suspect almost all therapists now use in their practice. For example, she taught me how to validate more effectively in ways that validate the person's emotion, even when the behaviour that followed the emotion wasn’t helpful. Instead of "why did you self-harm?", I might say "it sounds like you were really sad and unsure what to do." More importantly, she brought the idea of dialectics into therapy, where two opposing ideas can both be true; the most crucial one is "I'm doing the best I can, and I can do better.”

So, Dr. Linehan, thank you for developing a theory and a therapy that taught me many specific important skills. But more importantly, thank you for changing the entire psychiatric world so we understand extreme human suffering with more compassion and less patient blaming. Dr. Marsha Linehan, you changed the world.

Koerner, K. (2012). Doing dialectical behavior therapy: A practical guide. The Guilford Press.

Linehan, M. M. (1993). Cognitive-behavioral treatment of borderline personality disorder. Guilford Press

Linehan, M. M. (2020). Building a life worth living: A memoir. Random House.
