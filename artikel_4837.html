In Defense of Clever Hans

Posted November 21, 2026 | Reviewed by Ekua Hagan

Proponents of facilitated communication (FC)—a discredited intervention in which a nondisabled facilitator helps a profoundly autistic person spell out sophisticated messages on a letter board or keyboard—really do not like it when critics point to the famous case of Clever Hans, calling this analogy “indefensibly dehumanizing” and, as Marina Weiler recently argued on this site, “cruel.”

But the Clever Hans argument is not, as critics charge, about “comparing autistic people to animals.” It is about illustrating how subtly and unintentionally behavior can be cued.

No one explicitly taught this horse to stop tapping his hoof at some predetermined signal. He learned from observing tiny, unconscious movements of his trainer’s head that a commission of experts sent to observe Hans’ seemingly extraordinary abilities couldn’t spot, even under close scrutiny (a graduate student in psychology finally figured it out). If a horse can learn to respond to signs barely observable to the human eye, FC skeptics (such as myself) point out, humans can certainly do the same, and likely to even more imperceptible—or not so imperceptible—cues, such as changes in breathing, pitch, or posture; verbal prompts; or shifting the letter board in the air.

Importantly, facilitators are also subject to influence in the form of common psychological biases. An amazing example of how strong the desire to believe can affect facilitators aired earlier this year on "The Today Show." After publishing his first novel, speller Woody Brown and his mother, who facilitates for him, were interviewed by Jenna Bush Hager. As a camera positioned behind the pair documented, there was absolutely no correlation between Woody’s random jabs at the letter board and the articulate responses his mother “read.”

In short, the Clever Hans story is raised as part of the case that authorship in spelling cannot be judged based solely on how it looks to the observer. Especially because it is so easy to expose facilitator control: Just ask the speller to produce information that is definitively unknown to their facilitator, i.e., by showing them an object that the facilitator hasn’t seen and asking them to identify it. This has been done in dozens of studies dating back to the 1990s, and FC has failed every time. A recent review confirms that independent authorship in any form of FC—including more recent variants, Rapid Prompting Method (RPM) and Spelling to Communicate (S2C)—has never been demonstrated under controlled conditions.

In response, the spelling community has simply refused to participate in any more of these tests. I will never understand why the debate didn’t end there. That is just not how the scientific method operates. Good research starts with the null hypothesis—the assumption that the intervention under investigation doesn’t work. Once evidence is obtained of a positive effect, conscientious researchers try to account for any other possible factor that could explain their findings, such as the placebo effect—or, in the case of FC, facilitator control. It is only once these other explanations have been ruled out that efficacy can be established. Instead, FC researchers offer “qualitative research” (i.e., interviews with spellers who use FC products to prove FC) and indirect, technology-intensive studies involving eye-tracking apparatuses, computer algorithms, FMRI, and accelerometers that claim to prove FC works even though the facilitators in these tests are never blinded.

By far, the most baffling question I’m asked by spelling proponents is, “Why do you care?” In other words: If I object to spelling, I should just not use it with my profoundly autistic son, Jonah. Why do I have to oppose it so publicly?

I’ve thought about this a lot recently, because spelling has become the wedge issue that is fracturing the profound autism community. And I am heartsick, because I know this diminishes our power to effect the changes our kids desperately need to flourish.

But spelling is not like vitamins, diet, brushing, or any of the countless alternative therapies that parents of profoundly autistic children, myself included, have experimented with since our kids were small. We can’t just agree to disagree, because the demands of spelling proponents put my son at risk even if he is never introduced to a letter board. Their increasingly successful fights to force school districts and state disability agencies to pay for spelling instruction and facilitator access drain resources from desperately underfunded systems.

And, if spelling is normalized with no validation requirements whatsoever, what’s to stop an unscrupulous provider—at some point in the future when my husband and I are gone—from telling our four other kids, "Jonah spelled that he doesn’t want to see you, so you’re no longer allowed to check on him" or "Jonah spelled that he actually gave his iPad to the aide who had stashed it in the trunk of his car" or "Jonah spelled that he wants to transfer his guardianship/SSI rep payee to his group home manager" or "Jonah spelled that he consented to sex with another resident of his group home." The opportunities for exploitation are so obvious and so tremendous that I’m shocked that spelling proponents refuse to even consider them.

But even if this issue did not affect Jonah at all, I would still care about the spellers—how could I not? Profound autism has stripped so much agency from my son. But Jonah’s basic communication does allow him to exert some control over his life, and we try to maximize those opportunities as much as possible. He tells us what he wants: ketchup and hamburger and French fries, “fun list” with Mommy, Costco with Daddy, water park with… anyone who will take him. Many spellers have similar levels of verbal communication, but practitioners teach parents that this speech is “unreliable” or “scripting” and should be ignored in favor of what is spelled. I just can’t imagine how frustrating it must be to be completely stripped of any ability to communicate your needs and preferences.

Finally, as someone who has spent more than a quarter of a century loving someone with profound autism, and spending a lot of time with other profoundly autistic people and their families, I resent what I can only describe as the character assassination perpetuated by spelling proponents, who locate the refusal to participate in controlled studies with the spellers themselves—who allegedly, to a person, find these tests so dehumanizing and offensive they could not possibly enroll on principle.

Spelling skepticism in the face of both the overwhelming evidence of facilitator control and documented harm is rational, not “hate speech.” Spellers, if they were really as brilliant as their prose suggests, would understand that. They would eagerly partner with researchers because they would know that controlled tests represent the fastest path to insurance coverage, public funding, curricular inclusion, and legal standing.

So no, I just do not believe that every single one of the estimated 10,000 spellers in this country is so petty, so selfish, that they would let the estimated 30 million nonspeakers around the world languish, "locked in silent cage[s],” simply because they’re insulted that mainstream experts and policy makers would dare ask for evidence. I am certain, in fact, they would be demanding to provide it.

Spelling proponents have thrown around a lot of accusations, claiming that critics are uninformed or ableist or just protecting their professional turf. But this debate is actually the same one that has unfolded countless times in a community in which 90 percent of families have experimented with alternative treatments: Does it work—and not just according to anecdotal evidence, but under controlled conditions? Secretin failed that test. So have the gluten- and casein-free diet, chelation, hyperbaric oxygen therapy, and auditory integration training, among others. FC failed that test also.

If proponents believe those studies were faulty, they should absolutely test again. Until then, it is incumbent on our institutions—government agencies, school districts, the media, healthcare systems—to protect the fundamental right of profoundly autistic people to authentic communication.
