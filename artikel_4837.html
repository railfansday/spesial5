Moving by Numbers: The Trap of the Easily Measurable

In the previous post in this series on eating and moving by numbers, we reviewed many years of research (including hundreds of individual studies and over a dozen meta-analyses) on how tracking and quantification affect diet and exercise. And we found that the answer is, on average and in aggregate, “not much”.

In this post, I’ll explore a simple two-pronged hypothesis that’s compatible with that research literature, though not proven by it: that measuring allows you to change 1) only what is easily measurable and 2) only for as long as you keep measuring it.

This idea is sparked by the observation from last time that although none of the results from these meta-analyses of self-monitoring studies are particularly substantial, the biggest, clearest effect is that steps taken seem to increase while step-counting is happening. This raises the possibility that the tracking has more of an effect here because the behaviour of walking is more directly, simply, and meaningfully countable than other kinds of movement are, and a lot more so than eating is. A relatively simple machine can distinguish a step from a non-step, and once it has, then no more human effort is required: you walk, the machine records.

All other kinds of movement are trickier to track than a pace. For example, a recent New York Times article on “Why Weight Lifting Is Good for Your Heart” noted that the constraints and biases of measurement technology are one reason for the persistent sidelining of strength training in research on cardiovascular benefits of exercise: “One of the limitations of studying resistance training—and why it’s taken scientists so long to understand its benefit for the heart—is that exercises like squats and bench presses are hard to track with a wearable device. That means scientists must rely on self-reported data.” It’s frustrating to think just how much mere measurability has helped skew decades’ worth of public health advice towards endurance cardio and away from forms of movement that are increasingly recognised to offer a wider range of benefits.

The difficulty of measuring is even greater with eating than with squatting or cycling or skiing. Because you’re measuring the nutrition ingested rather than the behaviour of ingesting it, a lot more hassle is involved: measuring and weighing amounts of ingredients, calculating energy provision and macronutrient balance, comparing the amount of food prepared versus consumed. The sheer complication of committing to meaningful tracking of one’s eating is one reason why I think its cost/benefit ratio tends to be so poor, in ways I’ll explore in the next part in this series.

So the first step of our inference is that when you use measurement to incentivize change, the changes you end up incentivizing most are not the ones you care most about, the ones that will have the most benefits for anything else in your life—they’re merely the ones you can most easily measure. This leads to a second hypothesis, less directly supported by the existing evidence but certainly not contradicted by it: that these maximally measurable changes may be the least likely to be preserved after the measuring stops.

The formal follow-up data are patchy in general, and they seem to show significant decline across all the habits and “benefits” being studied; after you take away the measurement framework, things seem to revert to baseline at some point between quite slowly and very quickly!

(For example, Vetrovksy et al. (2022), analysing 75 studies, of which 24 included a follow-up phase, found that the step-count increase more than halved in a median of 12 weeks’ follow-up. Chaudhry et al. (2020), analysing 57 studies, report that within two years the increase is down to 10% of the four-month level, and by three to four years people are back to where they started. Freak-Poli et al. (2020), analysing 14 studies, report no change even at short follow-up points.)

Especially given that no other behaviours showed much change to begin with, we can’t confidently conclude anything about how the increases in step count decay relative to changes in other activities. Still, we might speculate that being guided by mere measurability entails a downplaying of other reasons why people might keep doing things: intrinsic rewards like in-the-moment pleasure, good fit with lifestyle and interests and environment, and good fit also with specific health and fitness goals.

Nothing against walking; I just published a personal blog post on what a lovely context it can be for thinking, and it can be delightful in many other ways too. But those delights are, I suspect, mostly not encouraged by the “how many steps have I taken?” lens. When done for counting’s sake, walking may foreground primarily the very quality of monotony that makes it so easy to measure.

A knowledgeable reader (Emily Nicholas Angl) shared an interesting extension of this thought: that immediate “improvements” generated by tracking technologies may have the exact opposite effects over the long term. It’s possible that where counting is strongly emphasised, there’s almost inevitably poor intrinsic drive to maintain the new habit, because the counting has been over-relied on to do all the incentivizing work—and has been applied only to activities that are uncomplicated enough to be readily measured. In this sense, the initial rewards may themselves become the vulnerability.

Looking at this research, it’s instructive to turn our attention to what’s being overshadowed by the quantification. In one paper I included in the review last time (Ferguson et al., 2022), there was a suggestion that quality of life tends to get ignored in the research because it takes longest to change. My intuition as a coach would be the opposite: that if you make changes that are genuinely right for you, greater happiness (or contentment, or purposefulness, etc) is likely to be an almost immediate result—indeed, that intending to enhance everyday experience should be a guide to which changes to make in the first place, because they’re the ones that are likeliest to get embedded and stay there. From this perspective, increasing day-to-day satisfaction is not a nice-to-have that may trickle in eventually, far “downstream” of the solid behavioural changes that really count. Instead, the very experience of having started doing it becomes the reason to keep doing it.

The absence of any mention of participants’ experiences of the changes being measured therefore seems telling in itself: it supports the idea of an overreliance on one ultra-simplistic extrinsic reward (the number) at the expense of any intrinsic rewards, and, in many cases, also of more personally meaningful extrinsic rewards (like specific kinds of ability regained or possibility unlocked). One might extrapolate from here to the benefits that mixed-methods research (including both quantitative measures and qualitative inquiry into participant experiences) could offer to medical research if it turned from an outlier to the norm.

A final dimension I’d like to weave into these speculations about why people do and don’t do the thing we’re asking them to and getting them to measure themselves doing is the question of individual attitude differences. I found myself wondering, as I trawled through the meta-analyses, whether what’s really being assessed in this research is less the cleverness of the tech in getting lazy, greedy humans to be more active and abstemious, but the pre-existing inclination of said humans to be more or less conscientious about the thing they’ve signed up for.

The differences between “compliers” and “non-compliers” (including “defiers”) are well documented as a confounding factor in health research. But I didn’t see any explicit acknowledgement of these dynamics in this literature, despite some studies finding big differences between “high-adherence” and “low-adherence” individuals (the most striking I found being Bertoli et al., 2025, where the former lost an average of 7 kg and the latter 3.5 kg over six months). As a participant, you get out what you put in—but you may not continue to once the study structures are over. And by definition, conscientious compliers won’t keep complying when there’s nothing to comply with! So if all that were driving the changes was conscientious compliance to a tenuous numerical rule, then once the study ends and the rule is lifted, the reasons to continue may seem similarly flimsy.
