University students are biggest spreaders of COVID

Heidi Ledford reports for Nature from Seattle, Washington.

Undergraduate students on university campuses played a big part in spreading viral variants during the COVID-19 pandemic.Credit: Leonard Ortiz/Digital First Media/Orange County Register/Getty

Universities have become hotbeds of SARS-CoV-2 transmission, according to a massive analysis of more than 85,000 viral genome sequences and other data collected from people with COVID-19.

The study, published on 19 November in the journal Cell1, finds that viral transmission between 2021 and 2023 was highest among adolescents aged 15 to 18 in schools, undergraduates at university, and residents in nursing facilities. Undergraduates, in particular, were consistently the first group to start spreading emerging genetic variants of SARS-CoV-2.

Surprisingly, however, staff members at all three types of institution had about the same risk of contracting COVID-19 as did those in their surrounding communities. So, for example, professors were about as likely to get COVID-19 from the coffee baristas in their university towns as they were to get it from a student at their university.

The analysis is among the largest to bring together viral genomic sequences with medical data from people with COVID-19. “The scale is truly impressive,” says Bill Hanage, who studies the evolution of infectious diseases at the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health in Boston, Massachusetts. “It’s an example of the depth and the detail of the picture that can be painted using the medium of population genomics.”

The findings were based on data collected from COVID-19 tests taken by people in Massachusetts, between November 2021 and January 2023. The genomic data allowed researchers to trace the path of SARS-CoV-2 variants that emerged over time, and to link this with epidemiological data about the study participants, such as their age and whether they had been vaccinated against COVID-19.

Overall, the team found that booster vaccinations helped to reduce SARS-CoV-2 transmission. This was true even for BA.1, an Omicron variant that started to circulate in Massachusetts in late 2021 — a striking result because COVID-19 vaccines at that time hadn’t yet been updated to protect against BA.1, Hanage says. “That’s an important illustration of how vaccines, even when they’re not perfect, can still be beneficial,” he says.

The team also used its data to compare SARS-CoV-2 transmission in specific environments, such as schools and hospitals, with the risk in surrounding communities. Young adults aged 18 to 22 surfaced as key actors in the spread of the virus, says Taylor Brock-Fisher, an infectious-disease epidemiologist at the Broad Institute of MIT and Harvard in Cambridge, Massachusetts, who is a co-author of the Cell study. “Variants emerged there first,” he says. “And these young adults are, at least in colleges, the most involved in transmission.”

That result fits with previous studies suggesting that universities are COVID-19 hotspots2. “It’s not surprising to find that the largest source of transmission on campuses was among undergraduate students, many of whom are required to live in dorms,” says Eleanor Murray, an epidemiologist at the University of British Columbia in Vancouver, Canada. Dormitories, where students live in close quarters, are “the hardest on-campus setting in which to control transmission”, she adds.

Brock-Fisher and his colleagues had hoped to look at how COVID-19 mitigation policies affected transmission, he says. But this was difficult to pin down against a backdrop of shifting mandates on face masks, social-distancing guidelines, viral variants and host immunity to SARS-CoV-2. “There was too much changing all at once to attribute [findings] to any one policy,” he says.

Many large studies of SARS-CoV-2 transmission have also struggled to draw conclusions about the effectiveness of such policies, because they varied so much among regions, cities, schools, care homes and other workplaces, says Justin Lessler, an infectious-disease epidemiologist at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill. “There are no good, central records of what was done,” he says.

Such data might be particularly helpful to unpick what was happening in schools, why the risk of infection was not as high for staff members as it was for students, and how best to protect both in the future. “It would be nice to know more about what worked and didn’t work,” Lessler says. “This is a step along that path, but it’s not the end of the path.”
