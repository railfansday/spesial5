How the ‘Elsinore’ Makeup Team Transformed Stars Andrew Scott and Olivia Colman to Bring the Story of Ian Charleson to Life

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

In “Elsinore,” Andrew Scott plays real-life actor Ian Charleson, who performed his final role while dying of AIDS.

Written by Stephen Beresford, “Elsinore” follows the deeply emotional and inspirational true story of Charleson as he confronts his mortality with courage, honesty and wit. On Nov. 13, 1989, Charleson walked onto the stage at London’s National Theatre to play Hamlet for the last time. He was dying of AIDS, a fact he kept secret. Very few of the 1,000 audience members at the Olivier Theatre knew his story. The biopic (currently in theaters) reunites Scott with Olivia Colman, who previously starred alongside him in “Fleabag.” This time, their bond is a special one as Colman plays Margaret Johnson, the doctor who treats Charleson.

For “Elsinore,” both actors needed to transform themselves — not only into the real people they were portraying, but also back to the 1980s. That’s where makeup department head Nadia Stacey played a key role. Stacey, who was honored at last month’s Middleburg Film Festival, spoke about her work and approach to the film, explaining that “when there are real people, you have to be sympathetic to them.”

Stacey described the ’80s as a tricky period to recreate, given it can easily become clichéd. “You immediately imagine everybody had a perm,” she said. “My approach every time is that you shouldn’t notice hair and makeup unless you actually want to notice hair and makeup. The story told itself. I just needed to tell his journey and put us in the era we were in without overdoing it.”

Stacey worked to achieve a look that embodied Scott’s portrayal of Charleson while keeping everything authentic. “We know Andrew as that — jet black hair and brown eyes,” Stacey explained. Charleson, however, had blonde hair and blue eyes. “We didn’t want to push something too far to jar the audience. Andrew and I talked about it, and we said we should approach it in the sense of how an actor at that time would have done it. They would have been dyeing their hair themselves.” Instead of bleaching Scott’s hair, Stacey gave him a full head of blonde highlights and blue contact lenses, capturing Charleson’s defining characteristics.

She would also track his journey after Charleson tested positive for HIV in 1986. As Charleson’s health declined, Stacey discolored Scott’s skin tone and added dark circles. For the sarcoma lesions, she used tattoo transfers, which she applied to Scott’s body.

Joining Stacey for the conversation was Colman, who also underwent a transformation to play Johnson. “The second you put a wig on someone, as an audience, you know they’re wearing a wig. So it’s hard to make sure that it looks good,” Stacey said. “I felt like we were telling a story about real people, and we needed to be as close to that as possible without pulling her out of the story. But from all the photos we had of Margaret, we know she had this blonde bob, so we tried it.” And it worked.

Colman had also done her research on playing Johnson and knew how important it was to stay as close to the character as possible. She recalled, “We tried on the wig in the makeup room, and because I had been watching Margaret, I was very much up for a wig.”

Colman pointed out that the film doesn’t glorify Charleson’s death. Throughout the film, casting director Jessica Ronane cast background actors who actually knew him. David Rintoul — Charleson’s actual best friend — plays the Ghost of Hamlet’s father. Some of the hospital cast were also friends of the late actor.

Colman shared, “The most interesting thing about finding out about Ian is every single person he spoke to said, ‘Ian was hilarious. Ian was funny.’ And so they wanted that. Everybody that they approached said, ‘Absolutely. Yes, please, count me in.’”

In the film’s final act, Johnson comes to see Charleson. The camera focuses primarily on Johnson being moved by his performance. “Andrew, bless his heart, came to be there that day. It was only on me, and they’d already filmed the other bits, but he insisted. On a very long, exhausting shoot, on his day off, he came in and said, ‘Absolutely, I’ll do it for you.’ So he was performing bits of ‘Hamlet’ for me.”

Fast forward to the film’s premiere at the London Film Festival, and Colman reflected, “I cried as it started till the end and had to pass tissues to the person next to me as well. And I think, for me, even though I’d seen it, it was seeing him in it and what it took to do that. He’s so incredible and so generous and so fun. Honestly, if I could just choose a handful of people to work for the rest of my career, he would absolutely be one of them.”

Colman also shared the film’s powerful response: “There’s something beautiful about film reminding you of these past heroes.”
