User Acceptance Testing for Founders: Six Steps Before You Sign Off

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Posted on Nov 19 • Originally published at ksofttechnologies.com

A feature can pass its tests and still fail the business.

That gap is exactly why user acceptance testing (UAT) deserves more attention during software handover.

For developers, passing functional tests may indicate that an implementation behaves as expected. For founders and operations leads, the question is different: can the people who will use this system complete their actual work with it?

This distinction becomes especially important when a company receives an MVP or SaaS product from an external development team. The build may be complete, but the business still needs to verify that the delivered product meets its requirements.

UAT provides a structured way to do that. Here are six practical steps I would follow before signing off.

Don't begin acceptance testing with random clicks.

Start by translating the agreed requirements into observable acceptance criteria. A requirement such as "users can manage orders" is too broad to test reliably.

Required customer and product information is validated.

The order can be updated according to business rules.

Relevant users can retrieve the order later.

Each criterion should describe an expected behaviour and a result that can be verified.

This also helps separate critical requirements from preferences. A missing function that blocks a core workflow should not be treated like a minor visual inconsistency.

I recommend maintaining a simple acceptance checklist linked to the relevant requirement or ticket. That gives testers and developers a shared reference rather than relying on memory or informal approval.

Watch out for: Requirements that describe screens or features but never define what successful business use looks like.

Acceptance scenarios should represent how people actually use the product.

A developer may know the intended workflow, but the operations team understands the exceptions, handoffs, and everyday decisions that happen around it.

Consider an inventory application. Testing whether a user can create a product record is useful, but it is only one part of the process. A more realistic scenario might include creating the record, updating stock, checking availability, and reviewing the resulting information.

Include both normal and exceptional conditions:

For each scenario, document the starting conditions, steps, and expected outcome.

Where possible, involve actual users or representatives of the teams who will operate the system. They often identify workflow gaps that aren't visible in a developer-led demonstration.

Watch out for: Testing only the happy path. Real users rarely operate software under perfect conditions all the time.

This is where I would spend particular attention during UAT.

Individual features can work correctly while the workflow connecting them remains broken.

A form might save successfully. A report might load. A notification might appear. But does the complete process work from the user's first action to the expected business outcome?

For a SaaS product, consider testing the journey from registration to account setup, subscription handling, and access to the correct features. For an internal operations tool, follow a request from submission through assignment, approval, completion, and reporting.

Check the transitions between modules, not just the modules themselves.

User permissions are respected throughout the workflow.

Errors are understandable and recoverable.

Repeated actions don't create unintended duplicates.

Use an environment that resembles the intended operating environment as closely as practical. Configuration, integrations, roles, and realistic data can expose issues that a simplified demo environment hides.

UAT doesn't replace security, performance, or integration testing. Those need their own technical validation. But acceptance should establish that the integrated product supports the business process it was built for.

Watch out for: Declaring a workflow successful because every individual screen passed a separate test.

A defect report should help someone reproduce the problem without having to guess what the tester meant.

"The dashboard is broken" isn't actionable enough.

Screenshot, error message, or relevant evidence

For example, "Order total is incorrect" needs more context. Which order? What inputs were used? What total was expected? What did the application display?

The business impact matters too. A layout issue and an incorrect financial calculation should not receive the same priority simply because both are defects.

I prefer a shared defect log that connects each issue to its test case and requirement. It makes discussions between developers, testers, and stakeholders more precise.

It also gives the team a record of what was found, what was fixed, and what remains unresolved.

Watch out for: Scattered feedback in chat messages, screenshots without context, and vague tickets that cannot be reproduced.

A development ticket marked "resolved" is not proof that the original problem has disappeared.

After a fix is delivered, repeat the original test scenario. Confirm that the expected behaviour now occurs.

Then consider whether the change could have affected related functionality. This is where regression testing becomes important.

For example, changing how customer records are updated might resolve an editing issue but affect a report that uses the same data. Testing only the edited field could miss that side effect.

A practical defect lifecycle might look like this:

Reported | v Under investigation | v Fix delivered | v Ready for retest | +---- Failed ----> Reopened | v Verified | v Closed

Keep the test result connected to the defect record. Where possible, note the build or release being tested so the team knows exactly which version was verified.

Not every fix requires repeating every test in the application. The scope of regression testing should reflect the risk and dependencies of the change. But important workflows deserve another end-to-end check.

Watch out for: Closing issues based only on a developer's status update.

UAT should end with a documented decision, not an assumption that the project is finished because the delivery date has arrived.

Accept: The agreed acceptance criteria have been met, and remaining issues do not prevent intended use.

Accept with conditions: The product can be accepted with known limitations, documented responsibilities, and agreed follow-up actions.

Defer or reject acceptance: Important requirements remain unmet, or unresolved defects make the product unsuitable for its intended purpose.

Record the decision alongside the test results, outstanding defects, and any conditions attached to acceptance.

Also review the handover requirements separately. Access credentials, deployment information, documentation, backups, third-party dependencies, ownership arrangements, and support responsibilities may all need verification.

This matters because UAT and operational handover are related, but they aren't identical. A product might pass its business acceptance tests while the organisation is still missing information needed to operate or maintain it.

If the project has contractual acceptance milestones or payment conditions, use the agreement as the reference for the formal decision.

Watch out for: Signing off because the team is tired, the project is late, or everyone wants to move on.

The following is my rough, indicative view of where attention tends to go during a typical acceptance process. Actual effort depends on the product, scope, and risk.

I give end-to-end workflow testing the most attention because that is where separate features have to work together.

Before accepting a build, I'd want to be able to answer these questions:

Are the acceptance criteria clear and agreed?

Do test scenarios reflect real business tasks?

Are defects documented with reproducible steps?

Are remaining issues and conditions documented?

Has the acceptance decision been recorded?

Are operational handover responsibilities clear?

If several answers are still no, the project may need more validation before formal acceptance.

How KSoft Technologies Approaches User Acceptance Testing

At KSoft Technologies, our work involves helping founders and growing companies build, modernise, and scale software systems. We consider acceptance criteria, realistic workflow validation, defect resolution, and clear handover important parts of delivering software that supports actual business operations.

This is our own software engineering service, and the same principle guides the work: a build should be evaluated by how well it serves its intended users, not simply by whether development tasks are marked complete.

Founders don't need to become developers to make a responsible software acceptance decision.

They do need a clear understanding of what was promised, realistic scenarios that reflect how the business works, and evidence that the important workflows behave as expected.

UAT is not an obstacle to shipping. It's how you avoid confusing a finished build with a usable product.
