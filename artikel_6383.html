Migrating birds may be bringing unusual ticks to Hungary

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

Volunteers in Hungary found 29 Hyalomma ticks over five years, raising the question of whether the ticks are starting to breed there.

Hyalomma ticks can carry the virus that causes Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever, a serious and sometimes life-threatening illness. They usually live in warm, dry places, but lately they keep turning up in Hungary.

For five years, scientists in Hungary have been asking people to send in any of these ticks they find.

What they’ve learned so far raises a simple question: are the ticks just passing through, or are they starting to settle in?

At the HUN-REN Centre for Ecological Research in Budapest, Éva Szabó, Gábor Földvári and their colleagues in the Eco-epidemiology Research Group run the project.

Hyalomma ticks usually live in warm, dry parts of Africa, the Middle East, Asia and southern Europe.

Young Hyalomma ticks often feed on birds, including birds that migrate long distances. Adult Hyalomma ticks feed mostly on large animals such as cattle and horses.

The way these ticks feed helps explain how far they can travel. A young Hyalomma tick usually stays on the same animal while it grows.

For Hyalomma marginatum, that’s about three weeks. Ixodes ricinus, the most widespread tick in Hungary, lets go after less than a week each time.

The authors linked the ticks’ spread north to climate change.

The European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control keeps a map of where Hyalomma marginatum has turned up in Europe.

Scientists watch these ticks closely because of a virus some of them carry.

The World Health Organization names Hyalomma ticks as the main carrier of Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever virus. The study’s authors wrote that it’s the most important tick-borne virus affecting people.

How many people get sick from it each year isn’t clear, because many infections cause no symptoms. The authors cited an estimate of 10,000 to 15,000 cases worldwide.

The World Health Organization estimates that 10% to 40% of reported cases in outbreaks are fatal.

A vaccine has seen limited use in Eastern Europe, and no vaccine is widely available. Treatment relies on supportive care.

Hyalomma ticks hunt their hosts instead of waiting on plants. Dragging a cloth through vegetation, the traditional way to collect ticks, doesn’t work well for them.

So the team relies on citizen scientists instead.

The researchers opened a website called TickWatcher in April 2021.

Four years later, they added a free phone app called PragmaTick. People send in a photo of an odd-looking tick, and an expert checks it.

By late November 2025, the website had about 107,000 visits. The app had been downloaded 2,500 times. Volunteers had made 1,014 submissions, and 29 turned out to be Hyalomma ticks.

The app won’t accept a report without a date, a place and a photo.

In the app’s first six months, people sent about as many reports through it as they had sent by email in the four and a half years before.

“Successfully involving the public in research can even help unravel problems like finding a needle in a haystack,” the authors wrote.

Thirteen of the 29 ticks were on horses. Five were on buffalo, two on dogs, one on a donkey and one on a cow, and reports came in between mid-June and the end of October.

Horse owners tend to check their animals closely and ride through grass and woods, which may help explain why horses topped the list, the researchers wrote.

News about the program may also have reached some groups of people better than others.

Even so, the authors concluded that owners of livestock and other large animals are likely more affected than other groups.

Three ticks came from people. Four more ticks were crawling with no host, including the one on the kitchen floor.

Most of the 29 were males, 21 in all. One possible reason, according to the authors, is that males may stay on a host longer while they look for mates.

At a buffalo farm near the village of Ágasegyháza, people found four Hyalomma ticks in 2022 and one more in 2023.

Two other finds came from within about 9 miles (15 kilometers) of the farm. One was on a cow in 2021, and the other turned up in a garden near the city of Kecskemét in 2023.

All 18 ticks that people mailed in tested negative for the virus. The other 11 were thrown away or destroyed by the people who found them.

Earlier studies in Hungary found antibodies against the virus in 10 of 2,700 people tested between 2008 and 2017, and in 11 of 1,905 cattle and sheep tested in 2017.

Antibodies show past exposure, not a current infection, and these tests may also react to related viruses.

The biggest open question is whether these ticks are only passing through or starting to survive and breed in Hungary.

Answering that will take time. The team plans to keep collecting reports, with close attention to livestock owners and to places where several ticks have already been found.

For now, the people in the best position to help are the ones already checking their horses and cattle.

Every tick they send in shows scientists a little more about where these ticks are turning up.

The full study was published in the journal Current Research in Parasitology and Vector-Borne Diseases.
