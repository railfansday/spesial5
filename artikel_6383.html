Webb discovers something unexpected in the ice of an interstellar comet

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Webb reveals unusual ice in interstellar comet 3I/ATLAS that closely resembles icy objects beyond Neptune, offering clues to planet formation.

Ice from a comet born around another star looks surprisingly different from the ice in most comets in our solar system.

But far beyond Neptune, scientists have found icy bodies that share its unusual characteristics.

If that match holds, the icy material that builds planets may take a similar form around more than one star.

The comet is 3I/ATLAS, only the third object from outside our solar system that astronomers have ever identified.

New measurements from NASA’s James Webb Space Telescope show that its ice grains are tiny and mixed with dust.

Silvia Protopapa of the Southwest Research Institute led the study.

“Interstellar comets are small icy bodies that formed in the disks of gas and dust around other stars and are now passing through our solar system,” she said.

“As 3I/ATLAS warms up, gas escaping from its interior carries ice and dust grains into the surrounding cloud, or coma. Those grains hold clues to the conditions where the comet was born.”

The observations came from a Webb program set up to study interstellar visitors.

Martin Cordiner of NASA’s Goddard Space Flight Center and the Catholic University of America leads that program and co-wrote the paper.

“We designed this program to be ready the moment an active interstellar comet was found and to follow it over time,” Cordiner said.

His team wanted to learn what the comet’s ice, dust, and gas could reveal about how planetesimals, the building blocks of planets, form around other stars.

The team observed 3I/ATLAS three times with Webb’s Near-Infrared Spectrograph, before and after the comet’s closest pass by the Sun.

The visits came on August 6, 2025, December 22, 2025, and April 1, 2026.

The instrument uses a prism to split light into its wavelengths, which shows what a material is made of and its physical state.

On the first visit, the comet was about 307 million miles (494 million kilometers) from the Sun. By the last one, it was about 530 million miles (853 million kilometers) away and heading back out.

Webb also detected water vapor, carbon dioxide, and carbon monoxide gas around the comet.

Water ice absorbs infrared light at specific wavelengths, creating dips in the light that astronomers measure.

Astronomers call the dips absorption bands. Infrared wavelengths are measured in microns, and one micron is about 1/25,000 of an inch.

Ice makes a deep dip near 3 microns and two weaker ones near 1.5 and 2.0 microns. Most icy comets show all three. In 3I/ATLAS, the 3-micron band was strong, but the 1.5- and 2.0-micron bands were very weak or missing.

“That combination was a puzzle,” Protopapa said. “The strong 3-micron band points to water ice in the coma. But the weak or absent shorter-wavelength bands suggest the ice is not in the form we usually see in solar system comets.”

To find out what kind of grain could produce that pattern, the team compared the measurements with detailed models of how light scatters off grains of different sizes and makeups.

The best fits came from grains about a micron across or smaller, made of water ice mixed with dust.

In grains that small and that dusty, light travels only a short distance through the ice. That weakens the 1.5- and 2.0-micron bands but leaves the 3-micron band strong.

Scientists think the ice grains in other comets are fairly pure, perhaps with a small amount of dust.

“The grains of 3I/ATLAS are about the same size as those in our own comets,” Protopapa said. “What seems to set them apart is how much dust is mixed into the ice.”

The grains around the comet didn’t stay the same from one visit to the next. By April 2026, as 3I/ATLAS moved away from the Sun, the data favored an extra set of ice-rich grains.

These were larger than the grains seen earlier, but still around a micron in size.

In April, Webb’s measurements also showed a faint feature that fits crystalline ice, meaning ice whose molecules are lined up in a regular pattern.

The ice may have been crystalline at the first and last visits, with the change coming mostly from grain size and the amount of dust. Or it may have started out in a more disordered form and become crystalline later.

Michael Kelley of the University of Maryland is the paper’s second author.

“We can’t yet tell whether the changes over time are the result of the Sun heating the grains differently at each observation, or of new material exposed as the comet’s surface eroded near the Sun,” Kelley said.

“Both are possible, and telling them apart is one of the open questions we aim to address.”

The team then compared 3I/ATLAS with Webb measurements of trans-Neptunian objects. These are icy bodies that orbit the Sun beyond Neptune, and scientists think they still hold material from the early solar system.

In 3I/ATLAS, the 3-micron band has a shape much like the one seen in several groups of these objects.

The resemblance is strongest for groups whose light also shows clear signs of carbon dioxide ice and organic compounds.

The likeness is in the water-ice band, not in the carbon dioxide. And like the comet, these objects lack strong 1.5- and 2.0-micron bands.

“At first glance, 3I/ATLAS looks different from the icy comets we know,” Protopapa said. “But when we placed it alongside distant icy worlds in our own solar system, it no longer looked out of place.”

If later observations confirm the pattern, the physical form of water ice may be established early in the disks where planets form. Icy bodies born in those disks may then preserve some of those original characteristics.

The team had just one interstellar comet to work with, and astronomers had identified only two objects from beyond the solar system before it.

Nobody can yet say whether ice this dusty is common among visitors from other stars.

Future Webb observations could reveal whether 3I/ATLAS is an unusual visitor or evidence that icy worlds form in surprisingly similar ways across the galaxy.

The full study was published in the journal The Astrophysical Journal Letters.
