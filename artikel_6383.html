Taking cold showers is good for your health and also the environment, according to a new study

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

In trials with 719 adults, people who took cold showers at least 70% of the time reported 26% fewer illness days and 31% fewer days off work.

Giving up a warm shower sounds simple until it’s time to step under the water. In a Swiss study, plenty of people signed up to try it, but many stopped early.

Those who kept going reported fewer days feeling sick and fewer days off work over 12 weeks. Their showers also came with a small estimated drop in carbon emissions, although extra encouragement did little to help people keep the habit.

Lena Glaser, a psychologist and psychotherapist at the University of Basel, knows from her clinical work how hard people can find change.

For her doctoral dissertation in the Faculty of Psychology, she studied whether a habit is easier to keep when people can fit it into their day and get something out of it.

“We need a more empathetic approach if we want people to change their behavior,” Glaser said.

Cars, food, and recycling usually come up when people discuss their climate impact, Glaser said. Yet European and U.S. energy agencies put water heating at about 15% to 25% of the energy used in homes in wealthy countries, a use that gets much less attention.

“Taking a cold shower isn’t exactly pleasant, but it’s a small change to make,” she said. “After all, we shower anyway.”

Ice baths and cold plunges have made cold water a popular health topic, but the evidence remains limited. A 2025 review found 11 trials in healthy adults, only one of which tested showers.

That Dutch trial, published in PLOS ONE, asked people to finish a warm shower with up to 90 seconds of cold water. They reported 29% less sick leave than a comparison group, but no fewer days of illness.

Glaser brought together results from three trials she and her colleagues ran between late 2019 and mid-2024. In all, 719 adults took part.

To join, people had to speak German, be healthy, and be between 18 and 65, with no cold-shower habit already in place. Pregnancy ruled people out, as did conditions such as severe asthma or severe heart disease that cold might make worse.

The instructions were straightforward: turn the tap to its coldest setting and stay under the water for two to five minutes. Once a month, volunteers filled out surveys about days with cold, flu, or other symptoms and days they’d missed work because of illness.

Two of the trials asked everyone to try cold showers, which left no randomly chosen comparison group. So for this analysis, Glaser’s team put people who showered cold at least 70% of the time in one group: 459 volunteers, or about three in five.

Before the trials, volunteers reported about three days of illness and one day off work each month. Over 12 weeks, regular cold showerers reported 26% fewer illness days and 31% fewer days off work than everyone else.

The difference was modest for any one person. The team’s calculation put it at one fewer day of illness among 16 people taking cold showers for a week.

The gap stayed similar from the first month to the last. Cold showerers also scored slightly higher on a standard well-being questionnaire, but their physical well-being scores did not differ reliably.

Skin health ratings were much the same in both groups, with complaints including dry or itchy skin and a few slips in the shower. No one reported a serious problem.

The team looked more closely at quitting among 586 people in the two later trials. Only 217, or 37%, kept taking cold showers for all 12 weeks.

People were most likely to quit in the early weeks. They became less likely to stop as time went on.

“That suggests people gradually get used to it,” Glaser said.

Women were more likely to stop than men. About 32% of women reached the end, compared with about 47% of men.

In the second trial, some volunteers watched short monthly videos giving them reasons to continue, from personal benefits to climate action. None of the four versions helped people last longer than basic instructions did.

Weekly emails didn’t help either. Of the 71 volunteers who received five emails with confidence-building texts and videos in the third trial, just 10 finished the 12 weeks, the lowest completion rate of any group.

The researchers suggested that weekly messages may have made a personal choice feel like an obligation. That could weaken people’s motivation instead of helping.

“When people see a personal benefit from changing their behavior and don’t feel that they’re giving something up or missing out, they’re more motivated to stick with it,” Glaser said.

The energy calculation started with a five-minute shower using 12 gallons (45 liters) of water. Taking that water from a warm 104°F (40°C) down to 59°F (15°C) would save about 1.3 kilowatt-hours.

That worked out to an estimated 33 pounds (15 kilograms) less carbon dioxide per volunteer over the study. Using the team’s comparison, that’s about as much as a car releases on a 62-mile (100-kilometer) trip.

For someone keeping up the habit for a year, the team estimated savings of about 143 pounds (65 kilograms). That equals about 2% to 3% of an average person’s annual household carbon footprint in wealthy European countries.

The researchers didn’t put meters in people’s homes. They assumed a daily five-minute shower and used average emissions figures for German and Swiss energy, so the savings in a particular home would depend on its water heater.

The volunteers averaged about 26 years old, and roughly two-thirds were women. They also chose to take on the challenge, so they were probably more motivated than most people.

Everyone knew which group they were in, and the researchers relied on survey answers for both shower habits and illness. No medical tests confirmed whether people were sick.

There’s another complication: people who managed to keep showering cold might have differed from those who gave up. Comparing those groups cannot tell us whether cold water itself made anyone less likely to get sick.

The researchers don’t know who carried on after the 12 weeks ended. Nor did they track whether volunteers went on to use more energy elsewhere because they felt they’d already done their bit, a possibility people have also raised about recycling.

For Glaser, keeping a habit needn’t mean doing it exactly the same way every day.

“The water doesn’t have to be equally cold every day,” Glaser said. “We don’t feel equally brave every day, and that’s okay.”

One question for future studies, Glaser suggested, is whether this small change could make bigger ones feel more manageable. Would people who get used to cold showers become more willing to change what they eat or fly less?

The full study is available as a doctoral dissertation through edoc, the University of Basel’s open-access repository.
