The Federal Communications Commission has approved SpaceX’s request to launch 1 million AI-equipped satellites to create vast data centers in space.

SpaceX initially filed an application in May asking for permission to launch the satellites, collectively known as Starmind. The FCC’s approval requires that SpaceX launch its first data center satellite by 2030 and meet the 1 million mark by 2040.

“It’s just really abrogating their responsibility to go, ‘Well, this is a project they’re proposing. Is this actually safe?’” Jonathan McDowell, an astrophysicist who tracks satellite launches, told CNET. “It’s a blank check. Launch as many satellites as you can, and we’ll just see what happens. I think this is a mistake on the FCC’s part.”

Orbital data centers have long been a goal of SpaceX CEO Elon Musk, becoming a pivotal part of his pitch to investors ahead of the company’s IPO in June.

“You’re power-constrained on Earth. Space has the advantage that it’s always sunny,” Musk said in March.

On its web page for Starmind, SpaceX touts its high-efficiency solar arrays on the planned satellites and the cooling benefits of space. It says that high-speed laser links will beam the AI results back to Earth through Starlink’s constellation.

Space experts I’ve talked to this year about Starmind have been skeptical. The technical challenges alone are dizzying; the scale of SpaceX’s ambitions is hard to swallow.

It’s taken seven years for Starlink to grow its constellation to 11,195 satellites, and that was already an unprecedented feat. Is it possible to get to 1 million satellites in 14 years?

“We do not have the launch capacity for any of this,” Ian Christensen, senior director at the Secure World Foundation, told me in a previous interview. “Not even within SpaceX to do their own constellation, let alone the other constellations planned.”

SpaceX has increasingly prioritized launching its own satellites instead of letting commercial customers hitch a ride on their rockets. Starlink’s share of SpaceX missions has gone from 54% of its Falcon 9 rocket’s missions in 2020 to about 79% in 2026, according to a Reuters analysis. One analysis estimates that SpaceX would have to launch 5,000 Starship rockets a year to meet its goal — a staggering increase from 330 rockets that launched last year in total.

Scientists have already been ringing alarm bells about overcrowded orbits as they exist now. The more satellites in space, the more difficult it is for operators to maneuver around each other. A single crash could turn into decades of orbital debris, which could cause a chain reaction that eventually makes space inoperable for everyone. And burning up all those satellites in the atmosphere when they reach the end of their lives has already started to leave ozone-depleting aluminum particles behind. A paper published this week in Earth and Space Chemistry estimated that SpaceX’s data center plan would multiply pollutants in the upper atmosphere by more than 150 times compared to last year.

“There are scientific papers out there raising issues,” McDowell said. “Yes, they’re unproven, but if I say, ‘Yeah, we’re not sure if this is going to destroy the atmosphere and kill us all, but it might,’ is the right reaction to go, ‘Well, you haven’t proven it, so we’re just going to ignore it’?”

The FCC’s approval came despite objections from Amazon, the environmental nonprofit Public Employees for Environmental Responsibility and the American Astronomical Society.

A representative for SpaceX did not immediately respond to a request for comment.
