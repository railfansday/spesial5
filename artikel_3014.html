Kirby Smart calls for mental heath support for athletes in NIL era: 'They deal with a lot of outside pressure'

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

While looking at the modern era of college football, Georgia coach Kirby Smart said athletes are facing "an all-time high of mental stress" amid mounting outside pressure. With that, he called on schools to provide more support on that front.

Smart cited the amount of pressure that comes with the NIL era, in particular. As athletes earn money, he said they then sometimes hear from family members about the amount they're making while some put those dollars toward supporting their families.

While evaluating what athletes deal with in 2026, whether it's NIL or noise on social media, Smart said schools should be able to give them more support to deal with their mental health. He added pressures they currently face are different than those he and others had during their careers.

"I think these kids are at an all-time high of mental stress," Smart said Thursday on his radio show. "They have stressors at home. A lot of them make NIL money that's large money for a college athlete. And they get pressure from home, people reach out. … People you don't even know come out of the woodworks because they find out what they make and they want to tap in. There's no greater pressure than to feel like you have to support not your immediate family, you've got to support your outside family.

"They carry a heavy burden. I don't think anybody in the country does enough to support them mentally and just making sure that they're where they need to be, in a good headspace. They deal with a lot of outside pressure – pressure that we didn't deal with."

Athletes in the NIL era have also grown up with social media. Many of them have used their followings to cash in off the field by entering into deals and create more value away from the playing field.

But on the flip side, Kirby Smart noted they can also hear more of the criticism that previous generations were able to avoid. That could also have an impact on an athlete's headspace and add to what they're dealing with off the field.

"They're comparing themselves to others," Smart said. "They're looking at what people say about them. They're hearing things we never had to hear. They were said, but we didn't hear them because nobody could write them. Now, you see everything."

The NCAA has taken steps to provide support for athletes dealing with any threats or harassing behavior on social media. It announced the Bad Actor Program late last month, which is an initiative aimed at helping identify such behavior online.
