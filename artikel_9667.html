Ant Group Open-Sources LingSante: Why Open Medical AI Matters

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Good medical AI has mostly lived behind paywalls and private APIs. Developers could use it. But they couldn't look inside it, tune it for their own patients, or run it in their own hospital. Ant Group, the tech giant behind Alipay, is changing that with LingSante, a family of open-source medical AI models built on its Ant Ling foundation model. The weights are arriving in phases, and the first two core models are already out. Open weights in healthcare AI are still unusual. So, this release deserves a close look from anyone building in the space.

LingSante is a family rather than a single model. It spans image understanding, long-context clinical reasoning, risk prediction, safety checks and live consultation. Two models lead the launch, and they are built for quite different jobs.

The family also comes with some real-world mileage. It has been tested on the Afu app, an AI health app which has 150 million users and handles 20 million consultations a day. Plenty of medical AI models are only ever shown on curated benchmarks, so traffic at that scale tells you something different.

Air-VL has 124B parameters in total, but only 5.5B are active when it runs. That sparse setup is what makes it light enough for on-device deployment, and it's aimed at personal users and smaller institutions. It takes text, images and video as input.

Flash sits at the other end. It has 560B total parameters, roughly 25B of them active, and a 1M-token context window. That context length matters for complex cases, because a real patient history is long, messy and full of details that a short window would simply cut off.

Ant Group points it at evidence-based retrieval and long-horizon clinical agents. Think of workflows where software has to plan several steps, pull in references and keep track of a case over days, not just answer one question and stop.

A model is only as trustworthy as the way it's tested, and vendors grading their own work rarely convince anyone. So Ant built InclusionMed, an open evaluation platform, together with 29 institutions including Stanford, Harvard and Peking Union Medical College. In the platform's inaugural rankings, LingSante-3.1-flash took the No.1 spot among all open-source medical models.

A ranking isn't clinical proof, and nobody should treat it like one. Still, outside institutions sharing the scoreboard is a better starting point than a company publishing its own numbers.

Three more models complete the family. Pulse predicts risk for more than 150 conditions. MedGuard handles safety, a layer that many healthcare AI projects bolt on far too late. Live is a real-time video consultation agent, and it's already running inside the Afu app.

Splitting these jobs into separate models is a useful design hint. Developers can mix and match pieces instead of betting everything on one giant system.

When the weights are public, a university lab or a small clinic-software team can study a serious medical AI model, fine-tune it for local languages and conditions, and host it on their own hardware. Closed systems rarely allow any of that, and teams with small cloud budgets usually get left out entirely.

Openness also invites scrutiny. Researchers can poke at the model, hunt for failure cases and publish what they find. In a field where errors can hurt patients, that outside pressure is a feature.

Benchmark wins and a huge user base are encouraging, but they don't replace clinical validation. Anyone building on LingSante should test it on their own patient population, keep a clinician in the loop and check local regulations before it goes near a real care decision. It's also worth remembering that the family is still rolling out, so the specialist models may take a while to reach the same level of access.

This release won't settle the debate over AI in medicine. It does hand the global health tech community something solid to work with: an on-device multimodal model, a long-context reasoning model and an open scoreboard to compare them on. It also shows what open-source medical AI can look like in practice. For developers, the practical next step is simple. Both models are available on Hugging Face and ModelScope, so you can test air-VL on your own images and flash on your longest case notes, then compare the results against whatever you use today. What people build with it over the next year will say more than any press release.
