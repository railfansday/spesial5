Official API vs a reseller API: what the invoice actually covers

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Two weeks after the integration went live, our operations lead forwarded the bill with a question I had not prepared for: which entity is this, and is it this month's?

Nothing about the model had changed: latency was fine, the prompts were identical. What had changed was the paperwork — a card receipt now had to become a ledger line with a period, a tax treatment and a counterparty.

That is the gap this article is about: two teams call the same model, get the same output, and end up with two different records — a card statement, and a document set.

Buying AI tokens through a reseller means purchasing metered model access from a party that holds the commercial relationship — contract, invoice, settlement, support and incident response — while inference still runs on the same upstream models you would otherwise call directly from your own account.

The tokens are not different. The invoice is.

Start with what each route produces when the month closes.

With an official API, you pay the model vendor. The charge lands on a card or a prepaid balance. You get a receipt. If your company is small enough and the amount is small enough, that receipt is the whole story, and nobody asks a second question.

With a reseller, you pay a company in your own commercial relationship. What comes back is shaped by whoever handles your accounts payable: a contract with a named counterparty, an invoice with a currency and a tax treatment, an acceptance act that ties delivery to the amount, and a settlement channel that does not require someone to personally guarantee a card.

The third route — self-hosting — produces almost no invoice at all, because you are buying hardware and electricity instead of tokens. That is a different article, and it is the right answer for a narrower set of cases than the internet usually admits.

If the coefficient is above 1, you are paying more per token than the upstream list price. That is arithmetic, not opinion. The honest version of the argument is not "we are somehow less expensive" — it is a description of what the coefficient purchases.

Four things, in the order they usually matter:

A contracting counterparty. Someone with a registration number, a jurisdiction and a liability position signs. When a purchase has no counterparty, the spend sits on a card and the risk sits on whoever owns that card.

An invoice you can book. Not a receipt. An invoice carries the entity name, the period, the currency and the tax treatment. Without it, the expense either cannot be booked in the right period or gets booked in a way your accountant will ask you about later.

An acceptance act. The document that says the service was delivered at the agreed volume and quality. It converts a prepayment from an advance into a delivered cost. In jurisdictions where prepayment is treated as an advance on the books, this is the document that closes the loop.

A settlement channel that works where you are. Which is the quiet one. A team can have budget approval, a signed contract and a working integration, and still stall for two weeks because the payment rail does not exist for their entity.

The coefficient is the price of those four things, expressed per token so it can be compared at all. You are allowed to decide the wrapper is not worth it. You are not well served by a supplier who pretends the wrapper is free.

With an OpenAI-compatible endpoint the migration is a configuration change: you point the base URL at the new host. The SDK, the prompts, the retry policy and the application code are untouched.

Two practical notes from having watched this go wrong.

First, run the same prompt against both endpoints and compare usage before you compare anything else. The token counts should match for the same model and the same input. If they do not, you are not comparing the same thing, and the invoice comparison is meaningless.

Second, keep the old configuration in version control. Rollback is restoring the previous URL. A migration whose rollback plan is "redeploy" is not reversible in the sense that matters to a finance approver.

Not a deck. Five questions, and a supplier who has done this before will answer them in one reply.

If those five arrive together and consistent, the purchase moves. If they arrive in pieces — entity in one email, invoice template "at contracting stage", formula "after a call" — expect the delay to land weeks later, once the integration is already live.

Keep the written reply. It becomes the reference document your finance team works from when the first invoice arrives, and it is the difference between a two-day booking and a two-week correspondence. Suppliers who answer in writing on day one tend to answer in writing on day ninety as well; the habit travels with the account.

There is a sixth question worth asking quietly: who answers when the endpoint breaks at 2 a.m.? With a direct vendor it is a ticket queue. With a card payment to an overseas platform it is often nobody you can reach. With a contracting reseller it should be the same party who signs the invoice, which is a structural advantage of the model that has nothing to do with price.

If your scenario has no personal data, no procurement requirement and a card that works, the official API is a reasonable default and this article does not apply to you.

If finance has already asked who the payee is, or if the scenario touches customer records, or if the payment rail does not exist for your entity, then you are not choosing between two token prices. You are choosing whether to buy the wrapper that makes the purchase possible.
