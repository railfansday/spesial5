Unblending: the IFS Approach to Learning

Artists, writers, and musicians shape experience and hold the interest of an audience by introducing things that are unresolved, promise resolution, and offer hints along the way. A crime novel asks whodunnit; music creates gripping tension and aesthetic chills; plays and movies pose and ponder problems; paintings invite, and so on. Artists know that we stay with what we do not yet understand and we feel thrilled at discovery.

The journey from not knowing to understanding by way of curiosity and exploration is called an epistemic arc. Sometimes we’re captured by a puzzle and we understand gradually, sometimes the epiphany comes quickly. In either case, that aha! moment signifies the soft thump of the tiny dopamine hit that culminates an exploration. But to reach this moment, we have to be open to our existing ideas about identity and relationships being incomplete.

From the Internal Family Systems (IFS) perspective, we all have parts. We may see, hear, sense, or feel them. They present at different ages and, often, act in ways that don’t fit the current moment because they’re stuck in the past. When a part takes over and we see the world through its eyes, expecting what it expects, IFS says the part is blended. This can cause epochal (and often epical) cognitive confusion. Who am I? How old am I? Why do I think this way, feel this way, act this way? In contrast, when the part separates, or unblends, we can notice and relate to it. When we pay attention, parts unblend and we can get into relationship with them, ask what they fear, and meet their need for validation, connection, and love.

IFS therapy sessions follow the epistemic arc. Clients arrive in therapy puzzled by their behavior (Why do I pull away when I want connection?), but wedded to some familiar certainty, like I am bad, or no one can be trusted. The IFS inquiry invites them to be curious about their behavior and beliefs, which almost always leads to formative experiences.

The phenomena IFS calls blending and unblending illustrate a paradox at the heart of subjective experience. We can be a part or we can be with a part. When we are a part, we think, feel, and act like that part, but when we are with a part, we have choices. If, for example, a blended part expects rejection, we are likely to hear silence as rejection and view a later offer of help as untrustworthy or dangerous. The part is too afraid to try something new and our present remains a perpetual repetition of the past. But the opposite is true as well. If the part unblends and we are with it, we can explore its fear and challenge distorted and inaccurate beliefs. I can feel and hear a part that expects to be rejected, but since I’ll be okay whatever happens, I can afford to try something new.

An IFS session follows an epistemic arc, a process we can understand through the lens of neuroscience and predictive processing, which says the brain builds a world model in childhood and then, conserving energy, predicts the familiar. Ideally, the brain updates its predictive model in response to prediction errors, but fear and negative identity beliefs can get in the way. A person who expects rejection and withdraws will not get a chance to learn how others might respond if they were to stay present and express a need.

From the predictive processing perspective, blended parts block the brain’s ability to learn. In contrast, unblending clears the runway. IFS aims to persuade parts to unblend. Our compassion and respect invite engagement, parts acknowledge us, and mutual curiosity sustains investigation. Once parts feel valid, legitimate, lovable, and connected, we feel—and are—more open to experiences that disconfirm old beliefs. Expectations relax, other information gains influence, and the brain’s world model updates.

An IFS inquiry doesn’t push for change. Pressing a part to step aside or move quickly can confirm an expectation that its wishes and limits will be disregarded. Patience conveys concern and respect, and is valuable in itself. The message to parts is: You can hesitate and stay connected. You can express fear and be heard. IFS invites all parts to take time exploring their fears and needs. Faster? Slower? You’re the boss.

If you want to discover the logic behind some apparently inexplicable behavior of your own, encourage your parts to unblend. Try noticing them and getting curious. You might have a protective part that keeps everyone happy because it expects disagreements to end relationships. Or you might have a part that withdraws because it anticipates unreasonable—or unmeetable—demands. Your protectors can explain the rationale for their beliefs and behaviors. Your job is to listen with an open heart.

As you listen, you can validate protective choices but also challenge negative identity conclusions (I’m worthless… something is wrong with me). You can offer to replace old survival tactics with better options. You don’t have to undo the past, convince transgressors to take it back, or prove your worth. The part you protect is valid, lovable, and—if you let me help—loved. Your protectors may need to move toward this invitation slowly, starting with a question they can now tolerate asking: Can things be different? But those aha! moments, when they come, are transformative.
