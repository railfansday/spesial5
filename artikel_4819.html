Why Smart People Still Believe These 5 Myths

Posted November 12, 2026 | Reviewed by Tyler Woods

Chances are, you will succumb to at least one common seasonal false belief. When you see a child queued up to see Santa having a full-blown meltdown, do you suspect excessive candy cane consumption? Maybe you insist that family members wear winter beanies because you believe most body heat escapes from the head? Have you moved a poinsettia out of a toddler's reach for fear of poisoning? If so, you are in great company, but also 100% wrong, according to science!

A dozen controlled trials show that sugar does not cause hyperactivity, the head accounts for only about 10 percent of body heat loss, and poinsettias pose no serious poisoning risks (Vreeman & Carroll, 2008). Despite what you might believe, each statement above is refuted by scientific evidence.

As I stated in my last post on popular psychology myths, the reasons why "smart" people reject facts are voluminous, but often include cultural and personal experience, inability or unwillingness to examine evidence, and herd mentality (whereby group endorsement supports believability). People also resist abandoning false beliefs when they perceive no compelling reason to change or fail to recognize the consequences of those beliefs. To fully understand how beliefs influence behavior, we must first distinguish beliefs that science can refute from those that cannot be objectively dispelled. For example, we won't debate things like how much sex is appropriate in a marriage, how much money should be contributed to charities, or whether stealing is acceptable if you are starving. Science cannot answer these types of questions. Instead, below I identify popular myths that hinder organizational performance, thinking, and decision-making, focusing primarily on motivation and achievement. I close by emphasizing that scientific understanding must remain open to revision as new evidence emerges, reflecting the evolving nature of research.

Generations of parents and educators have been conditioned to believe that offering rewards undermines the joy of doing something for its own sake. The myth suggests that paying a child to read means raising a child who never reads for fun again. The evidence tells a different story. The detrimental effects of rewards are narrow and occur only under specific conditions (Hoffman, 2025).

A 40-year meta-analysis revealed intrinsic motivation (i.e., doing things voluntarily and for pleasure) and extrinsic rewards (e.g., recognition, money, or praise) jointly predict performance, with internal drive better predicting work quality and incentives better predicting work quantity (Cerasoli et al., 2014). As I argued in The Paradox of Passion, the brain does not sort motivation into tidy "intrinsic" and "extrinsic" buckets. Rewards, whether a paycheck, praise, or a dopamine rush, covertly shape almost everything we choose to do (Hoffman, 2025). The question is not whether to use rewards, but how to use them productively.

"Follow your passion" is a motivational speaker's mantra and the foundational message in many commencement speeches. The advice implies that passion is discoverable, like buried treasure, and that finding it is the secret to a fulfilling life.

Research suggests this belief can backfire.

People who view interests as fixed and discoverable are less curious about topics outside existing interests, expect newfound passion to provide limitless motivation, and lose interest faster when work gets difficult (O'Keefe et al., 2018). In reality, passion is more often developed than discovered. It typically follows competence, effort, investment, and reward rather than preceding them (Hoffman, 2025). Waiting for passion to strike is a reliable way to wait a very long time.

Few constructs have moved from a journal to the bestseller list to school policy as quickly as “grit,” defined as passion and perseverance for long-term goals.

A meta-analysis of 88 independent samples representing 66,807 individuals found that grit's relationship with performance is modest, that it overlaps heavily with the established trait of conscientiousness, and that perseverance is what really drives long-term success (Credé et al., 2017). Persistence matters, but grit is neither new nor the secret sauce it has been marketed to be. Labeling a struggling student as "lacking grit" repackages the old claim that some people are simply unmotivated (Hoffman, 2017), using a memorable and emotionally charged term that describes someone who overcomes obstacles.

The "10,000-hour rule" suggests that intentional and sufficient practice will turn you into a bona fide expert. Talent, in this view, is optional. A meta-analysis of deliberate practice studies shows us otherwise. Dedicated practice explained about 26% of performance differences in games, 21% in music, 18% in sports, 4% in education, and less than 1% in professions (Macnamara et al., 2014). Practice is essential, but not a guarantee of expertise. Perfect practice is far different than engagement alone. Skills can be developed, but objective feedback, strategy changes, and openness to criticism also matter.

Believing that intelligence is fixed can limit intellectual growth. After all, if you think you cannot change, why try? For decades, the antidote to fixed intelligence was a brief growth mindset intervention, often a single session designed to persuade believers that neuroplasticity means the brain can grow. This myth permeates schools as the magic solution to academic apathy. However, a meta-analysis of 63 studies with 97,672 participants found little effect of mindset training on actual achievement. Ironically, studies conducted by authors who received financial benefits from mindset consulting reported more positive findings than those without incentives (Macnamara & Burgoyne, 2023). Believing you can change is important, but a brief training session from a mindset coach is no better a solution to academic achievement than watching an infomercial and expecting to look 20 years younger by using some random face cream.

The most likely answer is “YES,” and according to research, you will continue to embrace your beliefs despite the evidence presented here. If it is any consolation, scientists are not immune to harboring false beliefs either. Several "facts" in the original version of this article have subsequently needed revision, which is exactly how good science is supposed to work. Nothing lasts forever, including some of the most well-known findings in the history of psychology research!
