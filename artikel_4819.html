What Changed When the Clozapine Registry Ended

For 35 years, clozapine was an antipsychotic a pharmacy couldn't hand over without a blood test on file. That rule ended on June 13, 2025, when the FDA removed the registry program that enforced it. Patients, families, and a fair number of clinicians are still working out what that means.

The registry is gone and the blood tests stay. The FDA said the risk that the program was built around is still real, and that prescribers should keep monitoring on the schedule in the drug's label.

Clozapine is the medication for schizophrenia that hasn't responded to other antipsychotics. It also has a rare and serious risk. In a small share of patients, the neutrophil count, the white blood cells that fight infection, drops low enough to be dangerous.

The first U.S. surveillance data covered 11,555 patients in 1990 and 1991. Agranulocytosis, the most severe form, developed in 73 of them, and most cases occurred in the first three months. That finding shaped everything that followed: weekly blood counts, a national registry, and a rule that the pharmacy had to see a result before filling the prescription. I've put the most-cited clozapine studies in one place for anyone who wants the primary sources.

The program was called a REMS, for Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy. Prescribers, pharmacies, and patients all had to enroll, and a missed lab draw or a reporting error could leave someone without a medication that was working.

On February 24, 2025, the FDA said it no longer expected anyone to participate. On June 13, 2025, the program was formally removed. The agency concluded that the label and a new Medication Guide were enough to manage the risk, and that dropping the program should improve access. Prescribers no longer enroll, and pharmacists no longer verify a blood count before dispensing. I've written about who can prescribe clozapine now and where access still falls short.

The label still recommends a neutrophil count every week for the first six months, every two weeks through month 12, and every month after that. Treatment is interrupted if the count falls below 1,000 per microliter. People with benign ethnic neutropenia, a normal variant with a lower baseline count, have their own lower thresholds. The full table is on my page about the clozapine blood test schedule.

Fever or a sore throat on clozapine still means a call to the prescriber that day. A missed day of clozapine still means calling before the next dose, because the restart dose is lower.

A Finnish national study published in 2024 followed more than 61,000 people with schizophrenia for up to 22 years. Agranulocytosis occurred in 1.37% of those on clozapine and 0.13% of those on other antipsychotics. The excess risk was concentrated early. The odds were 36 times higher in the first six months of clozapine and about 4 times higher after four and a half years. One of 3,559 people starting clozapine died of it.

In 2015, the FDA had already lowered the count at which clozapine has to be interrupted. With Mark Olfson, Christoph Correll, and Erica Duncan, I evaluated that change in Veterans Affairs records. Among 160 patients starting clozapine, 3.1% met the criteria for interruption in the first year under the old thresholds and 0.6% under the new ones. We applied the new rules to historical records, so the study couldn't test safety after the change. It does suggest that most early interruptions under the old rules were triggered by counts the current thresholds no longer treat as dangerous.

A global expert panel went further in 2026 and recommended ending routine blood count monitoring after two years. The U.S. label hasn't adopted that. It still says monthly.

It may help. It's unlikely to be enough. A 2026 study of 75 countries found that clozapine use tracked the number of psychiatrists in a country and was unrelated to how strict the blood monitoring rules were. In the VA cohort we studied, 1.7% of patients with schizophrenia received clozapine.

The blood count gets the attention, and the other effects deserve as much. Constipation, weight gain, and early heart inflammation are covered in my overview of clozapine side effects.

Keep getting the blood tests on schedule. Refill a few days early, since a pharmacy delay can mean missed doses. If your relative has schizophrenia and two antipsychotics haven't worked, ask the psychiatrist directly whether clozapine has been considered, and I've laid out when clozapine should be started for that conversation.

The registry made a hard medication harder to get. Its removal is a reason to ask about clozapine sooner. The monitoring that keeps it safe hasn't changed.
