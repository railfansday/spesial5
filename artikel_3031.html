Victor Wembanyama has turned his NBA finals humiliation into a revenge tour for the ages

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

It wouldn't be exactly right to say Victor Wembanyama didn't miss a beat after a disastrous NBA finals in June that left him and his San Antonio Spurs defeated, memed and embarrassed. In the Spurs' first game this season, against an Oklahoma City Thunder team since decimated by injuries, Wemby went an appalling 1-for-13 from the field. Ten of his 12 points came via free throws. After getting blocked by Aday Mara, he leaned over his knees and muttered furiously to himself as if trying to exorcise an intrusive thought. It brought to mind an image from the NBA finals a few months earlier: Wemby on the floor after being fouled by the Knicks' Mitchell Robinson, crowing, "I'm in your head!" The Spurs went on to blow a 29-point lead, lose the game and the finals. Now Wemby seemed too preoccupied with the issues within his own dome to worry about wreaking havoc in anybody else's.

Game two saw Wembanyama elect to lay the ball up instead of dunking it in the dying seconds of a close tilt with the Houston Rockets, which the springy Amen Thompson blocked spectacularly. Not a great start, and in a league whose narratives calcify long before sample sizes reach an adequate scale, it allowed for whispers that the Spurs were in trouble. But you could say Wembanyama has not missed a beat since then. After beginning the season 0-2, the Spurs are now 13-3, with 12 of those wins coming in a row before a loss to the Detroit Pistons on Tuesday.

San Antonio's basketball hasn't been as seamless as those numbers suggest – the starting lineup includes three guards, leaving the offense a bit clunky and wanting for three-point shooting. It also includes Wembanyama, which papers over pretty much every possible problem.

Unofficially crowned the best player in the world during last season's playoffs, Wembanyama has since distanced his closest challengers for that honor. Somebody has stolen Nikola Jokić's three-point shot and hidden it in a deep hole, signaling what may prove to be the start of his decline after six consecutive imperious, MVP-caliber seasons. Meanwhile, Shai Gilgeous-Alexander's efficiency has inevitably suffered as he's tasked with keeping the injury-ridden Thunder afloat.

Wembanyama, meanwhile, has got better, averaging 28.6 points, 11.9 rebounds and countless plays in which his mere presence causes the opposing offense to spontaneously combust through the first 16 games of the season. The Lakers' Luka Dončić is playing the best basketball of his career this season, averaging gaudy offensive numbers that, if sustained, would represent the most dominant scoring season in modern NBA history. That Dončić is not the runaway early favorite for MVP is solely because of Wembanyama's two-way excellence.

Wembanyama's unprecedented impact on the game of basketball is already well-documented, though the man is only 22. He is at once a void and a force field on defense, vacuuming up plenty of shots with his inflatable-man arms and deterring others simply by standing in the paint. Before the Pistons beat the Spurs on Tuesday, their players warmed up by shooting over teammates who held long foam blocks over their heads, practicing shots with sufficiently steep arcs to escape Wemby's treacherous fingertips, trying to learn how to play the game on a vertical axis.

Wembanyama racked up plenty of offensive highlights during last year's playoffs – a half-court buzzer-beater, a logo three-pointer to tie a vital game in the final minute when the rest of the team had gone cold – but his defensive foundation and paint presence are so strong that any three-pointers he can generate are a bonus.

Wembanyama was maligned during the finals for the lack of a "go-to move", which clogged up the Spurs' offense as the Knicks erased double-digit deficits late in games as if they were playing a G League team. The Spurs' game against the Pistons offered a clue to what he's developing: twice in the first half, Wembanyama threw the ball off the backboard, to himself, so he could collect it and dunk as everybody else watched from the floor. A player this physically anomalous and versatile will eventually have a bag so deep nobody will be able to see the bottom.

Despite Wembanyama's visually obvious gifts, there are still times when the magnitude of their manifestation on a game is shocking. In a blowout against the Sacramento Kings, he needed only 25 minutes to rack up 40 points, 11 rebounds and eight blocks. In a close game against the Dallas Mavericks, he produced 52 points, 24 rebounds and eight blocks in 34 minutes. On one possession, he blocked three consecutive Mavericks shots, an entire defense unto himself. The Spurs won the game by one point; during Wembanyama's minutes, they outscored the Mavs by 23. "I haven't been feeling great lately," Wembanyama said after the game. "I'm sick. I felt like I got my legs back. So, it should be more like this more often." Well then.

Even what felt like a fairly quiet Wembanyama performance against the Los Angeles Clippers on Thursday night (he only recorded one block, just before the end of the third quarter) dictated the shape of the game. The numbers are one thing – 32 points, 11 rebounds, no biggie – and watching him is another. He threw down fierce alley-oop dunks. He galloped through the paint, an Ent trampling helpless orcs, for easy floaters. Late in the fourth quarter, he juggled the ball to himself above the basket, reminiscent of a child batting a balloon across a room at a birthday party, before tapping it into the basket.

He found himself with so much space on one play that he rose as if to dunk the ball and dropped it gently through the net instead, flicking his wrist as if he'd just shot a three. On one of those jammed-up Spurs offensive possessions, the Clippers' Isaiah Jackson was guarding Wembanyama tightly as the shot clock wound down. Wemby took a long two-pointer with Jackson's hand in his face, a bizarre shot created from thin air, and drained it.

Wembanyama's highlights won't get old until 7ft 4in generational talents become commonplace, but equally impressive at this point in his career is his maturity. The finals last season were a nightmare on a few fronts: he enabled a legion of detractors with overly violent fouls and dubious flops, then validated their harshest critiques by underperforming in vital games. It'd be enough to ruin plenty of mortals. Wembanyama has emerged from the adversity intact and evolved, keeping the Spurs firmly on track for a shot at redemption.
