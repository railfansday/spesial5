The Art of a Good Life

Most of us are instinctively drawn to beauty. We pause to admire a beautiful landscape, are moved by a piece of music, or find ourselves lingering in front of a painting. Yet, when it comes to life's priorities, beauty and art are often treated as second order: pleasant additions, perhaps, but hardly essential. Worse, an interest in aesthetics can sometimes be dismissed as superficial or unsubstantial, as though paying attention to beauty means neglecting more important things.

I must confess that this attitude has always been rather alien to me. I grew up in a family in which art and culture were considered fundamental ingredients of a good life. My father founded and managed a municipal Endowment of the Arts, and my parents believed deeply in Bildung—the cultivation of the mind and spirit through education and culture. Art deserved time, attention, and space, irrespective of other priorities. It was a human need, not an indulgence. My parents also believed in discernment: Quality mattered, and great art could inspire genuine awe. We went to museums like other people went to church.

But was this simply a cultural inheritance, or is there something more fundamental to the appreciation of beauty? What does science tell us?

One particularly intriguing finding comes from the Harvard Grant Study, one of the longest-running studies of adult development. A 2003 paper examined the relationship between personal strengths and life satisfaction across different adult populations, including the surviving men of the Grant Study.

Among these men, two characteristics emerged as significant, independent predictors of life satisfaction: the capacity for loving relationships and the appreciation of beauty.

Think about that for a moment. Appreciation of beauty appeared alongside loving relationships—hardly a trivial companion—as a characteristic associated with a more satisfying life. Beauty, it seems, deserves a place among the ingredients of human flourishing.

Neuroscience offers further clues as to why. Aesthetic experiences are not merely passive visual or sensory events. They engage brain systems involved in pleasure, evaluation, and meaning. A meta-analysis from 2020 examining 49 neuroimaging studies found that appreciating beautiful faces and visual art activated distinct brain regions. The findings suggest that aesthetic appreciation draws on some of the same valuation processes involved in other rewarding experiences, although different forms of beauty engage the brain differently.

Beauty can also inspire awe—that feeling of being confronted with something greater than ourselves. Awe has interesting social consequences. A series of five studies involving more than 2,000 participants found in 2015 that experiences of awe were associated with greater generosity, ethical decision-making, and prosocial behavior. Awe can make our individual concerns feel smaller and connect us to something beyond ourselves. Although these experiments were not specifically about art, they help explain one way in which encounters with beauty may extend beyond personal pleasure.

And what about the broader experience of engaging with art? Research has begun to examine how encounters with artistic imagination influence well-being. A 2025 systematic review found that engagement with art and the elevating emotions it generates were associated with emotional well-being, life satisfaction, and meaning in life. Importantly, art engagement need not mean visiting a museum: It can include reading fiction, watching a film, or experiencing theatre.

Taken together, these findings suggest a compelling possibility. Beauty can stimulate our minds and engage our reward systems; it can evoke awe, shift our perspective, and strengthen our sense of connection. Engagement with art, in turn, is associated with greater meaning, life satisfaction, and psychological well-being.

Beauty alone does not deliver a happier life, nor does everyone experience or appreciate beauty in the same way. But this research does challenge the idea that aesthetics is merely decorative.

Perhaps we have been getting our priorities wrong. We devote considerable attention to the things we believe make life good: relationships, achievement, health, and purpose. Yet we may underestimate the contribution of something that can enrich all of them: the capacity to notice, appreciate, and be moved by beauty.

A beautiful painting will not solve our problems. A piece of music will not necessarily make our lives easier. But they can make life feel larger, richer, and more meaningful.
