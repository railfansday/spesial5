Your Development Partner Writes Code With AI. What Changes for You?

Tanggal: 2026-11-11

An AI tool can suggest a change to an existing product. The harder part for a client is seeing which product rules the team gave it, where the tool must stop, and who owns the released result

A service manager asks for a change to the message customers receive when an appointment is rescheduled. The new time should appear immediately. The development partner says it will use AI to prepare the change. That sounds like a detail of the workflow until someone asks what happens to the provider’s calendar, an earlier confirmation, or a reschedule made manually by an operator.

This is a hypothetical service, not a Qtim client story. It makes a common gap visible: a tool may produce a plausible patch from the information it can see, while the actual product also depends on rules in operations, settings, integrations, and decisions made long before this request. The client does not need to write instructions for a coding agent. The client does need to know how the team turns its understanding of the product into a bounded task, and who resolves a gap in that understanding.

An engineer who has worked on a service for months may remember that an appointment update affects provider availability and the support queue. An AI coding tool has no such memory by default. It works from the files, descriptions, examples, and instructions made available to it. Someone decides which of those materials are relevant and current, and which actions the tool may take.

That decision is additional work specific to AI-assisted development. The team has to select context for the tool rather than merely gather information for its own understanding. It has to reconcile conflicting sources, mark missing rules, and set limits on access and changes. Handing over a large repository is not equivalent to explaining the business process. An old instruction can look as authoritative as a current rule when the tool has no reason to tell them apart.

In a 2025 study, Jiang and Nam examined developer-written rule files in 401 open-source repositories. The files covered project information, team conventions, guidelines, examples, and directions written for language models. This is evidence about the kinds of context developers choose to record. The study did not test whether adding such a file improved task success. Its sample of open-source repositories also cannot stand in for every commercial product.

The authors of CAFE(S) offered a useful framework in September 2026: context should be clear, actionable, faithful to the project, economical, and secure. It is a conceptual framework, not a measured promise of faster delivery. For a client, the practical question is more direct: what verified information did the team use to frame the task for its AI tool, and who will notice when that information is incomplete?

Consider the proposed message again. “Tell the customer the new time” says nothing about a confirmation already sent, a provider who is no longer available, or a change entered by an operator. Some rules may be in the application. Others may sit in configuration or in a support procedure. A generated change may fit the visible code and still conflict with how the service actually runs.

The team should establish the relevant slice of the product before it asks the tool to work on it. When is the appointment officially rescheduled? Who can trigger the change? Where is the time stored? Which notifications already go out? What happens when an operator intervenes? These questions also matter in development without AI. AI adds a distinct handoff: the team must choose verified answers for the tool, identify unanswered questions, and prevent a plausible guess from becoming product policy.

That handoff can be made visible without showing the client prompts or code. For one change, a short working agreement can cover four things:

Context: the situations in which an appointment changes and the current operations affected by the message.

Boundary: the messages and statuses the team may change, and the behaviors it must preserve.

Owner: the person who resolves a disputed rule and approves a change to it.

Evidence of completion: the customer, provider, and operator scenarios that should work after release.

The business owner confirms the operational rules and exceptions. The development partner translates them into a workable assignment, controls the tool’s scope, and checks the result in the product. This is relevant when developing an existing product. Qtim’s service page describes work on existing products; it is not evidence that the team used AI on a particular client project.

Suppose an operator can reschedule an appointment manually, but the system does not store a reason. Should the customer receive an explanation? A neighboring file may suggest one answer, while the real support procedure suggests another. The coding tool cannot settle a business rule by imitating surrounding code. A responsible team pauses the change, asks the process owner, and resumes once the rule is clear.

The pause may feel slow when a tool can produce several implementation options in minutes. It prevents the team from delivering a neatly assembled message that breaks the actual customer journey. The difference appears before code review: the team separates confirmed conditions from guesses when constructing the AI task. It also decides what the tool can access. CAFE(S) treats security as part of context quality. The right data and access limits depend on the product and the tool, but the responsibility for setting them must belong to named people.

There is a reasonable objection here: experienced developers also need context, and they can make the same mistake. True. AI does not create the need to understand a product. It creates another place where understanding can be lost or distorted. A human engineer may ask a colleague about an ambiguous rule; the tool may proceed from an incomplete input and offer a confident-looking answer. The team therefore needs a deliberate step to prepare and constrain what the tool sees, plus a clear rule for stopping when the answer is missing.

The client expects the agreed change to work across the relevant scenarios and to remain supportable. The team must inspect its proposed solution, explain why it fits the product, and release it through its normal process. The detailed mechanics of code review are a separate subject. Here the issue is who stands behind the decision to use a particular change.

A 2025 IBM study surveyed two cohorts of developers, 669 people in total, and ran 15 unmoderated usability tests of an internal coding assistant. The authors discuss ownership and responsibility for generated code. Those are developer experiences inside IBM, not contract terms for another company. They support asking who owns the output; they do not supply the answer for any specific engagement.

Nor does the method of writing code settle its future quality. A peer-reviewed 2026 experiment involved 151 participants, about 95% professional developers. In its second phase, new participants extended code created with or without AI assistance. The study found no systematic difference in the time or quality of that subsequent work within its tasks. The work used one Java web application and was conducted in late 2024, before newer coding-agent workflows became common. It does not establish that all AI-assisted code is harmless, or that it is inherently difficult to maintain. A client still needs the team to validate the particular change it releases.

The evidence on speed also depends on what is measured. Three field experiments reported by Microsoft Research in 2025 found more completed tasks among developers who had access to a code-completion assistant. In an earlier randomized study, METR found that experienced open-source contributors took longer on familiar repository tasks when AI tools were allowed. METR’s 2026 update warns that selection effects made its later estimates difficult to interpret.

These studies used different people, tools, settings, and outcome measures. Neither counts the time needed to learn the appointment service’s operating rules, prepare reliable context, resolve exceptions, validate the change, and release it. A partner can use AI in some parts of the work. A deadline or price for this product still needs to be based on the full scope and its known uncertainties. A claim that “AI makes it cheaper” supplies neither.

The appointment message gives the parties a manageable starting point. Agree when a reschedule is complete, which messages are in scope, who resolves disputed cases, and what the customer, provider, and operator should observe afterward. Then the team can choose how to use AI within that agreement. The order matters: a tool cannot responsibly answer questions the product owner has not settled.

Decision rule: assess the partner’s AI workflow through the verified context it prepares, the limits it sets for the tool, and the human who owns the released result. The tool can change how options are produced. It does not take the partner’s responsibility for the product change.
