Psoriasis treatment may work better with an over-the-counter supplement

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Pantethine, a vitamin B5 relative sold to lower cholesterol, calmed the immune cells behind psoriasis and improved skin in a small trial.

Pantethine is a vitamin B5 derivative sold as a dietary supplement. In a small blood-sample comparison, researchers found lower levels in people with psoriasis than in healthy volunteers.

Putting it back calmed the immune cells that drive the disease and eased psoriasis-like inflammation in mice.

In a small 12-week trial, psoriasis severity scores improved more among patients who took pantethine alongside a prescription ointment than among those who used the ointment alone.

Wei-Na Zhang of the State Key Laboratory of Biomedical Analysis in Beijing led the work with four colleagues, including Cheng-Xin Li of the Chinese PLA General Hospital.

The first authors, Ai-Ping Cao, Jun-Cheng Li and Rui Wang, work at the Beijing laboratory, Fudan University in Shanghai, and the hospital.

The trial was small and ran at one hospital. The authors wrote that it shows the approach is feasible, not that the mechanism they found in mice is what helped the patients.

“Our investigator-initiated clinical trial demonstrated encouraging therapeutic efficacy of pantethine as an adjunctive therapy in psoriasis, but these findings should be interpreted as proof-of-concept for clinical feasibility rather than direct mechanistic validation,” Zhang said.

Psoriasis is an immune disease that happens to show on the skin.

Immune cells called dendritic cells, whose job is to show the rest of the immune system what to attack, get overactive. They set off the inflammation that produces the scaly plaques.

The team went looking for enzymes that were unusually busy in those cells. In blood and skin samples from patients with psoriasis, lupus and other immune diseases, a family of enzymes called Vanins stood out.

In one set of skin biopsies, from 92 psoriasis patients and 82 healthy people, two of the three Vanin genes were among the most strongly switched on.

Vanins break pantethine down, and one of the products is vitamin B5. In blood from 12 psoriasis patients, pantethine was lower than in 17 healthy people, and mice with psoriasis-like inflammation showed the same shortfall.

The team worked in mice given a cream that triggers psoriasis-like skin inflammation over six days.

Mice whose dendritic cells lacked the main mouse Vanin gene, Vnn3, developed much milder disease, with less skin thickening, lower severity scores and fewer immune cells in the affected patches.

Giving ordinary mice a daily injection of pantethine during the six days did the same, and so did a drug called RR6 that blocks Vanin. Both raised pantethine and lowered cholesterol in the blood.

Was it the dendritic cells, or just lower cholesterol in general?

To check, the team removed those cells from mice and replaced them with cells that had been treated with pantethine beforehand. The mice with pre-treated cells still got less disease.

Why would a vitamin relative quiet an immune cell? Zhang’s team traced it to cholesterol.

When Vanin is busy and pantethine runs low, dendritic cells make more cholesterol and pack it into their outer membranes.

Cholesterol-rich patches of membrane are where the cells’ alarm receptors cluster. A cell with more of those patches responds harder to the signals that switch it on.

Pantethine reversed that. Cells treated with it carried less cholesterol in their membranes and fewer clustered receptors, and they sent weaker signals through the inflammatory pathways that drive the disease.

Dendritic cells taken from psoriasis and lupus patients and treated in the dish behaved the same way.

High blood cholesterol is already known to make psoriasis worse and its treatments less effective, the authors wrote, which is what made a cholesterol-lowering supplement worth trying in people.

The team enrolled 27 people with mild plaque psoriasis at the Chinese PLA General Hospital and randomly assigned them to two treatment groups. The 12-week analysis included 24 participants.

Everyone used a standard prescription ointment that combines a vitamin D relative with a steroid. Half also took a 300-milligram pantethine capsule twice a day, from a brand sold over the counter.

Two dermatologists scored each patient’s skin from photographs at the start, at eight weeks and at 12 weeks, using the two scales psoriasis trials usually rely on. The trial is registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry.

Over 12 weeks, the group taking pantethine improved more on both scales than the group using ointment alone. The dermatologists didn’t record any side effects tied to the treatment.

The difference was concentrated in people who started the trial with abnormal blood fats.

Among them, the pantethine group’s lipid profiles improved and their skin scores at 12 weeks were clearly better than those of similar patients on ointment alone.

Among patients whose blood fats were normal to begin with, the two groups ended up about the same, so the capsule didn’t add much.

The split fits the mechanism, the authors wrote, but it also narrows the finding.

“We found that pantethine administration effectively alleviated psoriasis in mice, suggesting that insufficient pantethine levels may be the crucial contributor to hyperactivation of dendritic cells and autoimmune inflammation following the up-regulation of Vanins,” Zhang said.

Twelve people took the supplement, at one hospital, for three months, and the comparison group took no placebo capsule. Whether the gain holds up in a larger, blinded trial over a longer period is the first open question.

The mechanism was worked out in mice and in cells. Other immune cells may have helped the patients improve, the authors wrote. And what switches Vanin on in the first place is still unknown.

Vanin shows up in many kinds of cells, not only dendritic cells, and the team wrote that the pathway may matter in other inflammatory diseases.

Beyond psoriasis, though, the evidence so far is a pattern in patient data, not a test in a disease model.

The next step the authors described is to test the pathway in those other diseases.

Whether people with normal blood fat levels can get anything from the supplement is a question this trial could not answer.

The full study was published in the journal Journal of Experimental Medicine.
