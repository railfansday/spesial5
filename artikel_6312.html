Hubble captures a dying star’s tangled farewell – and an intriguing mystery

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Hubble’s view of NGC 5189 reveals a twisting ribbon of glowing gas, enormous knots, and clues to the complex final stages of a star’s life.

Today’s Image of the Day from the European Space Agency features a dying star that has shed its outer layers, creating a twisting cloud of glowing gas.

Some of the small knots visible within it are roughly the size of our entire solar system.

The cloud is NGC 5189, a planetary nebula in the southern constellation Musca, the Fly.

Captured by the Hubble Space Telescope, this elaborate formation reveals dense knots and fine filaments within the glowing gas.

The image was released in December 2012, but its intricate details still offer a remarkable view of stellar change.

Despite their name, planetary nebulae have nothing to do with planets.

Early astronomers gave them that label because some appeared as small, rounded disks through their telescopes, resembling distant worlds.

Better observations eventually revealed something entirely different: expanding clouds of gas released by stars approaching the ends of their lives.

Stars with low to intermediate masses can produce these nebulae as they lose their outer layers, exposing their hot cores.

Ultraviolet radiation from the remaining core strips electrons from atoms in the surrounding gas. As electrons recombine with ions and atoms release energy, the material shines.

The result can take many forms, from relatively simple rings to hourglass shapes and sprawling structures with several lobes.

NGC 5189 belongs among the more complicated examples.

The most striking feature is a bright, golden band that bends across the nebula, contributing to its backward S-shaped appearance.

Look more closely, and that band breaks into numerous strands and dense clumps. Some resemble tiny comets, with bright heads and trailing material.

These “cometary knots” are features within the gas, rather than actual comets traveling through the nebula. Radiation and winds from the central star help shape them.

Around the golden structure, bluish lobes extend outward, giving the scene a layered appearance.

NASA describes two nested structures tilted relative to one another and expanding in different directions.

Their arrangement suggests that the star’s loss of material was uneven, changing over time and sending gas along different paths.

Hubble’s sharp view makes it tempting to treat the nebula’s finer details as small decorations.

Their true dimensions tell another story.

According to ESA, individual knots in NGC 5189 can rival the size of our solar system.

Gas flowing outward encounters these denser pockets, creating curved, bow-wave-like features.

The image therefore captures structure on very different scales: a broad, twisting nebula assembled from countless smaller formations, each enormous by everyday standards.

At its center, the stellar remnant appears only as a point of light.

Much of the spectacle comes from material that once belonged to the star and now stretches far beyond it.

When the image was released in 2012, astronomers suspected that a second star might help explain the nebula’s unusual shape.

A companion’s gravity could influence how material escapes, helping produce patterns more complex than a simple expanding shell.

Later research strengthened that possibility.

In a study published in 2015, researchers reported periodic changes in the central star’s measured motion, revealing a close binary system with an orbital period of about four days.

The researchers considered another white dwarf the most plausible companion.

That finding added an important piece to the picture, although identifying a binary system does not explain every filament and curve.

The nebula preserves the combined effects of escaping gas, radiation, stellar winds, and the system’s history.

Hubble observed NGC 5189 on July 6, 2012, using its Wide Field Camera 3.

The observations included filters selected to isolate light emitted by oxygen, hydrogen, and sulfur.

Other filters captured a broader range of visible and near-infrared light, helping record the surrounding stars.

Combining these observations produced the detailed color image.

The different filters allow astronomers to examine particular components of the nebula, separating signals that would otherwise blend together.

The resulting portrait reveals both its overall shape and the fine texture within its glowing clouds.

A planetary nebula marks a comparatively short phase of stellar evolution. Its gas continues expanding, while the exposed core eventually cools as a white dwarf.

Our Sun is expected to undergo a broadly similar transformation, although its surroundings may take a very different shape.

For now, NGC 5189 offers a vivid glimpse of that distant stage of a star’s life: material once held inside a star, spread across space in luminous folds.
