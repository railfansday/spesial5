UK court rules Apple can share the top secret order everyone already knew

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

The Investigatory Powers Tribunal – essentially a British court that deals with complaints about state surveillance – has ruled that Apple is allowed to reveal a secret order it received from the UK government.

Apple had already found a clever way to alert British customers to the classified order without breaking the law, but it can now confirm what we all already knew, and talk openly about the issue …

In 2025, the British government ordered Apple to create a backdoor to allow access by security services and law enforcement. Even worse, the order was issued under legislation that prohibits companies from revealing that they have received this demand.

This would have meant that British customers who enabled ADP would think that their data was safe when in fact it wasn’t. As we’ve said many times before, any backdoor created for use by the good guys is vulnerable to discovery and exploitation by the bad guys.

“Apple can no longer offer Advanced Data Protection (ADP) in the United Kingdom to new users and current UK users will eventually need to disable this security feature. ADP protects iCloud data with end-to-end encryption, which means the data can only be decrypted by the user who owns it, and only on their trusted devices. We are gravely disappointed that the protections provided by ADP will not be available to our customers in the UK […] As we have said many times before, we have never built a backdoor or master key to any of our products or services and we never will.”

Tell me you’ve been ordered to create a backdoor to ADP without telling me you’ve been ordered to create a backdoor to ADP …

The UK government must admit that it issued a secret order to Apple to allow it access to encrypted customer data, a court has ruled […] The Home Office has “not established that it would be damaging to national security” to reveal that Apple had received a “technical capability notice” (TCN), the tribunal said. The ruling means that […] “much” of Apple and the non-profits’ legal challenge can now take place in open court.

One of the great ironies is that most people had never heard of ADP until the government effectively made it headline news in the tech press three years after it launched – a classic example of the Streisand Effect.

This ruling is a win both for transparency and commonsense. To be clear, the actual requirement for a backdoor remains in place, but this can now be challenged in open court.
