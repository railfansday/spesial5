​How Much Autonomy Is Too Much For An AI Agent?

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Alexey Spas is the founder and CEO of Instinctools, a software engineering company focused on AI-powered digital solutions.

​What happens when an AI agent you trust breaks its limits of autonomy and won’t take no for an answer?​​

Neither the Australian nor the Canadian government was prepared for this kind of trespassing, yet it happened to their websites. An OpenAI model gained unauthorized access to Australia’s Medicare Statistics Reporting Service while researching public medical spending, while an unidentified AI agent attempted to hack Canada’s Library and Archives website. Neither incident resulted in a confirmed compromise of individual records or government systems, but both involved AI agents taking actions beyond their intended research tasks. The activity occurred in May and June 2026 but wasn’t publicly disclosed until September, when OpenAI and the research lab Transluce independently reported their findings.​​​

What this signals to me is that AI agents are forcing both public and private sectors to raise the drawbridge against a threat they didn’t even know was coming. For companies deploying agents at scale, the governance challenge extends beyond choosing a theoretical level of autonomy to ensuring that abnormal behavior can be detected, contained, reconstructed and assigned to a responsible owner.​

MIT recognizes five levels of AI agent autonomy. Starting from level 3, as the human gradually steps back to give the agent more room to act, a handoff problem arises. The system needs to recognize where its authority ends and when escalation to a human is required. The harder failure mode appears when an agent continues pursuing a legitimate goal after crossing that boundary, despite signals telling it to stop.​

This suggests a useful operating principle: the autonomy granted to AI agents can’t safely outpace the maturity of the company’s incident response and AI governance policies. Policies on paper are not enough; they need to be enacted. That maturity rests on four capabilities:

1. Detection: Identifying unusual actions as they happen

2. Investigation: Tracing back the agent’s actions

3. Response: Stopping agent activity and rolling back or mitigating the consequences

4. Accountability: Establishing clear responsibility when an agent’s actions affect a person or a company

A weakness in any one of those capabilities raises the cost of failure: unusual behavior may go unnoticed, incidents may be harder to reconstruct, containment may come too late or responsibility may remain unclear when an agent’s actions affect people or business operations.

Not every scenario can be rehearsed in advance. So the decision to give an agent any degree of autonomy depends on answering a few questions first:​

Every consequential output should have a way to be checked—a second system, a source link or a human-created reference—before the agent is allowed to act on it.​

The Medicare case above illustrates what can happen when an agent treats a block as something to overcome rather than a boundary to respect. A block should stop an agent in its tracks and trigger escalation to a human. It shouldn’t become an obstacle to work around.​

Impact should be classified by severity and blast radius. An agent accessing a public webpage poses a different level of risk than one accessing an internal server or sensitive business systems. The more sensitive the data and consequential the actions an agent can access, the tighter its permissions, oversight and safeguards should be.​

When several vendors are involved, incident ownership can’t fall through the cracks. From where I stand, someone on the customer side needs to be ultimately accountable, even if detection, investigation or remediation depends on external providers. That means defining from the get-go who detects an incident, who notifies whom and who can switch the agent off.

The autonomy ladder can only be climbed when the evidence is there. Autonomy should be earned, with each traceable and reviewable log supporting more trust in the agent. The ladder also runs downward. When the environment, model, tools or performance indicators change, the autonomy limits narrow again until the evidence rebuilds.​

Anthropic found an interesting pattern: new Claude Code users enable auto-approve about 20% of the time. Past 750 sessions, this rate rises to 40%. Yet experienced users also interrupted their agents more often. Anthropic reinforces that effective oversight is not about approving each action but being able to intervene when necessary.​

Enterprise deployments can follow the same principle: greater trust should lead to more selective oversight, not less accountability. Rather than relying on subjective confidence in an agent, organizations can use operational metrics to determine whether its autonomy is justified and where additional controls may be needed. NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework Playbook similarly emphasizes incident tracking, response planning and human oversight. Some useful indicators include:​

• How often humans intervene or override an agent​

• The frequency of rejected or blocked actions​

• How often actions require rollback or remediation​

• Actions that fall outside expected behavioral patterns​

• Incidents categorized by action type and severity​

For a professional services group’s AI operational platform developed at Instinctools, the autonomy was phased in: the low-risk employee assistant agent was deployed before higher-risk quoting and advisory agents. The email triage agent only reached all the business units after its accuracy was raised from 74% to 87%, past the client’s 85% target.

Following MIT’s definition of five autonomy levels mentioned above, I keep returning to this distinction between agents’ autonomy and humans’ authority. Freedom to act within defined boundaries belongs to agents. But authority stays with humans who answer for what agents do and decide if and when their scope is ready to grow.​

The test is simple: deploy only those agents that stop when told no and only when you have a framework in place to watch and contain them, investigate what happened and take responsibility if needed. If an agent can reason its way around a limit, it’s not a limit.​​
