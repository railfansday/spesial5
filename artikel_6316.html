Blood tests for a deadly infection have a lingering blind spot

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Blood tests for melioidosis, a dangerous infection caused by soil bacteria, can remain positive for nearly three years.

An antibody blood test for melioidosis, a potentially deadly bacterial infection, can remain positive long after someone becomes ill. A positive result does not necessarily mean the infection is still active.

Scientists followed 142 people in Thailand who had been in hospital with melioidosis. A bacterium called Burkholderia pseudomallei causes the disease.

The team found antibodies in the blood of the patients long after they first got sick. In the team’s model of a typical patient, the test stayed positive for about 20 months to nearly three years.

So here’s the catch. These tests can provide evidence of past exposure, but a positive result alone cannot establish whether someone currently has an active infection.

For a doctor picking a treatment, or a health agency trying to count cases, that’s a real gap.

B. pseudomallei lives in soil and water. It gets into people through a cut that touches contaminated dirt, through dust they breathe in, or through water they drink.

Patients do not all have the same symptoms. Some get pneumonia and some get an infection in the blood. In others the signs look like a more common illness.

Ninety of the 142 patients in the study had a fever when they came to the hospital.

The recorded diagnoses included sepsis in 60 patients and pneumonia in 45.

Hospitals confirm melioidosis by growing the bacterium from a patient’s blood or from another sample. A culture like this takes at least two days.

It misses some infections, and the lab has to take special safety steps to do it. In places where staff have not seen the disease before, they may record the organism as a less harmful bacterium from the environment.

A late diagnosis can be serious. B. pseudomallei resists several of the antibiotics that hospitals often give to patients with severe infections. Doctors have to identify it fast to give a drug that works.

Antibody tests are faster and cheaper than cultures. They can be used outside hospitals as well. Scientists can test people in a community to see who has been exposed.

This can show where melioidosis is present but has not been noticed.

Northern Australia and northeast Thailand are the places most often linked with melioidosis, but cases have been reported across Asia, Africa and the Americas.

How far the disease really extends is not known, in part because few places test for it or track it.

In Mississippi, investigators found three patients from the same county. The three got sick over a period of three years.

The investigators tested soil and water on the property of one patient. They found the same strain of the bacterium there.

This was evidence that the organism now lives in the environment of the continental United States.

Kristen Aiemjoy, an assistant professor in the UC Davis Department of Public Health Sciences, is a co-senior author of the study.

“One of the things that makes this organism concerning is its ability to persist in the environment for very long periods of time,” said Aiemjoy.

“Once Burkholderia pseudomallei becomes established in soil and water, it can continue to survive and replicate, creating an ongoing risk of exposure.”

For this reason, scientists want to know who has been exposed. That includes people who did not get sick.

The patients were treated in hospitals in Mukdahan, Roi-Et and Surin, three provinces in northeast Thailand.

A lab had confirmed melioidosis in all 142 of them. The team compared them with 76 healthy people who had given blood.

The patients had a median age of 54. Ninety-nine of them were men and 98 had diabetes. Diabetes is a major risk factor for severe melioidosis. Nine patients died.

The team used samples that had been stored from earlier studies. They tested 656 blood samples in total.

The samples were taken at different times. The first were taken in the hospital and the last were taken up to one year later.

They measured antibodies against two targets on the bacterium, a protein called Hcp1 that it produces during infection and a sugar structure on its surface called OPS.

For each target they measured two classes of antibody, IgA and IgG, so there were four markers in all.

The team saw antibody levels rise fast in the first days after symptoms began. The four markers reached a peak between about day four and day 17. The team then saw the levels fall, but very slowly.

In the researchers’ model, the two IgA markers stayed above the cutoff for a positive test for 597 and 710 days. The two IgG markers lasted longer, at 902 and 1,080 days.

Fifty-two patients were tested again a full year on. Depending on which marker you look at, 30 to 34 of them were still positive.

The tests worked well when patients were acutely ill. At the first visit, the best single marker found about 80% of infections. It gave a correct negative result for 96% of healthy donors.

It’s later on that things get murky, when a positive might just be the immune system remembering an old infection, not flagging a new one.

Pairing up the measurements helped. Combining measurements of the two antibody classes against OPS identified 88.7% of patients and correctly classified 94.0% of healthy donors.

And with a higher combined threshold, the estimated stretch of lingering positivity dropped to under a year.

Diabetes changed the results too. For one marker, peak antibody levels were about twice as high in patients with diabetes.

Three of the markers also went down more slowly in these patients.

“In places where melioidosis is rare or newly emerging, such as parts of the United States, these antibody tests may be especially useful because few people have been previously exposed,” Aiemjoy said.

“But in areas where the disease has been circulating for decades, previous infections can make results more difficult to interpret.”

The study has some limits. All 142 patients were adults in hospital. No children or people with mild infections took part.

The 76 healthy donors were younger than the patients. The team set the cutoffs for the tests with the same group of people, and nobody has validated the cutoffs independently.

The team measured antibodies in the samples. They did not test if live bacteria were still in the patients’ bodies. For that reason the team cannot rule out that some patients with a long antibody response still had an infection.

The findings could still help scientists read the results of future surveys. Some of those surveys may be done along the US Gulf Coast.

Scientists could test people who have never been diagnosed with melioidosis. That could show how far the bacterium has spread.

The pattern of antibodies might also help them estimate when a person was exposed.

“Better tools for tracking exposure will help us understand where this pathogen is today and where it may emerge in the future. As environmental conditions change, surveillance becomes increasingly important,” Aiemjoy said.

The full study was published in the journal The Lancet Microbe.
