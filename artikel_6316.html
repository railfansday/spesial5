People taking drugs like Ozempic report an unexpected change in their drinking habits

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Many people taking GLP-1 drugs like Ozempic reported drinking less alcohol within three months of starting their treatment.

People take Ozempic for many reasons, but cutting back on alcohol is rarely one of them. Yet some patients have noticed a change in their drinking habits after starting the medication.

A national survey of U.S. adults examined changes in drinking habits after people started taking GLP-1 medications.

Among those who drank alcohol before treatment, 44.2% reported drinking less within the first three months.

The change was even more common among people whose drinking habits put them at greater risk of health problems.

The research was led by Lori Uscher-Pines, a distinguished senior research scientist at the Brown University School of Public Health, along with colleagues from several other research institutions.

Ozempic and Wegovy contain semaglutide, a drug that helps regulate blood sugar and appetite. Both belong to a class of medicines called GLP-1 drugs.

Scientists are now exploring whether these medicines might also change how people respond to alcohol.

Early clinical trials have produced encouraging results. One possible explanation involves parts of the brain that control cravings and the sense of pleasure people get from certain activities.

Haiden Huskamp, the Henry J. Kaiser Professor of Health Care Policy at Harvard Medical School, is the study’s senior author.

“Because all of the evidence had been anecdotal when we started this study, it was hard to know what to expect,” said Huskamp.

“But finding that about half of GLP-1 users who drink reported reductions in their alcohol use after starting the medication is pretty striking.”

In March and April 2026, the researchers surveyed members of the RAND American Life Panel, a group of randomly selected adults from across the United States.

Of the 2,857 people invited to participate, 2,009 completed the survey. Their average age was just under 49.

Among the respondents, 368 people had taken a GLP-1 drug at some point. Some were still taking the medication, while others had stopped.

The researchers asked these individuals to think back to their first three months of treatment and describe any changes in their drinking habits.

Not everyone who took these medications drank alcohol. After excluding 126 non-drinkers, the researchers were left with a group of 242 people.

Just over half, 52.6%, noticed no difference in their drinking habits. Another 44.2% said they drank less than before.

Only 3.3% reported an increase in alcohol consumption.

In other words, reports of drinking less were about 13 times as common as reports of drinking more.

The researchers were particularly interested in people whose drinking could put their health at risk.

They used a screening question developed by the National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism.

Women were asked how often they had consumed four or more drinks on one occasion during the previous year, while the threshold for men was five drinks.

Across the survey, 37.3% of adults reported at least one such occasion. Among GLP-1 users who met this criterion and drank alcohol, 54.1% reported drinking less after starting treatment.

Another 40.2% noticed no change, while 5.7% said they drank more.

Age, income, sex, race, ethnicity and general health could all potentially influence a person’s relationship with alcohol. The researchers accounted for these factors in their analysis.

Even after doing so, people who screened positive for potentially unhealthy drinking had 2.3 times the odds of reporting a reduction in alcohol consumption.

None of the demographic factors showed a significant association with reporting less drinking.

The findings do not necessarily mean that the drugs work better for heavy drinkers. However, they give researchers a reason to study this particular group more closely.

Ask someone what Ozempic does, and they will probably mention weight loss. Far fewer people appear to know about its possible effects on drinking.

Nearly 80% of survey participants knew that GLP-1 drugs could help people lose weight, and 45.7% knew about their role in blood sugar control.

Yet only 9.7% had heard that these medicines might reduce the desire to drink alcohol. Awareness was somewhat higher among people with potentially unhealthy drinking habits.

Uscher-Pines expects public interest to grow as more research becomes available. Greater demand could also raise questions about insurance coverage and access to prescriptions through telehealth services.

Excessive alcohol consumption contributes to about 178,000 deaths in the United States every year, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention.

For some people, drinking less is not simply a matter of deciding to stop. Alcohol use disorder is a medical condition that makes it difficult to control drinking, even when it causes serious problems.

Medications already exist to help people manage this condition. However, many of those who need help never receive it.

Alisa Busch, a professor of psychiatry at Harvard Medical School, was also involved in the study.

“The national surveys show that far and away most people with alcohol-use disorder receive no treatment, period,” said Busch.

There is a considerable difference between asking people whether they drink less and proving that a medication helps them do so.

“We weren’t testing GLP-1s for efficacy in reducing alcohol use. We’d need a randomized control trial for that,” Busch said.

“We were trying to understand, in a population at higher risk for harmful drinking, is there a difference in their experience of drinking alcohol compared to individuals who screen negative.”

Without a controlled experiment, the researchers cannot rule out other reasons for the changes in drinking habits.

People answered questions about something that had already happened. Their memories may not have been entirely accurate, and some participants may have given answers they thought were more socially acceptable.

The researchers also don’t know how much less alcohol people consumed. Someone who skipped an occasional glass of wine and someone who stopped drinking altogether could both have reported a reduction.

Nor does the study tell us whether these changes lasted beyond the first three months of treatment.

Importantly, the screening question identified potentially unhealthy drinking habits rather than diagnosed cases of alcohol use disorder.

Researchers now need to find out whether GLP-1 drugs can produce meaningful and lasting reductions in alcohol consumption.

“If future studies lead to [Food and Drug Administration] approval of GLP-1s to treat alcohol use disorder, this could be a game changer in addiction treatment,” Uscher-Pines said.

“Clinicians we spoke to are very excited about the potential here.”

“The evidence isn’t there, but it will be very interesting to watch over the next couple of years,” Huskamp said. “There are trials underway. I don’t expect it to take too long before more evidence starts coming through.”

For now, GLP-1 drugs are not approved for treating alcohol use disorder. But what patients have noticed while taking these medications may help scientists develop new ways to treat a condition that remains difficult to manage.

The study is published in the journal JAMA Psychiatry.
