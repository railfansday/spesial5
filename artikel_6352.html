Laser scanner that maps the chemistry inside tissue samples has been miniaturized into a handheld portable device

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

A handheld probe maps tissue chemistry without stains, and in lab tests its images closely matched those from a full-size instrument.

Machines that can map the chemistry inside a slice of tissue, showing where proteins and fats sit without using dye, have been too big to leave the lab bench.

A research team has now modified the imaging head of one to fit a probe that is 8 inches (20 centimeters) long. In side-by-side tests, its pictures closely matched those from a full-size lab instrument.

That matters because full-size machines can’t be carried to where patients are. A probe this size could someday work in the room where a tumor is removed.

So far, though, it has only tested the chemistry in thin slices of preserved tissue and plastics.

The work was done in Rohith Reddy’s lab at the University of Houston, and a pathologist at MD Anderson Cancer Center supplied bone marrow samples. Reddy already has a use in mind.

“Ultimately, this technology could make it possible to assess tissue during cancer surgery,” said Reddy, who leads the team. “After removing a suspected tumor, a surgeon could scan the freshly excised tissue.”

This would “help determine whether it is malignant or whether cancer cells remain at the surgical margin. This complementary information would be available while the patient is still in the operating room instead of having to wait for results from laboratory testing,” Reddy explained.

The instrument works with mid-infrared light. Like all infrared light, it’s invisible, and its waves are longer than the ones a TV remote sends. Molecules absorb this light at wavelengths set by the bonds holding their atoms together. That gives proteins, fats and DNA each a pattern of their own.

The system uses two lasers aimed at the same tiny spot. One sends mid-infrared light in bursts, 1,000 times a second. Where molecules in the spot absorb it, the spot warms very slightly. A second laser, a red one, picks up the small changes that the warming causes.

Sweep that spot across a sample, point by point, and the result is a map of where a chosen chemical sits. Because visible red light does the detecting, the map shows tissue chemistry detail finer than a single cell.

No stain is needed. Pathology labs normally dye tissue before anyone looks at it, and dyes are often impractical outside the lab because of toxicity, regulation and cost, the team wrote.

Shrinking the machine meant solving one stubborn problem. The red light and the mid-infrared light have to land on exactly the same point. However, lens materials that work well for one tend to work badly for the other.

Materials that pass red light often absorb mid-infrared, while ones built for mid-infrared can bend different colors by different amounts and blur the signal.

Reddy’s team sent the infrared light through a special glass fiber made for those wavelengths, which did away with bulky open-air optics.

They also swapped lenses for mirrors, since a mirror reflects both kinds of light. A curved mirror at the tip focuses both beams onto one spot.

“Careful optical design and alignment helped to minimize the image distortions that mirrors can cause. We also designed the handheld scanner to connect to the light source through a fiber-optic cable, allowing it to be maneuvered easily around a sample,” Reddy said.

The lasers and electronics sit in a base unit of less than 1.5 square feet (0.14 square meters). Commercial instruments of this kind take up more than 9 square feet (0.84 square meters) of optical table, more than a card table’s top.

The team first scanned a test chart patterned onto a crystal slide. The handheld system picked out details about 0.00008 inches (2 micrometers) across. Its images of the chart closely matched a commercial benchtop instrument’s.

Next came human tissue: thin slices of cervical cancer from two patients and ovarian cancer from two others.

Images from the handheld probe and the benchtop machine correlated at 0.90, compared at a coarse level of detail, on a scale where 1 would be a perfect match.

In ovarian cancer tissue, the tissue chemistry patterns it recorded agreed with a standard lab infrared measurement at 0.935 on a similar scale.

In plain terms, the small probe recorded close to what the big machines did. Its scans also lined up with dyed slices of the same tissue, the kind that pathologists read every day. Connective tissue that the dye highlighted showed up strongly in the undyed chemical image.

A bone marrow biopsy from a patient gave a harder test. Doctors check marrow for a buildup of collagen, a kind of scarring linked to the marrow making fewer blood cells. They usually spot it with a stain that turns collagen blue.

Without any stain, the chemical scan picked out the dense framework of bone in the sample. It also showed the collagen wrapped around a blood vessel, matching the blue in a stained slice next to it.

The team tried a second scanning style on mouse kidney tissue, with the mirror at the tip both turning and moving. That scan showed a glomerulus, one of the kidney’s tiny filters, along with chemical detail that a plain image from the red laser alone didn’t have.

Every sample was a thin, preserved slice on a slide. The team hasn’t imaged living tissue, and none of the samples was fresh from surgery, which is the setting Reddy described.

“Although the current platform is still a research prototype, it establishes a technical foundation for field-deployable, label-free chemical imaging,” Reddy said.

The probe also used only part of the range its laser can reach. That laser is built from four chips that point in slightly different directions, so crossing from one chip to the next meant realigning the light into the fiber by hand.

In these tests, a moving mirror inside the probe or a stage that shifted the sample did the scanning.

The team hasn’t yet tested whether these scans can help a doctor tell cancer from healthy tissue. The researchers plan to widen the range of infrared light the probe uses and to speed up scanning, which would make holding it freehand more practical.

Before any clinical use, its repeatability, safety and diagnostic performance still have to be tested under realistic clinical conditions.

The full study was published in the journal Optica.
