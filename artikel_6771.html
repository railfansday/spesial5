In <i><em>The Good Daughter,</em></i> Rose Byrne and Meghann Fahy Are in the Pursuit of Truth, Justice, and Nailing Their Georgian Accents

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Based on the New York Times best-selling novel by Karin Slaughter, the series is coming to Peacock

In her 2017 New York Times best-selling novel The Good Daughter, Karin Slaughter introduced the world to Charlie and Sam, daughters of the local defense attorney who is both revered and hated by the citizens of Pikeville, Georgia. One night, the family is caught up in a horrific act of violence that leaves it in ruins. Years later, this traumatic night creeps up into the sisters’ lives, forcing them to grapple with their memory and the nature of truth.

Rose Byrne and Meghann Fahy bring the story to life as Sam and Charlie, respectively, in Peacock’s six-part adaptation of this sensational novel (also written by Slaughter herself). Despite their misgivings about their father, played by Brendan Gleeson, the two sisters pursue careers as lawyers, with Charlie staying in Pikeville and Sam moving away to New York. After violent crime continues to rattle their hometown, the estranged sisters find themselves back together to solve a seemingly never-ending series of misdoings plaguing their childhood home.

Ahead of the November 12 release, Byrne and Fahy discuss memorizing 10-page scenes, the pursuit of truth, and how forgiveness can set you free.

Rose Byrne: One of the producers, Bruna Papandrea, is an old friend of mine from Australia, and she’s incredible. We’d been wanting to work together, and she brought me the project. I read the novel and read the screenplay. They’re obviously very, very different forms, [but] I loved the genre, and I hadn’t done something in this space for a while. It’s a very suspenseful, edge-of-your-seat thriller, but really, at its core, it’s about an emotional bond between sisters. That was really fascinating to me, examining the sibling relationship and the effect it has on your life and who you are.

Meghann Fahy: I also read the novel before we started shooting, which I found to be really informative because there’s so much in it that’s not even a part of the show. It felt like extra information. Ultimately, I just really love the journey of these characters, processing their grief and their loss and their trauma and coming out the other side feeling lighter and more connected to the people that they hold most dear. That is such a worthy story to have on-screen.

MF: I loved Charlie because she felt very open and loose and had her own sort of energy that she was ruled by. She didn’t really care what other people thought, which, internally, is actually not true. The duality of those things really excited me.

RB: Karin [Slaughter] just writes such rich female roles, from the novel to the page. We were just blessed to have flawed, complicated women who were damaged but also resilient and survivors, which is so great—important to acknowledge and celebrate in that sense.

RB: We were really questioning [Karin] about her family and the origin story of these characters in her own world and the world of Georgia and Atlanta. It’s a very specific place and people. It feels like that in the book. We wanted the show to feel like that. I do remember really digging deep with her for her inspiration on where they came from. That was really fun because she’s very open and candid.

MF: Her dialogue is so specific. It was really important to her that we stayed very true to the words that she had written them. There’s a very particular rhythm to that and a very particular energy and cadence. That was hugely informative too, in terms of how Karin pictured these people moving through their story.

RB: We hung out in Brooklyn [where we live], and by the time I got to [Gerogia], they had [already] been shooting for a month. That was nice because it lent itself to Sam’s awkwardness, her fish-out-of-water family dynamic. We all worked with Kate Wilson, who’s an extraordinary vocal coach and the head of voice at Juilliard.

MF: We spent a fair amount of time sitting on a couch and running lines to get them into our bodies because there are some scenes that, for television specifically, are quite long, which is really nice to do because television scenes are typically quite short. It was like memorizing little pieces of a play here and there.

RB: We both enjoy [running lines]. Not everybody does. Some people don’t want to do that.

MF: I had a really hard time saying the word lawyer [laughs]. It’s not a sound I’ve heard before. And one time I was ad-libbing and I called one of the villains in the show a “redneck.” Karin came on set, and she said, “It’s actually ‘hillbilly.’” She basically taught me that the reason that both of those obviously derogatory terms exist is based on the landscape of where the people are from. Since Georgia is hilly, it’s “hillbilly.” She said, “When we are going to say something mean about someone, you’re gonna insult them, make sure you’re using the right word.”

RB: In general, it’s always a stretch for me because I’m from such a different part of the world. Also, my character has dysarthria, so that was the stuttering effect that she had. That was pretty challenging to try to get that exactly right. Then, the character has a limp, so I had a lot going on—a lot of spinning plates.

RB: You’ve spoken really well to it. It does have a specificity of location. It’s a very universal story. Everyone can relate to traumas of family and complexities of family, and then there’s this incredibly complex picture Karin presents of the justice system and how it works and how it’s corrupt. It can be rigged, but occasionally there can be some good guys in there. It’s nuanced, and I think she presents a pretty great portrayal of that in the novel and in the screenplays as well.

RB: They were extraordinarily long scenes, you’re right [looking at Fahy]. That’s really rare, isn’t it?

MF: It’s very rare. Television is usually three pages long. We were doing like 10 pages. We did a 10-page scene at one point.

RB: It’s also daunting to do a flashback. I find them very daunting. I find that’s a real leap of faith. Is this going to work? Are we going to be able to sell this flashback? We had a huge flashback scene that was really pivotal to everything, explaining these characters and where we meet them in the present day. Steph Green is our extraordinary director, and she really should be credited for piecing this massive tapestry together.

MF: I don’t even know how she physically was on set for that number of hours. Let alone in the capacity that she was there and present with everyone the whole time.

RB: We were dealing with big, sensitive issues, and she was really aware of that. Couldn’t have been more graceful and tactful.

RB: It has a huge part of the story. Forgiving their father too. Sam is so furious with him and has a lot of anger. She speaks about her anger issues, and she has such fury toward her dad and his decisions. It’s only through this experience they have that she understands him and his choices. Her sister’s choices as well. It’s hard to forgive people. It’s hard to forgive family, but it’ll set you free.

MF: What’s cool about the way that the show presents the ideas is that it’s a very human experience. We all feel like we know how something’s going to turn out, and we know we’ve decided how to feel about it, but we rarely allow ourselves to imagine that something is going to turn out better than we think or better than we hoped. This show lets us see that play out because each character in the show, at a certain point, when they do surprise themselves by having a relationship with their truth, with someone else’s truth, they’re all better off for it. That’s where the hopeful component of the show comes into play. I really hope that’s ultimately what people take away from it.
