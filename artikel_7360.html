This week’s Future of TV Briefing looks at the economics behind brands sponsoring creator-led long-form original video series.

A creator-led long-form original video series can cost a brand anywhere from $150,000 to $5 million, according to Cyndi Steele, a former TV executive producer who leads talent management firm G&B Digital Management’s &Beyond Creators’ Studio.

A six- to seven-figure investment may be an eye-popping amount for CFOs wary of buying into creators. But paying that premium begins to make more sense as video platforms like YouTube, Instagram and TikTok have started highlighting serialized shows and as brands treat creators less like media properties and more like creative collaborators.

Fifty-seven percent of brands said that generating content is a goal for their creator campaigns, according to a study by LTK and Northwestern University released last month. Your reaction to that stat may be something like, “Isn’t generating content 100% the point of brands hiring creators for campaigns?” Yeah. But the shift underlying the stat is how brands are looking to squeeze more content out of the content that creators produce beyond social.

Recycling creator content to this extent requires a lot of content. Which is where the long-form series pitch can come in.

Consider a brand-sponsored, creator-led series that spans 10 long-form episodes plus three short-form videos per episode for a total of 40 pieces of content. That can average between $300,000 to $500,000, according to Steele. Again, that’s a pretty penny. But consider the cost for a brand to produce a traditional TV show.

“When I was in unscripted reality TV, let’s say 10 episodes of a 40-minute-long show, the budget for that is usually around like $5 million. That’s only to do literally the physical production, nothing else,” Steele said.

IP ownership is the least settled aspect of the deal, as Digiday recently reported. For starters, it’s subject to how many ownership the creator retains as well as &Beyond’s ownership stake. The studio prefers first to agree to an ownership split with the creator.

“If it’s an original concept that we’re developing as the exclusive development and production partner, we do split ownership with our talent. A lot of times we like to have the talent typically have the majority ownership. So it a lot of times looks like a 60-40 or 70-30 [split],” Steele said.

But then where does the brand’s cut come from? Well, that becomes a re-appraisal of everyone’s equity. But only if the brand opts to pay for IP ownership.

“The way we’ve approached it so far is they’re not owning the IP. But we can set up specifically owned assets within there, and they can have first right of refusal for future seasons,” Steele said.

Then if a show succeeds and there are future seasons, the brand can pick back up the IP ownership conversation.

“From a brand’s perspective, I think a lot of them aren’t ready to fully dive in with a full IP investment at that level because it is something that takes a long time to really come back and to grow. But from our standpoint, it’s fine because we approach it [by] pulling a little bit from the model of how our managers are currently doing brand deals with the talent and then also how we experience doing brand integrations from the traditional side. It’s just a lot more flexibility,” said Steele.
