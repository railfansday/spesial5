When Your Loved Ones Are Your Child’s Biggest Critic

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

ADDitude readers share the hurtful ways family and friends treat their neurodivergent children, from spewing ignorant comments to purposefully excluding them, and how they combat them.

ADHD understanding has improved in recent decades, but many people still just don’t get it – and sometimes those people are the ones closest to us.

Hearing your child criticized or misjudged hurts. Hearing criticisms and barbs from family members and friends who don’t understand ADHD ushers in a complicated whirlwind of emotions. You may feel like an inadequate parent. You may worry whether your child will be accepted at family get-togethers. You may grow weary of explaining your child’s condition.

If this is you, you’re not alone. More than 73% of ADDitude readers say they have relatives or friends who criticize or dislike their neurodivergent child. Read about their experiences and how they navigate them below.

“The older generations are close-minded or under-educated about ADHD and make it seem like it’s made up or an excuse. They say, ‘Everyone says they have ADHD’ or ‘You need to be a better parent.’ I am mostly there to protect my children and correct the person who says it, so my kids don’t feel they are less than.” – Gina, California

“I had a friend who would sugar up my kids intentionally to ‘prove’ that I was over-reacting about the negative side effects of sugar. When the kids misbehaved, she blamed it on my ‘inconsistent parenting’ and not the ADHD brain on sugar and food-coloring!” – An ADDitude Reader

“We’ve had to deal with side remarks at times from relatives and friends. One example would be someone making retorts to their child like ‘Hey, what are you doing? Even John Doe (my son) would behave better than you right now.’ It’s using my son’s negative behaviors as a frame of reference that my wife and I don’t find helpful at all.” – Jono, Canada

“When my daughter was younger, I was still learning how to handle her behavior, but having family and others say that she was out of control, and that I let her get away with behaviors, really hurt. It made me feel like a bad parent. It took years of working with therapists for me to understand that her behavior and diagnosis were not my fault.” – Deneise, West Virginia

“They just don’t seem to understand that ADHD behaviors aren’t always choices. The only solution seems to be limiting time with the people who don’t understand. Plus, now that the kids are older. I explain to them that some people don’t understand.” – Jenny, Virginia

“My sister-in-law criticizes the amount of ‘stuff’ my children have and their ongoing projects. She doesn’t understand that ADHD brains organize differently and create on a whim, or that having the freedom to be creative is where they excel. I usually head off these comments by talking up their creativity and explaining that they are just brilliantly wired differently.” – An ADDitude Reader, Kansas

“My grandparents and siblings definitely thought we were failing at parenting for a while, and a few friends thought I was a bad mom. Most comments centered on not having strict enough rules/seeing my children’s behavior as bad or rude choices vs. dysregulated behaviors. My village now is almost entirely made up of parents of neurodivergent kids, and we’re all so supportive and understanding of how hard everyone works to provide support and education for our children.” – Acadia, Virginia

“My parents say my child is just lazy in school, and he needs to put more effort into everything. So sometimes I just stop telling them the details. For example, in addition to ADHD, my son recently got diagnosed with dyslexia. I won’t tell them. It’s unfortunate, because he is their grandson and they should know, but I just don’t want to discuss it anymore.” – An ADDitude Reader

“I remind them that they have never parented a child with ADHD, so they do not know what it is like to walk in my shoes. I have also sent them articles about ADHD.” – Sarah, Indiana

“Our dearest friends of 20+ years stopped inviting us to their home for Thanksgiving after a particularly difficult time in their home with our children… The best I have been able to engage with them about this is to ask how their nephew who lives in a different city and is also neurodivergent is doing. When they share difficult times in his regard, I focus the discussion on the antecedents to the disruptive behaviors and try to engage them in problem-solving sleuthing. I connect the conversation with my own experience and invite them to do the same. I have had some deep and meaningful conversations with them over the years, but we still are no longer invited to their home.” – Robyn, North Carolina

“I eventually just cut my family off from contact. The pain they caused my kids was not worth maintaining those relationships.” – Christina, Arizona
