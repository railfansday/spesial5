In Mississippi, Democrat Scott Colom’s lead in the race for US Senate expanded to 1,813 votes over incumbent Republican senator Cindy Hyde-Smith as a small number of provisional or affidavit ballots were counted on Thursday.

Up to up to 20,000 outstanding ballots remain to be counted in the state, including late-arriving mail ballots that are valid if they were postmarked by election day and arrive by 10 November, and an estimated 8,000 to 12,000 affidavit ballots, which are those set aside from the initial count due to technical errors, like a lack of photo ID or an incorrect home address, that voters have until next Tuesday to correct. Such errors on ballots can often be corrected through a process known as curing.

Colom told reporters earlier on Thursday that he is “very confident” that the remaining ballots will add to his lead. “Anybody out there who does an analysis of this knows where this is going. The affidavits always move in the Democrat’s favor in Mississippi.”

“I am overwhelmed by the outpouring of support for my campaign as we fight to make sure every lawful ballot is counted,” Colom wrote on social media. “My campaign, alongside the DSCC and Elias Law Group, Lisa Ross, and our great team of local attorneys, are leading the effort to ensure every eligible voter is heard and I am confident that when votes are counted, we will flip this seat.”

The Mississippi Republican Party chair, Mike Hurst, disagreed. “We believe if the laws are followed to a T and these 20,000 outstanding ballots are counted, that senator Cindy Hyde-Smith will be reelected,” Hurst said at a news conference.

When he was pressed to say whether that meant he would accept a Colom win, should the count go go in the Democrat’s favor, Hurst said: “Let me be clear: there is no election-denying going on here. If the laws are followed and the voters spoke, that is it and Mississippi Republicans will accept that.”

Gavin Newsom, California’s governor, commuted the death sentences of more than 500 people on the state’s death row on Thursday, a move that ensures they can never be executed under future administrations.

The Democrat’s mass commutation at the end of his term has converted all of the state’s death sentences to life without the possibility of parole, the largest clearing of any US death row in modern history.

The move comes after the failed execution of Christa Pike in Tennessee sparked global outrage and renewed calls for a nationwide pause on capital punishment.

His decision affects 529 men and 19 women who were sentenced to death in the state, making up the largest death row in the western hemisphere, the governor said in a letter announcing his decision.

“I take this action because I firmly believe that state-sanctioned killing is incompatible with California’s values, undermines the foundations of our legal system, and diminishes each of us,” he wrote. “I know we cannot execute people in our imperfect legal system and remain true to our core values, first among them justice and human decency.”

Newsom, considered a probable candidate for president in 2028, has long opposed the death penalty and issued a moratorium on executions in 2019. He also dismantled the state’s physical death row chamber in the infamous San Quentin prison, transferring people with death sentences to other facilities where they had more freedom of movement and access to programs – for some, a dramatic change after decades isolated in solitary cells.

In New York’s Hudson Valley, Democrat Cait Conley has defeated Republican congressman Mike Lawler to pad her party’s House majority.

Lawler, who is not Jewish but represented a district where one in five residents are Jewish told reporters in May that Republican senator Rand Paul’s son William had drunkenly accosted him at a bar in Washington, saying in “a 10-minute diatribe about Jews” at the Tune Inn Restaurant & Bar, “that he hates Jews and hates gays and doesn’t care if they die”.

Lawler added that Paul told him that he thought he was Jewish. When the Irish and Italian Catholic corrected him, Paul reportedly said: “Oh wow, I’m so sorry for calling you a Jew.”

In an email to reporters on Thursday, Alaska Democrat Mary Peltola’s campaign expressed confidence that she is on track to extend her current 637-vote lead in the US Senate race as tens of thousands of early and absentee ballots are counted, mainly from areas of the state that have favored her in the past.

“The numbers are clear: when every vote is counted, Mary Peltola will be elected as Alaska’s next United States Senator,” Peltola’s campaign manager, Cheryl Bruce, said in a statement. “Tens of thousands of early votes and absentee ballots are still outstanding, and the data shows that these will break for Mary and extend her lead. Despite Sullivan pushing fictitious paths to victory that exclude the voices of thousands of Alaskans, every Alaskan’s vote will be counted, and Mary will win this race.”

The campaign estimates that her lead will be large enough to prevent the incumbent Republican senator, Dan Sullivan, from overtaking her even with second-place votes in the ranked-choice system Alaska uses if no candidate obtains a majority.

“Data suggests that the bulk of outstanding votes are from Anchorage and rural Alaska, both of which have broadly favored Mary in the current count,” the campaign argues in the email.

The Peltola campaign’s estimate matches data analysis from the New York Times, which also reports that, in a surprising twist, more of the voters who backed the other Dan Sullivan, an independent candidate named Daniel J. Sullivan who was reportedly recruited by allies of Peltola to take votes from the incumbent, “ranked Ms. Peltola as their second choice rather than Mr. Sullivan.”

After the incumbent Republican senator’s campaign claimed on Wednesday that he was on track to win, Lakshya Jain, a data analyst who co-founded Split Ticket, called that claim “false”.

“Alaska Senate is >95% likely to go to Peltola at this point,” Jain wrote on social media. “Sullivan is completely screwed given what’s left.”
