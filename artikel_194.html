Multiple reporters said that the phrase “wartime consigliere” had been used to describe Miller by White House sources on Friday, which is a reference to what a close adviser to mafia boss is called in The Godfather.

The phrase has been used repeatedly in recent years by people close to Trump to describe what he is looking for in a right-hand man, who will use slash-and-burn tactics to defend him at all costs.

In 2020, Matt Gaetz, a Republican congressman from Florida at the time who was later Trump’s first choice to be attorney general in his second term, told the New York Times that Trump’s choice of Mark Meadows as his fourth White House chief of staff was “a great move” since “he’s a great wartime consigliere and we’re heading into election season.”

In 2023, as Trump faced multiple criminal investigations, Steve Bannon described his legal adviser Boris Epshteyn as “a wartime consigliere.”

The role Trump seems to be looking to fill is one that was first played in his life by Roy Cohn, his former personal lawyer and fixer, who had been a top aide to senator Joseph McCarthy during investigations into supposed communist infiltration of the US government in the 1950s.

It was Cohn who had first advised Trump in 1973 to respond to the federal government’s allegation that Trump and his father had violated laws against racial discrimination in housing by refusing to rent to Black tenants, by going on the offensive and countersuing the government for $100bn.

In 2017, when the support for Trump’s campaign from the Russian government was under investigation, Trump was enraged that his first attorney general, Jeff Sessions, decided to recuse himself instead of undermining the probe. “Where’s my Roy Cohn?” the president reportedly asked aides.

On Tuesday, voters resoundingly rebuked Donald Trump and his brutal mass deportation campaign that sent military tanks and masked agents though residential streets.

But on Friday, the president doubled down. He named Stephen Miller – the architect of the administration’s most aggressive immigration policies – as his chief of staff.

It was never likely that Trump – who rose to political prominence on the promise that he would expel immigrants en masse from the US – would dampen his agenda, even after voters rejected Republican candidates up and down the ballot, near and far from the southern border, in blue and deep-red states.

But the elevation of Miller – a zealous, anti-immigrant crusader who has been by Trump’s side since the president’s first term – is an ominous signal that the deportation machine that Trump has built up over the past two years will be driving ahead at full speed.

Miller more than any single other person is responsible for unleashing a series of violent immigration raids across US cities this year and last, for sending hordes of masked agents in military gear through residential streets to round up undocumented immigrants. He has pushed the administration to all but shut down legal immigration as well. In his previous roles – as a deputy chief of staff and policy adviser – he has stuck close to Trump’s side and stoked the president’s most extreme instincts.

“The elevation of Stephen Miller to White House chief of staff signals a doubling down on the administration’s cruelest impulses,” said Faisal Al-Juburi, the co-chief executive of Raíces, a Texas-based humanitarian aid and immigration services non-profit. “Miller’s promotion guarantees an acceleration of mass deportations and of brute force unleashed within our neighborhoods.”

After immigration agents shot and killed Alex Pretti, a nurse, amid immigration raids in Minneapolis, it was Miller, according to the Wall Street Journal, who raised the idea that Trump could invoke the Insurrection Act to send in military troops.

The US Department of Justice moved on Friday to dismiss the lawsuit it brought against the state of California, in which it sought to keep roughly 20,000 ballots it alleged went to noncitizens from being counted.

The supreme court ruled against the Trump administration on Monday, after several lower courts sided with California officials, who argued the federal government was seeking to interfere in state-run elections.

The request to dismiss the case comes three days after the final votes were cast in the midterm elections and about a week before a scheduled hearing where a special master would have been asked to evaluate the accuracy of the list provided by the federal government.

California officials argued that the list is out of date and includes legitimate voters — a sentiment that the federal judge presiding over the case echoed in his blistering order denying the federal government’s request.

Several newly elected Democrats and Senator Bernie Sanders have renewed calls for datacenter moratoriums in the days after the US midterm elections. The sweeping Democratic victory, which was buoyed in part by backlash to the tech industry, has added momentum to efforts to slow down the multi-trillion-dollar buildout of the controversial facilities.

On the social media platform X, Sanders posted a call for an immediate national datacenter moratorium this week. “Americans don’t want Big Tech oligarchs wrecking the environment, gutting jobs and threatening humanity’s future,” Sanders said on Wednesday.

Sanders was joined by several Democrats, including Representative Yassamin Ansari of Arizona – a state where Elon Musk’s SpaceX has proposed a datacenter project that would span more than 700 acres.

“Top of the Democratic agenda on day 1 needs to be a pause on advanced AI development, regulation of this out of control industry, and a ban on artificial superintelligence,” Ansari posted Wednesday on X. Musk mocked her post in a retweet, and the two sparred, with Ansari forecasting congressional hearings, telling the centi-billionaire: “See you soon.”

The pushback to the buildout of datacenters, which tech companies need to power the AI products that have become central to their businesses, was one of the most visible issues of the midterms. Opposition to datacenters figured heavily in campaigns across the US, while voters passed dozens of local ballot measures aimed at restricting datacenter projects in their states.
