The Black Box Might Have a Window

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

What happens if “nobody can understand these models” turns out to be wrong?

For years, one sentence has done a lot of work in AI: “We don’t really know what’s happening inside these models.”

We say it in standups, in conference talks, in regulatory hearings. It shapes how we evaluate models, how we write policy, and how we talk about risk. It’s close to a founding myth of modern machine learning.

Last month, that sentence got a challenge. The Economist ran a piece headlined “AI models may not be so opaque after all,” with the subtitle “A glimmer of hope for those worried about AI safety.” I couldn’t read the full piece, but the research it seems to be built on is worth talking about.

Nick Cammarata, a former OpenAI researcher now doing interpretability work at Goodfire, argues that the evidence for the previous understanding of neural networks, called superposition, was “misread.” The older picture says that individual neurons hold tangled mixtures of unrelated concepts, which makes models hard to decode. If Cammarata is right, neurons may be much cleaner than that. bankinfosecurity

I’m not an interpretability researcher, and a single claim isn’t a consensus. It will need replication and scrutiny. But the implications deserve attention even if you stay skeptical.

Tooling gets simpler. If neurons are cleaner, then frontier labs and AI researchers wouldn’t need to build special tools to untangle a neuron, so research would be simpler and cheaper. Interpretability could move from a few elite labs to a much wider group. That’s an open-source story, and DEV.to is full of people who build things when the barrier drops. bankinfosecurity

The “black box” framing has been load-bearing. The idea of inexplicable models has become the basis of AI evaluations and, in some cases, regulation. If the premise weakens, the structures built on it may need rethinking. bankinfosecurity

Who gets to explain the model? One thing in the reporting stuck with me. The labs that developed the earlier understanding of neural networks also used it to study their own models, and they could then present models as inherently opaque and difficult to understand. I don’t read that as a conspiracy. Researchers use the best tools they have. But it shows how much power sits with whoever defines what is knowable. And clean neurons would give regulators the possibility of requiring developers to show what’s happening inside the models, instead of relying on the AI companies to interpret the compliance of their outputs. That would be a real shift from “trust our evals” to “show us the internals.” bankinfosecurity bankinfosecurity

Readable neurons don’t automatically mean understood behavior. Seeing the parts is not the same as understanding the system, any more than reading every line of a huge codebase tells you how it behaves in production. Interpretability findings have also had a history of looking promising and then getting complicated. And there’s a quieter risk: if we believe we can see inside, we might trust the model more than we should.

The same goes for the hope in the Economist’s subtitle. “A glimmer” is the right word. It isn’t a solution.

I’d like to hear from people who build with these systems:

Does your day-to-day work change if models become genuinely inspectable? Would you debug a model differently?

Would you trust a model more if you could see inside it, or would that give a false sense of safety?

Should regulators require internal inspection instead of output-based testing, or is that unrealistic?

What would convince you that this claim holds up? What kind of replication would you want to see?

Tell me where you think I’m wrong, especially if you work in interpretability or ML research. I’d rather be corrected than comfortable.
