The AI Investment Race Has More Than One Winning Strategy

By the end of October 2026, Apple's shares had gained approximately 27% for the year. [1] Its performance raises a strategic question for anyone deciding how much their company should invest in artificial intelligence: how much of the technology do you actually need to own to benefit from it?

The company's financial statements reveal a less obvious pattern. In fiscal 2026, the company spent $9.0 billion on property, plant and equipment, down from $12.7 billion a year earlier. Its research and development expense moved in the other direction, rising from $34.6 billion to $45.8 billion. Apple identified infrastructure costs, including AI investments, and employee costs as contributors to the R&D increase. [2] [3]

These are company-wide accounting measures. Neither tells us Apple's total AI spending, and the stock's performance does not establish that a particular AI strategy caused the return. But the combination challenges the usefulness of treating capital expenditure as a scoreboard for AI ambition.

Apple is also choosing where to rely on another company's capabilities. In January 2026, Apple and Google announced a multiyear collaboration under which Google's Gemini models and cloud technology would underpin the next generation of Apple Foundation Models. Their statement said Apple Intelligence would continue to operate on Apple devices and Private Cloud Compute. [4]

A company can invest substantially in delivering AI to customers while making selective choices about which parts of the technology to develop itself. Whether that approach succeeds depends on how its industry evolves and where it can retain economic value.

I find it more useful to begin an AI investment discussion with strategic scenarios. A company can assess how its position would change if capable models became inexpensive and interchangeable, or if an AI assistant became the customer's main route to a purchase. Those futures put different assets at risk and create different opportunities.

Each possibility creates a different reason to invest. Several can apply to the same company at the same time. The resulting investment portfolio may include proprietary development, commercial software, partnerships, and experiments whose purpose is to resolve an important uncertainty.

Consider the first scenario: capable AI becomes widely available, while the company's existing products and customer relationships remain commercially valuable.

There is evidence that some AI capabilities have become dramatically cheaper to access. Stanford's 2025 AI Index reported that the inference cost of reaching GPT-3.5-level performance declined by more than a factor of 280 between November 2022 and October 2024. This describes a benchmark-specific historical change, rather than a forecast for total enterprise AI costs. [5]

Nevertheless, it illustrates a commercially attractive possibility for a company with valuable distribution. Competing technology suppliers could improve the capabilities available to that company while reducing the price of obtaining them. The company can then concentrate investment on the customer experience and the systems through which those capabilities are delivered.

This is one way to interpret Apple's partnership approach. If customers continue to value its devices and ecosystem, Apple could benefit as external models improve. Owning the customer experience could prove more valuable than independently recreating every underlying capability. That is a strategic hypothesis, not a conclusion that the partnership has already established.

The investment implication extends beyond consumer technology. A regional distributor could buy a capable language model while developing the integrations that connect customer requests to its inventory, pricing, and delivery systems. The distributor would still need to invest in reliable execution. It would have little reason to train a general-purpose model unless owning that capability created a demonstrable advantage.

This scenario favors buying or partnering for widely available capabilities and retaining control over the parts of the service that customers value. The company should also preserve its ability to switch suppliers. A low initial price is less attractive if changing providers later requires rebuilding the business.

The second scenario is that AI creates product differentiation customers will pay for, and the company possesses assets that make that differentiation difficult to reproduce.

John Deere's See & Spray provides a concrete example of the kind of capability involved. The system combines computer vision with machinery to identify plants and target spraying. Blue River Technology reports that the technology was used across more than five million acres in 2025 and that customers reduced non-residual herbicide use by nearly 50%. These are supplier-reported results, and reduced herbicide use is not the same as a 50% reduction in total farming costs. [6]

The strategic opportunity is the combination of perception, physical equipment, and dependable performance in the field. For an equipment manufacturer, investing in that combination can support a differentiated product. An agricultural customer buys an operating result: effective weed control at an attractive total cost.

Those are different investment cases for the same technology. Deere must assess whether its product development generates customer demand and returns it can sustain against competitors. A farmer must assess whether the savings and other benefits justify the equipment, licensing, maintenance, and operating costs under local conditions.

This distinction also limits the argument for building proprietary AI. Access to internal data is a starting point. The company must show that the data improves an outcome customers value, that competitors cannot readily reproduce the result, and that maintaining the capability is economically attractive. Custom development without those conditions can leave the company maintaining expensive technology with little commercial distinction.

Investments in this scenario deserve measures of differentiation: willingness to pay, customer retention, product adoption, or a sustained performance advantage. A product team that demonstrates a better model benchmark still needs to establish that the improvement matters to a buyer.

The third scenario is that AI changes how customers find, select, or purchase products, putting an existing source of revenue at risk.

Google Search illustrates the scale of the strategic exposure. Google Search & other generated $63.35 billion in revenue in the third quarter of 2026, up from $56.57 billion a year earlier. Those figures are revenue attributable to existing business, not necessarily one created by AI. [7]

If more customers begin their research or purchasing journeys inside AI assistants, the business that controls those interactions could gain influence over which suppliers are considered and how transactions are completed. For an incumbent, investing in the new interface can therefore have a defensive purpose even when its immediate monetization is uncertain.

The relevant comparison includes the outcome of allowing another company to take that position. A proposal can look unattractive against today's business and attractive against a credible scenario in which customer access deteriorates. That comparison requires evidence about customer behavior and plausible estimates of revenue at risk.

Calling an investment defensive should not give it an unlimited budget. Management should specify which customer behavior it is trying to protect, how quickly that behavior could change, and whether a partnership could achieve the same objective with less capital. Milestones might include retaining valuable customer journeys, matching an emerging service expectation, or establishing a viable way to monetize a new interaction.

Apple can face this scenario alongside the first. Affordable external models could strengthen its products, while independent AI assistants could also change how customers use devices and applications. Partnering for model capability and investing to retain influence over the customer experience can therefore be consistent parts of the same strategy.

The fourth scenario is that AI improves operating performance while the main reasons customers choose the company remain broadly intact.

A study published in The Quarterly Journal of Economics in 2025 examined an AI assistant used by 5,172 customer-support agents. Access to the assistant increased issues resolved per hour by 15% on average. Less experienced and lower-skilled workers saw gains of approximately 30%, while results differed substantially for more experienced workers. These findings came from one company and should not be treated as a universal forecast. [8]

For a business buying such a capability, the investment case can be attractive without requiring ownership of a foundation model. The work is to select a suitable system, connect it to reliable information, and redesign the service process so improved performance translates into a useful result.

The uneven gains in the study also matter for capital allocation. A company may obtain better returns by concentrating implementation and training on the employees or workflows that benefit most. Distributing identical tools to everyone is a deployment choice; it does not establish the best allocation of spending.

In this scenario, operating measures should carry most of the burden of proof. A customer-service investment might be judged on the cost of a successfully resolved issue, repeat contacts, and customer satisfaction. These measures help prevent apparent speed improvements from being purchased through poorer service or additional work elsewhere.

The farmer buying See & Spray also fits this scenario. The technology may be strategically differentiating for its developer while functioning as an efficiency investment for its customer. Its classification depends on whose business case is being evaluated.

The framework becomes more useful when we apply several scenarios to one company. Alphabet provides a particularly clear example because it develops AI, sells AI-related services, and uses AI internally.

Search gives Alphabet an existing business to defend as customer behavior changes. Google Cloud creates an opportunity to sell capabilities to other companies: its third-quarter 2026 revenue was $30.71 billion, with operating income of $11.12 billion. Those are segment results across Cloud's business, not a measurement of standalone AI returns. [7]

Alphabet also uses the technology to improve its own operations. On its October 2026 earnings call, management said selected teams across Chrome, Ads, and Search had recently used a new model to ship 35% more code with 30% fewer errors. This is a company-reported claim about selected teams over a short period, rather than an independently established company-wide productivity result. [9]

The three uses call for different investment tests. Search requires an assessment of customer access and monetization. Cloud requires an assessment of demand, utilization, and returns on the infrastructure needed to serve customers. Internal engineering tools require evidence about accepted output, reliability, and the time required to deliver useful changes.

They may share models and infrastructure. That can make a common investment more attractive, but it also creates an accounting challenge. Shared costs must be recognized, and benefits must not be counted repeatedly in separate business cases. Revenue from selling a capability and savings from using it internally need distinct, defensible estimates.

A single corporate label such as 'aggressive AI investor' conceals these differences. A company can rationally build a specialized capability for one business, partner for another, and buy conventional software for a third. The relevant unit of analysis is the business activity and the value it creates or protects.

Scenario planning should then influence the timing of investment. A company does not need certainty about the eventual market structure before taking action. It needs to understand which commitments are difficult to reverse and which actions improve its ability to respond as evidence develops.

Reliable data, integration skills, and the ability to evaluate AI on actual business tasks can be useful across several futures. Large infrastructure commitments or dependence on a single supplier may be harder to reverse. The sequence should reflect both the value of learning and the cost of delay. Where experience compounds or customer relationships are being established quickly, waiting can itself be expensive.

One practical approach is to ask each investment sponsor to document the scenario supporting the proposal, the capability the company must control, and the evidence that would change the decision. The initial funding can then match what is currently known, with additional commitments tied to specific findings.

For a distributor buying external models, the review could track whether suppliers remain interchangeable on actual customer tasks and whether switching costs are rising. For an equipment manufacturer, it could examine whether a specialized AI feature produces customer benefits that competing systems cannot match economically. For a business exposed to new AI intermediaries, it could track changes in where valuable customer journeys begin.

These signals should have consequences. A persistent performance advantage might support more proprietary development. Improving vendor alternatives could justify shifting resources toward integration and customer service. A sustained movement of customers to a new interface could accelerate a defensive investment. A project that repeatedly misses its operating objectives should face a funding decision even if the underlying technology continues to improve.

This also changes how an executive team should discuss the total AI budget. A company should be able to explain how much is supporting proven operating improvements, how much is developing a distinctive offering, and how much is protecting customer access or resolving uncertainty. The balance will change as evidence accumulates. There is no reason to expect the right allocation to be identical across businesses.

Apple's approach is interesting because it brings those choices into view. Its long-term success will depend on the capabilities it builds, the relationships it retains, and the terms on which it can obtain technology from others. The size of a capital-spending announcement captures only part of that decision.

An AI investment strategy should explain the future the company is preparing for, the value it expects to capture in that future, and the capabilities it must control to do so. It should also identify the evidence that would lead management to invest more, change partners, or stop. That gives leaders a basis for deciding how much to spend and where each additional dollar belongs.
