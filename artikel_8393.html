Grumpylocks and the Three Bears

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

The Ice Age Trail’s camping options are a little more restrictive than you might expect. Even the western region has plenty of segments where you can’t simply hit your miles for the day and just find the nearest flat spot. Here’s where I spent my nights on the IAT.

While some trailheads on the IAT had “No Overnight Parking/Camping” signs, the four where I stayed did not. Just four days into my hike, I camped at a trailhead next to a quiet dirt road where traffic wasn’t the reason I kept waking up. Heat, humidity, and stagnant air were the problem.

There was still no hint of a breeze by morning, and I was sweating before I left camp. I was filtering water at the next trailhead when a pair of day-hikers arrived. They parked their car, hit the trail, and it was probably half an hour before I caught up with them. They smelled nice and clean: a scent I picked up at least a minute before I saw them again. That’s how still the air was. I’m sure I smelled fine.

That morning’s trail was lined with some sort of tall grass which was shedding tiny disk-like seeds that stuck to my sweaty skin. By the time I reached the road to Haugen, my arms and legs had the texture of 60 grit sandpaper.

The three miles of blacktop between me and my intended destination were hot: probably the most uncomfortable of the entire trail. The Shady Rest Campground was about an hour away, and I was looking forward to a cool shower and a cold drink. FarOut comments even mentioned that they have air-conditioned cabins. When I arrived, the office was closed. It was a Monday.

Fortunately, one of the owner’s friends was nearby. She called the owner, who rented me a cabin. She also gave me a ride into town and some quarters for the shower. Again, I’m sure I smelled fine.

At Lona’s Corner Cafe, I sat as far as possible from the other customers. I discovered a tick on my arm during lunch. As casually and covertly as possible, I removed the tick using the array of utensils in front of me, making sure it would never bite anyone else.

After a quick stop at the grocery store for five days’ worth of food, I walked back to the campground to take a shower. Once I’d completed my chores, I literally chilled in my cabin for the rest of the afternoon.

I visited the store again on my way out of town the next morning. After breakfast on the bench across the street, I headed south on the Wild Rivers State Trail, then east on the Tuscobia State Trail. Both trails are wide (former railroads) but I was still a little surprised to be overtaken by a horse and buggy. At the end of the day, with no better options, I stopped at another trailhead.

The mosquitoes were hungry the following morning. I was carrying a small bottle of DEET for emergency use, but I wasn’t sure the situation qualified. I’d encountered worse. Still, after eight itchy miles, I wanted a break. I left the trail at the north end of Stout Road and enjoyed an hour of bug-free bliss. Even the hound that bounded beyond its house’s boundary to hound me wasn’t really a problem, despite its loud, raspy bark. It was never going to bite. It was, as they say, “All mouth and no trousers.”

I know some people hike every inch of official trail, but I need flexibility during a thru-hike. This wouldn’t be the last time I deviated from the trail, and in the end, my way was no more arbitrary than the IAT itself. The Blue Hills segments I hiked that day have since been re-routed, reducing the number of official trail miles.

This was to be my first of many days that were more road than trail. No surprise, the air was still and the mosquitoes voracious. I used some of my precious DEET to shield me while I broke camp and hiked 2.6 miles. For most of that distance, the trail crossed private land, and I could smell the cows long before I saw them. After an hour, I started a road-walk that would last all day.

At the gas station in Weyerhaeuser, I topped-up on fuel and coolant. On the other side of town, I spotted an animal that I initially thought was a large dog. I’d never seen such a lean, long-legged bear. She saw me and hurried across the road with two cubs close behind.

Eventually, the scenery started to change. Isolated farms were replaced with frequent lakeside houses. Having covered 21 miles by 1 PM, I stopped for a break at Round Lake County Park. I was way ahead of schedule, just three miles from the DCA were I intended to camp.

The IAT passes plenty of state, county, and city parks, each of which is a gift when you’re a thru-hiker. They typically have water, picnic tables, a pavilion, electrical outlets, trash cans, and pit (sometimes flush) toilets. Round Lake was my first county park on the IAT. I spent three hours at a picnic table in the pavilion, and used all the amenities. I also spoke to Paul and Cindy, the first of many hikers I’d meet who were hiking the entire IAT, one piece at a time. They offered to leave a beer for me at a local bar, and I eagerly accepted. This was a trail-magic first.

I hung out until four, went for a swim, rinsed my clothes, and wrung them out. They were almost dry by the time I arrived at The King’s Inn, two miles later. Paul and Cindy had indeed bought me a beer. I had three more with dinner, then walked one more mile to my campsite. Not a typical day, but an enjoyable one.

The morning was cool and overcast, and a light rain started at midday. A runner training for a 50k overtook me, slowing for a few minutes to chat before continuing his out-and-back. For the next 90 minutes, the rain just kept getting heavier. Eventually, on a wide section of trail, I put up my tent in “shelter” mode: footprint and rainfly only. I’d applied seam-sealant before my hike, but I discovered that the fly still leaked. The runner, Jacob, returned 45 minutes later. There was enough room in my makeshift shelter for two, even if it was a little cramped. We went our separate ways an hour after that, once the rain began to ease. It was still sprinkling when I reached my DCA an hour later, but a mylar heat-sheet beneath the leaky fly successfully redirected the drips.

Before leaving Chippewa County for Taylor County, I had a decision to make. If I spent two or three days on the IAT, I’d hike more than 40 miles of road and only half a mile of trail.

If I made my own way, I’d hike 25 miles of busy state highway and zero miles of trail. I chose option one.

I arrived in Cornell just after 9 AM, and the mayor pulled over to chat as I was walking down Main Street. After breakfast at Sam’s Place, I bought a five day resupply and hit the road at 11 o’clock. I stopped at Hidden Shady Nook campground mid-afternoon. The bartender introduced me to Spotted Cow beer, and I enjoyed a few air-conditioned hours overlooking Pike Lake.

The next day, Sunday, was even shorter. I left at 7 AM, and reached the south end of Otter Lake in less than four hours. The boat ramp was busy and the parking lot was half full. I ate some snacks while filtering water, walked a quarter mile back to the trailhead, and 0.6 trail miles to the DCA. The day was warm, with no rain in the forecast, so I didn’t bother with the rain fly. I put up my tent in the shade of an impressive tree, and settled down to read.

An hour later, although I didn’t hear anyone approach, I heard a voice say flatly, “Oh.” I raised my head just in time to see a guy turn around and leave. He was dressed more like a golfer than a hiker, and he wasn’t carrying anything. I guessed he was scouting the campsite before bringing gear and/or people.

At 4:30 PM, I had another visitor. This one wasn’t using the trail, and made no effort to hide its approach through dead leaves and dry brush. I raised my head again, and turned awkwardly to my right. An adult black bear. Realization took a second, but during that second, she continued to move deliberately, directly towards me.

“THAT’S CLOSE ENOUGH!” I was nearly at full volume.

She stopped immediately, calmly, almost casually. She avoided looking in my direction, but stayed where she was, about 20 yards away. Then, almost in unison, two furry little faces popped up out of the brush next to her. It was the cubs’ turn to take a moment. While they gathered their thoughts, I sat bolt upright. The nearest cub sprinted in my general direction.

Fortunately, the cub hadn’t been overcome by curiosity. It wasn’t helpless to resist the aroma of dried apricots coming from my bear canister. It had simply decided that, without checking for any alternatives, there was only one suitable tree in the vicinity. That tree was about 10 yards from my tent, a yard or two off the direct line of sight between me and its mother.

The sound of claws on tree-bark was surprisingly loud. Also, that little bear could climb surprisingly quickly. The second cub was close behind, just as quick, and climbed just as loudly. They went high enough that I couldn’t see or hear them.

Momma bear didn’t react when the cubs’ instincts/training kicked in. I didn’t see any change in her body language. She wasn’t aggressive, agitated, or even concerned. She just stood there, on all fours, shuffling a little from left to right, looking anywhere but at me.

They all needed some space, I decided. I narrated what I was doing as I unzipped my tent, put on my flipflops, and zipped up the door. I continued talking as I unscrewed the lid of my bear can, stuffed my toiletries into it, and screwed the lid back down. Click. Click. There wasn’t enough space in the canister for my evening meal, so I took it with me.

Armed with two blocks of ramen, I retreated down a short spur trail marked by little yellow flags. After about 20 yards, I shut the f*ck up, turned around, and waited. I was close enough to intervene if the bear went for my stuff, but far enough that the cubs would come down from the tree. That was the plan, anyway.

Momma was no longer interested in the canister, or any of my gear. It took her about 30 minutes to shuffle slowly, hesitantly to the base of the tree. She stood on her hind legs, front paws against the trunk, looking up, trying to coax the cubs down. I couldn’t see or hear any movement from above. All I could hear were Momma’s gentle calls, repeated every minute or two. Each call sounded like a sigh mixed with a groan, followed by three short exhalations through pursed lips. It was interesting, but ineffective. The cubs weren’t coming down.

By now, I’d accumulated 45 minutes’ worth of mosquito bites, and I’d run out of patience. I turned my narration back on and flipflopped back to my tent.

Breaking camp took about 15 minutes – a new personal record. I bundled everything haphazardly into my pack while giving a grumpy commentary. Mom, meanwhile, was still giving words of encouragement to the cubs.

I didn’t see the bears again, despite spending the night at the boat-ramp parking lot, only a quarter mile from the DCA as the crow flies.

This wasn’t a habituated bear, it was a food-conditioned bear. I don’t think she just happened to pick up the scent of my bear canister. With only one exception, all black bears I’ve previously encountered have been quick to show me their big jiggly ass when they ran away. This bear was brazen enough to come into camp in broad daylight, so even if she’d run away, she was probably coming back at night. The cubs did a fine job of distracting her from her mission.

I wonder how many times golfer-guy had hiked to the DCA only to find it occupied? Enough to justify a 1.2 mile round trip, apparently. Surely there can’t be that many IAT hikers using the DCA. And even if IAT hikers were always camping there, they probably know how to store their food properly. It seems likely that the DCA is frequented by non-outdoorsy types who don’t know that “A fed bear is a dead bear.”

At the very least, a fed bear is a f*cking nuisance. Its cubs might grow up to be just as much of a problem. I’m probably preaching to the choir when I tell you to store your pic-a-nic baskets properly.

I added a FarOut comment about the bear, and submitted a trail alert on the IATA website. I used a little less detail for those though.
