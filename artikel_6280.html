Scientists have found the remnants of supermountains in the seabed around Antarctica. They say these ancient peaks may have provided the nutrients that led to an explosion of complex life millions of years later. However, others are more skeptical about these conclusions.

About 600 million years ago, landmasses in the Southern Hemisphere collided to form the supercontinent Gondwana, which included what is now South America, Africa, Arabia, Madagascar, India, Australia and Antarctica. At that time, there was multicellular life, but it was very primitive.

Previous research suggested that the continental collision created massive Himalaya-like mountains in what is now Antarctica. Now, in a new study, published online Sept. 12 in the journal Earth and Planetary Science Letters, scientists say they've found the sediment that accumulated in the oceans when the Gondwanan mountains eroded over millennia.

"The results suggest that the assembly of Gondwana involved multiple major continental collisions, producing an extensive network of mountain belts comparable in scale to the modern Himalayas and Tibetan Plateau," Nathan Daczko, a petrologist at Macquarie University in Australia who was not involved in the research, told Live Science in an email.

"The study highlights just how much geological history remains hidden beneath Antarctica."

Most of Antarctica is trapped beneath an ice sheet, so it is difficult to understand its evolutionary history.

To address this, the team drilled into the seabed at 12 locations around Antarctica, extracting soil samples. They were looking for ancient crystals called detrital zircon. Zircon is a mineral that grows in rocks and is strong enough to remain chemically intact when the rock erodes.

"We can date it very, very precisely, and from the geochemistry, and especially the trace elements, we can determine where the zircon came from," Ian Campbell, a geochemist at the Australian National University and a co-author of the study, told Live Science.

They dated 1,712 zircon grains extracted from marine sediments and compared the abundance of zircons in Antarctica with a global zircon database, across different time scales.

"We have used the zircon record to show that the mountains that formed during the amalgamation of Gondwana were the largest and most extensive in the history of the Earth, and they included some very high mountains like the Himalayas today," Campbell said.

Alan Collins, an earth scientist at the University of Adelaide in Australia, said that he was surprised by the scale of the erosion from these rocks. "It beautifully supports ideas that the mountains that formed as the supercontinent Gondwana amalgamated were amongst the largest on Earth through deep time," he told Live Science in an email.

The formation of Gondwama produced one-fifth of the world's zircons, and the creation of the supercontinent Pangaea around 320 million years ago produced 14%, the study found.

Elias Rugen, a geochemist at the University of Southampton in the U.K. who wasn't involved in the research, was surprised by the "abundance of zircons associated with Gondwana's assembly in the Antarctica samples." "It is remarkable how much information about Antarctica's geological past can be recovered from these tiny minerals, particularly when so much of the continent remains hidden beneath ice," he said.

However, he cautioned against estimating the range's height from the zircon data. "It is really difficult to infer a mountain's height from data like this," he said.

An illustration of what the Pangea supercontinent would have looked like.

The formation and erosion of the Gondwana supermountains had consequences for the rest of the planet and the life on it, the authors said.

"In order to get life, you need nutrients," Campbell said. "At that time, most of the life was in the oceans, so you need to get nutrients into the ocean."

Today, the world's biggest ongoing erosion event is the Himalayas. This massive mountain range is eroding, and sending sediment down rivers and into the ocean. This deep-sea sediment deposit is called the Bengal Fan and is Earth's largest such deposit.

In a similar way, Gondwana's massive mountains also "sent lots of nutrients into the ocean," Campbell said, which "significantly advanced the evolution of life" on Earth.

Soon after the formation of Gondwana, the planet entered the Cambrian period. This era, which lasted from about 541 million to 485 million years ago, saw a dramatic diversification of life on Earth, known as the Cambrian explosion. The period saw the rise of creatures such as chordates (animals with a dorsal nerve, including humans) and arthropods (the ancestors of insects and crustaceans).

The study's authors drew a link between Gondwana's supermountains and this rapid evolution of complex life. While the supermountains don't explain the Cambrian Explosion directly, they "provide the opportunity for it to happen," Campbell said. The paper suggests that these nutrients fed organisms that formed the base of the animal food web.

However, Daczko and Rugen are skeptical. "The methods used in this study cannot directly demonstrate that this particular mountain belt was directly responsible for the rise in oxygen or the Cambrian Explosion," Rugen said. "However, the evidence for a Gondwanan supermountain belt that would inevitably have had a substantial impact on Earth's biogeochemical cycles is growing."

Daczko said he would frame the link between the Gondwana's supermountains and the Cambrian explosion as "an exciting hypothesis rather than an established causal relationship."

However, the study did show that there was much more to discover under the ice in Antarctica.

"The most exciting aspect is the possibility that some of the largest mountain systems in Earth's history are effectively hidden beneath the East Antarctic ice sheet," Daczko said. "Antarctica potentially holds some of the most important remaining evidence for understanding the tectonic and environmental evolution of our planet."
