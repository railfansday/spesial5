Why 2027 may turn into a land negotiation year for homebuilders

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Scott Cox says 2027 could become a negotiation-driven year in land, with re-trades and real price resets. He argues builders should focus on counterparties’ incentive structures to understand likely outcomes.  AI Summary

In the early ’90s, I worked in Wells Fargo Bank’s real estate workout group in Southern California. The aerospace collapse was crushing the local economy; Los Angeles County alone lost 400,000 jobs. Roughly 25% of WFB’s assets were in commercial real estate (including homebuilding), and another 25% in home mortgages, mostly in California. That was half the bank. Writing off loans and walking away to clean up the books wasn’t an option; we lacked the capital. So we fought for every penny.

We asked for re-margins early on, short-term extensions and collected from borrowers before they ran out of money. We pursued personal guarantees and were willing to fight for them. I realize it is not what an audience of builders wants to hear, but everyone fights to survive.

Bank of America was in a different position. It had less real estate concentration and wanted to expand by buying distressed banks, but it couldn’t until regulators were convinced it had moved past its own problems. So BofA cut deals or foreclosed, cleaning house. Its real estate recovery rate was far lower than ours. That doesn’t mean BofA was wrong. It simply had different incentives.

Everyone knows Charlie Munger’s line, “Show me the incentive and I’ll show you the outcome.”

We tend to apply it to individuals. But the coming year will be driven largely by company incentives. If you’re a builder, understanding the incentives of the companies across the table may be the most valuable market intelligence you can gather.

Why the coming year? As I’ve said before, I’m not a big fan of short-term market projections. Over longer time spans, demographics, the fundamental balance of supply and demand, and affordability play out; they’re directionally predictable, if not precise. The short term has so many unforeseeable temporary factors that it can, paradoxically, be harder to call. Strategy is for the long term; in the short run, it’s mostly tactics, adapting to what’s in front of you. But caution about predicting short-term conditions is different from dismissing contingency planning.

Housing market conditions have clearly deteriorated. Whatever the cause (war, politics, rising rates or all of the above), I suspect we never had the correction we should have after rates spiked in ’22. A frozen-in-place resale market and the effectiveness of rate buydowns made the following years difficult, but less so than many of us expected.

One of the most intriguing aspects is that I can’t recall a more difficult period in homebuilding (to be clear, I’ve been through far worse, twice) that had so little impact on the land market. The reason was the belief that land was in such short supply that builders couldn’t afford to give up what they had and would need to buy more. Builders weren’t staying in current deals because they necessarily made sense, nor were they buying new ones because they liked the prices.

The attitude was: what’s the alternative?

That belief rested on two assumptions. First, that today’s weakness is a short-term problem: the rate shock would wear off, and buying would pick up. Second, that long-term fundamentals hadn’t changed, so demand would rebound and buoy prices. Either could prove true, but I’m skeptical. As I’ve written over the past year, future demand looks weak relative to the past, not a temporary dip but the start of a regime change. Once that becomes conventional wisdom, why would builders feel the need to control six to seven years of lots? Remember when three to four was considered plenty? Why won’t that be the case again?

I think 2027 is the year land prices will correct, not just terms but real prices. It’s current economics meeting a dawning recognition that the future will need fewer lots. Beyond a few year-end closings, not much will happen in the rest of 2026, and some will wait to see whether a magic spring rebound arrives. But over the last few months, we’ve seen a sharp downgrade in builder expectations for next year. Few expect things to improve soon.

The public builders reinforce this. Many are eliminating forecasts or revising unit counts downward. Much like the difference between making a dollar and losing a dollar, which is much bigger than two dollars, once a builder admits it won’t be growing, it becomes much easier to walk from some positions. Once that threshold is crossed, Wall Street cares far less whether a builder shrinks 2% or 6%.

Reasonable people can differ on this; there are multiple paths forward. But I think the odds are high enough to warrant contingency planning. If I’m right, what happens?

To start, many land bankers (not the old vets, but the newcomers) will learn they weren’t offering a credit product. Like it or not, they’re in real estate and have taken real estate risk. Too many fundamentals in that business were stretched on the belief that big builders wouldn’t walk en masse.

Expect meaningful re-trades, not just extensions. Meanwhile, land sellers will hear builders say they have options and don’t need to rush: “We’ll pay less and take them down on a rolling schedule with a 3% annual escalator. We can’t pay a land banker 10% to hold them; the margin’s too thin.”

That’s where incentives come in. How each negotiation goes will depend heavily on the incentives of the companies involved, and each company has multiple layers. Using land bankers as an example, here are a few questions worth addressing.

How are their asset management fees calculated and collected? As a percentage of revenue, assets (write-downs matter), or cost? Are they amortized over the deal’s life, or literally eat-what-you-kill, paid at closing? Last cycle, it wasn’t uncommon for equity funds to earn asset management fees based on revenue, with half taken up front and the rest amortized over the deal’s projected life.

When deals stretched out, and total revenue fell, the fee shrank and had to last longer. Once it was used up, it was used up, and a manager could be left without enough fee income to run the business but still obligated to manage the assets. That argues for resolving deals. But many of those structures also triggered a true-up at close-out, which argues for delaying resolution. Which pressure drives a given manager? It depends, and that’s exactly why you need to know.

Is their profit from fees or from promotes? A manager who relies on asset management fees will protect them. A manager focused on promotes, with good profits already built up in a tranche, may prefer to resolve an asset quickly, even at a loss, rather than let the overall IRR erode further.

Millrose Properties is an interesting case. Its external manager, Kennedy Lewis (KL), is paid a fee based on the vehicle’s tangible assets rather than on performance, and Millrose is permanent capital with no fund life to force a resolution. That structure rewards keeping the asset base intact; a sale shrinks it, and so does a write-down. I can’t know how KL will respond, and there are counterweights, since Millrose is a public REIT with a dividend and a stock price to consider.

But if I were betting, I’d expect the fee base to weigh heavily. If conditions worsen, few builders will be seeking new land banking, so raising more capital matters less until a recovery brings new deals. One way to keep assets on the books would be to lower option fees and ease takedown terms to keep builders in contract, even at weak returns. A more classic fund structure, by contrast, might push a manager toward resolution rather than managing assets for little or no fee.

Similar dynamics will play out among land sellers. Traditionally, well-capitalized sellers are reluctant to cut prices at first. Doing so immediately crushes expected returns, and the weakness might prove temporary. But at some point, it becomes clear it isn’t temporary, and the concern shifts from return on capital to return of capital.

For fund-backed sellers, the next fundraising effort makes the difference between “we’ve never lost money on a deal” and “we’ve never lost more than 5% on a deal” stark. They don’t read the same in a pitch book. So structures that aren’t ideal from a return perspective get more attention: a lower lot price in exchange for a meaningful true-up or profit participation, with little to no deposit and very soft takedowns. These structures keep open the possibility of at least recovering capital.

How do you learn all this? Ask. Some won’t tell you, but more will than you’d expect. Your counterpart has their own pressures and concerns, and a gentle question can yield a lot of information. Ask before you’re in a workout. If you’re buying, ask what their key motivations are and listen carefully to their answers. In effect, you’re trying to determine whether you’re dealing with a Wells Fargo or a Bank of America.

So, what’s the point of these speculations? Just a reminder that we stand a decent chance of 2027 being the year of negotiations. Understanding the incentive structures of those on the other side of the table may be your most important piece of market intelligence.
