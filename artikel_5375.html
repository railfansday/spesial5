10 of the largest stock market debuts, from Tokyo to Tadawul

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Free daily briefing on global business news.

A look at 10 of the biggest IPOs in history, from NTT DoCoMo in 1998 to Saudi Aramco in 2019, spanning banks, tech, telecom, insurance, autos and energy

The size of an initial public offering sounds like a simple number, but it rarely is. Two lists of the biggest IPOs in history can put the same deals in a different order, and neither list has to be wrong. The gap usually comes down to what gets counted.

The first variable is the greenshoe, also called the overallotment option. It lets underwriters sell more shares than a company first planned to offer when demand is strong. Some tallies count only the base deal. Others count the full amount raised once the greenshoe is exercised. On a large offering, that choice can move a deal up or down a list.

The second variable is the dual listing. Some companies sell shares on two exchanges at about the same time, often one in mainland China and one in Hong Kong. Combining both portions produces a larger figure than counting either market alone. Currency conversion adds another layer. Many of these deals were priced in riyals, yen, yuan or Hong Kong dollars rather than U.S. dollars. Dollar totals depend on the exchange rate used.

For those reasons, this piece does not offer a definitive ranking. It covers 10 of the largest stock market debuts on record, presented in chronological order rather than by size. The selection runs from a Japanese mobile carrier in 1998 to a Saudi oil producer in 2019.

The deals span six trading venues: the Tokyo Stock Exchange, Shanghai, Hong Kong, the New York Stock Exchange, Nasdaq $NDAQ and Saudi Arabia's Tadawul. They also cross industries. Chinese lenders sit alongside a payment network, a social network, an e-commerce group, an Asia-focused insurer, a carmaker and two Japanese telecom operators.

The reasons behind the offerings varied as much as the companies did. Some were partial sales by state owners or parent companies. Others followed a corporate rescue or a breakup. A few gave public investors their first chance to own shares in a large internet business.

Each slide covers when and where the company went public, how the deal was priced or sized, and what set the listing apart at the time it happened.

NTT DoCoMo, the mobile phone unit of Nippon Telegraph and Telephone, went public in Japan in 1998. The offering was one of the largest IPOs of its era.

The deal turned a wireless division into a listed company of its own. Before the offering, outside investors could gain exposure to the mobile business only through its parent. After the listing, they could buy shares in the mobile operator directly.

DoCoMo remained a unit of Nippon Telegraph and Telephone after the listing. The parent kept its ties to the mobile business even as public shareholders came on board.

This kind of transaction is known as a carve-out listing. A large parent sells shares in one of its businesses while keeping a connection to that business. The parent can raise money this way. The market, in turn, puts a separate price on the unit.

The 1998 date makes DoCoMo's offering an early example of a very large telecom listing. It came years before the Chinese bank, U.S. technology and Saudi energy offerings of the 2000s and 2010s.

Industrial and Commercial Bank of China (ICBC) raised roughly $21.9 billion in October 2006 through a dual listing in Shanghai and Hong Kong. It was among the first very large Chinese bank listings.

ICBC is a state-owned commercial bank headquartered in Beijing. It is one of the large state-owned lenders at the center of China's banking system.

The bank divided its offering between two exchanges. One portion of the shares listed on the Shanghai Stock Exchange in mainland China. The other portion listed on the Hong Kong stock exchange.

Shares of mainland Chinese companies that trade in Shanghai are known as A shares. They are priced in yuan. Shares of mainland companies that trade in Hong Kong are known as H shares. They are priced in Hong Kong dollars. ICBC's offering used both share classes.

The two-market structure gave the bank access to two separate pools of investors. Domestic buyers could take part through Shanghai. Foreign investors faced limits on buying mainland-listed shares at the time. They could take part through Hong Kong instead.

The listing made ICBC a publicly traded company. The Chinese state remained its controlling shareholder after the offering.

Visa $V went public on the New York Stock Exchange in March 2008, selling shares at $44 each. The offering raised about $17.9 billion, making it the largest U.S. IPO at the time of its listing.

Visa runs a payment network. Its system carries transactions between the banks that issue Visa-branded cards, the merchants that accept those cards and the cardholders who use them.

Visa does not lend money to cardholders. The banks that issue the cards handle the lending. Investors in the offering were buying into the network that moves card payments, not a lender holding consumer debt on its own books.

Before the IPO, Visa was owned by the financial institutions that belonged to its network. Those banks were both its owners and its customers. The offering changed that arrangement. Outside investors could now hold a stake in the company alongside the banks that had built it.

The $44 figure was the offer price. Investors who received shares in the offering paid that amount. The price was set before the stock began trading. After the opening, the share price moved with buying and selling on the exchange.

The amount an IPO raises reflects the cash the offering brings in. It is not a measure of what the whole company is worth. Visa's total described the money raised from the shares it sold, not a valuation of the business.

The record Visa set applied to U.S. exchanges. No earlier IPO on a U.S. exchange had raised as much when Visa listed. The mark belonged to that moment rather than standing as a permanent high for U.S. offerings.

The timing also set the deal apart. Visa listed in the same month that Bear Stearns agreed to a sale to JPMorgan $JPM Chase in a rescue backed by the Federal Reserve. A record-size offering went ahead while U.S. financial markets were under heavy strain.

Agricultural Bank of China, a state-owned lender, went public in July 2010 in an offering that raised roughly $22 billion. It sold shares on two markets, Shanghai and Hong Kong.

The listing gave public investors on both exchanges a way to own shares in a Chinese state-owned bank.

Published totals for the deal differ from one report to another. The roughly $22 billion figure is an approximation, not an exact sum.

The offering arrived in a busy year for very large listings. AIA Group went public in Hong Kong in October 2010. General Motors $GM returned to public markets in November 2010.

AIA Group, an insurer focused on Asia, went public on the Hong Kong stock exchange in October 2010 and raised about $20.5 billion. The listing followed AIA's separation from its former parent, American International Group (AIG).

That separation set the deal apart from a typical IPO. In a standard first-time offering, a company that built itself up independently sells shares to outside investors. AIA had a different history. It was an established insurance business that operated inside a larger U.S. group before it became a standalone company.

A listing of this kind is often called a carve-out. A parent takes a business it already owns and sets it up as a separate company. Investors are then offered shares in that business directly. In a carve-out, the seller of the shares can be the parent group rather than the business being listed.

For AIA, the result was an independent insurer with its own shares trading in Hong Kong. Before the split, an investor who wanted a stake in its operations had to buy into AIG as a whole. After the listing, AIA could be owned on its own.

That change gave investors what markets often call a pure play. AIA's share price would track the results of one Asian insurance business, not the wider mix of operations held inside AIG.

The choice of exchange matched the company's footprint. AIA's business is concentrated in Asia. A Hong Kong listing put its shares on an exchange inside the region where it operates.

The amount raised is the reason the deal appears among the largest IPOs on record. At about $20.5 billion, the AIA offering reached a size more often associated with state-owned banks, large technology companies and national energy producers. Among the 10 offerings in this selection, it is the only one from the insurance industry.

General Motors $GM returned to public markets in November 2010, pricing its common shares at $33 each. The offering ranked among the largest U.S. stock sales of its time.

The listing followed the automaker's 2009 bankruptcy restructuring and a rescue by the U.S. government. That sequence set it apart from most deals on lists of record IPOs.

Many of the largest offerings involve a company selling shares to outside investors for the first time. A state-owned bank, a subsidiary separated from its parent or a privately held tech business would fit that pattern. General Motors had been a public company before its bankruptcy. Its 2010 sale was a return to the market rather than a first appearance.

The offering came after the bankruptcy process and the government rescue. It put the carmaker's common shares back in front of public investors.

Accounts of the total amount raised differ. The gap depends on whether preferred shares sold in the deal are counted alongside the common stock. The $33 price for common shares is the figure that stays consistent across those accounts.

The deal shows that a place on lists of the largest stock market debuts does not always mean a company was new to investors. A carmaker coming out of bankruptcy appears in the same company as banks and tech businesses that were going public for the first time.

Facebook $META went public on Nasdaq $NDAQ in May 2012, pricing its shares at $38 each. The offering raised about $16 billion and valued the social network at roughly $104 billion at pricing.

Those two figures measure different things. The $16 billion is the cash raised by selling shares in the offering. The $104 billion is the value that the $38 price placed on the whole company.

Seen together, they show that the IPO was a partial sale. Public investors bought a portion of the business, not the entire company. Existing shareholders kept the rest.

The $104 billion figure was also a snapshot taken before trading began. It reflected the offer price, not a price set by buyers and sellers on the open market. Once the shares started trading, the company's market value moved with the stock price.

Facebook also stood apart from many large IPO candidates. It was a consumer service known well beyond the investment world. The listing gave public investors a way to buy shares in the social network on an exchange.

The company chose Nasdaq over the New York Stock Exchange. Nasdaq has long been the listing venue for many large U.S. technology companies. That choice tied the debut to Nasdaq's own trading systems.

The first trading session did not run smoothly. Technical problems at Nasdaq disrupted the debut and affected trading in the new shares. The trouble drew attention to the exchange's systems as well as to the stock.

By the close, Facebook shares had finished close to the $38 offer price. The stock ended its first session with little change from the level set in the offering. Investors who bought at the offer price ended the day with little gain or loss on paper.

The company that sold those shares now operates as Meta Platforms. The May 2012 offering marks the point at which the social network first became a publicly traded company.

Alibaba Group's initial public offering on the New York Stock Exchange in September 2014 raised about $25 billion once the overallotment option was exercised. It was the largest U.S. IPO, and the company behind it was not American.

Alibaba is a Chinese e-commerce group. It priced its shares at $68 each for its NYSE debut.

The roughly $25 billion total includes the extra shares sold through the greenshoe. A count limited to the base deal would put a smaller figure on the same offering.

The deal also stood out for where it took place. Alibaba chose a U.S. exchange for its listing rather than a market in China.

That choice meant a foreign company carried out the largest IPO on a U.S. exchange. The record went to a Chinese e-commerce business listing in New York, not to an American company.

SoftBank Corp, the mobile carrier unit of SoftBank Group, went public on the Tokyo Stock Exchange in December 2018. The offering raised roughly $23.5 billion, which made it one of the largest IPOs ever.

The shares sold belonged to the carrier, not to its parent. SoftBank Group is a Tokyo-based conglomerate founded by Masayoshi Son. Son started the group in 1981. It holds stakes in technology businesses around the world. The mobile network is one business inside that group, and it was the part offered to public investors.

The carrier traces its roots to the group's 2006 purchase of Vodafone's Japanese mobile business. SoftBank rebranded that operation under its own name. By the time of the listing, it was one of Japan's three major mobile carriers, alongside NTT DoCoMo and KDDI. It also sells plans under the Y!mobile brand.

A parent company can sell shares in a subsidiary while its own stock stays listed. The deal raises cash from a business the parent already owns without selling the whole group. SoftBank Group kept majority control of the carrier after the offering. Buyers of the new shares gained direct exposure to the carrier's operations. They did not gain a claim on the parent's wider portfolio of investments.

The Tokyo Stock Exchange is Japan's largest equity market. Japan had seen a large mobile listing before. NTT DoCoMo, the mobile arm of Nippon Telegraph and Telephone, went public in Japan in 1998. Both companies were mobile units of larger Japanese telecom groups.

SoftBank Corp's debut followed Alibaba's 2014 listing in New York. It preceded Saudi Aramco's December 2019 share sale on Saudi Arabia's Tadawul exchange.

Saudi Aramco raised about $25.6 billion when it went public on Saudi Arabia's Tadawul exchange in December 2019. The oil company was valued at about $1.7 trillion at listing.

The shares were priced at 32 riyals each. Aramco sold them on its home exchange. It did not add a listing in New York or Hong Kong.

That choice set the deal apart from several other very large offerings. Some companies went public on a foreign market. Others sold shares on two markets at about the same time. Aramco's offering took place on a single domestic exchange and was priced in the country's own currency.

The $25.6 billion figure counts the greenshoe. Aramco's total reached that level only after the overallotment option was exercised. Any comparison with another offering depends on whether that deal's total was measured the same way.

The valuation and the amount raised measure different things. The roughly $1.7 trillion figure put a price on the whole of Aramco. The $25.6 billion figure is the money raised from the shares on offer. Investors in the IPO bought only a small slice of the company.

The two numbers sit on different scales. One is counted in trillions of dollars, the other in billions. That gap is what a small sale of a very large company looks like on paper.

It also explains how one listing could sit among the largest stock market debuts on record while most of Aramco's shares stayed out of public trading. The offering set a market value for the entire oil producer, even though the cash came from selling only part of it.
