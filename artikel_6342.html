Wildfire smoke is making two kinds of air pollution spike together

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

As wildfire smoke travels across the U.S., fine particle pollution and ground-level ozone are more often spiking on the same day.

Two air pollutants are reaching unusually high levels on the same days more often across the contiguous United States. A new study links that rise to wildfire smoke.

The research comes from a team at the Penn State College of Earth and Mineral Sciences. Researchers in the Department of Geography looked at public data from 48 states.

They studied the co-occurrence of fine particulate matter (PM2.5) and ground-level ozone.

PM2.5 includes microscopic soot, ash, and other inhalable particles.

Ground-level ozone forms when nitrogen oxides and volatile organic compounds react in sunlight.

Intensified by wildfires, vehicle emissions, and other sources, the pollutants are often evaluated separately.

Exposure to both pollutants at once may increase risks to the heart and lungs.

Starting in 2011, wildfire smoke was more often present on days when both pollutants spiked across large parts of the country.

During many of these widespread pollution episodes, both PM2.5 and ground-level ozone exceeded U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) thresholds.

The study did not evaluate individual health impacts. But researchers assessed high-level regional and national pollutant patterns known to affect health.

Increases in the combined pollutant levels appeared alongside steady growth in summertime wildfire smoke. They were most dramatic in the eastern half of the country.

The analysis incorporated observations from 2004 to 2023.

The researchers merged EPA monitoring data, satellite smoke mapping from the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, and a blend of weather information.

“We’ve been experiencing many summertime instances when wildfire smoke from the western U.S. or Canada has traveled into large parts of the eastern U.S., especially the Northeast,” said lead author Manzhu Yu from Penn State.

Researchers found growing odds of combined pollution hazards across the continental U.S.

In the West, the likelihood of both pollutants spiking on the same day during summer wildfire smoke events roughly doubled over the study period.

Those risks may move progressively eastward.

The paper’s overall findings may help public health researchers and clinicians study, evaluate, and prevent emerging health risks through a regional lens.

Yu said she is especially interested in the eastern U.S. Its smoke comes from several sources, not only the large wildfires in the West.

Regional smoke sources include prescribed burns, such as those to clear acreage for agriculture or general land management. Local wildfires are another source.

The distinct smoke types occasionally overlap, deepening health concerns.

Different smoke carries distinct components. These range from inorganic and organic carbon to heavy metals and influence PM2.5 co-occurrence with ozone.

“We are particularly curious about the links among smoke composition and cardiorespiratory ailments, which involve the heart, blood vessels, and lungs,” Yu said.

A larger goal at Penn State is to understand future wildfire risks and impacts in the eastern U.S.

“I can’t say we’re excited about the work – it’s not good news,” said co-author Erica Smithwick from Penn State.

“People have underestimated the effect of wildfire smoke in this region. But we are pleased to see progress in mapping out and estimating risk factors because of the impact on human health.”

The analysis should help local and regional decisionmakers plan for hazardous smoke conditions. That includes risk-reducing steps at public schools, in older buildings, and for people with medical vulnerabilities.

“In ongoing research elsewhere, we’re learning more about longer-term exposures to air pollution,”  Smithwick said.

“It may affect everything from acute stressors such as sore throats and eye irritation to longer-term effects like mental health and dementia.”

“We’re hoping that evidence-based information about the increase in compound pollutant exposures will help prioritize decision-making around mitigation and resilience to those stressors.”

Air pollution from wildfires historically centered in the western U.S. But longer-range distribution of western and Canadian smoke from bigger fires is posing sharply higher co-occurrence risks.

That now includes the Midwest and Ohio Valley, in addition to the Northeast.

During the study period, researchers found 2012, 2018, 2021, and 2023 were peak years for co-occurrence of the pollutants nationally.

They also determined that widespread co-occurrence days became more frequent, persistent, and intense.

A widespread day is when both pollutants exceed local 90th percentiles over more than 10% of the country.

For each pollutant, the local 90th percentile is the concentration that about 90% of daily values in that area fall below.

Light to moderate smoke raises co-occurrence risks. It introduces particulate organic compounds and nitrogen oxides that promote ozone production.

At the same time, more dense smoke can limit ozone production by blocking sunlight.

To predict the likelihood of combined pollution extremes, the researchers created a machine-learning model. They call it the Spatiotemporal Bayesian Neural Field.

Their model combines the effects of smoke, ground-level pollution, and weather patterns to establish risk.

Parts of the West have fire seasons more than a month longer than they were 35 years ago.

Estimated carbon emissions from forest fires grew 60% globally from 2001 to 2023, according to NASA.

Researchers attributed the changing fire conditions largely to global warming and a drier climate.

“We need to think about community awareness and community perception around smoke and have people be aware of the ecosystems they’re living in,” Smithwick said.

“There should be a general awareness of when not to burn a debris pile in your backyard, or when to be more careful with a campfire because of heightened risks.”

“We want to work with municipalities and communities to think about the educational backbone of this work.”

The study is published in the journal Environmental Science & Technology.
