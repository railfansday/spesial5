A man searching for a lost hammer in England accidentally discovered the richest Roman treasure ever found in Britain

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

In 1992, a search for a lost hammer in England uncovered the Hoxne Hoard, a Roman treasure containing 14,865 coins, gold jewelry and silverware.

On November 16, 1992, Eric Lawes was sweeping a field near Hoxne, in Suffolk, England, for a hammer that tenant farmer Peter Whatling had lost. His detector picked up ancient coins and silver spoons instead.

The find became known as the Hoxne Hoard. Together with coins recovered the following year, it holds 14,865 late Roman coins, along with gold jewelry and silver tableware.

The hoard is “the richest find of treasure from Roman Britain,” the British Museum says. Whatling’s hammer is now in its collection too.

Lawes, who lived in Hoxne, had received a metal detector as a retirement present, according to the Linda Hall Library.

Lawes and Whatling reported the find right away to Suffolk County Council, which owned the land.

Some objects had already been lifted, but Lawes stopped digging and left the rest for archaeologists.

The next day, a Suffolk Archaeological Unit team led by Judith Plouviez carried out a controlled emergency excavation of the remaining deposit.

The finds went to London for study the day after. The deposit had been lifted in small blocks of soil so that its layout could be recorded in the laboratory.

Catherine Johns and Roger Bland of the British Museum described these first steps in a 1994 report on the treasure.

The county’s Historic Environment Record lists 565 gold coins called solidi, 14,191 silver coins, most of them siliquae, and 24 bronze coins. Many of the silver coins had been clipped.

Another four gold solidi and 81 silver siliquae turned up in the plowed field in September 1993. That brought the totals to 569 gold coins and 14,272 silver coins, or 14,865 coins in all.

The gold jewelry included a body chain, six necklaces, three finger rings and 19 bracelets. One bracelet carries the words VTERE FELIX DOMINA IULIANE, a Latin good wish to a lady named Juliane.

The silver tableware included 78 spoons, 20 ladles, nine toilet implements, small bowls, a dish, two decorated vases, strainers, four pepper pots and a handle shaped like a tigress. A set of 10 spoons carries the name AVRVRSICINI, for Aurelius Ursicinus.

The four small bowls and the dish had been stacked together, possibly with hay and textile packing.

Fragments of ivory, bone inlay plates and two silver padlocks were also recovered.

The gold body chain measures about 33 inches (840 millimeters) when extended and weighs about 8.8 ounces (249.5 grams). Its complex loop-in-loop links form a flat strap rather than a round chain.

Its front setting holds an amethyst surrounded by four garnets, and four empty cells may once have held pearls. The clasp holds a gold coin of the emperor Gratian, struck between 367 and 375.

The body-chain type “had a long history” and appears in Hellenistic and Roman art, but “actual examples are extremely rare,” curators in London note. They estimate it fitted a slender woman or adolescent girl with a bust of about 30 to 32 inches (76 to 81 centimeters).

The so-called Empress pepper pot is a hollow silver half-figure of a woman, dated to between 300 and 400, about 4 inches (103 millimeters) tall, with gilded hair, jewelry and clothing.

A disc in its base could be turned to three positions: closed, open for filling and open for sprinkling pepper. The base rests on four small baluster feet.

The figure, which holds a gilded scroll in her left hand, probably represents a generic imperial image rather than a specific empress. Pepper reached the Roman world from India in the first century AD, and containers like this one are very rare finds.

The latest coins in the hoard are two of Constantine III, dated to 407 or 408.

The treasure was buried “for safety at a time when Britain was passing out of Roman control,” museum curators write. Its main container was presumably wooden, with iron nails and fittings along one edge.

The site also suffered extensive illicit digging, including trenching, in December 1992.

Geophysical surveys in 1993 revealed a single linear feature at the site.

In 1994, the Suffolk Archaeological Unit excavated a square about 98 feet (30 meters) across around the findspot. The dig found prehistoric and post-medieval features.

A coroner’s inquest in Lowestoft declared the hoard Treasure Trove on September 3, 1993. Under that ruling, the entire find went to the national collection.

Lawes received a finder’s reward of £1.75 million, which he shared with the tenant farmer.

The museum acquired the hoard in 1994 with major grants and many private donations.

By calling in the professionals, Lawes helped improve how archaeologists viewed metal detector users, the library notes.

The study was published in the journal Britannia.
