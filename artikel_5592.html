How long Thanksgiving leftovers last in your fridge, from turkey to gravy

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Learn how long Thanksgiving leftovers last in the fridge, from turkey and stuffing to gravy and pumpkin pie, plus USDA rules for cooling, reheating and freezing

The Thanksgiving refrigerator runs on a short calendar. Cooked turkey, stuffing, mashed potatoes and pumpkin pie reach the end of their fridge window on the Sunday or Monday after the holiday. Gravy runs out sooner, on Friday or Saturday. That leaves most of the meal about a long weekend in the fridge.

Those deadlines come from the U.S. Department of Agriculture. Its Food Safety and Inspection Service publishes storage times for cooked food, and FoodSafety.gov carries the same guidance for consumers. Under the USDA's general rule, cooked leftovers keep for three to four days in the fridge. Gravy is the exception among the common Thanksgiving dishes, with one to two days. Pumpkin pie and other custard-style pies belong in the fridge after baking, not on the counter.

Knowing how long Thanksgiving leftovers last in the fridge is only part of the job. Two cooling rules come first. The USDA advises putting perishable food into the fridge within two hours of cooking. That limit drops to one hour if the room is warmer than 90°F. Shallow containers help cooked turkey cool quickly. The fridge itself should stay at 40°F or below. A fridge thermometer is the only way to confirm it.

The USDA also treats safety and quality as separate questions. Food held at 0°F in a freezer stays safe indefinitely. Its quality is what declines over time. That makes the freezer the way to keep leftovers past their fridge limit. Before eating any leftover, the USDA calls for reheating it to an internal temperature of 165°F. Gravy and sauces should be brought to a rolling boil.

The 10 slides that follow cover a selection of common leftovers rather than every dish on the table. They include cooked turkey, stuffing, gravy, mashed potatoes and other sides, pumpkin pie and cranberry sauce. They also cover the rules for cooling, fridge temperature, reheating and freezing. Each slide focuses on one food or one storage step.

Thanksgiving leftovers should go into the refrigerator within two hours of cooking, according to the USDA. When the room is warmer than 90°F, the window drops to one hour.

The rule covers perishable food. On a typical Thanksgiving table, that includes the turkey, stuffing, gravy, mashed potatoes, casseroles and pumpkin pie.

The clock starts when the cooking ends, not when the meal does. A dish left on the counter while the rest of dinner finishes is already using up its two hours. So is a turkey resting before it is carved.

That timing matters on a holiday built around a long meal. Guests arrive late, go back for seconds and linger at the table before dessert. Every one of those minutes counts toward the same deadline.

Here is how the math works in practice. A turkey that comes out of the oven at 2 p.m. needs its leftovers in the fridge by 4 p.m. If dinner is served at 3 p.m., the table has one hour before the food should be put away.

The one-hour limit for rooms above 90°F cuts the standard window in half. It covers outdoor meals on days hotter than 90°F. It also covers a kitchen that climbs above that mark. A room thermometer shows which limit is in effect.

The deadline is easier to meet when the cleanup is split up. Leftovers from the main course can be packed away as soon as people finish eating, rather than after the last slice of pie. Each dish can go into the fridge on its own schedule instead of waiting for the whole table to clear.

Side dishes cooked early in the day need the closest watch. A casserole that comes out of the oven before the turkey starts its two-hour clock earlier. It reaches the deadline first, even if both dishes are served together.

The USDA says a refrigerator should hold at 40°F or below to keep leftovers safe. Every fridge storage window for Thanksgiving food assumes that temperature. A fridge thermometer is the only way to confirm the fridge is actually there.

The 40°F line matters because cold slows the growth of bacteria. It does not kill them. Bacteria already present on cooked turkey, stuffing or sweet potatoes keep multiplying in the fridge, only far more slowly than they would on the counter.

That is why the USDA's day counts for leftovers come tied to a temperature. A storage window for cooked turkey, stuffing or pie describes food held at 40°F or below. In a fridge running warmer than that, the published windows no longer describe what is happening inside the container.

The control inside most refrigerators does not settle the question. A numbered dial or a "colder" marking sets a cooling level. It does not report the temperature of the air around the food. A separate appliance thermometer placed inside the fridge shows the real reading.

Thanksgiving puts unusual pressure on a home refrigerator. Before the meal, it often holds a raw turkey, dairy, produce and drinks. After the meal, a wave of leftover containers goes in at once. The door also opens repeatedly as guests reach for drinks and desserts. Each of those conditions can push the inside temperature up.

Tightly packed shelves add to the problem. A refrigerator cools food by moving cold air around it. When containers are jammed together, that air cannot circulate freely. Some parts of the fridge can then sit warmer than others.

Smell and appearance do not reveal whether a fridge has been running too warm. The bacteria that cause foodborne illness generally do not change how food looks, smells or tastes. A container of leftovers can seem fine while still being unsafe to eat.

A thermometer reading taken before the holiday, again after the leftovers go in, and on the days that follow shows whether the fridge is holding its temperature. If the reading sits above 40°F, turning the control colder and clearing space on crowded shelves helps bring back the conditions the USDA's storage windows depend on.

Cooked turkey keeps for three to four days in the refrigerator, according to the U.S. Department of Agriculture. For a bird served on Thanksgiving Thursday, Sunday or Monday is the last day to eat it.

The same guidance covers more than the holiday bird. The USDA applies its three-to-four-day window to cooked poultry in general. Leftover roast chicken or duck from the same week runs on the same schedule.

The count begins on the day the turkey was cooked. Moving the meat into the fridge later in the evening does not restart it. A turkey roasted Thursday morning and refrigerated after the evening meal still reaches its limit on Sunday or Monday.

That window also assumes the meat went into the fridge promptly after the meal. Carved turkey left on a buffet through a long afternoon of grazing falls outside the USDA's guidance, however many days remain on the calendar.

Turkey that leaves the fridge in a new form keeps its original deadline. Slices stacked into a sandwich on Saturday are still Thursday's turkey. A lunch packed with that meat on Monday sits at the end of the window, not the start of a new one.

How the turkey goes into the fridge matters as much as when. The USDA recommends cutting the meat off the carcass before storing it. Refrigerating the whole bird is the slower route.

A roasted turkey is a large, dense mass. Its center holds heat well after the surface feels cool. Taking the meat off the bones lets each piece chill on its own instead of waiting for the core.

The choice of container follows the same logic. Breast meat and dark meat spread in a thin layer have more of their surface exposed to cold air. A deep tub packed to the brim cools far more slowly at its center.

Leftover stuffing keeps for three to four days in the fridge, according to the USDA. The agency also advises storing it separately from the turkey rather than leaving it inside the bird.

The count starts on the day the stuffing was cooked, not the day it went into a container. A batch made for Thanksgiving dinner reaches the short end of its window three days later. It reaches the long end four days later. After the fourth day, the stuffing falls outside the USDA's fridge guidance.

A batch of stuffing has one deadline, no matter when each portion is scooped out. A helping set aside the morning after the meal is no fresher than the rest of the pan. Every portion runs on the original cooking date.

The separate-storage rule matters most for stuffing cooked inside the bird. Before the leftovers go into the fridge, the stuffing should come out of the cavity. It then goes into its own container. The carved turkey meat goes into a different one.

Stuffing baked in a casserole dish is already apart from the meat. It needs only to be covered and chilled, either in its baking dish or in a separate container.

Leftover gravy lasts one to two days in the fridge, according to the USDA. That is the shortest window among the common Thanksgiving leftovers.

For a Thursday dinner, that leaves little room. Gravy made on Thanksgiving Day reaches the one-day mark on Friday and the two-day limit on Saturday. Gravy still in the fridge after Saturday should be thrown out.

That timeline sets gravy apart from the rest of the meal. The USDA gives cooked turkey, stuffing, mashed potatoes and pumpkin pie three to four days each in the fridge. Those dishes can last until Sunday or Monday. The gravy poured over them runs out first.

The gap affects the order in which leftovers get eaten. A turkey sandwich with gravy on Saturday falls inside both windows. The same sandwich on Sunday does not, even though the turkey is still within its own limit. Dishes that use gravy belong early in the weekend, and gravy-free dishes can wait until Sunday or Monday.

Those dates assume the gravy reached the fridge promptly. The USDA's two-hour limit for refrigerating perishable food applies to the gravy boat as much as to the turkey platter. Gravy left out past that limit falls outside the USDA's storage guidance.

Reheating gravy follows a stricter standard than most leftovers. The USDA calls for gravy and sauces to be brought to a rolling boil, not just warmed through. Gravy that is only hot to the touch has not met that guidance.

The freezer is the option for gravy that will not be used by Saturday. The USDA says food held at 0°F stays safe indefinitely. Gravy moved to the freezer before the two-day mark passes can be saved for a later meal. Its quality can decline the longer it stays frozen, but its safety does not. Thawed gravy still needs a rolling boil when it is reheated.

Mashed potatoes, casseroles and cooked vegetables last three to four days in the fridge. The USDA applies that window to cooked leftovers in general, and these Thanksgiving sides fall under it.

That means a single deadline covers a bowl of mashed potatoes, a dish of green bean casserole and a tray of roasted carrots or Brussels sprouts. No side needs its own separate count.

The guidance applies to the cooked dish as a whole rather than to each ingredient. A sweet potato casserole topped with marshmallows falls under the same limit as a pan of baked macaroni and cheese. A pot of steamed green beans follows it too. Butter, cream or cheese in a recipe does not change the count.

A leftover plate that combines mashed potatoes, vegetables and sliced turkey runs on a single clock. That holds when every part was cooked and chilled at the same time.

The count starts when a dish is cooked, not when it reaches the table. Sides cooked ahead of the holiday start counting sooner. A casserole baked on Tuesday reaches its fourth day on Saturday. That is two days earlier than a dish made on Thanksgiving itself.

The window is a range, and its three-day end is the more cautious deadline to plan around.

Pumpkin pie belongs in the refrigerator once it has been baked, and it keeps there for three to four days, according to the USDA. That window starts when the pie comes out of the oven, not when it reaches the table.

The filling explains the rule. Pumpkin pie is a custard pie. Its filling combines pumpkin purée, eggs and milk or cream, and the eggs set the mixture as it bakes. Those ingredients make the pie a perishable food, in the same category as leftover turkey. A firm crust and a sweet filling do not change that.

Other custard-style pies fall under the same USDA guidance. Sweet potato pie and classic egg custard pie are built on a similar egg-and-dairy base. Any pie with that kind of filling follows the same storage window.

Baking ahead shifts the deadline. A pie baked on Wednesday to free up oven space reaches the end of its fridge window on Saturday or Sunday. That is one day ahead of a turkey roasted on Thursday. The same arithmetic applies to any pie baked earlier in the week.

A pie made in advance should go into the fridge on the day it is baked, rather than sitting on the counter until the holiday meal. The USDA's two-hour limit for perishable food covers pie as much as any main course. That limit also applies to a pie left out on the dessert table after dinner.

Covering the pie loosely with plastic wrap or foil keeps the surface from drying out. It also stops the filling from picking up odors from other food in a crowded fridge. Whipped cream is a dairy product too. Slices topped with it go back into the fridge along with the rest of the pie.

Pie that will not be finished within four days can go into the freezer. It should go in before the fridge window closes, not after.

Leftover turkey, stuffing, casseroles and other cooked dishes should reach an internal temperature of 165°F when reheated, according to the USDA. Gravy and other sauces need more than a temperature reading. The USDA says to bring them to a rolling boil.

The 165°F figure refers to the inside of the food, not the surface. A plate of turkey slices can feel hot to the touch while the center of a thick piece stays cool. Steam rising from a dish is not proof either. A food thermometer is the tool that settles the question.

For an accurate reading, push the probe into the thickest part of the food. With a dense dish such as stuffing or a casserole, check the middle of the pan rather than the edges. Edges heat first and can give a misleading number.

The microwave is where uneven heating shows up most. The USDA recommends covering leftovers, stirring them and rotating the dish while they heat. A cover holds in moisture and helps the heat reach all the way through. Stirring and rotating break up cold spots. Bacteria can survive in those cooler pockets. If the microwave has no turntable, the agency advises turning the dish by hand during heating.

The USDA does not recommend slow cookers for reheating leftovers. They heat food gradually. That can leave a dish sitting too long at temperatures where bacteria multiply. The agency points to the stovetop, oven or microwave instead.

Gravy has its own standard. A rolling boil is a vigorous boil that keeps bubbling even while the pot is stirred. Reaching that point heats the whole batch rather than just the layer touching the pan. A gentle simmer or a few warm bubbles at the edge does not meet the standard.

Frozen leftovers can go straight to reheating without thawing first, according to the USDA. The process takes longer, but the 165°F target stays the same.

The 165°F target is a reheating standard, not a storage one. Cold storage slows bacterial growth, but it does not kill bacteria. Reheating a leftover to 165°F is the step that makes it ready to eat again.

Freezing extends Thanksgiving leftovers beyond their fridge limit. Food held at a steady 0°F stays safe indefinitely, according to the USDA, though its taste and texture decline the longer it sits.

That split between safety and quality shapes how the freezer should be used. Cold at that level does not kill bacteria. It stops them from multiplying. Frozen turkey or stuffing therefore does not become riskier with time. What changes is its flavor, moisture and texture.

Timing still matters. Freezing pauses the clock but does not reset it. A dish that has already spent most of its fridge window in the refrigerator is not returned to day-one condition by a trip to the freezer. Leftovers headed for the freezer should go in well before the refrigerator limit runs out.

For a Thursday holiday, the freezer deadlines follow the fridge calendar. Turkey, stuffing, mashed potatoes, casseroles and pumpkin pie should be frozen by Sunday or Monday at the latest. Gravy has a fridge life of one to two days. It needs to be frozen or thrown out by Friday or Saturday.

Packaging decides how well leftovers hold up. Air reaching the food causes freezer burn, the dry, grayish-brown patches that form on meat and other dishes. The USDA treats freezer burn as a quality problem rather than a safety one. Airtight containers or freezer bags with the air pressed out limit the damage.

Portioning before freezing also helps. Smaller packages freeze faster. They also let a household thaw only what it plans to eat. Labeling each package with its contents and the freezing date makes it easier to use older items first.

The 0°F mark needs checking, just as a fridge temperature does. A dial setting alone does not confirm what the freezer is actually holding. An appliance thermometer placed inside gives the real reading.

Thawing is where frozen leftovers can run into trouble again. Once food warms up, bacteria that were dormant can start growing. The USDA lists three safe ways to thaw: in the refrigerator, in cold water or in the microwave. Leaving a container on the counter is not one of them. Leftovers thawed in the fridge can go back into the freezer, with some loss of quality. Food thawed in cold water or the microwave should be heated right away.

Leftover cranberry sauce belongs in the refrigerator within two hours of cooking, in a fridge set to 40°F or below. The USDA gives no confirmed fridge day count specific to cranberry sauce. Its general rules for perishable food are the guide that applies.

That makes cranberry sauce different from the other dishes on the table. Turkey, stuffing and gravy each come with a published USDA storage window. Cranberry sauce does not. Any specific number of days for it would be a guess rather than federal guidance.

The sauce is easy to overlook when the table is cleared. A bowl left on a buffet through a long afternoon of grazing can pass the two-hour limit without anyone noticing. Putting it away with the turkey and sides keeps it on the same cooling schedule as the rest of the meal.

Cranberry sauce is often served cold, straight from the fridge. When it is warmed instead, USDA reheating guidance for sauces applies. It should come to a rolling boil before it goes back on the table.

The freezer is the option the USDA guidance directly supports for sauce that won't be eaten soon. Frozen storage does not depend on a day count. Over time, only the quality of the sauce declines.
