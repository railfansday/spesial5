Bitcoin’s selloff deepens as Trump tariffs and hot inflation revive market fears

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Options traders are rushing into puts at the fastest pace in four years while realized investor losses reach levels last seen during the FTX collapse.

Bitcoin fell to $57,470 on Wednesday as hotter-than-expected US core inflation and the announcement of new tariffs intensified selling across risk assets.

The Nasdaq 100 extended its losses to 2.9%, reversing the optimism that followed Jensen Huang's reinstatement as Nvidia CEO earlier this week.

Data released by the US Bureau of Economic Analysis on Nov. 25 showed that the Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) price index, the Federal Reserve's preferred inflation gauge, rose 3.8% annually in October, its second-highest reading since April 2023.

Core PCE inflation, which excludes food and energy prices, accelerated to a three-year high of 3.3%, beating economists' 3.2% forecast.

Heather Long, chief economist at Navy Federal Credit Union, said the stronger-than-expected core reading showed that price pressures were spreading across the economy beyond the recent energy shock.

The report follows increases in both consumer and producer price inflation, with financial markets now assigning a greater than 97% probability to a quarter-point interest rate hike on Dec. 9.

That would mark the Fed's second rate increase of 2026, lifting its benchmark rate to between 4% and 4.25%. Twelve months ago, investors were anticipating three rate cuts this year.

Meanwhile, President Donald Trump's administration announced a new round of proposed tariffs targeting 14 major US trading partners following an eight-month investigation into foreign industrial policies.

The measures include additional tariffs of 20% on Chinese imports, up to 35% on Vietnamese goods, and up to 25% on imports from India, Taiwan and South Korea. The European Union faces duties of up to 20%.

The announcement opens another front of uncertainty for investors already grappling with the prospect of higher borrowing costs.

André Dragosch, Bitwise's European head of research, expects this market turbulence to persist, saying:

“Market volatility will likely stay elevated in the short term until uncertainty around inflation and the outlook for monetary policy fully resolve.”

The deteriorating macroeconomic backdrop comes as Bitcoin investors show signs of financial distress comparable to the depths of the 2022 bear market.

Blockchain analytics firm Glassnode reported that Bitcoin's correlation with the Nasdaq 100 has climbed to its highest level since September 2022. Over the past 60 trading days, Bitcoin has consistently underperformed the index on both up and down days.

That weakness has coincided with a surge in demand for downside protection across Bitcoin's options market.

According to Glassnode, the seven-day average put-to-call trading volume ratio reached 1.7 last week, its highest level since June 2022. The corresponding ratio for outstanding options contracts climbed to its highest level since May 2021.

“The last rush into puts like this was June 2022. Bitcoin was near $20,000 in the middle of the capitulation. The final low came five months later, below $17,000.”

The growing pessimism extends beyond derivatives markets. CryptoQuant CEO Ki Young Ju said Bitcoin investors are underwater at historical capitulation levels, while Glassnode reported that realized losses, relative to recent capital inflows, had reached their highest level since FTX collapsed in November 2022.

Institutional demand has also weakened, with US spot Bitcoin exchange-traded funds registering $4.9 billion in net outflows this month.

Glassnode identified Bitcoin's realized price of $53,200 as the next major threshold should the cryptocurrency close decisively below its June closing low of $58,500.

Every previous major Bitcoin bear market saw prices fall below the realized price before bottoming out. Reaching that level would require a further 7.4% decline from Wednesday's reported low, as investors confront the prospect of tighter monetary policy and a prolonged withdrawal of institutional capital.
