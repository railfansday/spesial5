A living trust is a legal arrangement where you (the grantor) transfer ownership of your assets to a trust entity that you also control (as trustee). It sounds circular, and in practice, during your lifetime, nothing changes about how you use your money and property. The magic happens when you die or become incapacitated.

During your lifetime: You are both the grantor and the trustee. You control everything. You can add or remove assets, change beneficiaries, modify terms, or dissolve the trust entirely. Your Social Security number is the trust's tax ID — there is no separate tax return. For practical purposes, nothing changes about your daily financial life.

If you become incapacitated: Your successor trustee immediately takes over management of trust assets. No court petition needed. No guardianship proceeding. No delay. This is a major advantage over a will, which provides zero protection during incapacity.

When you die: Your successor trustee distributes assets according to your trust document. No probate court. No public record. No waiting 6-18 months. Beneficiaries can typically receive assets within days or weeks.

A living trust essentially creates a private, self-executing transfer system that operates outside the court system entirely. For FI families who have spent years building wealth, this means your heirs receive the full value of your estate without probate eating into it.

The terms "revocable" and "irrevocable" describe whether you can change or dissolve the trust after creating it. This distinction has major implications for control, taxes, and asset protection.

Revocable living trust — the standard choice for most families:

You retain full control. You can amend the trust, add or remove assets, change beneficiaries, or dissolve it completely at any time. Because you maintain control, the IRS treats trust assets as yours — they are included in your taxable estate, and creditors can reach them. The primary benefit is probate avoidance and incapacity protection, not asset protection or tax reduction.

Irrevocable trust — the advanced tool for specific situations:

Once created, you generally cannot change or revoke it. You give up control of the assets placed inside. In exchange, you get benefits that a revocable trust cannot provide.

For most FI families: A revocable living trust is the right starting point. It provides the two biggest benefits — probate avoidance and incapacity protection — while letting you maintain complete control.

Creating a trust document is only half the job. A trust only controls assets that are titled in its name. An unfunded trust is a worthless piece of paper.

The re-titling process for real estate: This is the most critical step and requires recording a new deed. You transfer ownership from "John Smith" to "John Smith, Trustee of the John Smith Revocable Living Trust dated January 15, 2024." Contact your county recorder's office or have your attorney prepare the deed. Most counties charge $15-$75 for recording. Notify your mortgage company (they cannot call the loan due under the Garn-St. Germain Act for transfers to a revocable trust) and your homeowners insurance carrier.

Ongoing funding discipline: Every time you open a new bank account, buy property, or acquire significant assets, ask: "Should this be titled in my trust?" Create a simple checklist and review it annually. The most common estate planning failure is a perfectly drafted trust with assets still titled in the grantor's personal name.

A common concern is whether transferring your home into a trust triggers a due-on-sale clause in your mortgage. Under the federal Garn-St. Germain Depository Institutions Act of 1982, lenders cannot accelerate a mortgage when property is transferred to a revocable trust where the borrower remains a beneficiary and occupant. This protection applies to residential properties with fewer than five units. Your lender may ask for a copy of the trust document, but they cannot call the loan. Always notify your homeowners insurance company of the title change to ensure coverage remains valid.

A living trust is most valuable if your net worth exceeds $500K, you own property in multiple states, you want to avoid probate, or you want incapacity protection. If your estate is simple and under $500K, a will may be sufficient.

Online services (Trust & Will $399-$599, LegalZoom $399-$599) work for simple trusts. For complex situations or estates over $1M, an estate planning attorney ($1,500-$5,000) ensures proper drafting and funding.

Your trust document should name your trustee (you), successor trustee, beneficiaries, and detailed distribution instructions. Include provisions for incapacity and specific instructions for each major asset category.

Transfer real estate by recording new deeds. Retitle bank and brokerage accounts into the trust name. Complete the assignment of personal property document. This is the most critical step — an unfunded trust is useless.

Draft a pour-over will that directs any assets not in the trust to pour into it upon your death. This will also names guardians for minor children. Review and update both documents every 3-5 years.

A living trust is a legal entity you create during your lifetime that holds your assets. You serve as trustee and maintain full control. When you die or become incapacitated, a successor trustee you named manages and distributes assets to your beneficiaries — without probate or court involvement.

A revocable living trust costs $1,500-$3,000 through an estate planning attorney, or $399-$599 through online services like Trust & Will or LegalZoom. Irrevocable trusts cost $3,000-$10,000+ due to their complexity. Additional costs include deed preparation ($200-$500) for transferring real estate.

A revocable living trust does NOT reduce estate taxes — assets are still part of your taxable estate because you maintain control. Only irrevocable trusts remove assets from your taxable estate. However, with the federal estate tax exemption at $13.61 million per individual (2024), estate taxes only affect very large estates.
