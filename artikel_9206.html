Doris Salcedo Traces the Shape of Memory

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

The Colombian artist reflects on the state’s attempts to erase history, and the people’s continued refusal. Also: How should artists decide whether to participate in documenta 16?

In the face of decades of violence and erasure in Colombia, artist Doris Salcedo has made memory her medium. Senior Editor Valentina Di Liscia writes that her poignant sculptures evoke the victims of the devastating guerrilla conflicts “not by simply remembering or looking back, but by delicately articulating the contours of their absence.” Recently, the Colombian government drew ire for removing LGBTQ+, leftist, and feminist objects from the Museo Nacional in Bogotá, later reinstalled after public protests. Di Liscia spoke with Salcedo about the significance of that resistance, the generational wounds of political violence, and how Colombian artists are building paths forward, as they always have.

Meanwhile, artists around the world are grappling with the upcoming edition of the embattled documenta. Two anonymous artists who participated in the last iteration write candidly about the vicious attacks they endured from German media and politicians for opposing the genocide in Palestine. Their practical advice is for artists who are on the fence about showing their work at documenta 16, but it’s also an essential guide to navigating institutional networks that seek to quash meaningful political dissent, the very kernel of artmaking and creativity.

Today, we also report on an investigation into an Israeli airstrike in Lebanon that killed artist Ali Cherri’s parents in 2024, an overdue step toward justice, and the sentencing of Daniel Sikkema, who was found guilty of ordering the murder of his husband, gallerist Brent Sikkema.

—Lakshmi Rivera Amin, associate editor, books

On October 15, dozens of objects were removed from display at the Museo Nacional in Bogotá under the orders of the country’s far-right president, Abelardo de la Espriella. Among them were artworks and materials related to LGBTQ+ and feminist movements; the country’s 2016 peace agreement with the Revolutionary Armed Forces of Colombia (FARC); and the campaigns of left-wing leaders Carlos Pizarro and Bernardo Jaramillo Ossa, both assassinated in the 1990s, whom de la Espriella has called “terrorists.” The objects were reinstated within two weeks by a court order, but the incident roiled the nation: Hundreds took to the streets in protest and faced police repression.
