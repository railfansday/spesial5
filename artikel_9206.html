Hilma af Klint Is Rolling in Her Grave

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

The Swedish artist’s painting went under the hammer for an obscene amount of money. Plus: Lowery Stokes Sims reviews Richard Powell’s new book on pigment, Blackness, and paint.

Nothing in visual culture is neutral, least of all the color wheel itself. Scholar Richard Powell explores this deceptively simple truth in his new book, tackling the theoretical entanglement of race and pigment and how Black painters have used color over time. Trailblazing art historian Lowery Stokes Sims, formerly a curator at the Met Museum and director of the Studio Museum in Harlem, parses his richly layered essays today, linking the critical theory of Josef Albers and Darby English to the practices of artists like Jacob Lawrence and Alma Thomas. The book, Sims writes, shifted the way she’ll look at art moving forward.

Meanwhile, on the other end of the spectrum: News broke that a 1920 painting by Hilma af Klint sold for $12.7 million. Yes, the same Hilma af Klint who described herself less as an artist and more as a channel for a higher power, who followed the guidance of spirits that took physical form through her paintings. But hey, at least the painting’s owner has a new addition to their investment portfolio?

In reviews, Alina Cohen spends time with the poignant, impactful photographs by Laia Abril, who evokes the systems of patriarchy and sexual violence through the quietly haunting garments and objects in their wake. As the Cornell 7 case continues and convicted abusers sit in office, Abril’s photographs “reject both the invisibility of some crimes and the all-too-visible nature of others.” More, as always, below.

—Lakshmi Rivera Amin, associate editor, books

I was aware of the place of color theory in the practice of art and knew the names of important theorists, from Johann Wolfgang von Goethe to Johannes Itten to Josef Albers. But otherwise, I presumed artists’ palettes were the result of personal predilection, technical explorations (i.e., the opening up of brushstrokes and form in the 19th century and the introduction of a wall of color in the 20th century), or cultural orientation. So it was therefore with great interest that I approached reading Richard Powell’s latest publication, Colorstruck!: Painting, Pigment, Affect (2026). | Lowery Stokes Sims
