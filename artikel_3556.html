Matt Damon Reveals the Surprising Secret Behind 'The Odyssey's Epic Final Battle

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Hundreds of choreographed moves, veteran stunt performers, and a little improvisation helped bring the film's climactic scene to life.

When Odysseus (Matt Damon) finally returns home in Homer's Odyssey, he discovers that getting back to Ithaca was only half the battle. His palace has been overrun by suitors hoping to marry his wife, Penelope (Anne Hathaway), and claim his kingdom. Naturally, Odysseus isn't particularly thrilled about any of this. What follows is one of the epic poem's most memorable confrontations, as the Greek hero takes matters into his own hands.

Christopher Nolan's The Odyssey brings that showdown to the screen in a climactic fight that required an extraordinary amount of choreography. But according to Damon, the secret to pulling off the sequence wasn't memorizing a complex choreography. Instead, it was surrounding himself with stunt performers skilled enough to adapt to whatever he did.

Speaking with Polygon ahead of the film's home release, Damon and co-star John Leguizamo discussed the sequence, which Leguizamo watched being filmed. The experience left him wishing he had some popcorn so he could sit back and enjoy the spectacle.

For Leguizamo, watching Damon take on the suitors was one of the production's standout experiences. He even got permission to remove the special contact lenses designed to portray him as the blind swineherd Eumaeus so he could really observe the action. "There were a few times when they'd let me not wear the contacts and just hang out," Leguizamo tells Polygon. "I walked on set when he [Damon] was doing the fight sequences at the end, when he took down all the suitors. That was incredible to watch. It was like watching a Kurosawa samurai movie."

Leguizamo wasn't part of the fight, but he had a front-row seat as Damon and the stunt team brought the elaborate sequence to life.

"It was so amazing," Leguizamo continues. "I wanted to have popcorn and just watch it over and over again."

The comparison to legendary Japanese filmmaker Akira Kurosawa is particularly fitting given the director's influence on generations of action filmmakers. But Damon is quick to point out that much of the credit should go to the people he was fighting.

"They had the best stunt team," Damon says, singling out stunt coordinator George Cottle.

According to Damon, the production benefited from an unusual situation in Los Angeles. With fewer productions underway, highly experienced stunt professionals were available to work on the film, including performers who ordinarily operated at the stunt coordinator level.

"Because there's not a lot of work in Los Angeles, there were all of these people who were coordinator-level stunt people who were available to come in and play those roles," Damon says. "And a lot of the suitors are around my age. They're older. So these are stunt guys who are incredible, enormously experienced, and really good."

That experience proved essential once the cameras started rolling. Stunt fighting isn't simply about memorizing a series of punches, blocks, and movements. It's a collaborative process that requires the performers to respond to one another.

"I've always thought stunt fighting is like dancing, really," Damon says. He also acknowledges that he wasn't necessarily the most accomplished dancer in the group. "When one, me, isn't as good a dancer, it gets made up for by the other being an expert."

Rather than expecting Damon to execute every movement with the precision of a professional stunt performer, the team could adjust its timing and positioning around him.

"I could move through these sequences, because these guys were adjusting in real time to my movement and my spacing," Damon says. "And if I was off, they could recalibrate and still make it work."

That level of experience was especially important given the sheer amount of choreography involved.

"How many moves did you have? Did you count them?" Leguizamo asks.

"I have no idea," Damon replies. "It was a lot. It was hundreds of moves."

"They must have been over a hundred," Leguizamo agrees.

"These guys were just so great," Damon concludes. "That really made that sequence what it was."
