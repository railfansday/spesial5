Tim Burton Partners With Loewe for a ‘Nightmare Before Christmas’ Capsule Collection

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

“Things in life are the best as surprises,” says Tim Burton, the iconoclast filmmaker, calling from London. More than 40 years after making his directorial debut with Pee-wee’s Big Adventure (1985), and penning the poem that would eventually inspire The Nightmare Before Christmas, Burton is expanding his artistic remit once more. The director has partnered with Jack McCollough and Lazaro Hernandez, the designers of Loewe, for a new capsule collection featuring memorable vignettes and characters from the latter film. “When they came to me and were talking about it,” Burton tells Vanity Fair, “I just got very excited, because I’m a frustrated designer myself.”

The capsule, available starting November 12, includes a range of handbags with whimsical recreations of the film’s most beloved characters. Zero, Jack Skellington’s dog, floats on the side of a handbag; the Harlequin Demon is rendered with feathers and leather scales; and Skellington himself is painted as a peekaboo surprise inside a carryall.

“They took something that means a lot to me and they put their own spin on it,” he says of working with Loewe. “The way they make things, the textures and the elements involved are just beautiful,” he continues. “It’s exciting for me to see it happen.”

Jack Skellington peekaboos from the inside of a handbag.

Snakes pop out of a woven leather bucket bag.

In the more than three decades since the film was released, Jack Skellington and The Nightmare Before Christmas have become synonymous with a certain brand of Halloween-like spookiness, a soft spot for Christmas and the holidays, and a wondrous gothic style and sensibility. The film originated from a poem Burton wrote in 1982 while working as an animator at Walt Disney Productions. “It was something that came not so much from a thought, but was just more slightly from the subconscious,” Burton says in a documentary short accompanying the Loewe collection.

But just as film and animation have changed over the years, the meaning of the film and its legacy have too. The Nightmare Before Christmas has become arguably one of the most renowned stop-motion-animation films. The painstaking process of making the film required the creation of 227 puppets and the shooting of 109,440 frames to bring the story to life. “I love [stop motion] because it’s all handmade,” Burton says. “When you see the puppets and the sets, you wish everybody could experience the tactile nature of what goes into making a stop-motion film, or any film.”

In the age of artificial intelligence, the movie has become a symbol of human craft and ingenuity. “The more it keeps going that way,” Burton says of the proliferation of AI, “the more I appreciate [the human] side of it. I think it’s something that’s very important to keep in the world, the tactile, not being on a screen all the time and doing all that sort of thing. To me, [humanity] is very important to life and art and anything, really.”

A still from a documentary short featuring Burton that accompanies the collection.

Rather than be overly protective about some of his most famous intellectual property, Burton wanted to let the legacy leather-goods house and its designers follow their own instincts. “They consulted me, and we talked about this and that, but I respect them and I respect what they do,” he says. “It’s more fun for me to see things beautifully done.” Fashion, after all, he says, is a medium different from film. “I mean, I’ve seen Nightmare merchandise, and some of it’s good and some of it’s really bad,” he says with a laugh. “And even the bad ones are kind of good sometimes.” One example? A “big fat Jack Skellington.” “Well, no, he’s not a big fat Jack Skellington, but as a stuffed little toy, he’s kind of cute,” Burton adds with a laugh. “Things change and mutate, and you just [need to] keep the spirit of something, but then do something [with] your own interpretation of it.”

Many members of The Nightmare Before Christmas’s original 1993 audience are now adults atop creative industries. Burton’s work, in a way, is part of their own imagination. “The things that inspired you from the very beginning, they just stay with you,” Burton says. “Whether it’s conscious or not, it’s just part of your DNA.”

Revisiting past work, Burton says, “can be a beautiful experience, but not always.” Though he says he “had no real desire” to make a sequel to Nightmare, Burton did direct 2024’s Beetlejuice Beetlejuice, a sequel to the 1988 film Beetlejuice, starring Winona Ryder and Catherine O’Hara. Returning to the film 35 years later “made sense” for him “emotionally.” “It was like, nobody’s getting any younger and blah, blah, blah,” Burton says. “I really did that movie not to make a sequel, but it was something that I enjoyed, and we tried to do it in the style of the first movie, shot it quickly, did live effects. So it was an experience that meant a lot to me. And I think for me, it’s always important to make something that I really, really feel.”

Burton illustrating some of his characters.

There’s plenty of interest in Burton revisiting other parts of his oeuvre too. Since the release of Corpse Bride in 2005, fans have been clamoring for a live-action remake of the film. Burton laughs when asked if he’d ever honor the request, and while he doesn’t deliver a straight answer, he doesn’t exactly say no. “I’ve got thoughts swirling around and all sorts of things going on, but I’ve been through too much to go, Okay,” he says, noting that he’s had projects mutate and change over the years. “I keep it open, and I’m just finishing something, so I’m in an open spot now, and I’m really excited to look for what the next thing is.”

Away from the big screen, Burton has experienced somewhat of a resurgence with the success of Netflix’s Wednesday, and he’s enjoyed ventures outside of the film industry. This capsule with Loewe is one of those projects, and the director also collaborated with A$AP Rocky, whom he describes as “a real artist,” for the visual rollout of his album released earlier this year, Don’t Be Dumb, creating characters for its cover and a music video. “I love this stuff because film has [become] such a business. It’s gotten so big,” he says. “That’s why I enjoyed doing stuff with Rocky. It’s what I love doing, and it’s just a different form of doing it.”

Burton hasn’t been on the lookout for new opportunities, but he likes being surprised by them. “It’s always just this nice, beautiful, strange surprise when somebody asks to do something or collaborate,” he says. “I mean, that’s what film’s all about. You’re working with different artists and you’re working with different people with one [goal] in mind. So it’s not that much different. I like those kinds of surprises.”
