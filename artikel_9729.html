Stop Paying Public Relations Firms Like They're Law Firms

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Co-founder of Gale Strategies, a marketing and public relations firm serving technology companies and tech investors.

Co-founder of Gale Strategies, a marketing and public relations firm serving technology companies and tech investors.

Co-founder of Gale Strategies, a marketing and public relations firm serving technology companies and tech investors.

Public relations is both expensive and influential. PRovoke Media estimates the global PR agency business at slightly more than $50 billion, up 5 to 6% in 2025, although published estimates range from $20 billion to $105 billion depending on what is counted.

The work shapes what the market sees. Muck Rack found that 86% of journalists say PR pitches inspire at least some of their stories, and Cision found that 66% rely on PR-provided content for story ideas. When companies spend that much on something that influences how customers, investors and recruits perceive them, how they pay for it matters.

Paying for media attention is a lot like paying for something between the services of a law firm and a sales force, with a number of complications. Corporate legal services are rarely revenue generating, and media relations can be defensive in nature. But the best media relations does both, it protects the company and helps it win business.

Compared to sales, media attention does not produce revenue by itself. At its best, it makes selling or fundraising easier, and that contribution is difficult to quantify. This ambiguity helps explain why most public relations I have seen is sold as a monthly retainer built on assumed hours and activity. Yet the buyer does not care how many hours are spent, except that they are paying for them. What the buyer wants is positive, credible media attention.

But then performance bonuses tied to actual media results, or sales or fundraising also make little sense, because a company’s ability to convert media attention into revenue varies widely and depends on factors the PR firm does not control. It’s better to pay for each result the media relations firm aims to achieve, priced by type of result and based on the average time it has recently taken to produce that result. Over time, we may be able to estimate the average value those results create as well. But that math has remained largely out of reach.

Defining a result also takes judgment. Media relations professionals do not create good news. If they manufacture a positive story while real problems sit underneath, they are only creating fragility. Good media relations finds what is genuinely good, helps it be seen in a risk-adjusted way and understands how not to ignore the bad. A results-based model should reward that discipline over time, rather than sheer volume.

Too many PR programs are still scoped around what an agency promises to do rather than what the client expects the work to produce.

Develop a media list. Write pitches. Conduct outreach. Hold briefings. Monitor the news. Create thought leadership. Meet every week. Produce reports documenting how many emails were sent and hours were spent. Those activities may be necessary to produce coverage, but they are inputs. The client is paying for what comes out the other side.

That distinction has become more important in 2026 because communications activity has become easier to produce at the same time that credible attention has become harder to earn. AI can accelerate research, writing, content development and reporting. Journalists are working with tighter resources while managing more information. Audiences are dividing their attention among traditional publications, social platforms, video, creators, search and AI.

The numbers around journalism make the case. Cision’s 2026 State of the Media Report, based on more than 1,800 journalists, found that 49% identify shrinking budgets, staff reductions and increased workloads as a major obstacle. At the same time, 66% rely on PR-provided content, including pitches, press releases and media kits, for story ideas.

That dependence creates an opportunity for PR, along with a much higher standard. Cision found that 72% of journalists say fewer than one-quarter of the pitches they receive are relevant, and Muck Rack’s State of Journalism 2026 found that 88% delete pitches that miss their beat.

A list showing that an agency sent 500 pitches can therefore document enormous effort or enormous waste. The number becomes meaningful when we know how many pitches reached the right journalists, generated responses, produced interviews and ultimately resulted in useful coverage.

AI makes this measurement discipline more important. Muck Rack reports that 82% of journalists now use at least one AI tool, and 80% of PR professionals say generative AI is already part of their workflow.

Companies should expect to benefit from that productivity. The value of the agency increasingly resides in choosing the right story, understanding the right journalists, creating credible evidence, preparing the right experts and converting all of that into results.

AI is also changing what happens after coverage appears. Seventy-three percent of PR professionals told Muck Rack that Generative Engine Optimization is at least somewhat important to their communications strategy. Fifty-five percent said they are securing coverage in high-authority publications to improve their visibility in AI-generated answers.

That creates another potential return from strong earned media. A substantive article can reach the publication's direct audience, circulate through LinkedIn and search, support sales conversations, influence future reporting and provide authoritative source material that may surface through AI systems.

The Reuters Institute Digital News Report 2026, based on almost 100,000 respondents in 48 markets, found that social media and video networks have passed television and news organizations' own websites and apps as ways people access news. Among 18- to 24-year-olds, more than half say social media, video networks or AI are their primary source of news. Weekly use of AI chatbots for news rose from 7% to 10% globally.

Trust is under pressure, the Reuters Institute reports average trust in news at 37% across the markets it studied, down three percentage points, while 62% of respondents are concerned about online misinformation. The 2026 Edelman Trust Barometer, based on 33,938 respondents across 28 countries, found that seven in 10 people are unwilling or hesitant to trust someone with different values, facts, problem-solving approaches or cultural backgrounds.

Content is abundant. Corporate claims are abundant. Pitches are abundant. Credible attention, independent validation and trust are scarce. PR programs should increasingly be measured around their ability to create those scarce outcomes.

There is a meaningful difference between a PR scope that promises ongoing media outreach to business, technology and trade publications and one that commits to a defined range of results during a stated period, with an agreed number coming from priority publications.

The second approach forces useful questions before an agreement is signed. What constitutes a media result? Which publications matter? How do we value a brief quotation versus a feature? Are contributed articles included? Do podcasts count? Does syndicated coverage count once or dozens of times? What exactly is the client buying?

Those questions become particularly useful six months later when a CEO, CMO or board asks whether the program is working. A serious communications program needs a destination, a baseline, a timetable and an agreed way of determining whether the investment is producing the expected results.

The PR industry recognizes the problem. In Muck Rack’s State of PR 2026, 71% of professionals identified low journalist response rates as a top challenge and 61% cited shrinking media lists in relevant beats. Only 2% said earned media had not become harder to secure.

Difficulty should strengthen the case for measurement. When something gets harder to achieve, clarity around what constitutes success becomes more important.

Journalists continue to need good PR. The opportunity lies in providing relevant ideas, credible data, useful experts and timely access that help journalists do better work.

If an agency contacts 47 reporters and nobody writes about the company. The story may need work. The evidence may be weak. The timing may be wrong. The spokesperson may need preparation. The reporter list may need to change.

Only a very, very few media relations firms put quantities of results directly into scopes. One recent 12-month program I’ve seen specified eight top-tier media results, 15 targeted trade, industry and regional results, and four opinion pieces placed in news outlets. The business objective was also included in the scope: coverage should become evidence of the client's access, insight and influence that its team could use in sales and renewal conversations.

Another scope priced at $5,630 per month specified 12 media results, including two top-tier results, four bylined articles and 18 LinkedIn posts during a 12-month period. A separate six-month program priced at $9,263 per month specified eight general media results, one top-tier result, four bylines and 24 LinkedIn posts.

Most firms avoid building programs this way because they are afraid they cannot actually deliver the numbers. Others point out, accurately, that the number of results depends on how much help the client can provide. That is a coward’s way out. The PR firm needs to take ownership of making it easier for the client to help produce results, and it cannot avoid its responsibility there. Committing to a number, with the client’s role spelled out, is how a firm accepts that responsibility.

The numbers should change according to the company, industry, starting position, available executives, news cycle and size of the engagement. The operating principle remains consistent. The client should know what the agency believes the investment can reasonably produce, and the agency should be prepared to report against those expectations.

A scope becomes valuable when everyone can see the score.

In a November 2026 client status report, one program we’ve seen had already produced its contracted top-tier media result through Axios and five of eight expected industry and transaction-media results. Three additional top-tier opportunities involving The New York Times, Bloomberg and Reuters remained pending.

The distinction between achieved and pending matters. An interview with a reporter represents progress. Published coverage represents a result. A promising conversation with a major publication belongs in the pipeline until something appears that meets the definition agreed upon with the client.

That gives the agency and client a much more productive set of questions. How many results did we agree to produce? How many have been achieved? How good are they? What remains in development? Are we on pace? Which subjects are generating results? Where does the strategy need to change? Those management questions allow PR to be discussed like an investment.

Counting placements gives us one part of the answer. The next step is to follow the result into the market. One example from work we have documented came through a healthcare technology engagement in a regulated clinical environment. The company needed validation from an important customer during an early-stage deployment, requiring careful coordination around claims, evidence and the customer's priorities.

The resulting announcement and national attention led immediately to more than 20 engagements with healthcare leaders. The work subsequently became the basis for a broader campaign led by the client's chief revenue officer.

That sequence can be measured. A communications result occurred. Relevant people in the company's market engaged. The revenue organization saw enough value to expand the effort.

Attribution deserves discipline. Communications can contribute to a business outcome alongside sales, marketing, product quality, timing and pre-existing relationships. Tracking what happened next allows us to identify that contribution without claiming credit for things the evidence cannot support.

A 2026 legal-sector engagement gives another view. From May 1 through early August, the client's LinkedIn activity generated approximately 12,714 impressions, 869 clicks, 434 reactions, 49 comments and 30 reposts. The weighted engagement rate was approximately 10.9%. From May through July, the account added 130 followers, with the audience aligned with the client's legal, staffing, business-development, executive and referral-source priorities.

The earned-media program developed alongside that activity. A national placement in The Hill featured one of the firm's executives, while additional opportunities developed with Law360, ABA Journal, Authority Magazine and Los Angeles Lawyer.

The useful questions become increasingly specific. Did people see the work? Did they engage? Were they people the company wanted to reach? Did earned media create material worth amplifying? Did that attention lead to another useful action?

The technology now available to PR firms gives us an opportunity to build much better performance funnels. At the outreach level, we can measure targeted journalists contacted, positive responses, interviews and published results. At the result level, we can measure priority-media penetration, executive quotations, message inclusion and share of voice. After publication, we can track relevant engagement, referral traffic, repeat journalist inquiries, search visibility and identifiable business follow-through.

Consider a campaign that sends 100 carefully targeted pitches and produces 15 journalist responses, eight interviews and five qualified media results. That produces a 15% pitch-to-response rate. About 53% of positive responses become interviews, and 62.5% of interviews become results.

Those percentages provide something the team can manage. A declining response rate points toward the targeting, timing or story. A healthy interview rate followed by weak publication suggests a different set of problems, perhaps involving the strength of the evidence, the differentiation of the company or the performance of the spokesperson. Strong coverage followed by little audience response raises questions about amplification, outlet relevance and the people the company is trying to reach.

This is where AI should raise expectations. If technology allows a team to research 100 journalists in the time previously required to research 20, the value lies in what happens with the 100. Judgment, relevance, relationships, access and results move closer to the center of the economics.

Measurement can expose another problem. In one healthcare AI engagement, an annual scope included 18 LinkedIn posts. By mid-July, our team had already delivered 30, equal to 167% of the full-year commitment with months remaining in the year.

That sounds positive until excess work becomes routine and invisible. The client loses visibility into the resources required to operate the account, while the agency may direct time toward work outside the agreed priorities. Measurement protects both parties. The client can see whether the program is delivering against the investment, while the agency can identify where requirements and resources have expanded beyond the original scope.

Leading businesses and organizations have also put explicit prices against individual deliverables. As of March 2026, one pricing example we’ve seen included $3,815 for a top-tier media result, $2,765 for another media result, $4,445 for an externally placed byline and $2,526 for a press release.

The price should reflect the strategic value, difficulty, labor and risk built into the work. Different kinds of coverage can also produce very different value. A substantive story in The Wall Street Journal may have broad influence, while a highly specialized trade publication may reach precisely the executives making purchasing decisions in a specific industry.

The best measurement frameworks increasingly extend beyond the placement itself. One proposed client framework we’ve seen tracks share of voice, top-tier media penetration, executive visibility, message pull-through, interview volume, analyst engagement, website activity from earned media, partner amplification, enterprise-lead influence and demand-generation contribution. It calls for monthly coverage and narrative analysis, quarterly competitive and executive-visibility reviews, and an annual assessment of market perception.

I find it useful to think of this as a progression. Start with delivery, what appeared? Evaluate quality, was it the right outlet, audience, executive and message? Measure response, what did relevant people do next? Track use, did sales, recruiting, investor relations or leadership put the result to work? Follow the evidence toward business impact, did it contribute to a qualified conversation, opportunity, partnership or change in market position?

AMEC's Integrated Evaluation Framework follows a similar progression by asking communications teams to establish objectives, benchmarks, targets and KPIs and then evaluate outputs, audience response, outcomes and organizational impact.

Activity helps explain performance. Outreach volume, reporter response, interviews, briefing activity and pitch performance identify where a campaign is working and where it is getting stuck.

Activity data is also useful when an opportunity gets declined. In one legal engagement, potential opportunities involving HR Dive, CNN and Bloomberg were passed on because the subject, outlet or partner comfort level did not fit the strategy at the time. An agency that measured every available interview as a win would encourage its team to pursue volume at the expense of judgment.

If one executive generates substantially more journalist interest, determine why. If one subject regularly produces interviews while another receives little response, adjust the editorial strategy. If coverage appears but the core message rarely survives into the finished article, improve the evidence, briefing and spokesperson preparation.

A defined scope makes those decisions easier. The model also creates responsibility for the client. Executives need to be available. Claims need evidence. Approvals need to happen on time. The organization needs to provide the expertise, data, customers and stories required to earn substantive coverage.

Our own research has also shown us where measurement needs improvement. Reviews of dashboards across programs found that they sometimes classified deliverables differently, failed to distinguish top-tier coverage consistently or omitted certain work that happened outside scope. That points to the need for centralized tracking that makes results easier to access, compare and filter, and it has reinforced my view that a commitment to measurement needs a reporting structure capable of supporting it.

The communications market of 2026 makes activity remarkably easy to create and credible attention scarce, which is why PR should be measured by what it produces.

What we have seen in our own research gives us practical evidence for approaching that market differently. We have seen a communications result followed by more than 20 healthcare-leader engagements, and a legal-sector program measured through impressions, clicks, a 10.9% weighted engagement rate and 130 new followers. We have seen programs managed against contracted results, with published coverage separated from opportunities still in development, and one that delivered 30 social posts against an annual scope of 18, which shows why excess work deserves measurement alongside shortfalls.

That brings us back to the question we started with. A law firm is paid for its time and judgment, and clients accept that because the work is hard to define in advance and the outcome is uncertain. A dancer is paid per performance: the client knows what is being bought, sees it delivered and pays for it. Most public relations has been sold the way law firms sell, as access to expertise and effort. The better model keeps the expertise and judgment of the law firm and adds the visible, countable delivery of the performance.

The discipline is straightforward. Define the expected results before the engagement begins. Put quantities and quality standards into the scope. Establish the baseline. Count what is achieved. Separate published results from opportunities in progress. Track the quality of the coverage and the response of the intended audience. Follow what happens after publication as far as the evidence allows. Use that data to change the strategy while there is still time to improve the program.

Companies know what they pay their PR firms every month. They deserve the same clarity about what that investment is expected to produce and what it actually produced. You are paying for results.
