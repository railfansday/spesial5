Barcelona Is Suffering From The Hydrogen Transport Death Spiral

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

On November 21, Barcelona had at least 23 of its 46 hydrogen buses out of service because the plant supplying them had stopped producing fuel. Transports Metropolitans de Barcelona (TMB) reported a 2.5% to 3.8% reduction in scheduled bus service, with waits on some routes rising from seven to nine minutes. The buses were available to carry passengers. The hydrogen was not. ARA and La Vanguardia reported the disruption.

The problem runs through a single industrial fuel supply. TMB has a ten-year, €14.5 million hydrogen contract with Iberdrola. Alternative deliveries from Tarragona have fallen short, disrupted by industrial action and restrictions on hydrogen transport. The contract provides for compensation when fuel is not delivered. It cannot make up the missed service.

La Vanguardia connected the Barcelona stoppage with Cummins’ recommendation that customers stop using affected electrolysers following a safety review. Iberdrola cited a valve-system problem at the plant. Cummins’ November quarterly filing confirmed the September HyLYZER stop-use recommendation and said no formal recall had been initiated. TMB now has to assess the price of safely restarting fuel production and the reliability of the system it would be restarting.

Barcelona has not decided to retire the hydrogen buses. But the agency has a more important decision than repairing a plant: whether it should commit public money to keeping this fuel system in service for the remainder of the buses’ lives. Other European cities have faced that choice and walked away.

Aberdeen paid approximately £13.9 million for 25 hydrogen double-deckers that entered service in 2021. Hydrogen supply problems stranded the fleet in July 2024. After prolonged efforts to restore the operation, the city decided in February 2026 to dispose of the buses. A proposed sale would bring in £30,000 each for 23 buses going to First Bus and £63,000 each for two going to GeoPura. The total expected proceeds are £816,000, just 5.9% of the original vehicle expenditure. My September Aberdeen resale assessment examined the proposed terms and the conversion economics. First Bus has also considered battery-electric conversion.

Liverpool chose conversion. Ten of its 20 hydrogen buses had never carried passengers; the other ten had completed only 450 passenger-carrying journeys. In December 2025 the Liverpool City Region Combined Authority decided to convert all 20 to battery-electric operation, with the supplier covering the conversion costs. The buses had years of potential mechanical life, but the hydrogen supply arrangements had not delivered a usable transport service.

A hydrogen bus can become an uneconomic asset long before it wears out. Refuelling plants, fuel deliveries, maintenance specialists, software, fuel-cell modules and replacement parts have to remain available. As hydrogen fleets retire and new orders thin out, each remaining operator has fewer other customers sharing those support costs. Rising costs and uncertain service then encourage another operator to exit. That is the hydrogen transport death spiral. Battery-electric buses draw on a much larger manufacturing, charging and maintenance market.

The pressures already extend through the supply chain. Cummins has stopped seeking new commercial electrolyser business while customers contend with its HyLYZER stop-use recommendation. Quantron’s insolvency disrupted confidence in specialist vehicle support. Alstom acquired Cummins’ relevant rail fuel-cell operations to retain the staff and expertise needed to fulfil long-term contracts. Each case transfers more risk to organizations that have already bought hydrogen equipment.

New Flyer still sells hydrogen buses, but the North American order book is thin. Publicly identified hydrogen-bus orders numbered 288 in 2023, 96 in 2024 and no more than 45 gross in 2025, with about 15 in a stricter reconstruction. Another 38 buses appear in 2026 procurement decisions across differing approval stages. These are small volumes for a specialist vehicle line that needs engineering, spares and continuing product development.

Ballard is still developing bus fuel cells. Its November 5 Solaris announcement describes a new engine entering series production. Its GeoPura acquisition also shifts business attention toward integrated power systems and recurring energy-service revenue. My Ballard–New Flyer framework assessment found that the March agreement covering 500 modules is not an order for 500 buses. For a transit operator, the crucial test is a binding, affordable commitment to parts, diagnostics and maintenance throughout the vehicles’ remaining service lives.

TMB should compare the cost of safely restarting the hydrogen plant and running the buses with the cost of delivering the same passenger service through replacement vehicles. That includes the repair bill, future fuel contracts, station maintenance, spare parts, service interruptions, replacement buses, depot charging and any temporary vehicles required to keep routes operating. TMB already runs battery-electric buses and has local operating data to test a replacement plan.

A modest repair may justify running the existing hydrogen buses while alternatives are prepared. A major refurbishment, fresh fuel commitments and another decade of specialist support are a different investment. Aberdeen chose disposal. Liverpool chose conversion. Barcelona needs to decide what delivers dependable bus service at the lowest credible forward cost, without treating the original hydrogen spending as money it can recover.
