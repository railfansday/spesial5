Wegovy Reduces BMI in Kids With Obesity as Young as 6

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

— Nearly 40% of young children taking the GLP-1 drug dropped below obesity threshold

NATIONAL HARBOR, Md. -- Young children ages 6 to 11 years with obesity lost significant weight with the GLP-1 medication semaglutide (Wegovy), according to results from the phase III STEP Young trial.

At week 68, mean body mass index (BMI) decreased by 13.5% with semaglutide compared with an increase of 2.7% with placebo, meeting the trial's primary endpoint (estimated between-group difference -16.2 percentage points, 95% CI -19.4 to -12.9, P<0.001).

By the end of the 104-week treatment period, mean BMI change was -9.4% with semaglutide versus +8.4% with placebo (between-group difference -17.8 percentage points, 95% CI -22.2 to -13.3), reported Ania Jastreboff, MD, PhD, of the Yale School of Medicine in New Haven, Connecticut, and colleagues.

In addition, 38% of participants in the semaglutide group reduced their BMI below the obesity threshold (the 95th percentile for age and sex) by week 68, compared with 2% in the placebo group. At week 104, those rates were 33% and 2%, respectively.

"What we cannot capture with the numbers is the transformation that occurred in these visits. You would walk into the room and the child and their family just felt so different. These children were thriving," Jastreboff said at the ObesityWeek annual meeting.

The findings were simultaneously published in the New England Journal of Medicine.

Semaglutide is approved for adolescents with obesity ages 12 and older, but obesity treatments for younger children are largely restricted to lifestyle modifications, with rare off-label medication use or bariatric surgery. An older daily GLP-1 drug, liraglutide (Saxenda), is approved in the European Union for children ages 6 and older.

"Although lifestyle modifications are a central part of obesity management and support overall health, associated reductions in the BMI are modest (-0.7), with limited evidence for durability," the authors noted, adding that an estimated one in five U.S. children ages 6 to 11 years have obesity. "Thus, the need for more effective treatments is leading to investigation of pharmacotherapeutic options for children."

During a presentation at the meeting, co-author Martin Wabitsch, MD, PhD, of University Medical Center Ulm in Germany, said children with severe obesity have a quality of life "similar to that of children diagnosed with cancer," adding that a 4-year-old with severe obesity has a life expectancy of just 39 years without weight loss.

However, using potent weight-loss medications in prepubertal children carries potential risks, particularly regarding linear growth and lean muscle development. In a subgroup of 50 children assessed via dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), semaglutide led to a greater reduction in fat mass percentage and a greater increase in lean soft-tissue percentage than placebo.

Median height gain was lower with semaglutide than placebo at both week 68 (5.7 cm vs 7.8 cm) and week 104 (8.8 cm vs 12.1 cm), accompanied by slower annual height velocity. Bone age, bone mineral density, and pubertal progression (Tanner staging) were similar between groups. Because children with obesity often experience accelerated growth prior to puberty, the researchers suggested the slower height velocity may simply reflect BMI normalization.

Conducted from July 2023 to July 2026 across 48 sites in nine countries, the double-blind STEP Young trial randomized 167 children (2:1) to once-weekly subcutaneous semaglutide (n=112) or placebo (n=55) for 104 weeks, followed by a 7-week off-treatment washout period. All participants received lifestyle counseling.

At least 30% of enrollees were required to have severe obesity (BMI ≥120% of the 95th percentile) or an obesity-related complication such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, obstructive sleep apnea, or type 2 diabetes. Children with syndromic or hypothalamic obesity, or prior or planned bariatric surgery, were excluded. Maximum weekly doses were capped at 2.4 mg for children weighing more than 132 lb at baseline and 1.7 mg for those weighing 99 to 132 lb.

Participants had a mean age of 10 years; 59% were female, 73% were white, and most were at Tanner stage 1. Mean baseline BMI was 32.4 in the semaglutide group (32.5 in placebo), mean baseline weight was 158.1 lb (159.2 lb in placebo), and just over half had at least one obesity-related complication.

In an exploratory analysis, reversion from prediabetes (HbA1c ≥5.7%) to normoglycemia occurred in 93% of semaglutide participants versus 56% of placebo participants at week 68, and in 94% versus 44% at week 104.

"When I am seeing children developing type 2 diabetes at a very young age, this is the type of data that we are really looking for to better understand how these medications can prevent progression," said Daniel Hsia, MD, of Emory University and Children's Healthcare of Atlanta, who was not involved with the study.

As expected with GLP-1 therapies, gastrointestinal issues were the most frequent adverse events. They were mostly mild to moderate, transient, and peaked during the 16-week dose-escalation phase.

When compared with clinical trial data of semaglutide in adults and adolescents, the proportion of participants who experienced vomiting was lower in STEP Young, Hsia pointed out. "We have to remember that children can have lots of gastroenteritis and other reasons for vomiting, so the high rate of vomiting can be partially explained by that."

Only one child in the semaglutide group discontinued treatment due to gastrointestinal side effects. Serious adverse events -- primarily respiratory or gastrointestinal infections -- occurred in 9% of participants in both groups (10 in the semaglutide group, five in the placebo group). Psychiatric events occurred in 21% of the semaglutide group and 24% of the placebo group. No deaths or new safety signals were reported.

Nearly 40% of children in the placebo arm discontinued injections by week 104, which the authors noted was unsurprising given the lack of weight-loss progress. Other limitations included a predominantly white study population and the absence of baseline screening for eating disorders.

Drugmaker Novo Nordisk has not yet announced plans to submit data from STEP Young to the FDA.
