A will and a trust both transfer your assets when you die, but they work in fundamentally different ways. Understanding this difference is the first step to protecting the wealth you have built on the path to financial independence.

A will is a legal document that tells a probate court how you want your assets distributed after death. It names an executor to carry out your wishes, names guardians for minor children, and specifies who gets what. The key limitation: a will must go through probate, a court-supervised process that is public, often slow, and can be expensive.

A trust is a legal entity that holds assets on behalf of your beneficiaries. When you create a revocable living trust, you transfer ownership of your assets into the trust while you are alive. You remain the trustee (controller) during your lifetime. When you die, a successor trustee distributes assets according to the trust document — no court, no probate, no public record.

The bottom line: a will is simpler and cheaper to create, but every dollar and asset in it passes through probate. A trust costs more upfront but saves your heirs significant time, money, and hassle.

Probate is the court process that validates a will and supervises asset distribution. For anyone building wealth toward financial independence, probate represents a direct tax on the legacy you have worked to create.

These costs include attorney fees, executor fees, court filing fees, and appraisal costs. In states like California, probate fees are set by statute — the attorney and executor each get a percentage of the gross estate value (not the net), meaning they are calculated before debts are subtracted.

Probate also takes time. The average probate case takes 6-18 months. Complex estates or contested wills can take 2-3 years. During this time, your heirs may not have access to the assets they need. For a surviving spouse who depends on those accounts for living expenses, this delay can be financially devastating.

A revocable living trust avoids probate entirely. Your successor trustee distributes assets directly to beneficiaries, typically within days or weeks. The upfront cost of creating a trust ($1,500-$5,000) almost always saves your heirs tens of thousands compared to probate costs on a $500K+ estate.

The right choice depends on your net worth, family situation, and the states where you own property. Here is a practical framework:

Most FI families start with a revocable living trust paired with a pour-over will. The trust holds your major assets (real estate, brokerage accounts, bank accounts). The pour-over will catches anything you forgot to title into the trust and directs it there upon your death. You also need the will to name guardians for minor children — trusts cannot do this.

The FI angle: You have spent years building wealth through disciplined saving and investing. A trust protects that wealth from probate fees, public exposure, and distribution delays. The $1,500-$5,000 you spend on a trust setup is a one-time cost that saves your heirs 3-7% of your entire estate. For a $1 million FI portfolio, that savings could be $30,000-$70,000. It is one of the highest-ROI financial moves you can make.

The most common estate planning mistake is creating a trust but forgetting to re-title assets into it. A pour-over will acts as a safety net — any assets not in the trust at death are "poured over" into it. This ensures your trust controls distribution of everything, even assets acquired after the trust was created. The pour-over will still goes through probate for those specific assets, but it prevents intestacy for anything you missed. Review your trust funding annually — check that bank accounts, brokerage accounts, and real estate deeds are titled in the name of your trust.

Calculate your total net worth including real estate, retirement accounts, brokerage accounts, and insurance. If you are under $500K with property in one state, a will may be sufficient. Above $500K or multi-state property, a trust is likely worthwhile.

For most FI families, a revocable living trust plus a pour-over will is the optimal combination. The trust avoids probate for major assets. The will names guardians for children and catches anything not in the trust.

Simple wills can be created with online services like Trust & Will ($159-$599) or LegalZoom ($199-$399). Trusts are more complex — consider an estate planning attorney ($1,500-$5,000) for a properly funded trust.

Creating a trust document is only half the job. You must re-title assets into the trust: update deeds for real property, change bank and brokerage account titles, and assign trust ownership where needed. An unfunded trust provides zero benefit.

Life changes — marriage, divorce, children, major asset changes, moving states — all require estate plan updates. Set a recurring calendar reminder to review your documents with an attorney.
