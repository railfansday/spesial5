The World’s Best Biennial Is in St. Louis

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

A sign of the times: Shortly after St. Louis’s Counterpublic triennial chose sites for its third edition, climate disaster and civic disintegration plucked them off one by one. First was the International Institute, a nonprofit for immigrants and refugees selected after the last census showed that migrants made up the region’s only population growth. Originally, the team behind Counterpublic had hoped to highlight and celebrate the Institute’s vital work. But on Trump’s first day back in office, an executive order suspending refugee admissions decimated the organization’s budget, forcing it to furlough 60 percent of its staff. Then, a tornado ripped through The Ville, a redlined neighborhood, tearing the shingles off Sumner High School’s roof—which, despite being the first Black high school west of the Mississippi and boasting alumni such as Tina Turner, Dick Gregory, and Arthur Ashe,was already suffering from civic neglect. Next, a historic warehouse—one of the show’s main sites—caught fire: It smoldered all Black Friday weekend long, even through the snow, until its roof collapsed.

The question for the organizers of Counterpublic, a civically minded public art exhibition, became not only one of how to create site-specific, civically engaged artworks in the age of the poly-crisis, but why? Then, the existential threat wrought an existential reckoning that meaningfully reimagined what a recurring exhibition of the biennial/triennial variety might be.

The team titled the 2026 edition “Coyote Time,” after a video game that artist Alice Bucknell made for the show. The titular term refers to the grace-period moment after a character in a game runs off a cliff but before gravity catches up, when the player can still jump. It’s named for Wile E. Coyote, who hovers over the canyon until he looks down and clocks the height.

INFRASTRUCTURE HAS BECOME a central topic in art and politics of late, a theme of the most recent Whitney and Medina biennials and a focus of the Mamdani administration in New York. That’s largely because infrastructure becomes most visible when it isn’t working as it ought to—or when you’re not the person it’s designed to work for—as the curators of Counterpublic experienced vividly while selecting sites. For their exhibition, infrastructural critique became both subject and method.

At the International Institute, the curatorial cohort installed several new commissions thematically related to the Institute’s work. On the façade, Petrit Halilaj’s metallic monumentalization of children’s drawings from Kosovo harks back to his childhood as a refugee during the Yugoslav wars. Inside, Rachel Youn’s kinetic sculpture sees fake flowers move mechanically like the brushes of a car wash; theirs is an homage to immigrant labor (including their own family’s). To steward these artworks, Counterpublic also raised funds to hire back some furloughed staffand covered building upgrades, too: fresh paint, a new ceiling.

The artworks at the Institute are not simply a means to an end, nor a mere reversal of the art washing, tax evasion, and money laundering that have become part and parcel with art institutions. They also bring levity, dignity, and warmth to a space where migrants often spend hours waiting for legal counseling and paperwork help. The Institute is a life-giving center that nevertheless could have a perfunctory, bureaucratic feel. Counterpublic hoped to change that in a gesture that feels honest about the reality of what art can meaningfully bring forth, and also what it can’t. Material change and symbolic gestures are not the same, but they can work hand-in-hand.

One artwork, by Rirkrit Tiravanija, ties these threads together. It’s a continuation of his famous communal Thai food meals—quintessential biennial art—but the most delicious and most meaningful version I’ve encountered. The recipes were developed using native fruit, pawpaws, and with the help of the Institute’s clients, who in the process received training for potential careers in hospitality. Running through Counterpublic’s threemonths, the temporary restaurant offers free food and a space to gather. Counterpublic also helped support a new ventilation system and brought the Institute’s kitchen up to code, which set up Tiravanija’s project for success—and will also be useful for more cooking to come.

Tiravanija’s is a familiar project given new meaning by its context, much like the reimagined triennial format itself. While the artist’s original meal, first served in 1990, became a hallmark of relational aesthetics—a critique of the white cube’s reification of static objects through art that expanded to include social encounters—this version responded to a different kind of site and its needs. In so doing, it swapped institutional critique for infrastructural intervention. Here and throughout its three editions, Counterpublic has married art and action, always careful to parse activism from organizing.

AS IF DIRECTLY COUNTERING the criticism political art often (and rightly) gets for confusing virtue-signaling with substantive change, several artworks in Counterpublic aremore or less invisible: You could enjoy a free meal at the Institute without realizing you were consuming art at all. That is the trouble or the triumph—depending on who you ask—of systems artworks like these: The art part can be hard to see once the world around it gets blended in. In the exhibition’s catalog, artist Margaret Honda states outright that she wants her work to “be kind of invisible”: She installed two Noguchi-inspired benches and a brick path in The Ville alongside newly planted trees in response to the community’s request for parks.

Another inconspicuous artwork, Brianna Leatherbury’s Appreciation (2026), sees sculptures buried underground: Made of phosphate reclaimed by an agritech company from the city’s wastewaters, the works absorb lead and cleanse the soil. Without a Counterpublic sign, Leatherbury’s intervention would surely go unnoticed—and yet, it is among the projects that stayed with me most vividly. However invisible it may be, the work is also both a pollution solution and a symbol: Foursculptures are planted in The Ville, a Black neighborhood that bears traces of St. Louis’s disinvestment, and five morerun along the riverfront, home to a reserve of development ambitions. Both sites have been deemed “unoccupied” by the city, and Leatherbury’s project scrambles notions of waste and value: Fine art made from feces makes each lot less toxic and turns it into better real estate, but redlining and racism keep their market value uneven.

Two other works of latter-day Land art effect bioremediation, but blink and you’ll miss them: Chris Carlinstalled a garden of largely native plants and a subtle soundscape near Counterpublic’s main hub, and Lynne Smith rewilded a postindustrial pond at the National Building Arts Center (NBAC)—home to the nation’s largest collection of architectural artifacts, just across the Mississippi River in Illinois. NBAC inhabits a decommissioned steel foundry in what was once the company town of Monsanto—the chemical corporation we have to thank for DDT, Agent Orange, Rachel Carson’s Silent Spring, and so much more. Foraging there for sumac berries one day, Smith came across a loading dock that had been converted into a retention pond for managing runoff, where frogs were laying eggs as young tadpoles slithered along. In an effort to make the site even more frog-friendly (and rehabilitate an arguably apocalyptic chemical crime scene), the artist installed a three-chamber structure—designed after the amphibian’s hearts—to filter rainwater and sediment, used to cultivate ancient native American lotus plants to mitigate both algae growth and the distinct chemical smells of the city.

Clearly, sustainability is a major theme of “Coyote Time,” but in more than just the ecological sense: The scale of the exhibition is considered and refreshingly humane for visitors, and every work by a living artist is a new commission. That means the organization does not take on more than it can meaningfully support, or give visitors more than they can meaningfully consume. Too often, biennial-goers leave beleaguered and artists end up in debt—suspended past the edge of the cliff in coyote time, wondering if it all will pay off.

Other memorable commissions include Li-Yi Fan’s absurdist video rating mirror renderings in various video games and Glenn Ligon’s riff on Thomas Jefferson’s famed feats of engineering—undulatingbrick walls that could stay sturdy with just one buttress-free layer—behind which he hid enslaved laborers. Titled Who Builds Anything In This Country (2026), Ligon’s work speaks to the enslaved labor that built St. Louis and its brick business, too;it is memorably installed just past St. Louis’s iconic Gateway Arch. Fan’s commission is sure to have afterlives in future exhibitions; Ligon’s is in St. Louis to stay.

Counterpublic’s models of redistribution, of art-as-infrastructure, and of sustainability find precedent, of course, in artists’ practices. Take duo Ryan Trecartin and Lizzie Fitch, who are part of the show: Whenthe sculptor and video artist received a major commission from Fondazione Prada for a 2019 exhibition, they used the funds to build a set—including a prefab barn with an amusement-park-like “lazy river”—where they now live and work. For “Coyote Time,” they created a similar structure blurring performance and life: A stage for the city that offers space for civic gathering, performing arts, and play. The duo’s signature frolicsome use of color and scale joins an equipment library with projectors, speakers, and microphones. The stage is one of 15 works—among them Honda’s, Ligon’s, Halilaj’s, Smith’s, and Carl’s—that is a permanent gift to the city.

ANYTHING THIS AMBITIOUS raises hard questions—in fact, that’s part of the point. One involves the slippery slope between well-meaning efforts to improve urban infrastructure and the gentrification that too often ensues. Where the displacement of poorer urban residents was once considered an unfortunate side effect of biennials and their ilk, we’ve now seen developers and municipalities enlist artists strategically for the purpose of increasing property value. Indeed, on opening night, I saw a lone protester with a sign that read our communites are not your brand.

But one could argue—indeed, Counterpublic seems to—that if we know art and real estate are now bedfellows, perhaps this can be wielded to a community’s advantage if organizers and not developers are at the helm. Certainly, this does seem worth a try. Notably, community partners—like The Ville’s 4theVille, on behalf of Sumner High School—reached out to Counterpublic to collaborate rather than the other way around. The school had nearly closed several times, but to remind the city of its cultural significance—which can seem at odds with its material neglect—Counterpublic commissioned Tony Cokes to craft a memorable homage to alumna Tina Turner, installed in a church nearby. And Timmy Simonds—whose ongoing project Miss Othmar School for Teachers imagines a school without students—made flags that pay homage to the first Black teachers college west of the Mississippi, once housed on the same site. A clever nod to how teachers—like the titular Miss Othmar, of Peanuts fame—are often only seen from the waist up, these pant-shaped former parachutes evoke their full personhood, while also dressing down the classroom’s hierarchy and the teacher’s authority. We’re all teachers and all students sometimes.

Where Counterpublic does make efforts to offer solutions to civic issues of varying scales, these gestures stand out for the way they never attempt to cover up the seams. Artworks lay issues bare as much as they attempt to ameliorate them, occupying an honest middle ground between critique and repair. Art, we all know, is hardly the ideal fix for civic issues, but still, solutions start somewhere. As Rebecca Solnit says, “revolution takes place first in the imagination,” or as (choose your fighter) Andrew Breitbart said, “politics is downstream from culture.” And yet, in “Coyote Time,” art is more than a start, because the exhibition is always thinking about what it leaves behind: a place to sit, a kitchen to cook in, a spot of shade, a stage to scream from, soil to grow in.
