How Do You Measure Data Quality?

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Posted on Nov 26 • Originally published at blog.anomalyarmor.ai

Measuring data quality is the practice of turning "is this data fit for use" into numbers you can compute on a schedule, compare over time, and act on when they move.

That last clause is the one most dashboards fail. A single 0 to 100 "data quality score" in green is easy to build and almost impossible to act on. When it drops from 97 to 94, nobody knows which table, which check, or whether anyone downstream is affected. When it stays at 97 while the revenue table is broken, it's worse than useless, because it told leadership everything was fine.

This post gives you six metrics that are concrete enough to compute from warehouse metadata, with SQL for Snowflake and Databricks, plus a scorecard that decides which of them go in front of leadership and which stay with the team. It assumes you know the six dimensions of data quality already. Dimensions tell you what can go wrong. Metrics tell you how much it went wrong this week.

A metric is vanity if it can improve without the data getting better, or if nobody changes what they do when it moves. Most composite quality scores fail both tests, for three structural reasons.

Averaging hides the failure that matters. Take 400 tables. 399 of them score 100 and the table behind the revenue dashboard scores 0 because it hasn't loaded since Tuesday. The average is 99.75. Round it and you're reporting 100% to a CFO whose board deck is wrong. Any score that averages across tables of different importance has this bug, and weighting doesn't fully fix it, because a weighted average still lets ten healthy tables compensate for one broken one.

The denominator is self-selected. Test pass rate is the classic case. Picture 2,000 dbt tests, 1,600 of which are not_null and unique on primary keys that have never failed. Six tests fail this week. The pass rate is 99.7%. Those 6 failures might all be on one critical model, and the number still looks excellent. Worse, you can raise the score by adding more easy tests, which is exactly the behavior a metric should never reward.

Coverage gets confused with quality. "We monitor 85% of tables" is a statement about your monitoring, not your data. It's a useful number, but it belongs on an engineering to-do list, not a quality report.

AnomalyArmor deliberately doesn't ship a single quality grade. What it ships is a coverage score: a 0 to 100 number weighted 30 points for freshness coverage, 25 for metrics, 15 for schema drift, 15 for alert routing, 10 for validity, and 5 for an up-to-date knowledge base. Its own docstring says partial coverage in one area compensates for gaps in another. That's the right design for a coverage number, because it's a checklist of what to set up next. It would be the wrong design for a quality number, for the averaging reason above, and that's why it's called coverage.

Six metrics, each tied to a dimension, each computable on a schedule without a human deciding what the answer should be.

Two composites sit on top of these: tier-1 uptime and detection share. Those are the numbers that go upward, and they're covered in the scorecard section below.

Notice what's missing. Accuracy has no row. You can't measure accuracy from metadata, because accuracy means agreement with the real world, and the warehouse doesn't know what the real world looks like. The only honest accuracy metric I know is a reconciliation check on the 3 to 5 numbers the business actually runs on: warehouse revenue against the billing system, active customers against the CRM. Anything that claims to score accuracy across 400 tables automatically is scoring something else and calling it accuracy.

Every metric below writes to a small dq schema. That's deliberate. Most of these need history to mean anything, and information_schema only tells you about now.

Freshness lag is the time since a table last changed, divided by how often it's supposed to change. The ratio matters more than the raw lag. A table that's 6 hours old is fine if it loads daily and broken if it loads every 15 minutes.

Then record the lag every hour. On Snowflake, INFORMATION_SCHEMA.TABLES carries LAST_ALTERED:

On Databricks with Unity Catalog, each catalog's information_schema.tables has a last_altered column too. Databricks documents it as when the relation definition was last altered, so before trusting it for data writes, check it against DESCRIBE HISTORY on a table you know just loaded:

A ratio above 1.0 is a breach. A ratio of 0.8 that was 0.3 last week is a pipeline slowing down, and that's the more valuable signal because you can act on it before anyone notices.

One caveat worth knowing. Snowflake documents LAST_ALTERED as changing on DML, DDL, or background metadata operations, so a table can look fresher than its data really is. If a table's freshness matters enough to be tier 1, it's worth swapping the metadata timestamp for MAX(loaded_at) on the table itself. On Databricks, DESCRIBE HISTORY gives you the actual Delta commit timestamps when you need to confirm what last_altered is telling you. The freshness monitoring post covers the column-based approach in more depth.

Volume deviation is how far today's row growth is from the typical row growth on the same weekday, expressed as a fraction. Use rows added, not total rows. A table with 400 million rows that received zero new rows today has a total row count that moved 0%, which is how a dead pipeline hides inside a big table.

Once snapshots exist, the deviation query is the same on both platforms. MEDIAN, LAG, DATEADD, and DAYOFWEEK exist in Snowflake and in Databricks SQL (MEDIAN needs Databricks Runtime 11.3 or later, or a SQL warehouse):

A deviation of -1.0 means nothing arrived. A deviation of +1.0 means double the usual load, which is usually a duplicate backfill. I'd start alerting at plus or minus 0.5 on tier-1 tables and tune from there. Same-weekday comparison matters: plenty of B2B tables load 70% fewer rows on Sunday, and a plain trailing average flags every one of those as an anomaly.

Schema change count is the number of columns added, dropped, or retyped per table per day. It's the cheapest metric on this list and, in my experience, the one most correlated with incidents that start upstream of you.

Snapshot the column list daily. The insert differs by platform only in naming:

Don't treat all changes equally. An added nullable column breaks almost nothing. A dropped column or a type change from NUMBER to VARCHAR breaks every model that selects it. The operating metric is all changes; the number worth tracking monthly is breaking changes on tier-1 sources that nobody announced, because that's the number that tells you whether you need a data contract with the team upstream.

Null rate drift is the change in the share of null values in a critical column, measured on the newly loaded rows only, compared against that column's usual rate. This is the one metric here that needs a real scan, so it should only run on columns that would break something if they went null.

Measure the new rows, not the table. If a 3-year table gets one bad day where 40% of customer_id values are null, the table-wide null rate moves by about 0.04 percentage points. You'll never see it. The same bad day measured on the day's load jumps from, say, 0.1% to 40%.

Drift is today's rate minus the trailing median rate, in percentage points. Report it in points rather than as a relative change. A column going from 0.01% to 0.03% null is a 200% increase and almost certainly nothing. A column going from 2% to 12% is a 10-point jump and almost certainly a broken join or a source that stopped sending a field.

As an operating signal, yes. As a reported KPI, no, for the denominator reason already covered. The failing tests matter; the percentage doesn't.

dbt writes every result to target/run_results.json, so you already have the data. This pulls out what's actually useful:

The line that matters is the warning count. Tests set to severity: warn don't fail the run, so they pile up silently. A team with 40 standing warnings has 40 known problems that its pass rate is quietly counting as fine. I'd report warnings separately from failures every time, and treat a warning count that only grows as its own incident.

If you want a test-based number that resists gaming, use "tier-1 models with at least one failing or warning test." It's a count of tables, it can't be inflated by adding trivial tests, and it maps to something a human can fix.

Time to detection is how long a problem existed before anyone knew. Time to resolution is how long it took to fix once known. Data downtime combines them, and that post covers the formula and cost model. What it doesn't cover is the part that makes TTD hard to measure honestly: the start time.

The last row is where most incident logs lie. When a stakeholder reports a broken dashboard, someone opens an incident and the detection time equals the report time. If onset isn't back-filled, the TTD for the worst incidents, the ones monitoring missed entirely, gets recorded as roughly zero. Your median TTD looks great precisely because your monitoring failed.

AnomalyArmor's incident record has the same gap, for the same reason. It stores when an incident was first detected, acknowledged, and resolved, so TTR falls straight out of it. It has no onset field, so TTD still needs one of the inference rules above.

Given an incident log with an inferred started_at, one query gives you the reportable numbers. Use the median and 90th percentile rather than the mean, because one 3-day incident will dominate an average of twenty 1-hour ones:

Count incidents by root cause, not by alert. If one broken connector produces thirty alerts across thirty downstream tables, that's one incident with one TTD. Counting it as thirty inflates incident volume and deflates TTD, since the downstream alerts fire after the root. The alert storm post covers how to group them.

Three. Everything else in this post stays with the team.

This is the scorecard. I call it the weakest-link scorecard because its core rule is that nothing averages across checks: a table is up in a given hour only if every check on it passes.

1. Tier-1 uptime. For each tier-1 table, each hourly freshness check counts as an up-hour only if the table is within its SLA and has no open incident. Uptime is up-hours divided by checked hours.

Volume, schema, null, and test failures enter through the incident clause. When any of them opens an incident against a table, that table is down until the incident resolves. That's the weakest-link part: a table with perfect freshness and a broken amount column is down, because it is.

Report two numbers from this: the overall uptime and the worst table. "Tier-1 uptime was 99.4%, worst table fct_revenue at 96.1%" is a sentence a VP can act on. 99.4% alone isn't. At hourly checks, 30 days is 720 hours per table, so 96.1% means about 28 hours down, and you can say that too.

2. Detection share. The fraction of incidents your monitoring caught before a human reported them. This is the single best measure of whether monitoring is working, and it's nearly impossible to game: the only way to raise it is to catch more things first. A team at 40% learns about most problems from their stakeholders. A team at 90% tells their stakeholders first. That difference is the whole value of a monitoring program, in one number.

3. Median and p90 TTD for tier-1 incidents. With the onset back-fill rule applied. Median shows the typical case, p90 shows the ones that hurt.

Everything else, freshness ratios, volume deviation, schema change counts, null drift, test results, is an operating metric. It tells the team where to look. Reporting it upward invites the question "is 0.3 volume deviation bad?", and the honest answer, "depends on the table," is the sign it doesn't belong in that meeting.

Measure for 30 days before setting any target. Your first month of tier-1 uptime is your baseline, and a target set before you have it is a guess that'll be either embarrassing or meaningless.

Then pick targets by tier, not globally. 99.5% monthly uptime allows about 3.6 hours down per table per month, which is a reasonable first goal for a tier-1 table loaded hourly. For a table loaded once a day, a single missed load is 24 hours late at worst, which on its own is 3.3% of the month. A 99.5% target on a daily table means you can never miss a load, and that's a target that'll be missed and then ignored.

For detection share, I'd aim for above 80% on tier 1 within a quarter of starting to measure. If a human keeps catching a class of problem, that class is the next monitor to write. The human-reported incidents are a ranked backlog of exactly what your monitoring misses.

Two targets I'd avoid entirely. Don't set a test pass rate target, for the gaming reason. Don't set a target on the number of incidents. Incident count goes down when monitoring gets worse, since undetected problems aren't incidents, and a team rewarded for fewer incidents is a team rewarded for looking less.

Tier-1 uptime, detection share, and median TTD are the three worth reporting. Underneath them, the operating metrics are freshness lag ratio, volume deviation, schema change count, null rate drift, and test results. Accuracy needs reconciliation checks on a handful of business numbers and can't be measured from metadata.

Avoid a single averaged score across tables and dimensions, because averaging lets healthy tables hide a broken one. Calculate tier-1 uptime instead: the share of hourly checks where a critical table was within its freshness SLA and had no open incident. Report the worst table alongside it.

A metric is anything you can compute, like volume deviation on one table. A KPI is a metric someone outside the data team is held to and makes decisions from. Most data quality metrics are operating metrics that should stay with the team; only outcome measures like uptime, detection share, and TTD work as KPIs.
