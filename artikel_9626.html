Amazon Global Product Scraper: How to Compare Prices Across Marketplaces with Python

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

An Amazon Global Product Scraper lets you pull the same product record from several Amazon country marketplaces so you can compare what a shopper actually pays in each one. The hard part is not the download — it is the normalization: each marketplace quotes a different currency, some show tax-inclusive prices and some do not, the Buy Box rotates between sellers, and the same product does not always carry the same ASIN everywhere. This guide builds a Python workflow that collects one record per (marketplace, product), converts to a single reporting currency, matches products across catalogs, and produces a parity index you can defend in a pricing meeting. It is for e-commerce operators, brand pricing managers, and the developers building their data layer.

The naive version is a spreadsheet with one column per country. It fails in five ways, and each produces a wrong decision rather than a missing one.

Tax display conventions differ. A US listing typically shows a pre-tax price because sales tax is added at checkout and varies by state, while a German or British listing usually shows a VAT-inclusive price. Compare the raw numbers and you will conclude the European shopper pays far more, when much of the gap is tax the seller remits. State whether your model compares gross or net.

Currency is not a constant. Rates move daily. Convert January prices at a November rate and your trend line measures the currency, not the catalog. Store the rate and its date with every converted value.

The Buy Box is a rotating offer, not a product attribute. Two requests minutes apart can return different sellers at different prices for the same ASIN. That is normal marketplace behavior: one snapshot is a sample, not a definitive price.

Catalog identity is fragmented. Amazon assigns ASINs per marketplace. The same product often shares an ASIN across several European marketplaces and often does not across the US, Japan, and newer marketplaces. Where it differs you need a second identifier: GTIN/EAN, brand plus model, or a reviewed title match.

Assortment and offer type differ. A product may be sold by Amazon retail in one country and a third-party seller in another, and be unavailable in a third. An unavailable listing is not a cheap listing; treating a missing price as zero corrupts every average.

Define the record before you write the collector. The field names below are the ones this workflow expects; map them to whatever your worker returns, and keep that mapping in one place.

Two storage rules pay for themselves immediately: keep the raw JSON payload next to the parsed columns, and write each run to an append-only table keyed by (run_id, marketplace, asin). When someone questions a number, you replay the exact response instead of re-scraping and hoping the price has not moved.

Start with the marketplaces where you sell or plan to sell. Four to eight is plenty for a first pass; each addition multiplies request volume and matching work. Write the matrix as configuration so adding a country is a one-line change.

Issue one request per pair. Batching many ASINs into one call is tempting, but a single bad ASIN then fails the batch. Space requests with a small interval and retry only on rate-limit and server errors, with exponential backoff.

Convert to a reporting currency using a rate table your finance team owns, and stamp the rate date. Record the tax basis per marketplace and compute net-price comparisons where needed. Do not hardcode a VAT percentage for a whole region — rates differ by country and category, and a wrong assumption distorts every conclusion.

Run matching in tiers and keep the confidence on the record:

Anything that falls through stays unmatched and is reported as such. An unmatched product is a gap to review; a wrong match is a false conclusion nobody will catch.

For each matched group, pick a base marketplace, convert every price into the reporting currency, and divide each price by the base price. That ratio is the parity index: 1.00 means the same price, 1.20 means 20 percent higher. Report the spread as max minus min across the group, and flag groups above your threshold. Start at 15 percent, then tune it — luxury and bulky goods legitimately vary more than accessories.

The script separates collection from analysis. The collection step reads its request body from an environment variable, because the payload your worker expects should come from your own console, not from a blog post. The analysis step runs offline against saved JSON, so you can test it without network calls.

Two details matter: to_float handles both 1,299.00 and 1.299,00 formats, and unavailable listings are dropped before the parity math instead of being coerced to zero.

A record that fails matching stays out of the table and is listed as unmatched: if the Japanese listing used a different ASIN and could not be tied to the same model, including it would measure two different products. Store the match method (asin, gtin, title) on every row and review title matches before they reach a dashboard.

A gap above threshold is a question, not a verdict. Check these in order:

Only a gap that survives all five represents a pricing position worth acting on.

Global pricing strategy. A brand entering a new marketplace needs a reference price that reflects local willingness to pay, not a converted home price. The parity index gives a defensible starting point and a way to measure drift after launch.

Price parity and MAP-style programs. Brands that set resale price expectations across regions need evidence when a reseller undercuts in one country. A timestamped, source-linked record beats a screenshot in a partner conversation.

Parallel-import and arbitrage detection. A persistent, large gap between adjacent marketplaces is the signature of cross-border resale — and a signal about where to tighten distribution.

Promotion impact. Before and after a local promotion, the index shows whether a discount moved the product's relative position or merely matched a competitor.

Assortment gaps. Products that will not match across marketplaces because identifiers diverge are often not the same item — useful input for catalog consolidation.

For most teams the answer is a managed source plus your own normalization layer: the matching logic is the part that stays with you when the provider changes.

Do I need a different ASIN for each marketplace? Often, but not always. Some marketplaces share ASINs for identical catalog entries and others assign their own. Treat ASIN as the primary key with a fallback chain, not as a global identifier.

Should I compare prices with or without tax? Compare like with like. Net-to-net suits margin analysis; gross-to-gross reflects what a shopper sees. Pick one, label it, and never mix the two in a chart.

How often should the parity report run? Weekly suits most catalogs. Run daily only during a launch, a promotion window, or a currency event — one run cannot distinguish a repricing lag from a pricing decision.

What if a product is unavailable in one marketplace? Exclude it from the parity math and report it as a coverage gap. Substituting zero or the base price creates a fake comparison that will be quoted in a meeting six weeks later.

Can I use this data to set prices automatically? You can, but automatic repricing against competitor data can trigger price spirals and, depending on use, raise antitrust exposure. Keep a human approval step and document the rule behind each change.

How do I handle a currency with no rate in my table? Leave the converted value empty and list the record under "missing FX". An imputed rate is indistinguishable from a real one once it is in the CSV.

How do I keep matching accurate as the catalog grows? Persist match method and confidence per row, review every title-based match manually, and re-run the GTIN check when identifiers change. Matching quality degrades quietly, so audit it on a schedule.

Start with one product family and four marketplaces. Collect a run, normalize it, and read the unmatched records first — they say more about your catalog than the matched ones. Once the match rate is stable, schedule a weekly run into an append-only table so you can chart the parity index over time instead of staring at one snapshot.
