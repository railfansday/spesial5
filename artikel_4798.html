The 7 Best Wired Headphones for Your Runs and Workouts

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

No more charging, no more lost Bluetooth connections, and no more chasing a bud under the squat rack.

WIRED HEADPHONES ARE popular again. The cords are showing up in NBA arena tunnels, where Golden State Warriors point guard Steph Curry walks in with them hanging from his ears. Bears quarterback Caleb Williams wears corded headphones as part of his pregame routine. Bose felt they were worth bringing back, too. In October, it started shipping the $99 Noise Cancelling Wired Earbuds, its first new wired headphones in years.While our favorite wireless workout headphones are more advanced than ever, they still have problems at the gym: connections drop, audio crackles, and you have to constantly manage the battery. And we've all had a bud knocked loose by a burpee and watched it bounce across the floor and under a squat rack. Just me? A wired pair avoids all of that. Sound travels through the cable, so there are no Bluetooth dropouts, and if a bud slips out, it hangs from the cord instead of hitting the ground.

Wired earbuds also have no battery. You'll never get to the gym and find out your headphones are dead, and a pair that's been sitting in your bag for a month still works as soon as you plug it in.

Sure, the cord can have its own drawbacks. It can snag on a rack in the gym, and when you run, it can bounce around into your body and arms. Its weight also pulls the buds downward, which can put pressure on your ears.

Most picks below have a way to keep the buds in place, including ear hooks, stabilizer wings, or a cable that runs over the top of your ear. Most also come with the right plug for your phone, so you don’t need an adapter. These are the best wired headphones you can get right now.

The Bose Noise Cancelling Wired Earbuds are the only pair on this list with active noise canceling. Each bud has two microphones that pick up the clang of plates and the music on the gym speakers, then cancel that sound. With the room quieter, you can keep your own music at a lower volume. Hold the button on the inline remote to switch to Aware mode. It uses the same mics to pipe in outside sound, so you can hear cars and cyclists on an outdoor run.

Noise canceling works best when each bud seals your ear canal, and silicone wing-tip stabilizers keep that seal in place while you move. Bose’s wings press against the inner ridge of your ear, so the buds don't shift during jumps or sprints. You get two wing sizes and three ear tip sizes, and you can remove the wings if you don't need them. The thick, rubbery 4.2-foot cable connecting them is durable. It tangles less than the thin cords on cheap USB-C buds, and it makes less noise when it rubs against a shirt.

Partway down that cable sits the remote, which holds volume buttons, a multifunction button (tap to play or pause, double-tap to skip, hold to change noise modes), and the call mic. All that hardware can be heavy, though — enough that you'll feel it pull on the buds during jumps and sprints. Running the cable under your shirt collar keeps the remote from swinging.

The noise canceling also determines what you can plug these into. The earbuds draw power from your phone to run the mics and processor, so they need a USB-C port. Any iPhone 15 or newer works, and most current Android phones do too. A 3.5mm jack, like the one on a treadmill screen or an airplane seat, won't work, even with an adapter. Once they're plugged in, the IPX4 rating protects them from sweat and splashes from any direction, though not from a dunk in water. They come in black, white, and a limited-edition mint.

The JBL Endurance Run 3 costs $25 and carries an IP65 rating, the highest on this list. That means no dust gets in on dirt trails and sandy beach runs, while they can survive water sprayed from a nozzle. A downpour or a sweat-soaked HIIT class won't hurt them, either.

JBL's FlipHook design gives you two ways to wear them. With the cable hanging straight down, they sit like regular earbuds. Rotate the hooks upward and the cable runs over the top of your ear and down behind it. That takes the cable's weight off the bud. It also stops the wire from thumping against your chest while you run and jump. The TwistLock tips sit into the bowl of your ear, so a bud stays put when you turn your head fast.

The remote has one button and a mic, handling play, pause, and calls, and a long press brings up Siri or Google Assistant. You can adjust the volume through your phone. The standard Run 3 plugs into a 3.5mm jack. JBL also offers the Endurance Run 3C USB-C, which has a three-button remote and costs a little more. The standard Endurance Run 3 has a 3.5mm plug and a one-button remote.

The MEE Audio M6 Sport uses memory wire, a thin, bendable wire inside the cable near each bud. You bend it around the back of your ear once, and it holds that curve. It works like a custom-fit ear hook that stays put through box jumps and hill sprints, and it also takes stress off the buds.

The three-button remote on the braided cable does more than most wired sport buds. The outer buttons raise and lower volume. The center button plays, pauses, skips tracks, answers calls, and starts your voice assistant. The IPX5 rating protects against heavy sweat and rain.

MEE Audio tunes the M6 Sport for heavy bass. The USB-C version works with modern smartphones, and it comes with a shirt clip and six sets of ear tips, so you can get the exact fit you want. You can also get them a with a 3.5mm jack plug..

The SE215 earphones are in-ear monitors, the same style of earphones musicians wear on stage to hear themselves over a loud crowd. The low-profile shells sit flush inside your ear. The cable runs up and over the back of each ear, pulling the cord's weight off the buds and keeping cable noise out of your music when you move. The included foam tips expand to fill your ear canal, and Shure says they block up to 37 decibels of outside noise, enough to drown out a gym's speakers.

The SE215 delivers deeper bass than other wired headphones we’ve tested. Beyond sound, the earbuds have also been designed with durability in mind. The SE215's cable is Kevlar-reinforced and detaches at MMCX connectors, so if it breaks, you only replace the cord. Shure also sells a Bluetooth adapter that makes the same buds wireless.

Shure doesn't give them an IP rating, though, so you should wipe them down after sweaty sessions. The standard 64-inch cable has no mic or remote and comes only in a 3.5mm configuration, so a USB-C phone needs a dongle.

Apple’s EarPods are the pair you see a lot of people using in TikTok videos, holding the mic close to their mouth. At $19, they’re a great pair to keep in your bag for the day you forget your main headphones. The remote has volume buttons and a center button to play, pause, and answer calls. Apple says they're protected against sweat and water, though it doesn't list an IP rating.

The EarPods have hard plastic shells shaped to rest in the bowl of your ear, with no silicone tips sealing the canal. That makes them comfortable for long walks and lets outside sound in. But nothing holds them in place for more rigorous movements like burpees and jump rope. Still, they're steady enough for walking, the elliptical, and light lifting.

The Avantree C171’s ear hooks are molded to curve over and behind each ear, so unlike the MEE Audio M6 Sport, no wire needs to be bent into shape. The cable runs down behind your ear instead of hanging from the bud, so it’s more out of the way when you’re moving.

A four-button remote controls volume, playback, calls, and your voice assistant, so you can keep your phone in your pocket. Avantree sells similar sport earbuds with USB-C, Lightning, and 3.5mm plugs; the C171 is the USB-C model, and the brand’s C171 manual includes troubleshooting for Galaxy S24 users who run into audio detection or distortion issues.

They don’t have an IP rating, but if fit is your main concern, these shouldn’t fly out of your ear during a HIIT workout.

The Avantree Resolve's speakers rest just outside your ear canal instead of sealing inside it, so you can still hear traffic and cyclists coming up behind you on a run. With no tip pressing into your ear canal, you'll also feel less soreness that builds up over a long session.

Since nothing sits inside your ear to hold the speakers in place, flexible hooks wrap over each ear to hold them in place. That grip won't be as snug as an in-ear tip, so the speakers can shift more when you move. Avantree makes a Small-Medium size for ears under 2.5 inches long and a Large size for everyone else, so measure your ear before you order.

While there is no IP rating, you do get inline remote controls for playback, volume, calls, and your voice assistant. Avantree sells 3.5mm, USB-C, and Lightning versions.

Wired headphones are better if your workouts keep getting cut short by dead batteries or bad Bluetooth connections. Without a battery, wired headphones play as soon as you plug them in, no matter how long they've sat unused. There's also no battery to wear out after two or three years of charging, which is when many wireless earbuds start dying mid-run.

Wireless buds are the better choice when you need total freedom of movement. Rolling on the floor, kipping pull-ups, and jump rope can all snag a cord. If your workouts are mostly lifting, steady running, or cardio machines, a cord rarely gets in the way.

Like any earbud, whether it’s wired or wireless, they can fall out of your ear. The cable's weight pulls on each bud with every step, so look for one of three fixes: ear hooks, like the memory wire on the MEE Audio M6 Sport or the FlipHook on the JBL Endurance Run 3, loop the cable over your ear. Stability wings, like those on the Bose, brace the bud against the ridge inside your ear. Over-ear cable routing, as on the Shure SE215, takes the cable's weight off the bud entirely. Plain earbuds with no tips or wings, like Apple's EarPods, are the most likely to fall out.

Yes, if you match the plug to your phone. USB-C earbuds plug straight into an iPhone 15 and later and most Android phones. Their digital-to-analog converter is built into the plug or cable, so you don't need an adapter. Since most modern smartphones no longer have a 3.5mm jack, earbuds with a 3.5mm plug need a USB-C-to-3.5mm dongle, and on a run that dongle adds weight and can snag on equipment. Choose a USB-C version when one exists.

The thumping you hear when the cord bounces against your shirt is called microphonic. Vibration travels up the wire into the earbud. Routing the cable over your ears is the most effective fix because it breaks the straight path from your chest to your ear canal. Running the cable under your shirt collar also stops it from swinging. A thicker, rubberized cable, like Bose's, transmits less noise than a thin plastic one.

IPX4 is the minimum for sweaty workouts. An IP rating has two digits. The first is dust protection on a scale up to 6, and the second is water protection on a scale up to 9. An X means the product wasn't tested for that category. IPX4 protects against splashing water from any direction, which covers sweat. An IPX5 rating means the headphones can withstand water jets, but it doesn’t guarantee protection in every condition, such as heavy rain. IP65, the JBL Endurance Run 3's rating, adds full dust protection for trail running.

Water and dust resistance is one area where wireless earbuds are well ahead. Most wireless workout models are rated at least IPX4, and many are rated IP55 or higher. Only three of the wired picks here have a rating: the Bose, the JBL, and the MEE Audio. If your pair doesn't have one, they can still survive light sweat, but remember to wipe them dry after each session.

I use Apple’s EarPods every day and have spent years testing wireless headphones and earbuds. That experience shaped what I looked for here. For this guide, I picked models based on factors like fit, durability, compatibility, and inline controls. The rest of the Men's Health team also reviewed and contributed to the list.

First, I looked at fit. A cable pulls on the buds every time you move, so I looked for ear hooks, stabilizer wings, or cables that run over the ear. I favored pairs with an official IP rating over vague "sweat-resistant" claims. I also checked that each pair is sold with a plug that fits current phones, because a 3.5mm-to-USB-C adapter is one more thing that can come loose mid-run. Inline controls mattered as well, since a remote you can reach mid-set means you don't have to dig your phone out.
