Optical digital formats (DVD, Blu-Ray, CD) were a huge improvement over optical analog formats (Laserdisc, CED). Laserdiscs, while miles better than VHS tapes, still had a fair amount of noise when compared to DVD, although the difference was hard to spot on a small television. Plus laserdiscs had to be a lot larger, and double sided, to store the same relative amount of information. The double sided aspect was achieved by gluing two discs together in a sandwich, and sometimes a bad pressing would end up in 'laser rot' where the glue starts to de-laminate, warping the disc and causing noise and signal dropout on playback. This wasn't a super widespread problem, but it was there. And either you had to flip the disc in the middle of the movie, or have a more expensive auto-reversing player. Most discs were CLV (constant linear velocity), but CAV (constant angular velocity) discs allowed for smooth jog-shuttling (fast forward / rewind) and perfect still images, but took up even more room, requiring multiple discs per feature. The fun things we had to deal with back then. DVDs started to really take over in the mid-90s. It was great as a standard-definition format, not to mention DVD-Rs for data and home video / small run productions in the early 2000s.

Compact discs are still the only non-compressed, no copy protection physical consumer format we had. (DAT was non compressed but not used to sell commercial releases.) Once they solved the skipping problem on portable players by buffering ahead a few seconds, it was the greatest thing. 44.1khz stereo. (you'd be hard pressed to tell the difference between 44.1 and the more common 48khz standard for professional applications.) A lot of early releases and re-releases of older music on CD tended to be 'tinny' because sound engineers hadn't quite gotten used to mastering for the format. After a few years (and before wild compression became vogue) there was nothing better than playing back music made for CD on a really high end sound system.
