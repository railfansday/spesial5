Necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) is the most devastating gastrointestinal emergency in preterm infants, frequently causing mortality and long-term neurodevelopmental impairment1,2. Although the gut microbiota has been implicated in NEC pathogenesis3,4,5, the microbial factors driving NEC and the following brain pathology remain unclear. Here we identify Clostridium perfringens pathobionts containing Delta toxin in the gut microbiome of individuals with severe and fatal NEC. Clinically, infected infants show worse outcomes, including aberrant systemic inflammation, severe neurological damage and coagulopathy. We establish a mouse model in which Delta-toxin-positive C. perfringens infection, or Delta toxin exposure alone, recapitulates clinical features of NEC, including intestinal necrosis and acute cerebral injury. Mechanistically, Delta toxin preferentially targets macrophages, potently activating inflammasome signalling and pyroptosis, triggering a rapid inflammatory cascade that compromises blood–brain barrier integrity and drives neuroinflammation. Through a genome-wide CRISPR screen, we identify the tetraspanin CD9 as the host receptor for Delta toxin. Using systemic, myeloid-specific and intestinal-epithelial-specific Cd9-knockout mice, we show that systemic and myeloid Cd9 ablation protects mice against toxin-induced lethality, blood–brain barrier dysfunction and multiorgan injury, whereas epithelial Cd9 deletion shows partial protection, implicating macrophage targeting as a primary driver of C. perfringens CD9-dependent pathogenesis. Furthermore, a neutralizing anti-Delta toxin monoclonal antibody confers protection against disease progression. These findings suggest that a subset of NEC is a specific-toxin-mediated disorder and validate the Delta toxin–CD9 axis as a therapeutic target for this subset.

NEC remains a major cause of mortality and long-term morbidity in preterm infants1,2. NEC pathogenesis is multifactorial, involving the complex interplay of immature intestinal development, intestinal barrier dysfunction, gut dysbiosis and excessive inflammatory response3,4,5,6. The specific triggers of NEC and the mechanisms driving its rapid progression remain poorly understood.

The impact of NEC extends beyond the intestine. More than half of postoperative survivors develop brain injury and neurodevelopmental impairment7,8, with TLR4 inflammatory pathways implicated in this pathology9,10. Many insights are derived from experimental models that use hypoxia, cold stress and hyperosmolar formula feeding to increase intestinal vulnerability11,12. Although these models have been valuable for mapping inflammatory pathways, they may be less well suited to interrogating microbiome-driven early initiating events.

Gut microbiota are implicated in NEC susceptibility: NEC is associated with dysbiosis3,4,5, germ-free mice are resistant to NEC-like injury13 and microbiota-modifying interventions are protective14,15. Several studies identified enrichment of toxin-producing C. perfringens in NEC16,17,18, but causal virulence factors remain undefined.

C. perfringens is a ubiquitous, Gram-positive anaerobe producing diverse toxins19,20. Strains are classified into seven toxin types (A–G) based on toxin repertoire19. Type C strains produce Beta and Delta toxins, which are both pore-forming toxins (PFTs) that induce cell death by forming transmembrane pores21,22,23. While Beta toxin is well characterized, Delta toxin remains understudied. Here we identify C. perfringens Delta toxin as a virulence factor that drives intestinal necrosis and brain injury in a subset of NEC, providing an avenue for targeted therapeutic intervention.

To investigate the role of gut microbiota in NEC, we profiled faecal samples from 28 preterm infants without NEC and 16 preterm infants with NEC. 16S rRNA-sequencing analysis revealed an enrichment of C. perfringens in NEC samples (Fig. 1a), consistent with previous reports16,17. Two fatal cases of NEC showed high C. perfringens abundance (Extended Data Fig. 1a). To validate this finding, we performed anaerobic culture of faecal samples from 49 individuals without NEC and 17 patients with NEC (Supplementary Table 1). C. perfringens-positive cultures showed visible gas production and were confirmed by selective tryptose sulfite cycloserine (TSC) agar isolation and PCR targeting of C. perfringens-specific genes (Extended Data Fig. 1b–d). Overall, C. perfringens was more prevalent in patients with NEC compared with in infants without NEC (56% versus 18%), supporting its association with NEC (Fig. 1b,c).

C. perfringens produces a repertoire of toxins, including Alpha, Beta, Epsilon, Iota toxin, enterotoxin (CPE), necrotic enteritis β-like toxin (NetB) and perfringolysin O (Pfo)19. We next examined whether specific toxin profiles correlated with severe outcomes. PCR-based toxin typing identified isolates from two deceased patients as type C C. perfringens strains containing the delta toxin gene (Delta toxin+ C. perfringens clinical-03 and clinical-15) (Extended Data Fig. 1e). Delta toxin production was further confirmed by western blot analysis of culture supernatants (Extended Data Fig. 1f,g). We further detected anti-Delta toxin antibodies in two patients with severe surgical NEC whose stool cultures were negative, probably due to antibiotic treatment, indicating previous infection with Delta toxin+ C. perfringens strains. Expanded serological analysis of ten additional patients with severe surgical NEC identified five anti-Delta toxin antibody-positive cases (Supplementary Table 2). Thus, 7 of 12 (58%) patients with severe NEC showed serological evidence of Delta toxin exposure, whereas Delta toxin was not detected in any infant without NEC by faecal isolate PCR or serology (Fig. 1b–d).

Clinically, Delta toxin+ infants showed more prominent systemic inflammation, coagulopathy and brain injury, compared with Delta toxin− cases characterized by elevated C-reactive protein and D-dimer, decreased monocytes, platelets and haemoglobin (Fig. 1e). Importantly, brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in these patients indicated more severe injury, including lateral ventricular enlargement (Fig. 1f,g).

To assess Delta toxin pathogenicity, we selected representative clinical isolates, the Delta toxin+ strain C. perfringens clinical-03 and the Delta toxin− strain C. perfringens clinical-01 for genomic analysis and animal infection studies (Extended Data Fig. 1e,f). Phylogenetic analysis revealed that the clinical-03 strain clustered with NCTC3181 (a known Delta toxin+ type C strain24), whereas clinical-01 clustered with CVCC1160 (a Delta toxin− type C strain), with the two clinical isolates sharing around 96% DNA identity (Extended Data Fig. 1h). Neonatal mice orally gavaged with these strains and formula for 4 days developed severe NEC-like intestinal pathology only after Delta toxin+ infection, including disrupted intestinal architecture, villus and crypt damage, inflammatory infiltration and mucosal fibrin clots, accompanied by increased mortality (Fig. 1h–k). During infection with the clinical-03 strain, Delta toxin was detected predominantly in the intestine, with lower levels in the kidney and lung, but was undetectable in the brain based on western blotting and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) (Extended Data Fig. 1i,j). Nevertheless, infected mice developed severe brain abnormalities, including lateral ventricular enlargement and ependymal cell damage (Fig. 1l,m). Reference strain comparison using NCTC3181 and CVCC1160 (around 99% identity) confirmed that Delta toxin+ infection caused more severe intestinal, brain and lung injury (Extended Data Fig. 1k–m). Finally, culture supernatants from Delta toxin+ strains induced cytotoxicity in HeLa cells, HT29 cells and intestinal organoids, which was neutralized by a Delta-toxin-specific polyclonal antibody (Extended Data Fig. 1n–p). These results support Delta toxin as a key secreted virulence factor responsible for cell damage.

Owing to technical challenges in performing some assays in postnatal day 7 (P7) neonatal mice, we included 4-week-old weanling mice for subsequent experiments. Intraperitoneal (i.p.) administration of purified Delta toxin caused dose-dependent lethality in both age groups, with neonates showing greater sensitivity (Fig. 2a,b and Extended Data Fig. 2a). We next quantified Delta toxin distribution in neonatal mice 4 h after i.p. injection of 10 μg toxin. Consistent with the infection model, toxin levels were highest in the intestine (around 61 μg per g), followed by kidney (approximately 15 μg per g) and lung (about 7 μg per g), with barely detectable toxin in the brain (Extended Data Fig. 2b). These levels were comparable to those in infected pups, supporting the physiological relevance of the toxin injection model.

We next challenged P7 neonates and 4-week-old mice using either an acute high-dose model to mimic fulminant infection, analysed at 4 h, or a single low-dose challenge with prolonged follow-up to approximate residual toxin activity after antibiotic clearance, analysed at days 5–7. Across both age groups and dosing strategies, Delta toxin induced a severe NEC-like multiorgan pathology. Intestines showed mucosal disruption, villus destruction, epithelial detachment and oedema (Fig. 2c,d and Extended Data Fig. 2c,d). Concurrently, the brain exhibited haemorrhage, ependymal disorganization, periventricular oedema, ventricular dilation (Fig. 2e,f and Extended Data Fig. 2e,f), neuronal shrinkage and degeneration, and inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 2g,h and Extended Data Fig. 2g–i). We also observed intravascular coagulation within the cerebral microvasculature in the hippocampal region (Extended Data Fig. 2j,k). In 4-week-old mice, brain MRI further confirmed that Delta toxin caused brain injury and ventricle dilation (Fig. 2i,j), mirroring the neuropathology observed in patients with NEC. In vivo photoacoustic imaging of brains showed a significant reduction in cerebral blood flow, vascular density and branching index, indicating Delta-toxin-associated hypoxic–ischaemic injury and cognitive impairment (Fig. 2k,l).

Beyond the brain, Delta toxin induced widespread multiorgan pathology, with particularly severe injury observed in the kidney and lung. These tissues exhibited distinct signs of coagulopathy, haemorrhage and inflammatory cell infiltration, notably affecting the renal medulla, glomeruli and pulmonary parenchyma in both age groups (Extended Data Fig. 2l–o). Moreover, toxin-treated mice showed dilated ear vessels and increased Evans blue extravasation in the peritoneal lavage fluid, indicating vascular damage25 (Extended Data Fig. 3a,b). Delta toxin further induced systemic coagulation abnormalities, including elevated red blood cell counts, reduced platelets and altered coagulation parameters, including prolonged prothrombin time and activated partial thromboplastin time,  and reduced fibrinogen (Extended Data Fig. 3c–h). These findings recapitulate the severe disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) observed in Delta-toxin-positive patients with NEC (Fig. 1e). Inflammasome activation is known to drive coagulation through macrophage-mediated release of tissue factor26. Consistently, Delta toxin promoted tissue factor release from macrophages and Caco2 intestinal epithelial cells (Extended Data Fig. 3i), suggesting that both cell types contribute to toxin-induced coagulopathy.

To define the specific contribution of Delta toxin to neurological injury, we evaluated the effects of Beta toxin, which has been implicated in type C C. perfringens enteritis27. Beta toxin exhibited selective cytotoxicity for endothelial HUVECs but notably spared HT-29 intestinal epithelial cells and organoids (Extended Data Fig. 4a–c), aligning with a previous report that Beta toxin preferentially targets CD31+ vascular endothelial cells28. In vivo, it induced rapid, dose-dependent mortality (Extended Data Fig. 4d), but did not induce brain pathology at any dose tested (Extended Data Fig. 4e). These data demonstrate the specific pathogenic role of Delta toxin as a driver of NEC-like multiorgan pathology.

We next examined whether Delta-toxin-induced brain injury is associated with blood–brain barrier (BBB) dysfunction and neuroinflammation. In 4-week-old mice, Evans blue extravasation revealed dose-dependent BBB leakage after Delta toxin challenge, with lipopolysaccharide (LPS)29 as a positive control (Fig. 2m–o). Acute high-dose Delta toxin (100 μg, 8 h) caused more-severe BBB disruption than either prolonged low-dose Delta toxin (20 μg, analysed at day 7) or acute LPS (1 mg, 8 h), indicating that Delta toxin rapidly disrupts BBB integrity. ZO-1 immunofluorescence in the hippocampus confirmed structural damage with localized disorganization and reduced signal intensity (Fig. 2p,q).

BBB disruption is often accompanied by neuroinflammation30,31 and intestinal CD4+ T cells migrate across the BBB into the brain, where they accumulate with microglia to trigger neuroinflammation32,33. We next characterized brain immune cell populations after exposure to Delta toxin. In both age groups, acute high-dose Delta toxin triggered an increase in total CD45+ cells (Extended Data Fig. 5a). This increase was largely attributable to a marked expansion of the microglial population, predominantly reflecting microglial expansion (Extended Data Fig. 5b,c), confirmed by increased IBA1+ immunostaining (Extended Data Fig. 5d–g). Three-dimensional analysis revealed a shift of microglia from ramified to amoeboid morphology, characterized by shorter branch lengths and attenuated processes (Extended Data Fig. 5d–g). GFAP+ astrocytes also increased, with increased branching and cell volume (Extended Data Fig. 5h–k), indicating pro-inflammatory activation34. CD4+ and CD8+ T cells remained at similar levels at this timepoint (Extended Data Fig. 5l). In neonates after low-dose prolonged challenge, microglial expansion and activation (Extended Data Fig. 5m–r) were accompanied by increased CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (Extended Data Fig. 5s,t), consistent with T cell recruitment 4 days after NEC induction32.

As patients with NEC exhibit a pronounced systemic and central nervous system inflammatory response1, we analysed cytokine profiles in the serum and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) of patients with NEC positive for C. perfringens Delta toxin. The results revealed abnormally high cytokine levels, including IL-6, IL-10, TNF and IL-1β (Fig. 3a,b). To examine whether Delta toxin contributes to this inflammatory activation, we administered Delta toxin to 4-week-old mice through i.p. injection and observed a significant increase in IL-1β levels in both the serum and peritoneal fluid (Fig. 3c), along with a dose-dependent upregulation of Il1b, Il18, Tnf and Nlrp3 mRNA levels in peritoneal lavage cells (Fig. 3d). IL-1β and IL-18 are major effector cytokines released after NLRP3 inflammasome activation35, and activated macrophages represent the principal cellular source of mature IL-1β36. The specific NLRP3 inhibitor MCC950 suppressed Delta-toxin-induced Il1b and Il18 in peritoneal macrophages (Fig. 3e), improved survival rates (Fig. 3f and Extended Data Fig. 6a) and mitigated Delta-toxin-induced BBB disruption, as evidenced by reduced Evans blue extravasation (Fig. 3g,h). These findings suggest that Delta toxin activates the NLRP3 inflammasome to drive local and systemic inflammation as well as BBB damage.

We next examined whether macrophages, well-established cells capable of NLRP3 inflammasome activation, are preferentially targeted by Delta toxin. In both neonates and 4-week-old mice, high-dose Delta toxin caused rapid, near-complete depletion of peritoneal macrophages within 1 h, and low-dose exposure resulted in reduced peritoneal macrophage numbers persisting for 5–7 days (Fig. 3i,j and Extended Data Fig. 6b–d). By contrast, neutrophils decreased at 1 h, accumulated by 4 h and persisted to days 5–7, suggesting a subsequent neutrophil-dominated inflammatory response (Fig. 3i,j and Extended Data Fig. 6b–d). In the neonatal intestine, Delta toxin selectively depleted macrophages, without significantly altering T cells, B cells, neutrophils or dendritic cells (Fig. 3k,l and Extended Data Fig. 6e,f). Consistently, neonatal mice infected with the NCTC3181 (Delta toxin+) strain showed both a reduced percentage and a reduced absolute number of intestinal macrophages at 4 days after infection, whereas infection with the CVCC1160 strain (Delta toxin−) resulted in no detectable change (Fig. 3m). To assess the inflammatory potency relative to other PFTs, we exposed primary peritoneal macrophages to Staphylococcus aureus α-haemolysin (α-Hla), C. perfringens Delta toxin, Beta toxin, Pfo or LPS. Macrophages were resistant to α-Hla, Beta toxin and Pfo, but exceptionally sensitive to Delta toxin (Fig. 3n). Delta toxin potently induced inflammatory cytokine mRNA at 1–5 ng ml−1, whereas Pfo and α-Hla required around 5,000 ng ml−1 and 50,000 ng ml−1, respectively; Beta toxin had a minimal effect even at high concentrations. Notably, Delta toxin was effective at approximately 100-fold lower concentrations than LPS (Fig. 3o). Consistently, IL-1β was detected in primary peritoneal macrophage culture supernatants after 10 ng ml−1 Delta toxin treatment (Fig. 3p).

To define the mechanism, we examined key inflammatory cell-death pathways in immortalized bone-marrow-derived macrophages (iBMDMs). Delta toxin induced a time-dependent increase in pyroptosis signatures, including accumulation of pro-caspase-1, cleaved caspase-1 and gasdermin D (Fig. 3q). By contrast, canonical apoptosis markers, BAX induction and caspase-3 cleavage37 were not detectably altered under the same conditions (Fig. 3r), supporting pyroptosis as the dominant cell-death program induced by Delta toxin in iBMDMs.

We next tested whether K+ efflux38, a conserved trigger for NLRP3 inflammasome activation after membrane permeabilization, mediates Delta-toxin-induced inflammation. Extracellular KCl, used to prevent intracellular K+ depletion, abrogated Delta-toxin-induced NLRP3 upregulation and caspase-1 cleavage (Extended Data Fig. 6g), identifying K+ efflux as a major upstream signal. Moreover, Delta toxin rapidly induced ERK phosphorylation (Fig. 3s), consistent with pore-forming toxin-induced calcium influx, which subsequently activates MAPK–ERK signalling39. In vivo, bone marrow cells from Delta-toxin-treated mice showed increased GSDMD cleavage (Fig. 3t), confirming pyroptotic signalling. Collectively, these findings reveal that Delta toxin drives potent inflammation through the NLRP3–Caspase-1–GSDMD pathway and activates MAPK–ERK signalling.

We next examined whether Delta toxin engages a specific host receptor to mediate cell binding and cytotoxicity. We first evaluated the sensitivity of various cell lines. HeLa and HT29 cells were found to be highly susceptible, indicating that they abundantly express receptor (Extended Data Fig. 7a). We therefore performed a genome-wide CRISPR–Cas9 screen in HeLa cells using the GeCKO v2 library40 against Delta toxin (0.01 μg ml−1) (Fig. 4a). Next-generation sequencing of surviving cells revealed Cd9 as the top hit (Fig. 4b). CD9 expression was confirmed across relevant cell lines, and higher CD9 expression was associated with increased sensitivity to Delta toxin (Fig. 4c and Extended Data Fig. 7a). Although Delta toxin has been reported to bind to GM221, genetic disruption (UGCG knockout (KO)) or pharmacological inhibition (EtDO-P4 treatment) of GlcCer-derived glycosphingolipids (including most gangliosides such as GM2) did not change cellular sensitivity (Extended Data Fig. 7b,c). By contrast, CD9-KO rendered HeLa cells resistant to Delta toxin with toxicity reduced by around 40,000-fold (Fig. 4d). Moreover, CD9-overexpressing (CD9-GFP-oe) A549 cells showed markedly increased sensitivity (Fig. 4e). KO of other candidate genes (LRP6 and CLEC12B) did not alter toxin sensitivity (Fig. 4b and Extended Data Fig. 7d). GST-tagged Delta toxin can bind to wild-type (WT) HeLa cells and CD9-GFP-oe A549 cells, but not to CD9-KO cells and A549 cells (Fig. 4f–h). Biolayer interferometry (BLI) assays confirmed specific binding between GST–CD9 and Delta toxin, but not Beta toxin (Fig. 4i). The measured interaction (KD) fell in the micromolar-range binding affinity, indicative of specific, low-affinity binding (Extended Data Fig. 7e,f). It is possible that a proper membrane environment is required for the high-affinity binding of Delta toxin on cell surfaces. Together, these data identify CD9 as a receptor for Delta toxin.

Human CD9 is a member of the tetraspanin family, containing four transmembrane domains, short cytoplasmic N and C protein termini, a short extracellular domain (EC1) and a long EC241 (Fig. 4j). To map the functional binding determinant, we first performed CD9 mutagenesis analyses. Disrupting EC1 by inserting a 3×Flag tag at three independent positions did not impair GST–Delta toxin binding when EC1 mutants were re-expressed in CD9-KO cells (Extended Data Fig. 8a), indicating that EC1 is dispensable for toxin targeting.

We next conducted domain-swap experiments with human CD82, which is structurally similarity to CD942 (Fig. 4j). Only CD9 mutants containing the EC2 domain retained the ability to bind to Delta toxin, whereas mutants with the EC2 domain replaced by that of CD82 did not interact with the Delta toxin (Fig. 4k and Extended Data Fig. 8b). To further confirm the functional requirement of the EC2 domain, we used a function-blocking antibody specifically targeting the CD9 EC2 domain. Immunostaining demonstrated exclusive binding to cells overexpressing WT CD9, but not to CD9 chimeras in which the EC2 domain was substituted with that of CD82 (Fig. 4l). This antibody protected HeLa cells from Delta-toxin-induced cytotoxicity (Fig. 4m).

We next evaluated species selectivity of the Delta toxin–CD9 interaction. CD9-KO cells reconstituted with CD9 homologues from multiple vertebrate species, including Mus musculus, Bos taurus, Sus scrofa, Danio rerio and Gallus domesticus, supported toxin binding, whereas reconstitution with Drosophila or Caenorhabditis elegans homologues did not (Extended Data Fig. 8c), suggesting that toxin binding depends on a conserved region across mammals. Sequence alignment revealed that EC2 is broadly conserved among toxin-binding species but diverges in Drosophila and C. elegans (Extended Data Fig. 8d). These results collectively indicate that CD9 is a specific cell surface receptor for Delta toxin, with the EC2 domain mediating binding and cytotoxicity. Single-cell RNA-sequencing analysis of intestinal cells43 showed that CD9 is highly expressed in monocytes and intestinal epithelial cells (Extended Data Fig. 8e). Flow cytometry analysis confirmed higher CD9 expression in macrophages than in other major intestinal immune cell populations (Extended Data Fig. 8f). These results are consistent with the strong cytotoxicity of Delta toxin toward macrophages and HT29 cells that we observed. CD9 expression patterns therefore dictate the cellular tropism of the Delta toxin.

To determine whether CD9 is functionally required in vivo, we generated Cd9f/f mice and crossed them with EllaiCre (systemic Cd9-KO), Lyz2cre (myeloid) or Vil1iCre (intestinal epithelial) mice for lineage-specific deletion. Western blotting confirmed loss of CD9 protein in BMDMs from Cd9-KO mice (Extended Data Fig. 9a). After lethal Delta toxin challenge, all WT mice died within 20 h, whereas Cd9-KO mice were completely protected, maintained body weight and activity, and showed reduced disease severity (Fig. 5a and Extended Data Fig. 9b). Myeloid-specific Cd9 deletion also conferred strong protection, while intestinal epithelial-specific deletion delayed mortality, indicating partial protection (Fig. 5a).

Consistent with improved survival, Cd9-KO mice showed reduced intestinal, lung and kidney pathology (Fig. 5b,c), and their intestines remained morphologically normal after toxin challenge (Fig. 5d). Intestinal organoids derived from Cd9-KO mice demonstrated resistance to the Delta-toxin-induced cytotoxicity (Extended Data Fig. 9c). BBB integrity and lung haemorrhage were preserved in Cd9-KO mice (Cd9-KO, Cd9f/fLyz2cre and Cd9f/fVil1iCre mice) (Fig. 5e,f). Ear engorgement and DIC-like phenotypes, including thrombocytopaenia and hypofibrinogenaemia, were markedly attenuated in Cd9-KO mice (Extended Data Fig. 9d,e). Notably, Delta-toxin-induced macrophage depletion and subsequent neutrophilic accumulation were rescued in Cd9-KO and Cd9f/fLyz2cre mice but not in Cd9f/fVil1iCre mice (Fig. 5g,h and Extended Data Fig. 9f), correlating with reduced serum IL-1β levels and Il1b mRNA expression in BMDMs (Fig. 5i and Extended Data Fig. 9g), suggesting an overall dampening of the inflammatory response. Cd9f/fVil1iCre mice also displayed partially reduced IL-1β levels, consistent with its partial survival protection after challenge with Delta toxin (Fig. 5a).

We next validated the requirement for CD9 during Delta toxin+ C. perfringens infection in neonatal mice. The survival outcomes and multiorgan damage phenotypes of mice were consistent with those observed after Delta toxin challenge (Extended Data Fig. 9h–j). These results demonstrate that CD9 is required for Delta toxin toxicity in vivo by facilitating macrophage targeting, activating inflammatory cascades and driving lethal multiorgan injury.

As Delta toxin triggers systemic pathology, we examined whether toxin neutralization could be used as a therapeutic strategy. We generated a neutralizing monoclonal antibody against Delta toxin by immunizing mice with a non-toxic Delta toxin mutant (V17E/T18E), validated by a cell viability assay (Extended Data Fig. 10a). The selected monoclonal antibody bound to Delta toxin with high affinity (Extended Data Fig. 10b), protected RAW264.7 cells from toxin-induced cytotoxicity (Extended Data Fig. 10c,d) and suppressed Delta-toxin-induced Il1b and Ccl2 expression (Extended Data Fig. 10e).

We next investigated the therapeutic efficacy of the monoclonal antibody in C. perfringens-induced NEC. Pups received anti-Delta toxin monoclonal antibody or IgG before and during gavage with Delta toxin+ C. perfringens from P7 to P11 (Fig. 5j). Anti-Delta toxin monoclonal antibody treatment improved survival after infection with the clinical-03 and NCTC3181 strains (Fig. 5k) preserved intestinal crypt-villus architecture with reduced inflammatory infiltration (Fig. 5l,m and Extended Data Fig. 10f), attenuated lateral ventricle and ependymal disruption in the brain (Fig. 5n,o and Extended Data Fig. 10g) and reduced lung injury, including septal thickening and haemorrhage (Extended Data Fig. 10h,i). These findings suggest that Delta toxin is a virulence factor driving C. perfringens-induced NEC, and therapeutic neutralization of this toxin offers a promising strategy for preventing lethal pathology.

NEC was considered to be a multifactorial syndrome driven by prematurity and environmental stress. Our findings suggest that a subset of NEC is a disorder mediated by a specific bacterial toxin. By identifying C. perfringens Delta toxin as a key virulence factor, we define a molecular mechanism that is sufficient to drive intestinal necrosis and remote cerebral injury. Notably, this toxin-based mouse model reproduces major clinical symptoms without exogenous stressors, such as hypoxia or hypothermia, that are typically required in current commonly used models. This result suggests that rather than acting solely as an opportunistic colonizer of a compromised gut, C. perfringens can function as a primary initiator of barrier failure and a key driver for the systemic progression of NEC. This mechanism is consistent with the enrichment of Delta-toxin-positive strains in fatal clinical cases, suggesting a direct link between bacterial virulence and the systemic progression of fulminant NEC. The prevalence of Delta-toxin-positive infection is probably underestimated. Antibiotic treatment, common in NEC, can rapidly reduce faecal bacterial loads, thereby limiting stool culture-based detection. Serologic detection of Delta-toxin-specific antibodies provides a complementary strategy to unmask previous infection with Delta-toxin-producing C. perfringens in patients with NEC.

NEC-associated brain injury is linked to systemic and central nervous system inflammation. CD4+ T cells were detectable in the developing brain in experimental NEC and human specimens, contributing to neuroinflammation and white-matter injury. This adaptive immune component is typically evaluated at later stages of experimental NEC (around 4 days after NEC induction32). Our findings add a toxin-driven dimension by identifying a microbial toxin that elicits rapid innate immune activation with early brain injury. A notable feature of Delta toxin exposure is the speed and magnitude of the response: macrophages were nearly depleted within around 1 h after challenge, followed by systemic inflammation, BBB disruption and rapid microglial activation. At examined timepoints, brain immune profiling revealed increased CD45+ immune cells dominated by microglial changes, whereas T cell proportions were not detectably altered on the basis of flow cytometry. At day 5 in the low-dose neonates, both CD4+ and CD8+ T cell numbers were increased, aligning with previous NEC models. These data suggest that Delta-toxin-associated brain injury is initially dominated by innate immune mechanisms, and likely later shapes or amplifies adaptive immune contributions. Notably, Delta toxin was undetectable within the brain during bacterial infection and toxin challenge, indicating that brain injury is probably driven by systemic inflammation rather than direct brain toxin accumulation. Consistent with this, the NLRP3 inhibitor MCC950 blocked peripheral IL-1β maturation, preserved BBB tight-junction integrity and prevented remote brain pathology without directly altering the toxin itself.

If innate activation represents the initial wave of injury, the key question is how inflammation is amplified so rapidly. PFTs, including Pfo, have been reported to activate NLRP344. Across the toxins tested, Delta toxin induced the strongest cytokine response, notably at sublytic doses at which other PFTs elicited minimal induction. Consistent with this severity, Delta toxin produced marked BBB disruption in vivo—exceeding that observed with LPS, a benchmark for inflammation-associated BBB injury29. This inflammatory gain is mechanistically explained by the toxin’s preferential targeting of macrophages, where it engages inflammasome signalling to trigger GSDMD-dependent pyroptosis. This establishes macrophages as highly sensitive sensors that convert toxin exposure into a systemic cytokine storm. Consistent with this, a study recently reported that the PFT aerolysin preferentially depletes gut-resident macrophages, promoting intestinal inflammation and epithelial barrier vulnerability45.

We identified the tetraspanin CD9 as a host protein receptor required for Delta toxin. This aligns with a growing paradigm in which host membrane proteins act as key receptors for PFTs and define their tropism, for example, S. aureus α-Hla with ADAM1046, C. perfringens Beta-toxin with CD3128, and Enterococcus Epx2 with MHC class I47. Previous studies showed that Delta toxin binds to GM2 gangliosides in cell-free liposome systems21. However, in mammalian cell assays, genetic disruption of glycosphingolipid synthesis by UGCG KO or pharmacological inhibition with EtDO-P4 did not detectably alter Delta toxin sensitivity. These results suggest that GlcCer-derived glycosphingolipids are not essential for Delta toxin cytotoxicity on the plasma membrane. Other membrane components such as cholesterol-enriched domains may contribute to pore assembly and membrane insertion48. Genetic ablation of Cd9 markedly attenuated Delta-toxin-associated intestinal injury, BBB leakage and neuroinflammation, placing the Delta toxin–CD9 axis as a potential therapeutic target. CD9 also raises an interesting link to extracellular vesicle biology49. CD9 is a common tetraspanin marker enriched on extracellular vesicles, implicated as carriers of inflammatory signals in systemic infection and organ injury. Given that extracellular vesicles act as protective decoys that trap PFTs50, and that Delta toxin specifically targets extracellular-vesicle-associated CD9, it is interesting to investigate whether the toxin exploits extracellular vesicles to facilitate systemic dissemination.

To overcome technical limitations in neonatal models, we used 4-week-old mice alongside P7 neonates. The small size of P7 pups limited in vivo MRI and photoacoustic imaging, retro-orbital dye injection for BBB assessment and bone marrow sampling. Haematological profiling and peritoneal cytokine analysis were assessed in 4-week-old mice, as severe coagulopathy and limited fluid volume in toxin-exposed neonates constrained sample collection. For assays performed in parallel, findings were consistent across both age groups. Additional limitations include the need for larger multicentre cohorts to define the prevalence of Delta-toxin-positive C. perfringens in NEC, and the lack of an isogenic Delta-toxin-deletion strain to further confirm Delta-toxin-dependent pathology.

Toxin-mediated injury can be initiated at a low bacterial burden and persist after bacterial clearance. Our results suggest the Delta toxin axis is a tractable precision medicine target. We developed a neutralizing monoclonal antibody that effectively blocks toxin binding, and confers protection against multi-organ damage including intestinal necrosis and brain injury. Collectively, this study suggests a subset of NEC as a toxin-driven disorder and establishes a foundation for mechanism-based therapies designed to protect the developing gut and brain.

Serum titres were monitored by ELISA. Splenocytes from the highest-titre mouse were fused with SP2/0 myeloma cells. The fused cells were seeded into plates and selected in RPMI 1640-HAT medium (Sigma-Aldrich, H0262) supplemented with 10%FBS, 1% penicillin–streptomycin and gentamicin for 2 weeks. Stable clones were characterized by ELISA and isotyped, with one lead clone chosen for small-scale antibody purification through protein A affinity chromatography, followed by large-scale production in ascitic fluid, yielding 1 mg of purified antibody.
