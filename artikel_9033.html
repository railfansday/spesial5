inside the graphic design history of the world’s underground networks

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

from hector guimard’s art nouveau entrances in paris to the custom typefaces developed for london’s underground, transportation networks have shaped how cities communicate with their inhabitants.

Subway maps, station signs, typography, and tickets are among the most familiar examples of graphic design, yet their visual histories are rarely considered outside the context of public transportation. In Paris, one of the most recognizable examples stands above the entrance to the Métro, Hector Guimard’s Art Nouveau lettering, framed by sinuous green cast-iron structures, glass canopies, and ornamental lamps. Designed around 1900 for the new underground railway of the French capital, the entrances brought a decorative quality to public infrastructure, making the word Métropolitain part of a composition that has since become synonymous with Paris. Guimard considered the lettering, ironwork, lighting, and surrounding structures as parts of the same composition, giving the newly established railway a recognizable presence above street level. More than a century later, the surviving entrances remain a reminder of a time when even the practical business of getting people underground offered an opportunity for design experimentation.

As subway networks expanded throughout the twentieth century, this attention to their appearance developed into a more systematic approach to graphic design. Cities needed maps that simplified complex routes, signs that passengers could read while moving through crowded stations, and typefaces designed specifically for public transportation. From London’s Johnston Sans to Adrian Frutiger’s lettering for the Paris Métro, these commissions produced graphic identities that extended beyond their original purpose, while tickets, travel cards, and other printed objects became part of the visual culture of urban life.

Some of the most recognizable typography in graphic design was first developed for public transportation. Designed to make station names, directions, and route information easier to read, these typefaces gave underground networks a consistent look that passengers encountered throughout their daily journeys. In London, typographer Edward Johnston was commissioned by Frank Pick in 1913 to develop a standardized lettering system for the Underground. Introduced in 1916, Johnston Sans replaced the inconsistent typography previously used across the network with a distinctive sans-serif alphabet. Its balanced proportions and characteristic circular letterforms established a recognizable identity across station signs, maps, posters, and printed materials. The typeface, later developed into New Johnston, remains an essential part of London’s transport identity.

Paris followed a different path. In 1973, the Régie Autonome des Transports Parisiens (RATP) commissioned Swiss typographer Adrian Frutiger to create a standardized alphabet for the Métro. Based on his Univers typeface, the Métro lettering used relatively narrow uppercase characters to accommodate station names and directional information across different sign formats. The commission addressed an increasingly visible problem: the lack of consistency between the various lettering styles used throughout the network. The evolution continued in 1996, when French type designer Jean-François Porchez developed Parisine. Designed to unify the visual identity of different RATP transportation services, the typeface introduced a lowercase-based system adapted to multiple applications, from station signage to maps and digital displays. Its extended family subsequently became an important part of the organization’s visual identity.

Another interesting example comes from the Paris regional express network. In 1976, designer Roger Tallon commissioned Albert Boton to develop Formula One, an exclusive typeface for the newly established RER. With its rounded forms, the alphabet helped give the regional railway a distinct identity.

In Milan, the relationship between transportation design and architecture became particularly explicit with the opening of the first underground line of the city in 1964. Architects Franco Albini and Franca Helg worked with graphic designer Bob Noorda to develop a coordinated system for the new Metro stations. The team considered signage from the beginning, integrating it into the architecture. Continuous bands of color, station names, directional information, and a clear hierarchy of signs created a consistent experience throughout the network. The graphic system was conceived in relation to the dimensions, materials, and spatial organization of the stations. The project received the Compasso d’Oro in 1964, recognizing the collaboration between architecture and graphic design, and remains an important reference in the development of environmental graphic design, demonstrating how information can become an integral part of the built environment.

New York’s subway system offers another influential example. In the late 1960s, the New York City Transit Authority commissioned Unimark International to establish a standardized graphic identity for its network. Working with Massimo Vignelli, Bob Noorda, and other members of the design team, the studio developed a comprehensive system of typography, arrows, colors, and station signs. Helvetica provided the typographic foundation, while a clear hierarchy of information helped replace the inconsistent visual language that had accumulated across the network. The project reflected the modernist ambition to make public information systematic and legible. Its influence extended to Vignelli’s celebrated 1972 subway map, which approached the transportation network as an abstract diagram rather than a geographically accurate representation.

Designing a subway map is quite challenging, as it must communicate a complicated network of routes, connections, and stations at a glance. In doing so, it often sacrifices geographical precision for clarity. Perhaps the most influential example is Harry Beck’s London Underground map, introduced in 1933. Working from the visual logic of electrical circuit diagrams, the engineering draughtsman developed a representation based on horizontal, vertical, and 45-degree lines. The actual geographical positions of stations became secondary to the relationships between routes and connections.

Beck’s approach fundamentally changed how passengers could understand the underground. Central London received greater visual space, distances between stations became more uniform, and individual lines were distinguished through color. The diagram simplified the network without changing the physical infrastructure it represented.

The idea of representing a city through an abstract visual system also found an influential expression in New York. Unimark International’s 1972 map, designed by Vignelli with contributions from Joan Charysyn and Bob Noorda, replaced the familiar geography of the city with a geometric arrangement of colored routes and station points. Waterways and landmasses became simplified background elements, while the transportation network took precedence. Initially criticized by passengers who found the map difficult to relate to the actual city above ground, Vignelli’s design was replaced in 1979. Nevertheless, it has since become one of the most discussed examples of modernist information design.

Beyond architecture, typography, and maps, public transportation has its own history of everyday graphic design, found in objects such as paper tickets, cardboard passes, metal tokens, magnetic cards, and printed timetables. Designed to be used only once or for a limited period, tickets have to fit a surprising amount of information into a small space, from fares and dates to route details, serial numbers, and security markings. Their colors, typefaces, and layouts also vary from one city and transport authority to another, reflecting the graphic style of the system that produces them.

The materials and formats have changed alongside the technology of public transportation. Paper tickets and cardboard passes were followed by magnetic-stripe tickets and plastic travel cards before contactless systems began to replace many of these printed formats. Each change altered not only how passengers paid for their journeys but also what a ticket looked like and how they used it.

Despite their practical purpose, these objects often outlast the journeys for which they were issued. People keep tickets as souvenirs, collect them from different cities, or find them tucked away in books and drawers years later. A discarded travel pass can bring back a particular trip, while a collection of tickets offers a record of changing graphic styles, printing methods, and transport systems. Unlike station signs and maps, which are designed to remain in place and be seen by thousands of passengers, tickets are made to be carried, handled, and eventually thrown away. Their short lifespan is part of what makes them interesting: they bring graphic design into the hands of almost every passenger, even if only for the duration of a journey.

Today, much of the information passengers once found on printed maps, station signs, and timetables is accessed through smartphone apps, digital displays, QR codes, and contactless payment systems. These tools make it easier to check service updates, plan routes, and respond to delays, providing information that changes in real time. But navigating a network on a phone is different from reading a map on a station wall. A printed subway map shows the system as a whole, making it possible to see how different lines connect and where they lead, even when those places are not part of the journey.

Apps tend to focus on a particular trip, showing passengers how to get from one point to another, which train to take, and when to change. The information is more tailored, but the wider picture can be harder to grasp. Physical maps and signs therefore still have a role beyond providing directions. They make the transport network visible within the station and give passengers a common point of reference, whether or not they are carrying a smartphone. As more services move onto digital platforms, the question is how these systems can work alongside the maps, signs, and other graphic elements that have helped people navigate cities for generations.
