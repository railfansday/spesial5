Since 2020, the Canon Has Grown Bigger Than Ever Before. Will Museums and the Market Continue to Listen?

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Art history does not divide itself easily into decades, let alone into centuries or even eras, and that makes the 2020s so far a pretty unusual period of time. Not long into 2020, the world was shut down by the Covid pandemic. What followed from there was more chaos: wars in Ukraine, Gaza, Sudan, and elsewhere; two Trump administrations; culture wars around the world; and economic upheaval that has touched all corners of the world, including the art world.

And yet, not all has been bad. During Art Week NYC, a festival held earlier this month by ARTnews, Art in America, and Artforum, ARTnews convened art historian and curator Joseph L. Underwood along with Art in America deputy editor Emily Watlington to discuss the decade so far. Both agreed that some good has resulted, particularly when it comes to the art historical canon. But it remains unclear whether those changes will leave a permanent mark on the art world. “We’ve recovered a lot of individual figures that have totally deserved [a spotlight], and I’m looking forward to how we can now use that research and rediscovery to tell different stories about art history,” Watlington said.

Underwood noted that this impulse was already underway pre-2020 and pointed to scholarship by historians such as Chika Okeke-Agulu, who published an influential book on Nigerian modernism in 2015. “There have been articles and books about single artists, but there hasn’t been that kind of interconnectivity,” he said. “That’s what was starting, and there is still some of it in art history. But museums and the market have cooled on it.”

That trend, he added, is evident when looking at the past few Venice Biennale editions. The 2022 edition, curated by Cecilia Alemani, contained many female Surrealists; the 2024 edition, organized by Adriano Pedrosa, presented a global view of modernism. “That cooled off in 2026,” he said, referring to Koyo Kouoh’s recently concluded exhibition, which had fewer dead and under-recognized artists.

“It’s certainly been a decade of two steps forward, one step back,” Watlington said. “Change the number of steps!”
