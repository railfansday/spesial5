Beyond the Snowflake Myth

Posted November 6, 2026 | Reviewed by Davia Sills

“Snowflake generation” is a term applied to young people who are quickly overwhelmed or “melted” by commonplace pressures and challenges. The rise in young people’s mental health problems and diagnoses of conditions such as autism and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) are sometimes put in this framework, whereby young people today are less resilient and robust than in previous generations of youth who navigated equally, if not more difficult, environments.

Getting a sound perspective on young people’s well-being is essential to assessing appropriate levels of support: How much do we help them by offering practical, emotional, financial and professional support, and how much does such support hinder their capacity to grow resilience themselves?

Across the past three decades, my views of young people’s challenges and how much support the grown-ups in their lives should offer have changed. At the beginning of this century, I wrote about the split between young people’s realities on the one hand and cultural expectations about maturity on the other. [1] I argued that expecting young people to be adults when they reach the age of legal adulthood was unrealistic and damaging—damaging because parents often felt disappointed or frustrated with what they saw as the delayed development of their children, and young people absorbed this disappointment. I pointed out that the conditions of young people today are thin on intergenerational social capital—a deep sense of belonging and a community on which they can draw for informal, personal support. Instead, they are left to get on with pressured social lives, rife with competition and little individually tailored input from teachers, preachers, neighbors or even relatives.

When I subsequently served as a dean in a Cambridge College, I witnessed new trends whereby young people were increasingly keen to label themselves with “special” needs. These involved a host of adjustments, from extra time for examinations and dedicated teaching tools to demands for modifying the entire syllabus to avoid “triggering” bad feelings. This was a time, too, when diagnoses of disabilities were rising rapidly. With more extensive screening for disabilities and broadening diagnostic criteria, the number of young people identified as dyslexic grew to more than 20 times from the baseline recorded in the 1990s by 2015, while cases of autism increased by 175 percent between 2011 and 2022. [1]

Like many people in educational institutions, I worried that young people were seeking a formal assessment of disability partly to obtain in-group status with a distinctive set and partly to demand additional input from their teachers. There was a risk that instead of taking on the task of learning how to learn, how to communicate and interact, how to accept the variability of their talents, young people were now primed to outsource their challenges, demanding that others make adjustments to spoon-feed and coddle them. As an increasing number of their peers sought more input and adjustments, it was common to feel that being mainstream or neurotypical meant being left out.

The UK government, concerned about the rise in young people’s mental health needs, ordered an investigation of young people’s mental health and appointed Peter Fonagy, clinical psychologist and adviser to NHS England, to lead the research. I anticipated that the report would show that ordinary difficult feelings—anxiety, frustration, stress, pressure, uncertainty—were all too often pathologized as disorders or conditions when the best approach would be to support young people to manage normal ups and downs of human emotions.

The review of young people’s mental health showed something very different and has prompted another shift in my perspective—not because of my previous concern that some mental health and disability diagnoses are too broad, but because the charge of overdiagnosis, while valid, clouds the main issue that there has been a significant rise in mental distress among young people. This is affecting both boys and girls, but from the age of 12, the impact on girls is greater. The close-up view is heartbreaking: many young people struggle with anxiety so powerful that they find school intolerable. Some are too depressed to sleep or eat, and some feel their lives are “not worth living.” [2]

In reflecting on the report, Peter Fonagy said, “I am in awe of their courage. Young people are contending with more powerful forces than I ever faced. They are not fragile. They are outnumbered.”

The new UK review complements the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 2023 report finding that nearly three out of five teen girls experience persistent sadness, double that in boys. [3] Misery and a sense of being weird, disliked, and also very special might be a signature element of adolescence, but today’s adolescence is crushing our children, who inhabit a digital ecosystem that inflicts persistent social comparison and self-doubt, socioeconomic instability and a precarious future, and a fractured support system that incentivizes the search for a special diagnosis because that is the route through which they might begin to get professional support.

I have circled back to my conviction that we need to offer sympathy and understanding and proactive support to young people—in the home, in school and in the community—to meet their core needs for autonomy (confidence in navigating risk), competence (skills to succeed academically and socially), and relatedness (belonging, community and purpose). Only with our support will these challenged generations of youth discover resilience.

2. Fonagy, P. (2026) Independent review into prevalence and support for mental health conditions, ADHD and autism. London: Department of Health and Social Care/UK Government.
