Léa Seydoux Isn’t Trying to Act. She’s Trying to Live

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

The French actress continues her string of emotionally demanding roles in The Unknown and Gentle Monster

Léa Seydoux first stepped into the limelight in Christophe Honoré’s The Beautiful Person, where her role as Junie de Chartres earned her the Trophée Chopard award for rising actors at Cannes. From there, she moved seamlessly between highbrow French cinema and Hollywood, achieving a critical breakthrough with Blue Is the Warmest Color in 2013 and starring alongside Daniel Craig as a Bond girl in Spectre and No Time to Die. Most years, she’s starred in at least two films, and 2026 is no exception: She plays a woman with a man’s soul trapped in her body in Arthur Harari’s psychological fantasy The Unknown, and in Marie Kreutzer’s Gentle Monster, releasing on Netflix on November 18, she’s a pianist who unearths her husband’s hideous crimes. The words thorny and barbed read like understatements for both films, and it’s exactly what interests Seydoux about the stories. Like in life itself, she avoids easy answers, opting instead for exploration.

Seydoux got the chance to explore an especially ambitious idea in The Unknown. It tells the story of David Zimmerman (Niels Schneider), a photographer who wakes up in the body of a woman he sleeps with. Seydoux plays both that woman, Eva, and the man trapped within her body. “I felt the movie was something new to me, to play an impossible idea and make it believable, try to give it a truthful dimension,” Seydoux says. She’s no stranger to high-concept stories, previously starring in Bertrand Bonello’s The Beast and David Cronenberg’s Crimes of the Future. Each time, she imbues these characters with humanity, despite their far-fetched sci-fi dilemmas.

“I like movies that make you think, movies that bring up questions, when there’s a debate around the film,” she says. “What I love the most is to explore human nature, our human condition. This is what cinema allows us to do.”

Seydoux might vehemently love movies, yet acting remains a demanding process for her. She relies on the art form to dig into feelings and emotions. “I don’t feel necessarily very comfortable with acting. It’s not a place where I’m like, ‘I love it,’ but at the same time, I find it a bit painful. It’s more a way to experience things and ideas,” she says.

Gentle Monster provides another knotty premise, this time grounded in reality. It follows Lucy Weiss, a famed experimental pianist who gives up her career to raise her young son with her doting husband, Philip (Laurence Rupp). One day, the police knock on their door, and it’s revealed that Philip is accused of distributing child pornography.

“The subject matter is tough, but the fact that it’s through the woman’s perspective—and not through the victim or perpetrator—it’s through her emotions that the story unfolds [is what interested me],” she says. “Things are not too defined, but it’s also because of the subject matter; it was the only way to talk about it. [Marie] gave me a lot of freedom.”

Born in Paris in 1985, Seydoux spent her childhood wedged among seven siblings and half-siblings. She remembers feeling isolated, practically invisible, as a child. “I was always in the shadows in a way,” she says. “As a kid, I felt transparent. I was never at the center of [my] big family.” This plays a big part in her surprise at her acting career, never thinking of herself as someone deserving of center stage, of fame, of attention. In fact, acting never even crossed her mind until she befriended some actors when she turned 18. “I loved the way they lived their lives,” she says. “I admired their life—the fact that they were free.”

And yet, she rejects the label of “actress.” “I try to be truthful as much as possible,” she says. “I try not to act. This is what I try to be, and I’m more into reaction than action.” She adds, “It’s more reactive than acting.”

For Seydoux, “reacting” and really experiencing life is only possible if she “abandons” herself to her characters. One of the most intense examples of this is Abdellatif Kechiche’s controversial lesbian love story Blue Is the Warmest Color, in which she stars alongside Adèle Exarchopoulos. The film’s schedule stretched from two months to five months, and it has since become notorious for its grueling pace. (The famous sex scene took 10 days alone.) “The greatest and most intense experience I’ve had was with Adèle when we did Blue Is the Warmest Color,” she recalls. “It was crazy, and really the connection between us had to be very, very intense. I think, with Adèle, it’s something that will always be a part of me now. There was an exchange, and it was more than making a film. It was really a life experience.”

One scene from Gentle Monster lingered with me for weeks after I watched it: Lucy’s snotty, tearful confrontation with her husband at their family home as she pleads with him to tell the truth. This close-up of Seydoux not only accentuates her famous blue eyes but also shows her uncanny control of this character’s emotions. It feels, for lack of better words, real. Seydoux partly credits her empathy to being a mother of two children, but this scene goes beyond compassion; it shows how much of herself she gives to the character. Before filming this scene, Kreutzer offered little direction, letting her tap into how the character felt. “I prepare myself, and I have to just let myself go and be in the moment and just open up,” Seydoux says, suggesting that perhaps these emotions otherwise lie dormant within her.

I found this is what Seydoux means by “reaction.” Every role in her varied and extolled filmography functions as an avenue through which she can explore what it means to be human, what it means to feel. Her natural curiosity about the world and its inhabitants existed before she decided to act in her teens and will exist long into the future, even if acting jobs don’t remain on the table. “If it ended tomorrow, I’d be happy,” she says. “I will find a way. Now, I really feel that I’m acting because I still have the opportunity. But I have more things to explore, and I would love to have more experiences.”
