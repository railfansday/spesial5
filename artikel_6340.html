Tiny changes in your pupil size and blinking rate reveal how hard your brain is working

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

An eye tracker in a VR headset can determine how hard people are concentrating by reading how pupil size changes.

A person’s concentration level during a difficult mental task shows up in the eyes. Pupils open wider and blinks come faster, and a VR headset with an eye tracker inside can record both.

A team in England asked whether a virtual reality headset could use those signals to tell when a person with chronic pain is working too hard. The answer was mostly yes for healthy volunteers, and not yet for the patients.

Jordan Tsigarides of the University of East Anglia‘s School of Health Sciences led the study, which was part of his Ph.D., with colleagues at UEA and the University of Southampton.

Virtual reality is already used as a drug-free way to ease pain, he said. His goal is a headset that changes what it shows, based on how a patient is coping.

“Imagine putting on a VR headset that can recognize when you’re becoming mentally tired or overwhelmed and automatically adjust the experience to suit you,” Tsigarides said in an interview with Earth.com.

“On a difficult day, it might simplify an activity or guide you through a calming environment.”

The study tested only whether the headset could read the effort. It didn’t treat anyone’s pain.

Between March and June 2025, 84 adults put on a consumer VR headset with built-in eye tracking and played a memory game.

Half had lived with musculoskeletal pain for at least three months, most often rheumatoid or psoriatic arthritis, osteoarthritis or fibromyalgia.

The other half were healthy university students and staff. They were younger on average, 35 years to the patients’ 58.

Letters appeared one at a time on a gray panel, each for three-quarters of a second. At the easiest level, a player pressed a button when a letter matched the one just before it. At the hardest, the match was four letters back. The whole session lasted under 20 minutes.

Players found it hard, and said so. Their own ratings of effort kept climbing through the top level, even after their scores had stopped getting worse.

Pupils widened as the levels got harder, by about a fiftieth of an inch (half a millimeter) at most, which is less than the thickness of a credit card. Blinks came more often too.

Both changes showed up in people with chronic pain and in people without it, and pain and fatigue scores made no difference to either one.

“What fascinated me was that people’s eyes responded to mental effort in remarkably similar ways, whether or not they lived with chronic pain,” Tsigarides told Earth.com.

A plain average of pupil size told a computer almost nothing. A simple model that used average pupil size alone to sort the easiest level from the hardest guessed right 56 percent of the time in healthy volunteers.

In patients it managed 51 percent, which is barely better than chance. The pattern was in the way the pupil changed second by second, not in its size.

Machine learning models that read the full, second-by-second trace did much better, at least in healthy volunteers. Tested on people they had never seen, the best models picked the easy level from the hard one with 88 percent accuracy.

In the chronic pain group, the same models managed 58 to 65 percent.

Age didn’t explain the gap. When the team matched volunteers and patients by age, or adjusted for it, the patients’ eyes were still harder to read.

The two groups also differed in education, medication and other illnesses, and the authors said a sample of 84 can’t untangle all of that.

The patients were a varied group. Nearly all had two or more long-term conditions, and 45 percent took opioids. A quarter had dry eyes or another eye problem. None of those factors, on its own, predicted whose eyes the models misread.

“Rather than expecting one algorithm to work for everyone, we may need headsets that learn how each individual responds,” he said.

One result was encouraging. People with chronic pain rated their motivation above 90 out of 100 at every level of the game, the same as the healthy group. Motion sickness scores were low in both groups, and pain scores didn’t rise during the session.

Tsigarides holds an honorary chief medical officer role at Revolve Labs Ltd, a company that helped fund his Ph.D. The paper discloses both.

Virtual reality for pain has so far been a fixed experience, Tsigarides said: everyone gets the same woodland walk or the same puzzle, however they feel that day.

His earlier work tested VR therapy in people with fibromyalgia, and he said this study is a step toward a version that adjusts itself.

Everyone was measured once, on one day. The researchers can’t say whether a patient’s eyes behave the same way on a good day and a bad one, and Tsigarides says investigating that question comes next.

“Someone’s ability to concentrate might be very different on a good day compared with a day when their pain or fatigue is particularly severe,” he said.

The team also wants to move from a letter-matching game to the kind of calming scenes a real therapy uses, where changing light levels will complicate the pupil readings.

All of the models in this study were run afterward on a desktop computer rather than live inside the headset. None was tested on an outside group of patients.

Tsigarides told Earth.com he hopes early adaptive systems can be tested within a few years. Whether a headset that reads mental effort and responds to it actually eases anyone’s pain is a trial that hasn’t been run.

The full study was published in the journal Scientific Reports.
