9 of the Most Impressive Undercover Crushers in Climbing Right Now

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Shanjean “SJ” Lee sending on the Liberty Bell massif. (Photo: Mikey Schaefer)

The climbing world may love its celebrities, but they also love their underdogs. Which is why we all seem to collectively lose our minds when an undercover crusher emerges from the undergrowth, sends some famous project, and then once again vaporizes into obscurity. The silent crusher archetype has been around since the beginning of climbing history, but in the days of climbing influencers and social media, they seem rarer than ever. To prove the paradigm isn’t dead, we decided to track down a handful and show them a little love.

The trick with finding undercover crushers is that many of them are, indeed, undercover. I initially put out a request for nominations on Instagram and watched the recs pour in. At that point, I figured writing this story would be easy. But many of the people recommended to me were impossible to find. Others were findable but declined to be mentioned. A few, I learned, were undercover because they had been canceled, and I decided that crafting their PR comeback was very much not my job. So, below you’ll find nine climbers who were recommended by one—if not several—of their friends, are in excellent social standing, and were kind enough to dip a toe into the spotlight for this story.

Here are nine of America’s most impressive undercover crushers.

This Bozeman-based young gun is responsible for establishing the hardest climb in Montana: Fox-Like Creature (5.15a), which has yet to see a repeat. But the 20-year-old says his proudest send to date is World Without End (5.14d). “It’s not my hardest route ever, but I think it took me the most effort, in terms of projecting as well as bolting,” he says. “It was also the first 9a [5.14d] route in Montana, which I’m proud of.” In between FAs, McIntosh works as a routesetter at local climbing gyms and spends his weekends cragging in Refrigerator Canyon. “I think it’s one of the best crags in the country and no one’s caught on yet,” he shares.

A lifelong climber based in Squamish, B.C., the 23-year-old Quinn Mason cut her teeth on the U.S. competition circuit before diverting her attention to outdoor pursuits. Last fall, she sent her hardest sport climb to date, Spirit Quest (5.14d), and this spring sent Squamish lines Tainted Love (5.13d R) and Free Dimensional (5.13d PG-13), both on gear. When she’s not climbing, Mason’s either plowing through her coursework for her filmmaking degree or coaching the local youth climbing team. She also does a bit of brand photography and has two sponsors—which means she might not be undercover for long.

One of the few Americans to send V16, Eli Perry learned to climb at a young age but didn’t get serious until he was 15 (he’s now 21). In that time, he started ticking through the grades at a frenetic pace, sending Pegasus (V15) in Joe’s Valley, Utah, and Grand Illusion (V16) in Little Cottonwood Canyon. “Both of them I spent a lot of sessions on and multiple seasons,” he says. In between sessions, he busses tables at an Italian restaurant, does ceramics, and reads. Perry is currently based in Salt Lake City, which he calls “definitely the best spot to be for climbing in the States.”

In between shifts as an orthopedic surgeon at the VA Medical Center in Reno, Nevada, Shanjean “SJ” Lee, 42, manages to send hard across multiple climbing disciplines. She’s redpointed sport routes up to 5.14, boulders up to V10, and is no stranger to Yosemite big walls: In 2021, she notched a free ascent of Free Rider (5.13a; 3,000ft). Lee is also a developer herself. “I put up a route on Liberty Bell in the Washington Pass area called The Dark Side of Liberty with my partner Mikey Schaefer [in 2020],” she says. The 10-pitch pump-fest clocks in at 5.13+, making it the formation’s hardest route to date. Liberty Bell was her local crag at the time, and the opportunity to add to its legacy was invaluable, she says. “It was really special to add a new testpiece route to a place that had given me so much.”

Tadros “Teddy” Eyob isn’t just a connoisseur of long finger cracks—he’s a master of them. The Squamish-based climber recently sent the Joshua Tree triple—Asteroid Crack (5.12d), Equinox (5.12c), and Acid Crack (5.12d)—a trio of stout finger cracks first freed by John Bachar. “I love lines that have finicky gear and keep you feeling like every piece you place is what’s keeping you from decking,” he says. “Something about the level of focus those lines demand really gets me excited.” Before that, he became the first Black climber to free El Cap (NBD), nabbing a proud, ground-up free ascent of Free Rider in early 2026. Now 31, Eyob has climbed all over the world, but his backyard crags remain his favorite place to climb. “The more I travel the world, the luckier I feel to get to call a place like Squamish home.”

Lani Chapko started climbing 12 years ago when her friends convinced her to take an outdoor adventure course for physical education credit in college. She fell in love with the sport right away, became a guide, and went on to establish big routes around North America, including La Sombra de Muerte, (5.12b; 1,500ft), which she and her partner recently authored in El Portrero Chico, Mexico. Chapko, 32, now lives in Index, WA, which remains her favorite place to climb. “There is always another 4-star route, a new way to get humbled, and something to learn from the climbing there,” she says.

Based in Truckee, California, the 33-year-old Sam Daulton is a modern-day Sierra legend. “I was incredibly psyched to make the first flash ascent of Grand Illusion (5.13c) in January 2026,” he says, referring to the finger crack corner near Lake Tahoe, CA. “Grand Illusion was the hardest climb in the world when Tony Yaniro made the first ascent in 1979.” He also bagged the second gear ascent of Tahoe’s Empath (5.14c/d). Daulton first learned to climb as a toddler and grew up training alongside future phenoms like Michaela Kiersch and Isabelle Faus. “At the time, I was a little punk, more interested in shenanigans than training, but after a hiatus in high school, I got back into it.” Now, Daulton works as a research scientist for Meta, a remote gig that allows him to maximize his time in Yosemite, the Sierra, and the French Alps.

Born and Raised in Durango, Colorado, and now a physics Ph.D. student at UC California, Berkeley, Liam Foster is one of the kindest (and most obsessive) rock climbers you’ll ever meet. His meticulous approach to training has served him well, propelling him to redpoints of Line Above the Sky (D16), the hardest drytooling route in the world at the time of the send; Dures Limites (5.14b) in Céüse, France; and The Arsonist (5.14d), a Connor Herson line on California’s Jailhouse Rock. As for what inspires him? “It doesn’t have to be aesthetic as long as the holds are cool and the movements are cool,” he says. “I think people get snooty about climbs, and only want to climb good climbs. But there are so many good bad climbs. It doesn’t have to be perfect to be worth a weekend out.”

West Virginia-based guidebook author Andrew Leich was on the verge of sending his project, Green Magic in the Hills (5.14c) at Seneca Rocks when a rattlesnake bite almost killed him. While he survived, a slow recovery forced him to start over, slowly building through the grades until he was back to authoring new 5.13s, his previous baseline. Then, in December 2025, he finally sent Green Magic, more than three years after bolting it with a friend and nearly two after his near-death snake encounter. Now 32, Leich works as a part-time climbing guide and is the author of two guidebooks: Cheat Canyon Bouldering and Cheat Canyon Rock.
