What Size Christmas Tree Is Right for Me?

First and foremost, the height of your fake Christmas tree should depend on the height of your ceiling. A good rule of thumb is to keep your tree at least one-and-a-half feet lower than your ceiling, otherwise, it will look crowded. “I like to leave about six to12 inches between the top of the tree and the ceiling, so it feels intentional, not squeezed in,” says Shea McGee (more on the designer’s holiday advice below). Both McGee and Jeff Leatham, the floral designer who helped mastermind Kendall Jenner’s festive holiday house, agree that seven to eight feet hits a sweet spot in most homes. “The goal is balance: A tree that feels full and joyful but still lets the room breathe,” says McGee.

Some people want to avoid the hassle (and extra cost) of buying a fresh tree each year, while others are allergic to real trees or are concerned about the fire safety hazard that comes along with real trees (they can become highly flammable as they dry out). Others (including people with pets) know that a real Christmas tree can be bait for disaster. Fresh or faux is really a matter of personal preference, and anyone who wants a fake Christmas tree should go for it. Leatham points out that in faux trees, the branches don’t drop, the coloring stays vibrant, and there’s often more structure to add extra ornaments.

Is It Better to Get a Pre-Lit Tree Or to Do It Yourself?

Choosing if you want your tree pre-lit is one of the first decisions you’ll make when navigating the vast artificial tree world. Pre-lit options are the preference of holiday decor gurus like Leatham. “Always, always, always go for prelit—it makes the tree look more full and realistic,” he tells us. Our editors like that pre-lit Christmas trees are convenient to set up (opposed to blindly garlanding string lights) and how they help guarantee your lighting scheme will look uniform. That said, drawbacks include potentially difficult repairs. While you can easily unstring removable lights to troubleshoot, built-in LEDs might pose bigger technical issues. On the other hand, stringing your own lights may be a more complex, time-consuming process, but you’ll ultimately have more control over which areas of your tree are spotlighted.

Fluffing your artificial Christmas tree is the key to convincingly faking the real thing. Most of the time, your tree will arrive with all the branches up-turned straight out of the box, and you’ll first want to mount the tree in its stand or collar. Next comes the actual fluffing—as reviewer Pujah Shah mentioned, it’s a smart idea to wear some work gloves to avoid any pricking that might occur while you’re working through the branches. Starting at the bottom, you’ll fan the largest branches down, layer by layer, until you reach the very top (where the branches become much shorter). If your branches have smaller tips built in, we like the center-right-left method of starting toward the base of the tree and positioning the smaller tips first center, next towards the right, and lastly towards the left, until you’ve reached the end. After that, you’ll go branch by branch, noticing that your tree looks full and much more lush with each adjustment. Afterwards, all that’s left to do is make any minor fixes to eliminate bald spots and voila!

What’s the Best Real Christmas Tree to Buy?

Again, that’s completely a matter of personal preference. Trees come in a variety of hues, some with a dark green, some with a blue tint, and others with a bright shade. Some have spiky needles while others are soft to the touch. Trees vary by geographical region and what's native to your area, but some of the most popular options used as Christmas trees across the U.S. include the Fraser Fir, Balsam Fir, Douglas Fir, and Blue Spruce (all of which are available in faux varieties, as well!).

How Does the Lifetime Cost of a Faux Tree Compare to Annually Buying a Real Tree?

While how much your real Christmas tree costs will vary depending on its size as well as where you live in the United States, expect to spend anywhere from $40 to well over $100. In a few years, that running total will start to cost you, but with an artificial tree, you’ll pay the total cost up front. High-quality trees can also last you up to 15 years, making the sometimes extravagant price tags more digestible, especially in the later years when you’re no longer burdened with figuring out the logistics of strapping that netted Douglas Fir onto the car roof.

We tapped the designer and creative force behind one of our favorite interior brands, McGee & Co., on the best ways to make your artificial Christmas tree really pop this season. Read on for her advice on how to make merry with a beautiful, yet intentional holiday set up.

Is There Such a Thing as an Ideal Sized Christmas Tree?

“When you’re choosing a tree, the ideal size really depends on your space. I like to leave about 6–12 inches between the top of the tree and the ceiling, so it feels intentional, not squeezed in. For most standard spaces, a 7-7.5-foot tree hits the sweet spot. If you have tall ceilings, you can absolutely go bigger—9-12 feet fills the volume beautifully. And in smaller rooms, a slimmer silhouette keeps things festive without overwhelming the layout. The goal is balance: A tree that feels full and joyful but still lets the room breathe.”

What are Some of the Design Benefits of Choosing an Artificial Christmas Tree Versus a Real One?

“I love the flexibility an artificial tree gives you. You know exactly what shape and fullness you’re getting, and the branches stay right where you want them—which makes decorating so much easier and more polished. They also hold up all season long, which is great if you like to decorate early. And of course, you can reuse them year after year, which means you can invest in the style you love and bring it back every holiday.”

How Do You Make an Artificial Christmas Tree Look More Realistic?

“Start with a tree that already has a natural, realistic base—good branch texture goes a long way. From there, keep your palette cohesive so the whole tree feels curated instead of busy. I love layering garlands because they add that organic movement you see in real trees. Then finish with meaningful ornaments, ribbon, and a few organic touches—like dried citrus or pinecones. Those add warmth and authenticity, making even a faux tree feel beautifully lifelike.”

“Both options have their place. A pre-lit tree is all about ease—the lights are perfectly placed, and you get that warm glow the minute you plug it in. It’s great if you want a beautiful tree without the extra steps. An unlit tree gives you more creative freedom. You can layer different styles of lights, change the mood from year to year, and really customize the look. It takes a bit more time, but it can feel incredibly intentional. So, if you want quick and polished, go pre-lit. If you love the process of styling every detail, unlit is the way to go.”
