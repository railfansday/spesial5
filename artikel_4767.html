To Heal Your Heart, Purge Your Playlist

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Relationship experts explain why your choice of music can provide a fast-forward fix or replay the pain.

Since I was old enough to record playlists on cassette tapes, I’ve paired music with my intense crushes. Each guy basically had his own song, sometimes an entire soundtrack.

I fell in love with my college boyfriend to the tune of Bruce Springsteen’s “Dancing in the Dark,” while Miles Davis’s Kind of Blue was the anthem that played throughout a 15-year situationship with an elusive artist. Then, there was the whirlwind romance with a former smoke jumper turned ER doctor whose last concert was Emmylou Harris, which reminded me of the then-popular song “Emmylou” by First Aid Kit.

Each time, I became as obsessed with the song as the guy associated with it. My brain hyperfixated on the lyrics, first looking for hopeful clues, and then after the romance ended, sparking memories of sweet moments.

Music is often seen as a source of comfort for those dealing with heartbreak, but it can actually “arouse feelings of romantic longing, particularly searing melody,” says Walter Frisch, PhD, a professor of music at Columbia University. Some composers call such a haunting melody “l’idée fixe” or “fixed idea.”

Once that “fixed idea” gets stuck in your head—in the form of a song played on repeat—it can actually trigger the thoughts that land you in limerence, the involuntarily obsessive rumination on an idealized love interest. It’s fueled by both the hope that the person you like likes you back and the uncertainty of whether they do.

For a long time, I thought I was alone in my lyric-fueled limerence, but as I interviewed experts and other limerents as research for my memoir, When Longing Becomes Your Lover, I learned that it’s incredibly common—and normal—for our brains to connect music with unrequited lovers.

Neuroscience would call this melody intermittent reinforcement. When we are infatuated with someone, we are anticipating connection with that person, which results in our brain’s reward system releasing the “feel good” hormone dopamine. As a result, we are motivated to return to the behavior of deeply thinking, or ruminating, about them to get that reward.

In one famous MRI brain scan study, when heartbroken individuals were shown pictures of their heartbreakers, their dopamine pathways lit up just as they would from doing cocaine. Similarly, music can trigger someone to ruminate about what could be or what could have been with a romantic interest, says psychiatrist Jud Brewer, MD, PhD, the director of research and innovation at Brown University’s Mindfulness Center and author of The Craving Mind. The result, he says, is an itchy, contracted, restless feeling—quite the opposite of the expansive feeling associated with the mindfulness used to stop craving and anxiety.

Nostalgia also triggers the release of dopamine, and a song is like a time machine. Anytime I hear “Dancing in the Dark,” I’m instantly transported to kissing my college boyfriend in a dark, musty stairwell off campus. I can feel his hands resting on my hips and taste the Kentucky bourbon on his lips.

“There’s this certain charge you can get remembering somebody that you've been with before,” says Geoff MacDonald, PhD, a social psychologist who studies singlehood and sexuality at the University of Toronto. “You can replay, sort of viscerally, what it felt like to have somebody who was into you [and] that has the potential to provide real validation.”

He points to one study on song lyrics and attachment styles that, he says, “provides some evidence that people are drawn to a self-reinforcing bubble when it comes to intimacy.” In other words, it’s a form of confirmation bias. You’re drawn to songs with lyrics that reinforce what you already think about relationships, and you’re more likely to read into those lyrics and apply them to your own life.

For example, if you have an anxious attachment style—which many limerents do—you typically feel insecure in relationships and fear abandonment. Consequently, you may need constant validation, which is exactly what a certain song can provide. When I heard “Emmylou” playing on the radio one morning after the sexy smoke jumper had grown more distant over text, I decided it was a sign from the universe that we were still meant to be together. (It wasn’t.)

So, what’s a forlorn lover to do? Purge your playlist. “When someone is addicted to a behavior, if they avoid the people, places, and things [associated with the behavior], they’re less likely to get triggered to do the behavior,” says Brewer. “It’s not a great long-term solution, but it's one step that they can take.”

Think of songs that trigger you to ruminate, whether you’re obsessing over a crush or languishing over a lost love. Maybe it’s a song whose lyrics remind you of them, or a tune you danced to together. Whatever it is, consider removing that song from your playlist—at least until hearing the first few notes no longer launches you into a two-hour daydream about your love interest.

Now, if you've got a playlist that you play once every couple of weeks when you're feeling lonely, that can probably stay in your Spotify rotation. “It's good to remember that you can be loved,” MacDonald says. “But if you're leaning into that as a replacement for actually seeking real intimacy in your life, that's where you're going to run into problems.”

Instead of relying on songs to dream about relationships that would never materialize, I deliberately poured that relentless energy into hobbies that gave my life purpose. That might look like spending quality time with a loved one, creating art, or planning a trip—anything that takes your mind off the individual and fills your self-worth bucket.

Now, I can listen to Kind of Blue and “Emmylou” without yearning for past lovers, but I prefer playing songs that reinforce the security I feel in my marriage, like “Ain’t No Mountain High Enough,” the song we walked down the aisle to.

Amanda McCracken is a journalist, writing professor at the University of Colorado, the author of When Longing Becomes Your Lover, and the host of The Longing Lab podcast.
