Home for the Holidays? 4 Survival Tips

Are you headed to a gathering of friends or family in the coming weeks? Here is a heads-up: A November 2026 survey of 538 people found that 8 out of 10 respondents anticipate that political differences will come up during holiday meals (The Conversation). Further, a meta-analysis controlling for various factors found that dinners involving folks with diverse political perspectives were shorter by 24 minutes than dinners with politically uniform ones (Frimer & Skitka, 2020). How exactly these meals ended 24 minutes earlier is unknown. Hopefully it wasn’t a food fight.

The bottom line? Many folks consider political talk at holiday meals anxiety-provoking, preferring to focus on bonding and nostalgia. And yet many still may feel it is important to “return fire” on political views they deem uninformed, misinformed, or downright harmful. Post-midterm elections, a meme circulating on social media suggests that you bring up politics at holiday dinners so you can save money on gift purchases this year. Alternatively, when first sitting down at the table, one could ask, “Are we sick of ‘winning’ yet?” If you love conflict during meals, saying these sorts of things is likely to spark it. If, however, you'd prefer to keep your gatherings calm, here are some tips to survive your get-togethers:

Set clear boundaries ahead of time. Decide beforehand the topics considered off-limits (for you or others to bring up). If folks insist that you engage with them on an off-limits subject, communicate your limits using a calm tone and then have a plan to step away should they persist. One option is to stand up and apologize for having to take a call, real or imaginary.

Forget about winning. When exchanges are conceptualized as sharing perspectives, it's easier to shift away from the idea that this is a high-stakes battle and that victory is a necessity. The goal could instead be civil conversation. Keep your eyes on that prize, no matter what Uncle Charlie says to provoke a reaction.

Adopt a curious stance. If an exchange on politics does happen, show strong interest in how the person came to the views they're expressing. If tension rises, acknowledge the difference briefly and steer back to food, shared memories, or helping out. The merits of pumpkin versus bourbon chocolate pecan pie can present a useful distraction.

Try listening. Intentional, active listening is an important skill, especially now in a world where people desperately seeking to be victors in verbal battles are simply preparing their next salvo rather than paying attention to what the other person is saying. Really listening sends a palpable message that the person we are speaking to matters.

In conclusion, people may not remember specific details of an exchange of political views later on, but they will likely recall how they felt while speaking with us, and this brings us back to the context of a holiday get-together. Warm memories are still possible if we permit our better angels to prevail. Please pass the bourbon chocolate pecan pie.
