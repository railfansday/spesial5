Parthenon’s east pediment, ‘the most famous image of antiquity’, recreated in Frankfurt exhibition

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

Marble marvels: the Liebieghaus team deduced that the sculpted group on the Parthenon’s east pediment featured the two Dioscuri horsemen seen on this Roman sarcophagus lid at the Galleria Borghese Photo: © Galleria Borghese

Vinzenz Brinkmann’s quest to solve the mysteries of the destroyed east pediment of the Parthenon, which depicted the birth of Athena, dates back 40 years.

In 1986, he scaled ancient Greece’s most famous temple at night by rope to examine the base of the east pediment under special lamps that function only without ambient light, hoping to find traces left by 39 lost sculpted figures over the main gate.

“Nothing came of that,” says Brinkmann, who is now the head of antiquities at the Liebieghaus Sculpture Collection in Frankfurt—except, he adds, rumours about ghosts on the Acropolis that flew around Athens.

But now, Brinkmann says his team has solved a riddle akin to “putting together a jigsaw puzzle without having all the pieces”. The results of decades of research will go on show in an exhibition, Athena, the Stage is Yours: the Parthenon Puzzle, opening at the Liebieghaus on 18 November.

Long a flashpoint in Greek-British relations, the politics of the Parthenon surfaced in the run-up to the opening. The exhibition was to include marble fragments from both the British Museum in London and the Acropolis Museum in Athens. But, as The Art Newspaper reported last month, the Greek government withdrew its loan of nine fragments: according to Greek media reports, the request was rejected by Greece’s Central Archaeological Council on the grounds that they would be shown alongside British Museum pieces removed from the Acropolis by Lord Elgin. The Greek ministry of culture’s press office did not respond to emailed requests for comment and confirmation.

Greece has for decades demanded the return of the Parthenon Marbles taken by Elgin, which have been housed in the British Museum since 1817. The Liebieghaus has, however, secured a rare loan of two fragments from the British Museum. After the Greek government’s withdrawal, a British Museum spokesperson confirmed that the museum “will still be loaning”.

“The cancellation of the loans from Greece does not detract from the exhibition’s concept, or its scholarly significance,” declares Elisabeth Pallentin, a spokesperson for the Liebieghaus. “The exhibition is based primarily on new findings resulting from many years of research at the Liebieghaus.”

The east pediment is probably “the most famous image of antiquity”, Brinkmann says, with the centre of the sculpted ensemble composed of 3.5m-tall figures. The pediment was destroyed at some point between the fifth century AD and 1670—it is not known when or why, though there are plenty of theories—and 80% of it is still missing.

The rest is in fragments in museums around the world. Until now, no one had succeeded in fully reconstructing the central figures, Brinkmann says. “In the last 200 years, there have been a lot of proposals, but we saw that there were always gaps.”

The most serious work on the east pediment had been undertaken more than a century ago by a British Museum curator, Arthur Hamilton Smith, Brinkmann says. Smith partially recreated the east pediment using plaster casts from the British Museum and cooperated with colleagues in Athens to obtain more casts; his reconstruction went on display in the museum in 1908.

“He did a fantastic job in preparing the way,” Brinkmann says. “That was our basis.”

Although the Liebieghaus team recreated these fragments using scans and a 3D printer, the majority of the pediment was still missing. A breakthrough came when the team began investigating reliefs from the lids of Roman sarcophagi, which proved the key to unlocking the mystery.

“It was really a chance discovery,” Brinkmann says. “During a visit to the Hermitage in St Petersburg in 2014, we noticed a figural relief on the lid of a sarcophagus that was clearly closely related to the composition of the east pediment. It refers to the few surviving figures from the corners of the pediment, such as Helios and Selene with their chariots, but also to figures that have repeatedly been proposed as having belonged to the east pediment, such as the Horae/Moirai.”

They discovered nine sarcophagi in total with relevant reliefs. Most were in the Vatican Museums and elsewhere in Rome. “They had been overlooked,” Brinkmann says. “But the Romans copied everything the Greeks did.”

None of these reliefs showed the entire east pediment ensemble; each depicted a different subset of the 39 figures. But with the aid of computer technology, the team established that the remaining fragments of the east pediment and the depictions on the reliefs were a 100% match.

The team’s research has yielded some surprises. It was not previously known, for instance, that the Dioscuri, the twin horsemen known as Castor and Pollux to the Romans, were portrayed on the pediment. The figures served as models for the giant figures on the Quirinale in Rome, which are almost identical and date from antiquity, Brinkmann says. The twins are portrayed standing next to their bucking horses with anxious expressions, straining at the reins in an effort to control them.

This portrayal of the Dioscuri makes sense only in the context of the birth of the goddess, Brinkmann says. In mythology, Athena burst forth from her father Zeus’s forehead after he had swallowed her mother. Her birth is described in the Homeric hymns as causing Mount Olympus to shake, the earth to shriek, and the sea to swell and rage.

“The Dioscuri are heroic horsemen in mythology,” Brinkmann says. “They don’t lose control of their horses. But the birth of Athena was a primordial cosmic event that unleashed a natural disaster. What we see are the consequences.” Other figures in the recreated ensemble are also responding to the cataclysm: Hermes, for instance, is depicted holding onto his hat.

Brinkmann is known internationally for his research into polychromy in ancient Greek and Roman sculpture. An exhibition he curated, Gods in Color—Painted Sculpture of Classical Antiquity, has travelled the world, having been shown in several US locations, Athens, Oxford, Istanbul, the Vatican Museums and elsewhere.

It is possible that the new Liebieghaus exhibition will travel too, Brinkmann says. But not, it seems, with marbles from the Athens collections, with recent developments giving little room to hope that a lasting settlement between Greece and the British Museum is on the horizon.
