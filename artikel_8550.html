Infinite banking means paying extra into a whole life policy so you can borrow against its savings, the cash value, instead of a bank. Premiums run high, and the cash value trails what you’ve paid for years. Unless you earn a lot, max out your retirement accounts, and need coverage for life, buy term life and invest the difference.

Pay into the policy faster than the federal tax code’s seven-pay test allows in its first seven years, and it becomes a modified endowment contract for good, which ends the tax-free borrowing the pitch promises. Its loans and withdrawals are then taxed as income up to the policy’s gain, and before age 59½ you can owe an extra 10% on that part.

The pitch still calls for funding fast, usually through an add-on called a paid-up additions rider, which lets extra premium buy small chunks of fully paid coverage that add to the cash value. Kept within the limit, the policy has real appeal. Whole life guarantees a minimum cash value, the cash value grows without being taxed each year, and you can borrow against it with no credit check. But that loan is the insurer’s money, not your own, lent against your cash value at the interest rate your policy sets. Whatever you leave unpaid, plus interest, comes out of the death benefit.

The policy also costs more than the pitch lets on. Premiums run higher than they would for term coverage with the same death benefit, since you’re also paying for lifelong coverage and a savings feature. Commissions and fees come out of what you pay, and the Texas Department of Insurance notes that a whole life policy usually builds almost no cash value in its first few years. Cancel in those years, and surrender charges cut what you get back.

Infinite banking suits only a high earner who already maxes out a 401(k) and an IRA, keeps an emergency fund, needs coverage for life, and can pay the premiums for decades. Everyone else should buy term life and invest the difference.

Ask any agent who pitches it for the policy illustration, the insurer’s year-by-year projection, then find its guaranteed cash value at year five and set it beside the premiums you’ll have paid by then.
