Could comets have sparked life on Earth? Scientists tested the odds

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

New simulations suggest comets struggled to deliver life’s ingredients to early Earth because of destructive impacts and scarce exposed land.

Comets are loaded with the carbon and nitrogen molecules that chemists can turn into the building blocks of a living cell.

For more than a century, that has made comets a possible source of the ingredients that helped life begin on Earth. The trouble is the arrival.

A comet hits Earth at around 17 miles (28 kilometers) per second. The heat of that impact destroys most of the fragile molecules on board.

In new computer simulations that replayed Earth’s first 500 million years 100,000 times, the usual outcome was that not one comet delivered a useful dose.

Richard Anslow of the Institute of Astronomy at the University of Cambridge led the work, with colleagues at Cambridge and the University of Edinburgh. The result is a model rather than a measurement.

“It’s kind of a romantic idea, but our results suggest it’s highly unlikely,” Anslow said.

The authors trace the idea to a 1908 paper, and it has lasted because comets really do carry the right molecules.

Anslow’s team focused on one of them, hydrogen cyanide. In the lab, a strong enough solution of it can be turned into sugars, fats and the building blocks of DNA and RNA.

Comets carry hydrogen cyanide at about a tenth of a percent of their water ice.

It’s the only such raw ingredient, or feedstock molecule, found in comets at a level that would matter, and it holds up to heat better than most of them.

If comets struggled to deliver hydrogen cyanide intact, delivering more fragile molecules would be even harder.

Comets are porous and weak, and a planet’s atmosphere breaks them apart.

Anything smaller than about 330 feet (100 meters) across explodes high in the air, spreading whatever it carried over a wide area.

A shallow angle means more air to cross, and even some comets more than half a mile (1 kilometer) wide never reach the ground.

Losing the shallow ones matters, because a shallow, slow impact is exactly the kind that lets hydrogen cyanide survive the heat of the crash.

Fewer than 7% of comets arrive at an angle shallower than 15 degrees, and only the largest of those get through the atmosphere.

The team ran Earth’s comet bombardment 100,000 times, each with a different random mix of comet sizes, speeds and angles.

A hit counted as a success only if it landed on exposed land and left hydrogen cyanide at the concentration the lab experiments need.

Every uncertain assumption was chosen to favor the comets.

Across those runs, the median number of successful impacts was zero. In 54% of the runs, not one comet qualified, out of more than 100,000 comets striking the planet in each run.

When a run did produce a success, it came early, most often around 4.4 billion years ago.

That is more than 100 million years before the last common ancestor of all living things, which other researchers place at about 4.2 billion years ago.

In an interview with Earth.com, Anslow was asked what surprised him most when the simulations ran.

“It was a surprise how strongly our simulations – building on recent advances in the modeling of cometary impacts – disfavored this long-standing hypothesis in origins of life research, finding in most cases no comets delivering sufficient feedstock molecules for the origins of life,” he said.

By the team’s count, all the hydrogen cyanide that comets delivered intact over the whole period, spread through the oceans, would be more than 10,000 times too dilute to matter.

So the only version of the idea that works is a local one: a single comet landing on dry land and leaving a concentrated pool.

A comet strike shatters and melts the ground, groundwater floods into the new crater, and rain and runoff dilute the cyanide within days to weeks.

The chemical reactions needed to start life would have to occur within that window. By the authors’ rough calculation, the expected wait for such an event is longer than the age of the Universe.

“We can’t rule out for certain that it happened that way,” Anslow said. “But it seems to be a really rare occurrence – at best, a one-off fluke.”

The team ran the same model for Mars and Venus, and the picture changed. Mars is smaller, so comets arrive more slowly, and its thin air lets almost every comet reach the ground.

In the model, Mars took about one-fifth as many comet hits as Earth. Even so, the simulations gave it 744 successful deliveries on average, against 87 for Earth and 24 for Venus, before land was taken into account.

“This is not to say it definitely happened on Mars, of course, but suggests it would be unlikely if it happened on Earth and didn’t happen on Mars,” Anslow said.

“Or the other, perhaps more likely, alternative, is that it didn’t happen on either planet.”

Asked where Earth’s first building blocks came from if not from comets, Anslow said he wouldn’t speculate. He pointed instead to research he finds promising.

Molecules might have been made and concentrated on Earth’s own surface, or made after large asteroid impacts, or delivered by carbon-rich asteroids such as Ryugu and Bennu.

Several of the model’s inputs are still open. The formula for how much hydrogen cyanide survives an impact leaves out a comet’s internal strength and porosity, and the authors wrote that this probably makes their estimates too generous.

Early Mars was modeled with today’s atmosphere. If its atmosphere was once thicker, fewer comets might have reached the ground intact.

The team expects the same physics to hold for exoplanets, the planets around other stars. Bigger planets pull comets in faster, and most rocky planets found so far are bigger than Earth.

“Exoplanets significantly larger than the Earth are expected to be much poorer candidates for the successful cometary delivery of these key feedstock molecules,” Anslow told Earth.com.

Astronomers could use that to shorten the list of targets when NASA’s proposed Habitable Worlds Observatory begins its search for Earth-like planets.

The full study was published in the journal Scientific Reports.
