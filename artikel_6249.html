A biologist who has helped name more than 55 frog species just added four more to his list, one with half-red eyes

Tanggal: 2026-11-15

Four new frog species from the Peruvian Andes raise the Oreobates genus from 27 to 31. One has a red-and-bronze iris and hides in holes under tree roots.

In July 2022, biologist Juan D. Valencia Málaga collected seven small brown frogs of a still unnamed species on the forest floor near Canaan, a village in Peru’s San Martín department, at about 6,900 feet (2,100 meters).

Their eyes were red on top and greenish bronze below. All of them were caught next to holes under raised roots, and they ducked into those holes when threatened.

The Canaan frog is now one of four new frog species in the genus Oreobates.

It was described by a team based in Peru and the United States that includes Alessandro Catenazzi of Florida International University, who has helped describe more than 55 frog species.

Each of the four is known from one or two places, and the paper gives no population counts.

The paper, led by Pablo J. Venegas of Rainforest Partnership and the Instituto Peruano de Herpetología in Lima, raises the genus from 27 to 31 species. The six authors also include James W. Ttito, Luis A. García-Ayachi, Michael Gulman and Juan D. Valencia.

The frogs came from rapid surveys in the departments of San Martín and Huánuco between 2018 and 2025. The team walked slowly along streams and through the forest with headlamps, from 7 p.m. to 3 a.m., and caught the frogs by hand.

The specimens now sit in the herpetology collection of CORBIDI, the Centro de Ornitología y Biodiversidad in Lima.

Like all their relatives in the Terrarana group, these frogs breed on land and skip the tadpole stage, as do three other frogs that develop without tadpoles.

Its name, Oreobates foliaspectrum, joins the Latin for leaf and apparition. Red eyes, long toes and mottled color led the authors to compare it to a ghostly image in the leaf litter.

The female holotype, the specimen that anchors the name, measures 1.3 inches (32.4 millimeters) from snout to vent.

The forest at Canaan is a patch of low trees under 20 feet (6 meters) tall, with tree ferns and Chusquea bamboo on poor sandy soil. The patch sits in a matrix of cattle pastures, and the authors saw signs of frequent trampling by cattle on the forest floor.

In an October 30, 2026 article from his university, Catenazzi described how the team tells one frog from the next, by traits such as “this one is black, this one is brown, this one has a little tubercle, this one has smooth skin, and so on.”

Valencia did the Canaan fieldwork with companions including Benoît Segerer and Fabrice Schmitt, and he stayed with Artemio Chávez and his wife Francelit Espejo in the village.

Oreobates nigrumcompletum, Latin for entirely black, is deep brown to nearly black above and below. Ttito and Gulman found it at Finca Oso Pecho Blanco, a farm in the Uchiza district of San Martín, between July 30 and August 4, 2025, at 3,790 to 5,545 feet (1,155 to 1,690 meters).

The farm’s owner, Jorge Díaz Cotrina, guided them. This species has a dark iris flecked with gold.

The same two biologists collected Oreobates azulensis in July 2025 in the Azpusana Valley, in the Pucuyacu district of Huánuco.

The site lies in the buffer zone of Cordillera Azul National Park, and the name refers to that mountain range. The males measure 0.8 to 0.9 inches (20 to 23 millimeters), and all specimens have copper irises.

Oreobates chirimoto is named after Nuevo Chirimoto, a settlement in the upper basin of the Huayabamba River. García-Ayachi and field assistant Juan Gamboa collected three specimens there on December 13, 2018, at about 6,370 feet (1,942 meters), with local guide Francisco Galoc.

All three were in leaf litter between 8 and 11 p.m. on a steep slope cut by blackwater streams. The species has a yellow belly and orange groin and thighs.

APECO, a Peruvian conservation group, paid for that trip with a Critical Ecosystem Partnership Fund grant for work on an endemic harlequin frog.

Money for the Cordillera Azul survey came from CIMA Cordillera Azul, and a 2024 Carlos Ponce del Prado award paid for the Oso Pecho Blanco work.

The team extracted DNA from nine specimens and sent the amplified pieces of five genes to MCLAB, a lab in San Francisco, for sequencing.

The team set those against sequences of 42 other specimens from the public GenBank database, in an alignment 3,538 DNA letters long.

The resulting family tree, the most complete so far for the genus, placed O. azulensis, O. foliaspectrum and O. nigrumcompletum in one northern Peruvian group. O. chirimoto landed in a different branch, near species from southern Peru.

O. azulensis differs by only 2 percent in one mitochondrial gene from its closest relative, O. shunkusacha. The authors kept it separate because the two have different skin texture, with pointed, uneven granules in the new species.

According to Catenazzi, describing a frog also means checking old type specimens, the original reference animals, at the British Museum, other European museums, New York or the Smithsonian.

Many were described in the 19th or 20th century, and seeing them means “going to all those places and actually handling them,” he said.

All four frogs live in the Peruvian Yungas, the humid forests on the eastern slope of the Andes, between about 3,800 and 6,900 feet (1,155 and 2,100 meters).

The authors suggest that deep dry valleys may act as barriers for these frogs. The Marañón Valley separates the central Andes of Peru from the northern Andes, and on the eastern slopes of the northern Andes only one Oreobates species, O. quixensis, occurs, apparently spilling over from the Amazon lowlands.

Catenazzi’s lab at Florida International University is also studying two other frog species whose skin peptides have antimicrobial, wound-healing and antifungal properties. Similar work on frog skin compounds as a source of new antibiotics is under way in other labs.

Other recent descriptions from the country include three new frog species from Peru with an uncertain future.

The university article says the team uses calls along with body traits and DNA to separate species. The paper itself rests on morphology, color and genes, and it contains no call recordings.

All four new species lack vocal slits, openings in the mouth linked to the calls males use to attract females. The authors wrote that bioacoustic and behavioral studies are needed to find out why.

The support for the position of O. chirimoto on the family tree is low, at 59 out of 100, and the role of the river valleys as barriers needs wider sampling. O. chirimoto is known from three specimens, and O. foliaspectrum from a single forest patch at Canaan.

The paper gives no threat assessment for any of the four. At Canaan, O. foliaspectrum still hides among the roots of a small forest surrounded by pasture.

The full study was published in the journal Diversity.
