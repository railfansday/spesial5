The James Webb Space Telescope has spotted a tiny, extremely red object in the infant universe. It may be one of the earliest black holes, but researchers can't yet rule out a very different explanation.

Astronomers using the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) have spotted a tiny, extremely red object that existed just 450 million years after the Big Bang.

The object, called CW-LRD-z10, may be a "little red dot" — a mysterious class of objects that many researchers think are black holes growing inside dense cocoons of gas. If confirmed, the newfound object would be the earliest known example, potentially offering a window into how these enigmatic objects form.

"Such an object would be one of the most distant accreting black holes known," Roberto Maiolino, professor of experimental astrophysics at the University of Cambridge, who was not involved in the study, told Live Science in an email. The object may provide further evidence that little red dots trace some of the earliest phases of supermassive black hole formation, he added.

The study was published Nov. 24 in the Astrophysical Journal Letters.

Little red dots, or LRDs, are a population of tiny, very red objects in the early universe. The farther away they are, the more their light is stretched to longer, redder wavelengths. (This phenomenon is called redshift).

LRDs were only recently discovered thanks to JWST. The space telescope's near-infrared camera could only capture objects out to a redshift of about 9, corresponding to a lookback time of about 13.1 billion years ago. So in 2025, the team behind the new study also used JWST's mid-infrared instrument (MIRI) to search for even more distant sources.

CW-LRD-z10 was the only candidate that the search turned up, the study’s first author Takumi Tanaka, a doctoral student at the University of Tokyo, told Live Science.

The James Webb telescope's view of the little red dot CW-LRD-z10

In the study, the team measured the redshift of this compact, red object. They found it to be 10.36, suggesting it emitted its light when the universe was just 450 million years old. They measured it using a sharp drop in its light, interpreted as a ‘Lyman-alpha break.’

The result looks solid in one key respect, according to Maiolino. "The continuum drop attributed to a [Lyman-alpha] break appears relatively convincing, so I would say that the redshift determination is reasonably robust," he said.

The strongest clue that this object is a little red dot is its color, which is much redder in the mid-infrared than in the near-infrared band. The spectrum (its signature of emitted light) also shows the "V-shaped" dip typical of little red dots, possible iron emission, and tentatively detected emission lines whose ratios resemble those in other little red dots and active black holes. None of these three factors is conclusive on its own, Tanaka said, but together they support the little red dot interpretation.

However, the spectrum does not show wide hydrogen lines, which are the telltale sign of a little red dot. This is because the region where these hydrogen lines can be found in the spectrum falls outside the wavelengths covered in the new study. Maiolino said that "at this stage, this should only be considered an LRD candidate."

Tanaka agrees that it is more appropriate to continue calling it a very promising little red dot candidate.

The object's spectrum could instead point to a Balmer break, a sharp drop produced by older stars at closer distances. When redshifted, this would suggest that the object is a much closer galaxy at a redshift of about 3, or about 11.4 billion light-years away.

Tanaka said the data doesn't support this interpretation, as the object's color is hard to explain for such a galaxy, and the emission lines expected at that distance were not seen. "The observed break feature is too strong to be explained by a normal Balmer break from a typical stellar population," he said.

The spectrum of the little red dot CW-LRD-z10, showing its likely composition

Alternatively, an ordinary star-forming galaxy at redshift 10.36 could in principle produce the extreme red color, but it would need an unusually strong signature spectral line. That line falls outside the current spectrum, so future observations should help test it.

CW-LRD-z10's light also shows possible emission features from iron, which are thought to be emitted from the gas close to actively feeding black holes such as in quasars, rather than ordinary star-forming galaxies. "If confirmed, this would support the presence of actively accreting black holes and suggest that gas containing heavy elements was already present in the environment of the possible black hole," Tanaka said.

The nature of little red dots is highly debated. One of the leading theories suggests they host accreting black holes wrapped in dense gas, while other theories involve supermassive stars, black hole stars, or dense star clusters. Because the true nature of the little red dots and their position in black hole evolution scenarios are still uncertain, Tanaka stressed they cannot draw firm conclusions about what CW-LRD-z10 could mean for how supermassive black holes first formed.

Maiolino said that if CW-LRD-z10 really hosts an accreting black hole, then its properties could hold important information about how the first supermassive black holes formed. "Hence, studying its properties could shed light on the different black hole seeding scenarios, he said."

JWST's mid-infrared instrument is scheduled to take a spectrum of the object in 2027. The observations are designed to detect hydrogen and oxygen emission lines as well as the underlying continuum, which should let the team separate a genuinely red glow from the effect of strong emission lines. "We’re really looking forward to seeing the data," Tanaka said.
