Coffee drinkers show surprising differences deep inside the gut

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

A study of 61 adults links frequent coffee drinking with differences in intestinal acidity, contractions, and gut bacteria.

Some people finish their morning coffee and soon need the bathroom. There’s also a less visible side to the coffee habit, according to new research.

The researchers worked with 61 healthy adults in Denmark.

The small intestine was more acidic in people who often drank coffee than in those who rarely did. Their stool contained a wider range of bacteria as well.

Francesca Bucci, a PhD student at the University of Copenhagen (UCPH), is the first author.

Previous studies have also linked coffee consumption to differences in gut bacteria.

“But perhaps more importantly, frequent coffee consumption appears to affect the physiology of the gut itself, in other words how the gut functions,” Bucci said.

Whether coffee caused these differences remains unclear. The study cannot say.

Nobody was asked to drink more coffee or to give it up, so the volunteers kept to their usual habits.

The records come from PRIMA, a project that UCPH’s Department of Nutrition, Exercise and Sports ran over nine days in 2021.

For eight days, the volunteers weighed all their food and drink and put it in writing. They noted their bowel habits as they went.

Frequent coffee drinkers made up most of the group. Forty-eight volunteers had coffee on at least four of the eight days. Only 13 had coffee on two days or fewer.

Among the frequent coffee drinkers, the average coffee intake was about 11.6 fluid ounces (343.5 ml) a day, a little less than one soda can holds.

The team had daily urine samples as a check on those records.

The researchers tested for caffeine and four substances produced as the body breaks caffeine down.

Fifty volunteers also swallowed a wireless capsule roughly 1 inch (26.8 mm) long.

As the capsule moved through the stomach and intestines, it measured acidity, temperature, and pressure. It radioed each reading to a receiver on the volunteer’s belly.

The acidity scale is called pH. Its numbers run lower as acidity rises. Those readings put frequent drinkers’ small intestines at a lower pH than those of rare drinkers.

The pattern extended beyond that two-group comparison.

Across the volunteers, a lower small intestine pH tended to go with greater coffee intake in the records or higher caffeine levels in urine.

Earlier studies have linked coffee to acid release in the stomach, but here stomach pH showed no link to the drink.

Is a more acidic small intestine good or bad for health? The team cannot say yet. The authors raised a possible indirect route between coffee and microbes.

Acidity helps determine which microbes can live in the gut. Coffee might affect them by changing that acidity, they suggested.

A contraction is a squeezing movement of the gut wall.

The team measured contractions with the capsule’s pressure readings, and in the colon, or large intestine, there were more of them in volunteers whose urine had more caffeine breakdown products.

Bucci said those movements can aid digestion and help prevent constipation. The extra squeezing did not speed the capsule up.

Neither the coffee records nor the urine tests showed a link to the capsule’s time in the colon. The authors think the extra movements may stir the contents rather than push them along.

More stirring could allow the colon to absorb more water. That might help explain another observation: heavier coffee intake went with drier stool.

The link with contractions came from the urine results alone. A direct comparison of frequent and rare drinkers found no reliable difference in colon contractions.

In stool from frequent coffee drinkers, there were more distinct kinds of bacteria than in stool from rare coffee drinkers.

A second measure of bacterial variety found no reliable difference between the two groups. Coffee intake had no link to the bacterial community’s overall mix either.

Among the frequent coffee drinkers, three types of bacteria were more abundant. Lawsonibacter is one of them.

Lawsonibacter had already appeared in coffee studies across several groups of people.

A separate study this year found related bacteria at higher levels in people who drank coffee.

Testing so many bacteria makes chance findings a concern. That’s why the researchers applied a statistical correction.

After that correction, none of the three differences met the cutoff for statistical significance.

Lawsonibacter did have support in a separate analysis using the daily records. People who drank more coffee had more of the bacterium.

Coffee wasn’t PRIMA’s original focus. The project examined how diet, gut microbes, and the conditions inside the gut interact. In 2024, an earlier analysis of the volunteers was published.

Nobody asked the volunteers to write down the type of coffee. The records cannot separate regular from decaf.

Caffeine also comes from tea and energy drinks. Seven people whose records listed no coffee still had caffeine in their urine.

The group that rarely drank coffee had only 13 people.

Many comparisons were also run on the capsule readings without the usual statistical correction for doing so. The authors described the study as exploratory.

Henrik Munch Roager, the study’s senior author and an associate professor at UCPH, pointed to findings from outside the study.

“For example, we already know from other studies that coffee can influence a range of processes in the digestive system, including acid secretion in the stomach,” Roager said.

“So there are good reasons to believe that what we are seeing is not merely coincidence. Anecdotally, many people also report that coffee affects their digestion.”

In the paper, Roager disclosed a consulting or advisory relationship with Nestlé.

Roager said that if researchers can work out how coffee affects contractions, the knowledge might be used to stimulate the gut naturally in people with constipation.

As a next step, Roager proposed a trial in which researchers control how much coffee people drink.

Periods of heavy coffee drinking would be compared with periods of none to see whether a person’s gut changes when they start or stop drinking coffee.

Testing both regular and decaf, the authors wrote, could separate caffeine from the other compounds in coffee.

The study is published in the journal Gut Microbiology.
