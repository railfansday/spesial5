Ahmed Morsi, Whose Poetic Paintings Long for His Egyptian Homeland, Dies at 96

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Executive Editor, ARTnews & Art in America

Ahmed Morsi, an Egyptian-born, New York–based painter and poet who once said he had been “displaced most of my life,” died on Wednesday at the age of 96. His death by natural causes in Cairo was confirmed his daughter Sherine Morsi.

In an Instagram post announcing the artist’s death, Sherine wrote, “His profound understanding of our shared humanity, in all its complexity, fragility, and beauty, found expression in both his art and his words.”

Born in Alexandria, Egypt, in 1930, Ahmed Morsi studied drawing in his early years with the Italian artist Silvio Bicchi and became part of the so-called Alexandria School devoted to Surrealist imagery around the time of World War II. But, as he told ARTnews’s Alex Greenberger in 2021, “We need to distinguish between the geographic place that is Egypt’s second largest city from my ‘home.’ My Alexandria is a city of my youth and also myth and legend. This is the ‘home’ that has fueled my imagination, spilling into my poetry notebooks and pouring over my canvas. After developing his distinctive painting style—poetic figuration with a quiet sense of fantasy suffused with muted hues—in Egypt and Iraq in the 1950s and ’60s, Morsi moved to New York in 1974 when his wife, Amany Fahmy, got a job as chief of the United Nations Arabic Translation Service. She helped translate some of Morsi’s writing, which took different forms ranging from poetry to art criticism, including the founding of Galerie 68, an influential journal devoted to Arabic modernism based in Cairo.

About his writing, which was as integral as painting to his practice, Morsi once said, “Poetry and painting may be separate media used in a constant attempt to understand the Self, but my vocabulary on paper and on canvas is one.”

In 2021, Morsi featured in MoMA PS1’s survey show “Greater New York,” and, in 2024, his work was part of Adriana Pedrosa’s “Foreigners Everywhere” exhibition for the Venice Biennale. But the world he worked in was his own: As Morsi said in a quote featured on his artist page for his gallery Salon 94, “I am not building a world—for me, that world, my world, exists and has existed since time immemorial. It is a world where everything is seen, perceived and understood differently. Your world is bound by conditioning, preconceived habits. In mine, the senses are free.”
