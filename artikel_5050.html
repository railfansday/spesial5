Smart Contracts, Gas, and Blockchain Trade-offs

Ethereum is a Layer 1 blockchain designed for programmable applications. It uses accounts, smart contracts, the Ethereum Virtual Machine, ETH, gas fees, and Proof of Stake to support decentralized applications and compete within a wider ecosystem of Layer 1 blockchains.

While Bitcoin is viewed as digital gold, an inflation hedge, or digital money, other crypto entrepreneurs wanted to push the envelope further. A few years after Bitcoin, Ethereum outlined infrastructure for smart contracts, decentralized applications, and new financial systems. This guide explains Ethereum’s premise, ecosystem, advantages, and the hurdles Layer 1 blockchains face.

Bitcoin proved that a decentralized network could maintain money and a transaction ledger without a central authority. Ethereum extended that idea by making the blockchain programmable. Instead of only transferring value, Ethereum made it possible to run code on-chain.

The core innovation is the smart contract: a piece of code that runs on a blockchain and automatically executes predefined actions when specific conditions are met. In the vending-machine analogy, you insert money, and the machine dispenses your snack without needing human intervention.

Smart contracts make decentralized applications, or dApps, possible. These applications function without traditional intermediaries, with use cases that range from financial services to games.

To understand how a vending machine can live inside a blockchain, start with Ethereum’s accounts. Ethereum has two main account types. Externally owned accounts, or EOAs, are user-controlled accounts secured by a private key. They can send and receive ETH, interact with smart contracts, and initiate transactions.

Contract accounts are controlled by smart-contract code, not private keys. They cannot initiate transactions on their own. Instead, they act in response to transactions sent by EOAs or other contracts, executing predefined code when triggered.

Ethereum also uses an account-based model, where balances are stored directly in accounts, similar to a bank ledger. This differs from Bitcoin’s UTXO model, which tracks individual transaction outputs. The account-based approach makes it easier for smart contracts to check balances and interact with users or other contracts.

Smart contracts execute inside a virtual environment called the Ethereum Virtual Machine, or EVM. The EVM resembles a universal translation service: just as a translation service helps everyone in a multilingual meeting understand the same message, the EVM provides a framework where smart contracts run the same way on every node in the network.

That consistency matters because every participating node needs to agree on what the smart contract did. Ethereum’s application-focused account model and the EVM together power decentralized applications, including the broader ecosystem of decentralized finance.

Ethereum’s infrastructure was not set up this way by accident. The aim was to go beyond decentralized money and expand how financial services could work. This is where decentralized finance, or DeFi, comes in.

DeFi aims to replace or rework financial functions such as lending, borrowing, trading, and earning interest with blockchain-based protocols. Instead of relying on a bank or broker, users interact with smart contracts that enforce rules automatically.

ETH, or Ether, is the fuel or incentive that powers the Ethereum network. Every interaction, from sending tokens to deploying smart contracts, requires ETH to pay gas fees.

Gas fees are the cost that comes with every transaction or interaction with a smart contract. They serve two main purposes: they compensate validators for keeping the network running, and they help prevent spam by making every action on the network carry a cost.

Gas fees work like a toll system. Ethereum has limited blockspace, and when more users want to use the network at the same time, the cost of getting into the next block can rise.

Gas prices are commonly quoted in gwei. Under EIP-1559, a transaction fee includes a network-determined base fee and may include a priority fee for the validator. This gives users a clearer fee structure, although the total cost still changes with demand and the amount of computation required.

Ethereum initially operated with Proof of Work, where miners validate transactions by solving complex computational puzzles. It later transitioned to Proof of Stake in the upgrade known as The Merge.

Proof of Stake works differently. Instead of miners competing with hardware, validators are selected to create new blocks, with their role tied to ETH staked as collateral. Staking acts as a financial commitment to follow the rules, because dishonest behavior can put a validator’s staked funds at risk through slashing.

Ethereum Improvement Proposals, or EIPs, are formal proposal documents that describe potential changes to the Ethereum network. They resemble blueprints for upgrading a city bridge: an idea is written down, reviewed, debated, and, if accepted, implemented as a network upgrade.

Ethereum was one of the first major highways built for smart contracts, but as more people used it, traffic jams and higher tolls became common. That opened the door for other blockchain platforms to emerge with different design choices.

Chains such as Bitcoin and Ethereum are called Layer 1 chains because they are base, independent networks that do not rely on another blockchain for operation. Other Layer 1s may optimize for speed, cost, decentralization, developer experience, or specific use cases.

The common challenge is the blockchain trilemma: improving two of the major cornerstones often comes at the cost of the third.

Layer 1 tokens such as ETH are not only traded assets. They play operational roles in paying for blockspace, incentivizing validators, and securing the network through rewards and fees.

Other important topics include Web3 infrastructure: wallets as user access points, middleware such as RPC nodes and oracles, and security/audit services that help applications interact with blockchain networks. These surrounding tools fill needed roles around the base blockchain.

Balancing the blockchain trilemma is difficult. To support broader adoption, scalability becomes a critical problem: without effective scaling solutions, blockchains can become too slow or too expensive for everyday use.

In a city-road analogy, a single-lane road works when only a few people drive, but if everyone starts driving at once, the road becomes gridlocked. Blockchains face a similar challenge when demand rises faster than the base layer can process activity.

Ethereum expanded blockchain use cases by introducing smart contracts and decentralized applications.

Ethereum uses EOAs, contract accounts, an account-based model, and the EVM to make smart-contract execution consistent across the network.

ETH is the native currency used to pay gas fees, which price computation and help prevent spam.

Proof of Stake replaces miners with validators who stake ETH and can be penalized for dishonest behavior.

EIPs provide a structured way to propose and coordinate Ethereum upgrades.

Alternative Layer 1s make different trade-offs across decentralization, scalability, and security.

Scaling is the natural next topic because base-layer blockchains face throughput and cost limits as adoption grows.

This 101 subject is designed for readers who already have a basic grasp of Bitcoin and blockchain terminology. It helps to understand what a blockchain is, why decentralization matters, how wallets and private keys work, and why blockchains need consensus before moving into Ethereum and Layer 1 design.

Ethereum: A Layer 1 blockchain designed to support smart contracts and decentralized applications.

Layer 1: A base blockchain network that does not rely on another chain for its core operation.

Smart contract: Code that runs on a blockchain and automatically executes predefined actions when conditions are met.

dApp: A decentralized application that uses smart contracts instead of relying only on traditional intermediaries.

EOA: An externally owned account controlled by a private key.

Contract account: An Ethereum account controlled by smart-contract code.

EVM: The Ethereum Virtual Machine, where smart-contract logic executes consistently across the network.

Gas fee: The cost paid in ETH to process a transaction or smart-contract interaction.

Gwei: A small unit of ETH used to price gas.

Proof of Stake: A consensus model where validators stake ETH and participate in block creation and validation.

Slashing: A penalty mechanism that can reduce a validator’s stake for dishonest or faulty behavior.

EIP: An Ethereum Improvement Proposal describing a possible network change.

Blockchain trilemma: The trade-off among decentralization, scalability, and security.

Web3 infrastructure: Wallets, RPC nodes, oracles, and related services that help users and applications interact with blockchains.
