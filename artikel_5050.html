Michael Saylor’s Net Worth Plunges $6 Billion: The Cost of His Bitcoin Bet

Michael Saylor’s net worth has fallen by approximately $6 billion since the cryptocurrency market peaked in October 2025. The Strategy co-founder, whose fortune was estimated at $9.3 billion at the time, is now worth around $3.3 billion, according to Forbes.

The decline has wiped out nearly two-thirds of Saylor’s estimated wealth. Yet the billionaire’s company continues to accumulate Bitcoin despite mounting paper losses, showing little willingness to reconsider its investment strategy.

For Saylor, the downturn presents a problem that extends well beyond the price of Bitcoin. Much of his fortune depends on a company that must convince investors to keep financing its accumulation strategy.

Saylor was already among the cryptocurrency industry’s biggest financial casualties in February, when Forbes estimated that his wealth had fallen by $4.7 billion from its October 2025 level.

His fortune briefly stabilized in the following months, reaching an estimated $4.7 billion in June, before suffering another setback.

The latest estimate puts Saylor’s wealth approximately 64.5% below its October 2025 level.

Bitcoin’s return to the $60,000 level has added pressure, but the cryptocurrency alone does not explain the scale of the decline. Saylor’s exposure through Strategy makes his financial position considerably more complicated than that of an ordinary Bitcoin investor.

The $6 billion represents a reduction in estimated wealth, not a confirmed cash loss or the proceeds of any disclosed asset sales.

In October 2020, Saylor disclosed that he personally owned 17,732 BTC, acquired for approximately $175 million.

At the Bitcoin price of $60,325 reported by Strategy on November 26, those holdings would be worth approximately $1.07 billion, assuming he still owns the entire disclosed amount.

That would leave his original personal investment with an unrealized gain of roughly $895 million.

His current personal Bitcoin balance has not been independently confirmed, but the calculation illustrates an important distinction. Saylor can lose billions in estimated wealth while remaining substantially profitable on his original Bitcoin purchases.

The situation at Strategy is quite different.

While Saylor began buying Bitcoin at considerably lower prices, his company has spent much of the past several years increasing its exposure at higher valuations.

According to its November 23 SEC filing, Strategy acquired another 457 BTC for $29.6 million, bringing its total holdings to 851,305 BTC.

The company has spent approximately $64.23 billion accumulating Bitcoin at an average purchase price of $75,446.

Its official Bitcoin dashboard valued those reserves at approximately $51.35 billion on November 26.

That leaves a gap of nearly $12.9 billion between the company’s cumulative acquisition cost and the market value of its Bitcoin holdings.

These figures do not represent realized trading losses, nor do they capture the full financial performance of the company.

Nevertheless, the numbers reveal how dramatically the economics of accumulating Bitcoin have changed during the downturn.

For years, investors were willing to pay a premium for Strategy shares. The attraction was not simply the Bitcoin it owned, but the prospect that management could raise additional capital and increase shareholders’ exposure to the cryptocurrency.

That premium is no longer something the company can take for granted.

Strategy’s dashboard showed an mNAV ratio of 0.94 at the November 25 stock market close, using its own net Bitcoin value per share methodology.

The company’s latest investor briefing acknowledges the implications:

A widening discount can cause MSTR to underperform BTC even when bitcoin rises.

This is where Saylor’s investment model faces a more difficult test.

When investors are willing to pay a premium for Strategy shares, raising capital to acquire more Bitcoin can improve the economics of the business. When that premium disappears, issuing additional shares becomes a more complicated proposition.

Strategy’s own sensitivity analysis shows that a Bitcoin price of $40,000 would reduce its estimated net reserve value per share to $45.37, compared with $163.17 at a Bitcoin price of $100,000. The figures illustrate how sensitive the company’s underlying reserve valuation is to Bitcoin price movements.

Strategy’s third-quarter financial results offer a clearer picture of why its cash reserves and financing obligations now matter almost as much as the price of Bitcoin.

Buying more Bitcoin is only one part of the equation. The cost of financing those purchases matters just as much.

There is a credible case for recovery. Strategy still owns more than 850,000 Bitcoin, giving it considerable exposure to any sustained cryptocurrency market rebound.

The company also reported approximately $4.82 billion in combined cash and dollar reserves in its latest investor briefing, providing a buffer against its financing obligations.

But a recovery in Bitcoin would not necessarily produce an equivalent recovery in Saylor’s wealth. Strategy’s share price also depends on its debt, preferred-stock obligations, access to capital and the valuation investors are willing to assign to its business model.

The next test for Saylor is not whether he can buy another thousand Bitcoin. It is whether he can continue financing those purchases on terms that benefit existing shareholders.

His personal fortune has already demonstrated how expensive that distinction can become.
