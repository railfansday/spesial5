Aesthetics Can Be a Strategy for Boosting Well-Being

Aesthetic experiences can arise from appreciating artworks—music, poetry, sculpture, the visual arts, nature, and the built environment. A distinctive feature of aesthetic experience is that it is not goal-directed but focused more on the activity itself. The focus is on the pleasure from doing something rather than achieving an ultimate personal goal.

Perception and interaction with art can evoke strong feelings, usually a sense of pleasure (Mastandrea, 2019). In the presence of beautiful things, we experience a broad range of feelings and emotions—fascination, awe, wonder, and admiration, chills, goosebumps, and crying. And being moved feels good (watching a joyful movie). For instance, most aesthetic perceptions of landscapes, natural scenes like sunrises and sunsets, tend to produce positive feelings of peacefulness, relaxation, and harmony.

Aesthetic emotions do not require us to act; they invite us to savor (Winner, 2019). Aesthetic emotions motivate prolonged and repeated experiences (you never get tired of watching a beautiful sunset). We also desire to possess aesthetically pleasing objects. Consumer-facing design and fashion largely focus on prompting consumer behaviors that drive purchasing decisions.

Aesthetic experiences can improve health and well-being in many ways. An aesthetic experience provides an escape from everyday practical life, and escaping one’s reality may improve mood. Art therapy and engagement with the arts are shown to reduce symptoms of depression and anxiety, ease pain, help people heal from trauma, protect against cognitive decline, and enhance social cohesion, among other benefits.

Aesthetic pleasure can be an antidote to addictive pleasure. Aesthetic pleasure differs from physical pleasures (alcohol, pornography, or gaming). We tire less quickly of artworks in one sitting than the pleasures we physically consume. The research on the role of aesthetic appreciation in addiction recovery is promising because it has the potential to provide a less costly treatment option.

There is increasing evidence that the aesthetic pleasure from art and music is no different in origin and function from the pleasure induced by food, drugs, and sex (Nadal and Skov, 2013). Art triggers the same pleasure as food or sex. For example, listening to highly pleasurable music can induce dopamine release in the same part of the brain as drugs of abuse. If the pleasure from art is no different than the pleasure from drugs, then one can substitute one for another (Christensen, 2017). If the high is the same, then getting it from a safer, more refined source should be even more obvious.

Education and Experience Shape Art Appreciation

However, one has to learn to see art. Art can seem intimidating to people with little experience of it. There’s no right way to respond to art. Aesthetic judgments are informed by a person’s background, emotions, and experiences. Education and culture profoundly influence our experience of art. We have no art module in the brain (Chaterjee, 2014). In contrast, we are genetically programmed to crave fat, salt, sugar, and so on.

We can nurture sensitivity (taste) for art with careful observation and education. To realize the benefits, Chatterjee (2014) suggests that we examine one work of art for 15 minutes, devoting five minutes to each of the three components of the aesthetic triad: What are its sensory features? What emotions does it evoke? What associations and knowledge does it stir up? This exercise may foster deeper engagement and create a more meaningful experience for participants.
