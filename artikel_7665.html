5 Books About Small Town Crime, from ‘Baby Queen’ Author Ty Landers

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

You expect danger in the city. Adjectives like streetwise and street-smart exist to acknowledge the specific intelligence needed to navigate the perils of urban living. Country intelligence has no succinct adjectives. Country-strong, that’s a thing. But, country-wise? More often than not, there is a misconception that life in the hinterlands will dull your edge, diminish your reflexes, lull you into a false sense of security.

There’s plenty of menace in small places. In towns with no interstate ramp, nameless desert stops with no cell phone service, a desperate farm community surrounded by an ocean of corn.

I write crime thrillers set in places where people only recently started locking their doors. These don’t have to be set in the South—mine are—but they can happen anywhere. Maybe it’s Southern noir when done south of the Mason-Dixon line. Rural noir is a good name, and throws its arms around the whole genre, but it’s a mouthful. It comes out sounding slurry, even when I’m sober, and might not come out at all if I’ve had a cocktail. This subgenre of crime fiction goes by many names. I wish I could just tell people I wrote Southerns and have them understand—the way most people understand the Western. Till then, here are five of my favorite examples of novels that take place out past the last lights of town.

Important note: I had to leave out a lot of authors I adore and beg you to read anything and everything by the following for outstanding examples of small-town, insular, tight-nit, and Sun-Belt crime: Flannery O’Connor, S.A. Cosby, Carson McCullers, Henry Wise, Attica Locke, Dennis Lehane, Megan Abbott, Ivy Pochoda, and Elmore Leonard. Please find them.

Daniel Woodrell is incredible. I love every word he has ever written but Tomato Red is my favorite. It opens with a gorgeous run-on sentence that’s almost three hundred words long and, after reading it, it’s almost impossible not to collapse and read the whole book in one sitting. The novel’s hapless anti-hero, Sammy Barlach—recently fired from a dog food factory and fresh off a speed-fueled burglary attempt—throws in with siblings Jason and Jamalee Merridew. Jason, a local object of female obsession, and his sister Jamalee want to weaponize Jason’s beauty, blackmail wealthy woman, and get them both out of Venus Holler. Sammy hitches his wagon to the Merridew’s scheme. But for Jason, a gay man in the hills and hollows of the Ozarks, choosing to live an authentic life proves to me more dangerous than one of crime.

One of my favorite sayings from Tod Goldberg is that he writes about bad people doing terrible things to even worse people. In The Low Desert, he demonstrates this across twelve sometimes-connected stories set on the outskirts of California and Nevada. There are lonely deserts, fading resort towns, lost highways. Each story is a banger, but the fact that Goon Number Four isn’t your favorite TV show of the last decade is a miscarriage of justice. A story about a hired gun who enrolls in a community college, seeking to be rid of the life, only to find exploitation waiting in the halls of normalcy. The Low Desert has something I have never been able to fully articulate—feeling more like a great album, than the seminal work of crime fiction it is.

A personal favorite, Thompson’s Pop. 1280 is a novel that perfectly balances menace and comedy. Nick Corey, a terrible sheriff, oversees the 1280 souls of Potts County, Texas. Many of Nick’s constituents don’t want a sheriff, prefer to skirt the law, and tolerate him because he is pathologically shiftless. Our sheriff eats five meals a day, sleeps with any woman who’s up for it, sees murder as the only option rather than a last resort, and will do anything to keep his job. Not because he is ambitious, but because being sheriff allows him to do almost nothing. But, on the precipice of a difficult reelection, he reluctantly springs into action. Through the ensuing morass, Nick confronts sassy pimps, his pushy wife and her brother, his mistress and her husband, the town as whole, and the very concept of righteousness. Corey is a man who seems dumb, but isn’t. Someone dangerous and ruthless in the most peculiar and befuddling ways.

Cormac McCarthy’s most accessible novel. It is a literary crime novel that dissects the corrosive effects of money, heightened to break-neck entertainment by the inclusion of one of fiction’s most unforgettable bad dudes. In many ways, this is Anton Chigurh’s book the way Jaws belongs to the shark. Chigurh, a relentless hitman who’s as dangerous to his employers as he is to his targets, is evil with biblical scope. Ed-Tom Bell is a good man fading. A sheriff having and existential crisis, struggling to adapt to the requirements of his calling in a world where criminality has moved past opportunism and into something inconceivable. Throw in Llewelyn Moss, a Vietnam vet who stumbles across two million dollars after a drug deal goes wrong, and you’re in for a ride. When Chigurh’s tide of malevolence come in. One man tries to stem it, the other retreats. Fates are decided by the flip of a coin.

Frank Bill’s debut, Crimes in Southern Indiana, is a cold look at a brutal version of Indiana that exists off the interstates. Southern Indiana is an anomaly—not quite the South, not quite the Midwest. Claimed by none—like the folks Bill writes about. He drops seventeen stories about this distinct region across two hundred and seventy pages in an achingly authentic voice. He can do more damage in ten pages than most other savvy word-geeks could with a lifetime supply of paper. The Penance of Scoot McCutchen will wreck you. I think about it all the time.

Ty Landers holds an MFA in Creative Writing and Writing for the Performing Arts from the University of California, Riverside. Born and raised in Alabama, he spent more than twenty years in Nashville, Tennessee, before moving to Milwaukee, Wisconsin, with his wife and sons. His debut novel BABY QUEEN, out on November 17, 2026, is being adapted by HBO. For more on Ty Landers, visit: https://www.tylandersfiction.com/
