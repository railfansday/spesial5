‘Downstate’ Broadway Review: Provocative Play About Sex Offenders Questions The Nature Of Forgiveness

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

Among many other things, Downstate, Bruce Norris‘ tricky new play that’s gained considerable acclaim and controversy, is an exercise in cognitive dissonance, a commandingly written and shrewdly directed (by Joe Mantello) work that asks us to confront some of the very things we prize most in ourselves, namely the limits of our compassion and empathy.

Set in a shabby, cheaply furnished middle-class home we’re told is located in downstate Illinois, the play immediately introduces us to the house’s residents: four middle-aged men who bicker over who owes what for the week’s groceries, personal belongings left in common spaces and other petty irritations of daily, group living. And we’re also introduced to a younger man, maybe in his 30s, who is nervous and uncomfortable. Andy (Thomas Sadoski) has come to confront the most frail of the group home residents: Fred (Francis Guinan), an aging, vaguely confused man in a wheelchair – we learn why he is so confined later – who has something terrible in common with his housemates: He is a convicted sex offender and child predator who has officially served his time in prison but, with no where to go, is living in this group home under strict observation and restrictions. Fred is a former piano teacher, Andy his former student and you can probably guess the rest. It’s been 30 years since Fred sexually assaulted Andy on two separate occasions, and the former student’s life has been a shambles of shame, depression and struggle ever since. Andy’s wife, Em (Cassie Beck), is by his side for moral support as he attempts to finally face his abuser with a list of the crimes and their impact on his life. Em, too, has a list, its very existence an indication of how abuse spreads well beyond the first victim.

Sharing the home (flawlessly designed by the ever-reliable Scott Pask) with Fred are Dee (K. Todd Freeman), an aging Black gay man and a sort of de facto house mother who does the grocery shopping, makes sure Fred takes his pills and nags Gio (Al’Jaleel McGhee), the youngest of the group, to move his gym equipment out of the common area. Gio, talkative, aggressively friendly and very funny – somehow, Downstate has an improbable amount of genuine humor – holds himself above his housemates, convinced that the relatively advanced age of his victim – she was 17 – bestows on him a moral superiority and a brighter future. It does neither, particularly after he shows up with a new, clueless young friend (Ivy Wolk).

Finally, there’s Felix (Adrian Martinez), the quietest, most antisocial and, frankly, most outwardly pathetic of the bunch, lonely, morosely religious, secretive and resentful at having to live with people he disdains. It’s Felix who seems to draw the most attention and concern from Ivy (The Bear‘s Liza Colón-Zayas), the visiting probation officer who keeps all the men under a watchful eye and whose initial conviviality can’t mask the seriousness with which she takes both her job and the crimes the men in her charge have committed.

Playwright Norris (he wrote the excellent Clybourne Park) builds the tension and momentum of Downstate almost entirely through character interaction – it’s only in retrospect that we realize there hasn’t been much by way of plot, or at least action, in what we’ve just witnessed – and yet the entire play is compelling from beginning to end. We watch as Dee, the most outwardly compassionate and intelligent of the bunch, gradually shows his meaner side, and Gio, for all his lip service to self-improvement and goal-setting, constantly puts himself (and others) in danger. Sad Felix, whose denials of rule-breaking we want to believe, at last gives us no choice but to acknowledge the worst.

And make no doubt, what these characters do, and are capable of, is the worst. Prior to Broadway, Downstate had productions in Washington D.C., Off Broadway, London and at Chicago’s Steppenwolf, drawing some right-wing ire – after a positive review in the Washington Post, Ted Cruz tweeted, “So now the corporate media is praising pedophilia” – but those reactions couldn’t be more wrong-headed. True, Downstate insists that we see and consider the humanity of these men, but that is most certainly not the same as asking us to excuse their crimes. Downstate dares us to forgive the unforgivable, challenging the very concept of grace.

“Praising pedophilia?” Nonsense. If anything, I came away from Downstate entirely convinced that not one of these four characters should live unrestricted among society. Age and infirmity has rendered Fred the former piano teacher somewhat harmless, though his blithe, ostensibly sympathetic demeanor around the still-devastated Andy speaks volumes. Dee, so fiercely protective of Fred, still harbors delusions that excuse his crime, a rationalizing that masks itself as intellectual debate but even that falls to the side when his cruelty and destructiveness surface. The fates of Gio and Felix might be shocking, but not surprising.

Director Mantello guides his excellent cast through Downstate‘s nuances subtly ands unerringly, with the performers creating fully credible characters cursed by – whose to say? – trauma or demons or nature. What Downstate leaves us with, then, is both understanding and the limits of understanding, the frustration of somehow wanting to forgive but knowing that forgiveness can’t provide these victimizers what they crave: A home in the world, a place that isn’t a four-bedroom prison with crummy furniture and an ankle bracelet.
