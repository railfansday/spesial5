FAA rule changes: 6 flight delays airlines no longer pay for

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

A new federal rule takes effect Feb. 1, 2027. See which delays no longer bring meal or hotel help, and which protections remain.

When a flight sits at the gate for hours, most travelers assume the airline will at least buy them dinner and find them a bed for the night. That help can easily feel like a right. Even so, meal and hotel vouchers rest on goodwill rather than law. They remain voluntary commitments, according to Insurance Business, and travelers generally shouldn't expect them once a disruption is no longer classed as within the airline's control. A final rule required by the FAA Reauthorization Act of 2024 will take 10 kinds of disruption out of that category. Travelers $TRV will lose the meal and the room for those delays even though no passenger right has been repealed, because regulators changed only who is to blame.

Federal regulators expect travelers to come out behind, with airlines handing passengers less in perks and payouts overall, according to Insurance Business. "These newly exempted causes of flight delays are not especially common, but we understand that the new rules might make travelers feel less protected when they fly," Robert Gallagher, chief executive for North America at Zurich Cover-More, told Insurance Business. The rule hasn't started yet, because regulators pushed back its start after Airlines for America asked for more time. Until then, today's commitments mostly still apply, and travelers have time to learn which delays will still earn a meal and a room.

Fox 5 DC laid out which disruptions airlines no longer must compensate passengers for, and which still count. The causes range from a sudden repair and a government system outage to a cabin that has to be cleaned after a passenger dies. Through all of them, a traveler's right to a cash refund stays in place. The new rule takes effect Feb. 1, 2027, after regulators moved it from its original start of Oct. 19, 2026, and it covers flights from Jan. 1, 2027. The categories sound tidy, but the line between what an airline caused and what it didn't is blurrier than the labels suggest.

A maintenance problem is the hardest of these delays to picture as beyond the carrier's control, because the airline owns, flies and services the aircraft. Once the new rule applies, sudden repairs, such as fixing overheated brakes, will count as outside the airline's control, Fox 5 DC reports, and travelers shouldn't expect meal or hotel vouchers for them. The permanent version of the change is a rulemaking titled "Revisions to Cause of Airline Delay Categories," according to the U.S. Department of Transportation. It sorts delays into categories and makes no finding about an airline's diligence.

The rule has not taken effect, but travelers have already lost the perks for one kind of maintenance. On Nov. 28, 2025, the FAA required airlines to carry out specific maintenance through an emergency airworthiness directive, according to the U.S. Department of Transportation. A federal safety order is the clearest case of work an airline did not choose to do. In a notice posted Dec. 10, 2025, the department said it would not hold carriers to their delay commitments for maintenance that directive required. That relief is enforcement discretion tied to a single directive, not a permanent rule. The permanent rule will reach further and cover sudden repairs of any kind, not only work done under one federal order.

A mechanical delay announced at the gate will no longer be a reliable sign that the airline owes a traveler dinner and a room.

This exemption covers planes and equipment physically damaged in extreme weather, not flights that bad weather merely slows down. A traveler who hears "weather" at the gate will probably picture a snowstorm holding flights in place, but an ordinary weather delay is not part of this category, Insurance Business reports. The category covers damage to an aircraft or the infrastructure it relies on from lightning, debris or sabotage, according to Fox 5 DC, so the deciding question is whether something was damaged.

When a damaged plane is taken out of service, the cost of a stranded night becomes largely the traveler's own risk to manage. Travelers $TRV on a plane grounded after a debris strike generally shouldn't expect the airline to provide a meal voucher or a hotel room, and travel insurance is one place to look for that help instead. Travel Guard's trip-delay coverage generally extends to most of the causes the new rule exempts, according to Insurance Business. The words "most" and "generally" matter, because coverage depends on the policy. A traveler who buys it still needs to confirm that the plan names delays caused by damage to an aircraft.

What changes here is not a flight delayed by a storm but a plane damaged by one. Travelers worried about that second kind should plan for it before they book.

A federal system failure is the easiest exemption to accept, because no airline runs air traffic control. Under the new rule, travelers held up by a government outage that threatens safe flying should stop expecting meal or hotel vouchers, according to Fox 5 DC. The nationwide ground stop in November 2026 fits that description even though nothing actually failed. CNBC reports that FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford said the agency would not accept any risk that the problem might cut an air traffic center off from its planes. No FAA facility went dark, and the cause of the instability stayed unknown for about 45 minutes. Once the rule applies, a cautious decision by federal officials such as this one will cost travelers the airline's help without any fault on the airline's part.

The government label can also cover a failure that a private contractor may have caused. "What we're working to determine was whether we had incompetence, meaning was it user error, installator error from L3 Harris, or did they install a faulty router," Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said, as reported by CNBC. No answer to that question would move the delay back into the airline's column, because the airline neither ran the system nor installed the equipment. The traveler's claim depends on the category, not on whose mistake caused the trouble.

The cancellations did not end when flights resumed. Friday's cancellations were the second-highest since the January blizzard, and roughly 6,500 more flights were canceled on Saturday, according to Cirium and FlightAware figures reported by CNBC. Disruption that runs into a weekend is when a hotel room and a meal matter most, and travelers caught in it will no longer get either from the airline.

An outage that seems to be no one's fault can turn out to be the most expensive kind for the traveler waiting at the gate.

A delay blamed on cabin cleaning can now leave passengers with very different outcomes, depending on why the cabin needs cleaning. Cleaning a cabin after a passenger dies on board is among the 10 types of disruption newly excluded from the airline's control, according to Fox 5 DC. Travelers $TRV held up by that cleanup generally should not expect the airline to cover a meal or a hotel room. Ordinary cleaning delays stay the airline's responsibility, and only a death changes how the delay is classed. The exemption therefore gives airlines no general excuse for a slow turnaround between flights. When a gate agent says the plane is being cleaned, that word alone tells a waiting traveler nothing about what the airline owes. The reason behind the cleaning decides it.

An airline does not cause a passenger's death, and it does not cause every medical crisis on board either. SF Gate reports that medical emergencies the carrier did not cause are also covered by the exclusion, and so are unruly passengers. In each of these events, a person in the cabin starts the delay, and the airline does not. That makes this exemption tightly drawn and hard to argue with on grounds of fairness. The airline did not create the problem, and the rule does not ask it to pay for a meal or a room because of it.

Even so, a delay after a death on board can still cost a traveler a connection and a night away from home, and the airline's voucher for that night is no longer part of the deal.

The new rule leaves the most familiar causes of delay with the airlines. Routine upkeep and crew scheduling problems remain the airline's responsibility, according to Fox 5 DC. A plane held for its regular checks, or a flight stuck because the crew schedule fell apart, still counts against the carrier. Maintenance and crew problems were among the Department of Transportation's examples of controllable delays, according to a CNBC report published in 2024. This is the part of the old bargain that survives for travelers. When the cause is plainly the airline's own housekeeping, its promises of meals and rooms still apply, but only as long as everyone agrees the work was routine.

The word "maintenance" can now either cost travelers their meal and hotel or keep those perks in place, and the difference turns on whether the work was planned. A carrier still owns the delay when it pulls a plane for planned work, but a repair that comes up suddenly or that regulators order now falls on the other side of the line. Airlines therefore have money riding on how they describe a delay, because under one description they still owe the vouchers and under the other they don't.

Even a delay that stays the airline's responsibility comes with no set federal payout, and what a stranded traveler gets is up to the carrier's own policy. "There is this kind of gray area where we're at the mercy of what the airline's policy is," Eric Napoli, chief legal officer at AirHelp, said in 2024, as reported by CNBC. After a crew no-show or a scheduled check, the help a traveler receives still depends on which airline they booked.

When a delay is blamed on maintenance, travelers should ask whether the work was scheduled, and read the carrier's written commitments before they fly rather than at the gate.

Travelers $TRV who lose a meal or a hotel room under the new rule keep one protection that has nothing to do with who caused the delay. Fox 5 DC reports that the reason behind a cancellation or a major schedule change has no bearing on refunds, and a passenger who refuses the altered trip can get the money back. Besides a long delay, a significant change includes a switch to another airport, extra connections or a downgrade to a lower class of seat, according to the U.S. Department of Transportation. A switched airport counts the same way whether a storm or a mechanic forced the switch, and a traveler can collect the refund even when the cause is one the rule now exempts. The catch is that the traveler has to turn down the trip. Passengers who accept a rebooking and wait it out give up the refund, because this right offers a way out of the trip rather than comfort during the wait.

A delay becomes a cash matter at a fixed point on the clock. The U.S. Department of Transportation defines a significant change on a domestic flight as a departure or arrival moved by more than 3 hours, and an international flight has to move by more than 6 hours to count. Travelers abroad have to sit through a bigger disruption before they can walk away with their money. The federal government set these lines, and no airline can decide on its own that a long delay was minor.

Refunds were once the main thing passengers fought airlines over. In 2020, when such grievances peaked, 87% of the air travel complaints filed with federal regulators concerned refunds, according to the U.S. Department of Transportation. The department wrote refunds into a federal rule, while mandatory compensation and amenities were only at the proposal stage when it issued that rule. The refund right remains unchanged under the new rule. "Passengers deserve to get their money back when an airline owes them — without headaches or haggling," then-U.S. Transportation Secretary Pete Buttigieg said.

Once a delay passes the threshold, the strongest move may be to decline the rebooking and claim the cash rather than wait for a voucher that may never come.
