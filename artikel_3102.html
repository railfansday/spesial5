The Stock Market Sounds an Alarm as Investors Get Bad News About President Donald Trump's Economy

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

The stock market could decline sharply as persistent inflation and rich valuations fuel investor anxiety.

By Trevor Jennewine – Nov 22, 2026 at 4:21AM EST

The S&P 500 (^GSPC -3.70%) and Nasdaq Composite (^IXIC -5.28%) delivered positive returns for much of 2026, supported by strong corporate earnings and enthusiasm surrounding artificial intelligence. But both indexes recently entered market correction territory, falling more than 10% from their respective highs.

Unfortunately, the situation could get worse. Investors recently received troubling updates about President Donald Trump's economy: Inflation accelerated in September, and subsequent estimates suggest pricing pressures have continued to intensify. Meanwhile, the stock market is sounding an alarm last seen during the dot-com bubble.

President Donald Trump sits with his hands clasped during a meeting at the White House, surrounded by administration officials. Image source: Official White House Photo.

Inflation accelerated in September, complicating the Federal Reserve's effort to restore price stability. The Consumer Price Index (CPI) increased 3.7% from the previous year, up from 3.4% in August and the highest reading since May.

Research from the Federal Reserve Banks of St. Louis and Minneapolis helps explain why prices are rising more quickly. President Trump's tariffs were the primary driver of inflation earlier in the year, but supply shocks driven by energy sector prices have since become the primary culprit. The Iran war has disrupted Middle Eastern oil shipments, pushing crude oil prices and transportation costs higher.

Unfortunately, the inflation outlook remains troubling. The Cleveland Fed estimates that headline CPI inflation is trending toward 3.9% in November. And core CPI inflation (which excludes volatile food and energy prices) is projected to accelerate to 2.9%, up from 2.6% in September, suggesting that elevated energy prices are spreading to other areas of the economy.

The Federal Reserve raised its benchmark interest rate in September, the first rate hike in three years, but investors expect another rate hike in December due to persistent inflation. That bodes ill for the stock market because higher interest rates increase borrowing costs for consumers and businesses, potentially slowing economic growth and creating a drag on corporate profits. They also make bonds more attractive relative to stocks, which can put downward pressure on equity valuations.

The price-to-earnings (PE) ratio is one of the most widely used equity valuation metrics. It divides a company's share price by its earnings per share from the last 12 months. But the cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings (CAPE) ratio takes a different approach, dividing the inflation-adjusted price by the average inflation-adjusted earnings from the last decade. That methodology smooths fluctuations in corporate profits caused by the business cycle.

The S&P 500's CAPE ratio was 40.1 in October, marking the sixth straight monthly reading above 40. Previously, the index's CAPE ratio last exceeded 40 during the dot-com crash in September 2000. Unfortunately, such rich valuations have often preceded dismal returns, as shown in the table below.

Historically, following months in which the S&P 500's CAPE ratio exceeded 40, the index returned an average of 0% over the next six months; it declined by an average of 3% over the next year; and it declined by an average of 19% over the next two years. In other words, history suggests the stock market is headed much lower.

However, one limitation of the CAPE ratio is its backward-looking nature. It does not incorporate forward earnings estimates, meaning it may overstate valuations when corporate profits are growing quickly. Artificial intelligence has already contributed to higher profit margins and higher earnings growth across the S&P 500, and that trend is projected to continue.

Additionally, the historical returns shown in the table reflect overlapping periods during the dot-com bubble, rather than numerous independent market episodes. Consequently, they illustrate the risks associated with extreme valuations, but do not definitively predict what will happen next.

Nevertheless, investors should not dismiss this warning entirely The stock market remains expensive by historical standards, and the prospect of higher interest rates could put additional pressure on valuations. Together, those headwinds could push the S&P 500 into a bear market. If that happens, remember that dips have historically been great buying opportunities: The S&P 500 has rebounded from every past drawdown.
