Wawa Brings Community, Connection, and Hoagies to Nashville

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Ask any Philadelphian about Wawa, and they won’t just tell you about the Pennsylvania-born chain of convenience stores known for their freshly prepared foods, coffee, fuel services, and surcharge-free ATMs. They’ll tell you a story about that time they ran into their childhood best friend at Wawa and caught up while waiting in line for their morning cup of coffee. They’ll tell you their favorite hoagie and a story about which location they were standing in the first time they tried it. They’ll talk about that last Wawa rest stop before arriving at their family’s annual beach trip and how their mom would always buy a few extra bags of chips to keep the kids happy on the last stretch of the journey.

That’s because Wawa is more than a convenience store. It’s a way of life for those who know it. So, there were no two better longtime customers to meet up at Nashville’s first-ever in-town Wawa store than Pennsylvania native Joseph Hudak, host of Rolling Stone’s Nashville Now podcast, and Philly-raised Devon Gilfillian, a singer and songwriter now based in Music City.

It was a homecoming of sorts for the two, who toured the new location with all the excitement of a couple of kids on Christmas Eve, the day before the store’s grand opening. As Gilfillian enjoyed his meatball hoagie and Hudak reveled in an Italian Shorti, the two talked about the importance of finding community, putting down new roots in Music City, and, of course, all things Wawa.

“Community is everything to me. That’s why I’m here in Nashville,” Gilfillian said. Having moved to town in 2013, the singer-songwriter says the city’s inclusive spirit has played a significant role in his evolution as an artist. Unlike some of the competitive environments he’s seen in other major music cities, Nashville offered something different.

“It’s supportive. It’s collaborative,” he says. “They put their egos away when it comes to Nashville.”

For Gilfillian, that willingness to work together is part of what makes the city’s music scene so special. Regardless of genre, he believes musicians are better when they support one another rather than compete. “We’re stronger together,” he says, echoing a sentiment that extends well beyond the recording studio. It’s a philosophy that has helped shape his own musical journey. Since relocating from the Philadelphia area, Gilfillian says his musical tastes and songwriting skills have expanded, allowing him to reach new audiences along the way. He recently wrapped a five-week tour in support of his latest album, Time Will Tell, released in June, performing the new material with his four-piece band.

Nashville has become a home for more than just his music. Gilfillian’s parents have also relocated to Middle Tennessee, where his father has found his own musical community. Every Wednesday, he plays congas in a jam session at Flamingo Cocktail Club in the city’s Wedgewood-Houston neighborhood. It’s a fitting example of the connections that can grow when people find their place in a new city.

And just like the musicians who move to Nashville to find their own creative community, Wawa is focused on putting down meaningful roots in each of its new locations — be it inside the store or out in the community. The company’s investment in Tennessee and its people is apparent. Since opening its first store in the state in June 2026, Wawa has expanded to six cities across six counties. With each store employing an average of 35 associates, the Southern expansion has created more than 250 long-term jobs for Tennesseans.

As part of each of the seven grand openings in the state, Wawa and The Wawa Foundation have contributed more than $27,000 to support Special Olympics Tennessee athlete programs, USO morale programs at Fort Campbell (which straddles the Tennessee/Kentucky state line), Second Harvest Food Bank of Middle Tennessee hunger relief efforts, and Monroe Carrell Jr. Children’s Hospital at Vanderbilt child life programs. Each celebration has also featured Wawa’s signature “Hoagies for Heroes” hoagie-building competition, where local first responders compete to build the most hoagies in two minutes. Wawa makes contributions to charities selected by each department, resulting in $14,000 in support of local organizations so far.

And it gives Wawa fans new and old a sense of connection. As Gilfillian and Hudak debated the best chips to accompany their hoagies (long hots for the latter and kettle-cooked jalapeño for the former), they marveled at the fact that their beloved Wawa was now so close to home.

“When I moved to Nashville, Tennessee, in 2012, I found everything I needed. But what I didn’t have…was a Wawa,” said Hudak before asking Gilfillian, “Did you ever think you’d see the day?”
