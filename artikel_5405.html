Michigan’s next governor hasn’t talked to Trump yet—but wants to work with him on a 50% auto tariff set for her first day

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Michigan Governor-elect Jocelyn Benson hasn’t spoken to President Donald Trump since winning the state’s governor’s race as part of the blue tsunami that swept across the nation earlier this month.

“Not yet, but I intend to,” Benson said Monday at the Fortune 500 Innovation Forum in Detroit in an interview with Fortune editorial director Kristin Stoller. “We’ve all got to work together to better our economy, to better Michigan.”

Top of mind, though, is a tariff that could land on her first day in office. After negotiations between Washington and Ottawa broke down in August, Trump threatened to raise tariffs on Canadian-made cars, trucks, and parts to 50% beginning Jan. 1, 2027. That’s the same day Benson takes over from term-limited Gov. Gretchen Whitmer.

Benson said she hopes for opportunities to collaborate, “first and foremost, in reducing the impact of these tariffs.” She recalled touring auto parts distributor Detroit Axle in October, where she said the owner told her: “I’m sending millions of dollars into paying these tariffs, and it’s money that I had saved to expand my business, to create more jobs.”

Last year, Detroit Axle said it might have to shut down a warehouse and lay off more than 100 workers, although it later backtracked. But owner Mike Musheinesh told the Associated Press, “It’s a market of who is able to survive; it’s not a matter of who can thrive.”

Benson said a governor can uniquely go to Canada and broker a Michigan-specific agreement to protect jobs and lower costs, something she promised to pursue on the campaign trail. She also said she intends to negotiate “aggressively” with the federal government for carve-outs that lessen the tariffs’ impact. While campaigning, she argued levies on Canadian imports had set the average Michigan household back $5,600—more than any other state.

Tariffs are just one piece of Benson’s broader plan to fix affordability.

“Our campaign was about affordability through the lens of costs down, wages up, knowing that there’s two sides of that coin,” Benson told Fortune. Michigan’s per capita incomes “have also been stagnant for far too long,” she said, adding that most jobs in the state’s economy pay $60,000 or less. In 2025, Michigan ranked in the lower half of median wages in the U.S., according to the Michigan Center for Data and Analytics.

Benson said part of the governor’s job is bringing in new, higher-paying jobs and training workers to fill those roles so paychecks will “go up and not stay stagnant.” On the cost side, she pitched herself as “the CEO of state government,” saying she plans to streamline how the state delivers health care, housing development, and childcare to save money.

“You’re actually running an institution, and you have metrics and a strategic plan and delivering results,” she said regarding referring to herself as the CEO of Michigan. As Michigan’s secretary of state, she overhauled the state’s DMV offices, which she said eliminated wait times and made the agency “a poster child for efficiency.”

Michigan voters also sent progressive Democrat Abdul El-Sayed to the U.S. Senate over Republican Mike Rogers. Benson said the commonality between the two wins was “costs down and wages up,” along with ending corruption.

“It’s a team effort,” she said of lowering costs. “It’s partnerships, but there’s a lot you can do as the CEO of the state to drive down costs and bring more well-paying jobs in.”

Sydney Lake is an associate editor at Fortune, where she writes and edits news for the publication's global news desk.
