Who calls NFL plays? How a play-caller differs from a coordinator

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Free daily briefing on global business news.

NFL play-caller vs. coordinator roles explained, from helmet radios and booth versus sideline setups to why head coaches keep or hand over the play sheet

The coach with "offensive coordinator" as a job title is not always the coach deciding what happens on third down. In the NFL, the title and the decision can belong to two different people. One is a job title. The other is a game-day task. The NFL play-caller vs. coordinator split explains much of how a coaching staff divides its work on game day.

A coordinator is an assistant coach who runs one of a team's three units: offense, defense or special teams. The coordinator reports to the head coach and oversees the position coaches in that unit. During the week, the coordinator usually builds the game plan, runs meetings and teaches the scheme to players.

A play-caller does something narrower. That coach picks the specific play, formation or defensive call for each snap while the game is underway. On offense, the call travels by radio to the quarterback. On defense, it goes to one designated player wearing a radio helmet.

In many cases, the coordinator does both jobs. The play sheet can still sit with someone else. Some head coaches keep play-calling for themselves while a coordinator holds the title. Andy Reid, Sean McVay and Kyle Shanahan have each been known for calling their own offense. In those setups, the coordinator's role leans toward planning and teaching rather than snap-by-snap decisions.

The difference shapes how credit and blame get handed out. Fans and media often judge an offense or defense by the coordinator's title, even when another coach chose the plays. It also affects how a head coach spends a game. A head coach who calls plays is managing the clock, challenges and personnel at the same time.

An NFL play-caller is the coach who chooses the specific play, formation or defensive call for each snap of a game. The role is defined by that in-game decision, not by a job title.

On offense, the play-caller picks what the unit runs next. The choice can cover the play itself or the formation the offense lines up in. The call reaches the quarterback through a radio in his helmet.

On defense, the task means selecting the defensive call for the snap. That call does not go to the quarterback. It travels by radio to one designated defensive player instead.

The work repeats throughout the game. A play-caller does not make one choice before kickoff and then step back. Every snap requires a fresh decision, and that decision has to be made before the ball is snapped again.

Who holds the task varies from team to team. The coordinator for that unit usually calls the plays. A head coach can keep the task instead. That is why the play-caller and the coordinator are not always the same person.

Whichever coach holds the play sheet on game day is the one choosing, snap by snap, what the offense or defense runs.

An NFL coordinator is an assistant coach who runs one of a team's three units: the offense, the defense or special teams. The title describes a position in the staff hierarchy, not a single game-day task.

Each unit has its own coordinator. The offensive coordinator oversees the side of the ball trying to score. The defensive coordinator oversees the side trying to stop the opponent. The special teams coordinator oversees the groups that take the field for kicks, punts and returns.

The title sits in the middle of the coaching staff. A coordinator answers to the head coach, who holds authority over the whole team. Below the coordinator are the position coaches assigned to that unit.

That supervisory line is the core of the role. On offense, position coaches typically work with specific groups such as quarterbacks, running backs, wide receivers, tight ends and the offensive line. On defense, they typically handle the defensive line, linebackers and defensive backs. The coordinator directs those coaches so each position group fits the unit's overall approach.

Special teams is built differently from the other two units. Its coordinator does not oversee a fixed group of starters. Kick, punt and return units draw many of their players from the offense and the defense. They play alongside specialists such as the kicker, punter and long snapper. Many of those players also work under position coaches on offense or defense during the week.

The reporting line also limits a coordinator's independence. The head coach sets the direction for the team. The coordinator runs one unit within that direction and answers to the head coach for how it performs.

A coordinator title, on its own, does not settle who selects plays during a game. That task is assigned separately and is not automatically part of the job. An offensive coordinator may or may not be the coach sending in each call on a given Sunday.

Play-calling is one task inside a coordinator's job, not the whole job, and it can belong to someone else. A coordinator usually builds the weekly game plan, leads meetings and teaches the scheme. Choosing plays during the game is a distinct duty.

The two responsibilities sit at different points in the week. Coordinating happens mostly before kickoff. The game plan, the meetings and the teaching all shape what a unit is prepared to run on game day.

Play-calling starts once the game does. It is the choice of a specific play, formation or defensive call for each snap. That choice draws from the plan, but it is made in real time.

The tasks can be separated. One coach does not have to hold both. The coordinator title describes the assistant who runs the offense, the defense or the special teams unit. Nothing in that title requires the same person to pick each play.

A team can have a coordinator who designs and teaches the scheme while another coach makes the in-game calls. In that setup, the coordinator's work shows up in how the unit is prepared. The play-caller's work shows up in which part of that preparation gets used, and when.

The split can happen on either side of the ball. A head coach may keep the offensive calls. A head coach with a defensive background can take over the defensive calls instead. In either case, the coordinator keeps the title and the weekly preparation duties. Whoever holds the play sheet, the coordinator still reports to the head coach and oversees the unit's position coaches.

The overlap also explains why the two roles are easy to confuse. The coordinator usually holds both tasks. That makes the title and the task look like the same thing. They are not. One is a position on the staff chart. The other is a responsibility the staff assigns.

Andy Reid, Sean McVay and Kyle Shanahan have each been known for calling their own offense as NFL head coaches. In that arrangement, the head coach picks the offensive plays on game day while an assistant holds the offensive coordinator title.

The setup separates a title from a task. The offensive coordinator keeps the title and still reports to the head coach. The head coach keeps the play sheet and makes the snap-by-snap offensive calls.

All three examples come from the offensive side of the ball. Each coach is associated with choosing the plays himself rather than passing that job to the assistant whose title suggests it.

A play-calling reputation describes what a coach has been known for. It does not show who holds the play sheet for a particular team in a particular season. A head coach can keep the duty or give it to a coordinator, and that choice can change.

The coordinator title alone cannot tell a viewer who chose a given play. In staffs run the way these three coaches have been known to run them, the answer sits with the head coach, not the coordinator.

The NFL lets a play-caller speak directly to the quarterback through a radio receiver inside his helmet. That helmet is marked with a green dot. The link cuts off when 15 seconds remain on the play clock.

Only one quarterback helmet carries the receiver, and the green dot shows which one it is. The coach choosing the play talks into a headset. The call travels from there to that single receiver, and no other offensive player hears it by radio.

The play has to reach the quarterback before the cutoff. After that point, the radio goes silent and he runs the snap without coaching input. Any adjustment from there has to come from the players on the field.

The cutoff makes timing part of the play-caller's job. Choosing the right call is not enough on its own. The call also has to arrive with enough time left for the quarterback to hear it before the link drops. A slow decision on the sideline or in the press box shrinks that window.

The defense uses its own version of the setup. One designated defensive player wears a helmet with a radio. That player is the defense's link to the coaching staff. The defensive call reaches the rest of the unit through him.

The two sides gained this tool at different times. League rules have allowed quarterback radios since 1994. Defensive radios followed in 2008, 14 years later. Through that stretch, only the offense could receive a call by helmet radio.

The 2008 change put both units on matching terms. Each side now works with the same basic arrangement: one connected helmet and one shared cutoff point on the play clock.

Those two limits shape how a coach uses the link. One helmet per unit means a single player carries each call to the rest of his teammates. The fixed cutoff sets how much a coach can say before a snap. It also marks the moment the players take over.

An NFL play-caller works from one of two places on game day: the press box or the sideline. The press box offers a higher view of formations, while the sideline offers direct contact with players.

The choice applies to offense and defense alike. Any coach who holds the play sheet, whether a coordinator or a head coach, works from one of these two spots.

From the press box, the play-caller looks down on the field from above. That elevated angle makes it easier to see how an opponent lines up before the snap. Formations are harder to read from ground level, where players block one another from view.

The sideline trades that overhead view for proximity. A caller standing on the field can talk with players in person between series. Those conversations happen face to face instead of being passed along by another coach.

Neither location cuts the play-caller off from the rest of the staff. Coaches in both spots wear headsets that link them during the game. A caller upstairs can stay in contact with coaches on the field. A caller on the sideline can hear from staff in the box.

The location shapes which kind of information reaches the play-caller most directly. Upstairs, it is the wider picture of the opponent's setup. On the field, it is the players who carry out each call.

A defensive play-call tells 11 players three things before the snap: the front, the coverage and whether to blitz.

The front describes how the defense lines up near the line of scrimmage. It sets where the defensive linemen and linebackers stand before the ball is snapped. A front choice shapes which gaps each player is responsible for against the run.

Fronts are often named by their personnel. A 4-3 front uses four down linemen and three linebackers. A 3-4 front uses three down linemen and four linebackers.

The coverage governs the players farther from the ball. It tells cornerbacks, safeties and linebackers how to handle receivers once the play begins. In man-to-man coverage, a defender follows one assigned player. In zone coverage, defenders guard areas of the field instead of specific opponents. Coverages are also named by structure. Cover 2, for example, splits the deep part of the field between two safeties.

The blitz is the optional part of the call. A blitz sends extra defenders after the quarterback, beyond the players who rush on a standard down. A linebacker or a defensive back can be the added rusher. An extra rusher usually leaves one fewer defender in coverage. That trade-off means the blitz and the coverage have to fit together.

Those three pieces travel as one call, not as separate instructions. The play-caller sends the full call to the one defender wearing a radio helmet. That player then passes it to teammates, either in the huddle or at the line of scrimmage.

The relay makes the radio-helmet player the link between the coaching staff and the rest of the unit. Every teammate depends on him to repeat the call accurately before the snap.

The defensive coordinator is the usual caller. A head coach with a defensive background can take that role instead. When that happens, the coordinator keeps the title. The head coach, not the coordinator, chooses the front, coverage and blitz on each snap.

The head coach generally has final say on fourth down, choosing whether to punt, kick a field goal or go for it. The special teams coordinator advises on that choice, and the head coach makes it.

Fourth down is a decision that sits above any single unit. It does not belong to the offense, the defense or special teams alone. It falls to the coach in charge of the whole team.

Each option sends a different group onto the field. A punt brings out the punting unit. A field goal try brings out the kicking unit. Going for it keeps the offense on the field.

The question on fourth down is not only which play to run. It is which unit takes the next snap.

The special teams coordinator manages the kicking, punting and return units. Two of the three fourth-down options use those units. That places the coordinator's advice inside two of the three choices a head coach weighs.

The return unit covers the other side of the kicking game. It takes the field when the opponent punts or kicks. That group also falls under the special teams coordinator.

The division matches how authority runs on a coaching staff. A coordinator runs a unit and gives input. The head coach decides questions that cross from one unit to another.

A coordinator calls plays only when the head coach hands over that duty. The decision often gets made when a team hires a coordinator. At that point, the head coach chooses whether to delegate the play sheet or keep it.

The authority to decide follows the reporting line. A coordinator answers to the head coach. The head coach therefore sets the terms of the job, including who picks the call for each snap.

The title alone does not settle the question. Two coaches can both be offensive coordinators on different staffs and still do different work on game day. One chooses the call for every snap. The other leaves that choice to the head coach once the game starts.

That split changes how much control one coach has over a unit. A coordinator who calls plays has more direct influence over the unit during games. The order of calls and the response to what the opponent shows run through that coach's headset.

A coordinator who does not call plays fills a different role. That coach works mainly as a planner and a teacher. The coordinator still builds the weekly game plan and teaches the scheme in meetings. That influence shows up in how the unit is prepared, not in the choice of each snap.

Game-day workload is one reason the duty gets delegated. A head coach who calls plays has to handle other game-day decisions at the same time. Many head coaches hand over the play sheet for that reason.

The same choice exists on defense. The defensive coordinator is the usual caller there. A head coach with a defensive background can take that role instead. In that case, the defensive coordinator moves into the planning role.

For a coaching candidate, the assignment shapes the job. Two openings with the same title can carry different authority on game day. One includes control of the play sheet, and the other does not.

Fans and media often give credit or blame for an NFL offense or defense to the coordinator, based on the title. That habit can miss the coach who actually chose each play during the game.

The title is the public label attached to a unit. Who holds the play sheet is harder to see from the stands or on a broadcast. A head coach can keep play-calling while an assistant carries the offensive coordinator title.

The same split can happen on defense. The defensive coordinator usually calls that side of the ball. A head coach with a defensive background can take over that role. The coordinator's name then stays attached to calls made by someone else.

When the roles sit with different coaches, the coordinator's name still tends to absorb the reaction. A stalled drive can be pinned on the coordinator even when the head coach made the call. The reverse also happens. A strong performance can be credited to the coordinator even when the in-game choices came from someone else.

The split is not all-or-nothing. A coordinator who does not call plays usually still builds the weekly game plan and teaches the scheme. That coordinator can shape the plan the calls come from without choosing a single snap.

The head coach's game-day load adds another layer. A head coach who calls plays is also managing the clock. That coach is deciding on challenges and handling personnel at the same time. Each of those duties competes for attention with the next call.

The gap between title and task shapes how a unit's results are judged. The coordinator title names who runs the unit. The play-calling assignment names who chose its plays in a given game. Those can be the same coach or two different ones. Knowing which coach holds the play sheet, not just the title, shows who made those calls.
