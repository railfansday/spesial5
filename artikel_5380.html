She was a go-to Manhattan babysitter as a teen. Now she’s running a $100 million-a-year AI startup whose clients include the billionaire Koch family

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

From a nationwide FAA ground stop to Microsoft and Chase outages that disrupted millions of customers, a string of recent technology failures has highlighted the need to modernize aging infrastructure while raising new fears about the risks of an increasingly AI-dependent world.

But for Jaclyn Rice Nelson, cofounder and CEO of Tribe AI, that tension is exactly why businesses should embrace the technology—not run from it.

“I don’t think that anxiety around AI is unwarranted. I think it’s very real,” Nelson told Fortune.

“[AI] is ours to shape,” Nelson said. “And the only way you can shape it is if you understand it, and the only way you understand it is if you use it—and use it a lot.”

Nelson, 41, has built her business around helping companies do just that. Founded in 2019, Tribe AI has grown to a $100 million annual run rate by helping organizations—from Fortune 500s like Hyatt and MetLife to multibillion-dollar private conglomerates like Koch Inc.—put AI to work.

Tribe said on Nov. 18 that its annualized revenue had crossed $100 million, up 3.8 times from a year earlier, as more clients moved AI projects out of the pilot stage. “The hard part is getting AI into production inside a complex business,” Nelson said in the announcement.

Nelson’s instinct to build something of her own predates the AI boom by decades.

She grew up in New York City with a single mother raising two children. After transitioning from teaching to business, her mother managed to send both kids to private school in Manhattan—a sacrifice that left a lasting impression on Nelson.

“It was someone in my life who had done really hard things, who I had seen do hard things, and who also refused to let things slip for me,” Nelson said.

That set Nelson in motion to seek her own financial independence. As a teenager, she started babysitting and grew the side hustle into what she once described as a “babysitting empire.”

“I became this trusted, fairly scarce asset,” she said, adding that she became the go-to babysitter for entire New York apartment buildings, with parents competing to book her on Saturday nights.

But beyond the money, it also taught her an early lesson in business: “Be the trusted person, be dependable.”

After graduating in 2008 with a degree in political science from the University of Michigan, Nelson then took a more traditional route to financial independence: Wall Street. She spent two years as an analyst at Citigroup, before moving to Silicon Valley to work for Google—and brought the same work ethic that had served her in banking.

“I moved to a new city where I didn’t really know anyone,” she said. “And so I just worked banking hours at Google. I was always like the person still in the office, waving my hand to get the light sensors to go back on.”

Her work ethic, and willingness to take on responsibilities outside her core role, led her to make a career jump to Google’s growth equity fund, CapitalG (then known as Google Capital) in 2015 as its first business-focused employee among a team of dozens of engineers. Three years later, she left to pursue an opportunity of her own, cofounding Tribe AI in 2019.

Since 2019, the company has grown to roughly 250 employees, after its team grew fivefold this year, the company said—all while raising less than $10 million in venture funding and reaching profitability.

As Tribe continues to expand, Nelson said she’s looking for people who are deeply curious about AI, willing to take on difficult challenges, and humble enough to recognize how much they still have to learn.

“You have to be kind of humbled by how little you know every day and motivated by learning and getting better,” she said.

One of Tribe AI’s major customers is Koch Inc., one of the world’s largest privately held companies. The business conglomerate employs about 130,000 people and generates more than $125 billion in annual revenue.

“There’s a lot of businesses that run through the smartest guy in the room telling people what to do. We are the opposite of that,” Chase Koch, executive vice president of origination and partnerships at Koch and son of Charles, told Fortune.

Koch wanted to bring its decades-old management philosophy—centered on bottom-up principles developed by chairman and co-CEO Charles Koch—into the AI era. Working with Tribe, the project went from an idea to a launched product in just five months. The result is an AI-powered coaching tool designed to help employees put the principles into practice. Employees can describe a challenge at work and receive personalized guidance on how to approach it.

Chase Koch said he tried the tool himself after his boss told him he was taking on too many responsibilities. Talking through the problem with the AI helped him develop a plan to prioritize his work, delegate more effectively, and focus on his strengths.

For Nelson, the project and its speed points to a bigger opportunity for AI: making personalized coaching and development more accessible, without losing sight of the people it’s meant to help.

“The goal of this is not just to scale AI, it’s to make people better, to help them lean in on the things that they are going to be the most uniquely suited to do,” Nelson said. “I believe AI is giving people these superpowers, and this is a good example of that.”

For young professionals facing the same shift, Koch and Nelson had similar but straightforward advice: Figure out what makes you uniquely valuable, then learn how to use AI to make the most of it.

“If you understand the technology and how it works, what are the places you can use that power and strength to then change the trajectory of how it is used and the impact that it has on people and workforces and society?” Nelson said.

Koch echoed an emphasis on individual strengths: “Understand what your comparative advantage is. What is your gift combined with what you’re really passionate about learning and what gets you excited and gets you out of bed in the morning?”

Preston Fore is a reporter on Fortune's Success team.
