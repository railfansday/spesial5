‘The Altruists’ Review: Netflix’s Slight Sam Bankman-Fried Series Tells a Strange, Crooked Love Story

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Netflix’s “The Altruists,” the latest limited series about a modern-day tech scandal, posits a few sound-enough theories about what drives its featured fraudster to steal $8 billion of customers’ money. For instance, Sam Bankman-Fried (Anthony Boyle) grows up playing games but never actually grows up, so he struggles to perceive his cryptocurrency exchange, FTX, as anything beyond a game he’s meant to win. The unkempt, dead-eyed co-founder similarly has a hard time distinguishing between theoretical and actual consequences, so when a “mathematically optimal” strategy doesn’t pay out, all he sees is a smaller-than-expected number on the balance sheet — not a retirement account squandered or a college education lost. Arrested development and severe personalities are part and parcel for any tech founder story, so much so that, as a bleak sign of the times, they’re already being parodied. (In “The Audacity,” Billy Magnussen’s insecure, second-rate CEO loses his shit when a psych test shows he’s not on the spectrum.) On top of these familiar traits, “The Altruists” adds familiar language in the form of ear-piercing dog whistles — Sam speaks of of “moving fast” and “breaking things” one too many times — which make clear our antihero belongs among the villainous Silicon Valley set. He’s as much a product of the times as a very “special” boy.

Such echoes lend “The Altruists” a sense of “been there, broke that” amid a crowded Founders Fall. (Even if “The Social Reckoning” wasn’t for you, we all just rewatched “The Dropout,” no?) But creator and co-showrunner Graham Moore, alongside co-showrunner and executive producer Jacqueline Hoyt, center their scammer’s analysis around a fresh(ish) yet timeless vantage point: Sam is in love with Caroline Ellison (Julia Garner), and he has no idea how to express it. Their doomed romance sparks the creation and destruction of the third-largest digital currency exchange in the world (plus, you know, the subsequent prison sentences), as their inability to make sense of their emotions sullies their ethical ambitions for making billions (which were already pretty muddy).

It’s a shame, then, that their relationship proves as shallow as it is annoying. Wrapped in interminable, indicative nerd quirks — stutters and stammers, over-explanations and misplaced pride, regular references to Harry Potter, LARP, and math camp — Sam and Caroline’s grand romance isn’t sustainable enough entertainment to support an eight-hour story, nor is it explored with enough sincerity to break your heart (or expand your mind). Boyle and Garner’s performances are committed and their transformations convincing, but they’re in service of one theory about two people’s core deficiency that isn’t as revealing or rewarding as it needs to be.

Sam and Caroline’s meet-cute takes place — where else — at work. In 2016, Sam is an ambitious go-getter on Wall Street. His custom algorithms have earned attention from the higher-ups after successfully predicting the election results and then trading off those results to the tune of $150 million in profit. …but then those same algorithms doubled down and ended up losing twice what they initially made. So, as punishment, Sam is sent to interview interns, where he meets and bonds with a fellow trader.

During an introductory quiz, Caroline determines there’s no prize for second place, which means it’s the same as last place, so the “mathematically optimal strategy is to go all-in every time.” It’s the same strategy Sam used to win when he was an intern, and although Caroline goes bust, she’s hits big with Sam. His parents taught at Stanford, so he went to MIT to get away from them. Her parents taught at MIT, so she went to Stanford. They both were expected to be child math prodigies, but neither “prodigied.” Her ultimate goal is to solve the global water crisis. His is goal is equally altruistic, if much more vague.

“Look, I know I haven’t done it yet,” Sam says to Caroline. “Little things here and there, sure, but the big thing? I’m a little behind schedule. But I’m going to do it.”

His conviction wins her over — especially when they meet again, by chance, at a meeting for Effective Altruists — and soon enough they’re misreading flirtations and fumbling through his first sexual encounter. As they leave Wall Street behind to focus on “the big thing,” “The Altruists” jumps ahead to its second predominant timeline: Where their initial courtship and company start-ups take place in “Part 1: Rise,” even more screentime is dedicated to “Part II: Fall,” which picks up in 2022 when evidence of their theft starts to leak amid a massive party in the Bahamas.

The multiple-timeline structure ostensibly juxtaposes past promises from present betrayals, but it’s hardly necessary to get the point across (“how things have changed” doesn’t hit as hard when the characters look the same) and actively harmful to the supporting cast. Using “Rise” as the A-plot and “Fall” as the B-plot keeps Sam and Caroline in the center of things, which proves exhausting when they keep repeating themselves and strands everyone else in one-note (or sometimes no-note) archetypes who mainly exist to yell at the two leads.

Sam Trabucco (Alex Lawther) is the mirror version of Sam Bankman-Fried, a sweet, self-assured kid who actually believes in what FTX is doing, until the hard truth of what they’ve actually done sends him fleeing to the open seas. (SBF stays put, living in his delusion of innocence.) Nishad Singh (Karan Soni) is another mirror, first as a friend from the neighborhood who bonded with Mr. Bankman (Paul Reiser) and Mrs. Fried (Robin Weigert) in a way Sam never could, and later as a fellow sufferer of unrequited love for a co-worker, only his heart is pure where Sam’s is tainted.

Eight hours of the immoral, motormouthed, future-then-former billionaire is a lot to tolerate, especially when his greatest obstacle is one we know he’ll never overcome: Sam and Caroline may be stupidly brilliant with numbers, but they’re just plain stupid when it comes to emotions. “I don’t think you can smart your way out of feelings,” Sam is told when our central couple first starts dating — but that doesn’t stop them from trying. Start to finish, Sam never says the words, “I love you,” despite each episode pivoting around a noxious will-they-or-won’t-they relationship that ebbs and flows between a full-on, loud-and-proud romance and a business partnership manufactured to keep them together when logic alone could not.

Tolerance may vary, but after a few hours, “The Altruists” is too cutesy, too aware of its own cuteness, and ultimately too dedicated to the wrong questions: What went wrong with FTX has been explained again and again, and the series doesn’t pass up its chance to play the hits. (It’s “inspired by” a series of New York Magazine articles.) But “The Altruists” has the chance to consider what went wrong with Sam and Caroline’s souls, and it doesn’t dig deep enough to expose anything more than emptiness.

At its best, “The Altruists” is a whirlwind depiction of love not as a beautiful, life-saving devotion, but as an illogical and immoral excuse to wreak havoc. (Think of it as the most unlikely spinoff to “The Last of Us.”) Sam and Caroline use each other to justify unscrupulous (and illegal) actions that stack up until they’re trapped by the sheer scope of their misjudgment. But at least they’re trapped together. It’s a love story and an anti-love story at once; the audience sees the latter and the subjects convince themselves it’s the former.

That’s fascinating territory to explore, thematically and internally, but Moore and Hoyt’s series tells you what it’s about — explicitly, repeatedly, unmistakably — and then reiterates that statement over and over as the situations escalate. Ultimately, it gets caught in the same quandary as its subjects: The relationship’s pointed lack of evolution proves more redundant than revelatory, so when it comes to emotional enlightenment, “The Altruists” is out of its depth.

“The Altruists” premieres Thursday, November 19 on Netflix. All eight episodes will be released at once.
