Opinion: Why medical students should keep going to class

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Zhang is a second-year M.D. student at the Stanford University School of Medicine.

This article is part of a new First Opinion series on the future of medical school.

By this time of year, the sky is black when I step outside. The day has carried me from lectures on gastrointestinal cancer disparities to TAing microbiology, from student government debates to helping free-clinic patients complete paperwork for expensive medications. With the cold nipping at my skin, I look back at the Stanford medical school building, its windows glowing. There is still so much to do, and even more to feel. I call my mother, my home base in human form. Three thousand miles away, she is preparing for bed. Circumstances beyond her control have kept her from taking a domestic flight for decades, let alone visit me in California. Her first question is always, “Did you remember to eat dinner?” Her second: “How were your classes today?”

I am a class-goer. Most of my classmates are, too, though preclinical lecture attendance among medical schools has been declining nationally since the pandemic and the Step 1 exam’s transition to pass/fail. Given the flexibility of recordings and the time students devote to service, research, and other commitments, I understand why some choose to study independently and firmly believe in preserving students’ freedom to make that choice.

For me, though, attending lectures in person has become an important part of feeling at home. It is the most powerful way I take care of my mental health and the classmates I have come to see as my newfound family. I would like to make the case why it could for other medical students, too.

Part of that happiness comes from my daily calls with my immigrant mother. What I learn gives me something to share with the woman who gave up her dream of becoming a clinician so I could pursue mine: dissecting a cadaver for the first time, suturing, administering flu vaccines for undocumented folks, wrapping wound dressing around an unhoused man’s arm. Putting these experiences into words is an exercise of understanding the unmistakably human profession I am entering.

I once worried that telling her the good parts of my day would make the distance between us more painful. Her unconditional love for me throughout the years has taught me otherwise. There are many students like me who left the comfort of our communities to pursue higher education. We should not have to compromise on maintaining relationships with loved ones, which influences medical student well-being and belonging.

In graduate school, where life is more independent, class also gives us a place to come together. During lecture breaks, I learn about friends advocating against the medical bloodletting of horseshoe crabs, making spaces more accessible to people with disabilities, and bringing care to under-resourced youth through mobile vans. We ask to become involved in one another’s work. Other days, happiness is resting my head on a friend’s shoulder, sharing my next bucket-list read — Bryant Lin’s “Sunshine: An Exploration of Living When You Are Dying” — or suggesting that we try the new cafe with robot baristas. It is remembering I am not walking this road alone.

Just as importantly, being physically present gives me opportunities to check in with others. As smartphones and artificial intelligence mediate more of our lives, I worry how easily we can grow used to being alone. Hard workers are remarkable at hiding stress. Intelligent people are good at forgetting to be kind to themselves. Class gives us a place, an excuse, not only to struggle through challenging coursework in real time together, but be part of one another’s lives. In between lectures, I am giving a friend a relationship pep talk as we complain about dating in the Bay Area. The next moment, I am making plans to cheer on another friend at a marathon. After lab, I am curled up on the reading room couch beside a peer whose mother has just been diagnosed with cancer. Our parents are getting older and sicker. It is infuriating. You would think learning medicine would brace us for inevitable losses. False. But knowing there is an abundance of people here to catch you is a peace in its own form.

In the classroom, I have also come to see my education as one I help shape. Conversations with professors following lectures have allowed me to successfully advocate to integrate various topics in our curriculum: sex differences in aortic stenosis pathophysiology, the relationship between ALDH2*2 deficiency and esophageal cancer, overdose response training. Medicine is evolving by the day, and these exchanges make education a collaboration between learners and teachers, showing students that our voices matter.

I think often of watching Celine Song’s “Past Lives” with my mother in December 2024, when I was still in New York. The film introduces in-yun, the idea that an encounter between two people is the result of countless threads from their past lives intertwining. Today, I still wonder if this, too, is a past life, and we are already something else to each other in the next. Perhaps in the next, I am the uninsured one who needs translation, and she is the medical student sitting across from me at the free clinic. In this life, it is 2 a.m. Eastern, and she is calling to ask about my day. It is 11 p.m. in California, and I am at the beach with friends, squinting into the Pacific because someone yelled that they spotted a whale. I imagine the animal’s song, sung at a frequency so low it travels for thousands of miles underwater, enough to carry me home.

I crave a big future, expansive in experience and generosity. For others, that may mean studying independently to make room for research, paid work, service, or rest. Those choices deserve grace.

For me, it begins with being physically present during these four brief years. Honoring my mother means doing good when her familiar applause is far away and nurturing the classmates who will become tomorrow’s doctors. Here is my chance to find where I fit, then widen that space for the next person. I don’t plan to squander a second of it.

Brian Zhang is a second-year M.D. student at Stanford University School of Medicine, where he currently serves as student body president.
