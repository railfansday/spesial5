When AI Pulls the Comps, Make It Show Its Work

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Happy African American real estate agent looking at some leaflets of a house for sale

RISMedia’s September 25 Newsmakers roundup, “Tech Sharpens Strategy: Staying Ahead With the Right Tools,” asked agents and executives which systems are actually helping them capture market share and demonstrate value to clients.

One answer stuck with me. Veroushka Volkert of ONE Sotheby’s International Realty described using AI to produce listing remarks and email campaigns almost instantly, and also to pull real-time comps and list-to-sale data.

Those are two very different jobs, and I think our industry is starting to treat them as one. When AI drafts listing remarks, a mistake is cheap. You read it, fix a sentence and move on. When AI hands you a comp set, or a number a client will base an offer on, a mistake is expensive, and it usually isn’t visible on the page. A paragraph that reads well and a figure that is wrong look exactly the same.

I’ve invested in rental property for more than 20 years and own 50+ units across five states. Most of my expensive lessons came from numbers that looked finished and weren’t. One offering memorandum for a Chicago six-unit described the building as fully occupied on page two. The rent roll, around page 25, showed five of the six units vacant. Nothing flags that unless someone, or something, is told to reconcile the two.

So here is a simple standard I’d suggest for any agent or broker bringing AI into conversations about value or income. Before a number goes in front of a client, you should be able to answer three questions about it. Where did it come from? A comp, a vacancy rate or a rent estimate should trace back to a source you could name out loud if the client asked. “The model said so” is not a source. A rent figure delivered as a single confident point is also less useful than a range, because markets rarely hand you one number.

Was the arithmetic actually done, or approximated? Language models are remarkable at writing and at reading documents. They are not reliable calculators. A DSCR or a cap rate that is approximately right is worthless to a lender, and a buyer who relies on it finds out at the worst possible moment. The tools worth trusting read with AI and calculate with plain math.

What did it leave out? This is the question that protects investor clients most. In most jurisdictions property taxes reset at the purchase price, so a cap rate built on the seller’s current tax bill is overstated before the buyer even closes.

Roofs, HVAC and other core systems need reserves tied to their age, not an assumption that nothing will break. Who pays the water bill can swing expenses by $500 to $1,000 per unit per year, and it is often buried deep in the documents.

An AI that only answers what you asked will share your blind spots. None of this is an argument against AI. Agents who use it well are going to look much sharper than those who don’t, especially to investor buyers who expect underwriting and not just marketing.

But the question RISMedia put to its Newsmakers was about demonstrating value, and in anything involving money, demonstrating value means showing your work. The next time a system gives you a comp or a projected return in seconds, ask it where each figure came from, and whether you can change an assumption and watch the answer move. If it can’t tell you, that’s your cue to slow down before your client does.
