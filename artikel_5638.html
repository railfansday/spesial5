Rick: Well, let's turn to the one that I think is most surprising. And that is the question, does lowering Lp(a) levels reduce cardiovascular events? Now, for people that aren't familiar, Lp-little-a, or what's called lipoprotein(a), is known to be a risk factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease. And on many different levels, it's been shown to be associated with atherosclerosis. For a long time, we've not had any really good way of lowering it. Until recently, we have a number of new medications that have been shown to very effectively lower it, as much as 75% to 90%. And the presumption was, if you did that, you'd lower the risk of cardiovascular events.

But that really wasn't tested until this study just came out in the New England Journal of Medicine. Pelacarsen, it's an antisense oligonucleotide, which is very effective at lowering Lp(a) levels. And they administered it to about 4,100 patients subcutaneously on a monthly basis. And they followed these individuals over the course of 4.7 years.

Those that received pelacarsen had a significant decrease in their lipoprotein(a), over 70%. They looked at death, heart attack, stroke, or someone needing coronary revascularization. There was no difference at all in those that received pelacarsen and those that received placebo. What about those that had a baseline Lp(a) that was a little bit higher? Even in that group, there was no benefit with regard to decreasing cardiovascular events.

Elizabeth: I think a lot of the analysis of this trial are just really fascinating for people speculating on why in the world didn't this work. Is this just a stand-in for something else? And the other thing is, even in people who have that genetic predisposition to elevated Lp(a) levels, these folks were already on other things to try to keep them at lower risk. And so maybe the delta just wasn't high enough to show up.

Rick: We know that when you reduce cardiovascular risk factors, the more that you reduce, the better outcome you get. And so it was hypothesized that, sure, we could get your cholesterol down, but lowering Lp(a) would even be more advantageous. But apparently, it doesn't appear to be so. There are other agents that are being tested. It'll be interesting to see whether they have any benefit.

Elizabeth: More to come. Let's turn then to retatrutide once weekly in adults with knee osteoarthritis pain and obesity.

Retatrutide is an investigational triple agonist of GIP, GLP-1, and glucagon receptors. Sort of this idea that if we take a multifactorial approach to all of these things, is that going to result in a better outcome? They enrolled adults without diabetes who had painful knee osteoarthritis and a BMI [body mass index] of at least 27. They were randomly assigned to receive once-weekly subcutaneous injections at a couple dosages of this med or placebo. And they were looking at the change in their pain score as far as their knee osteoarthritis was concerned, and the percentage change in body weight at week 68. They had 445 participants in one arm of the trial, 574 in another.

And what they showed was that, sure enough, the retatrutide was able to have a significant impact on this knee osteoarthritis score. And clearly, they also had percentage changes in body weight that were significant as well. Sure enough, if you use this triple agonist in patients who come in with painful knee osteoarthritis and obesity, you reduce both their pain score and you reduce their obesity. For me, this trial really harkens to that same question of which is it, chicken or egg? Is it that you unweight the knee and therefore you result in less painful symptomatology?

Rick: Yes. This particular agent lowered weight and reduced knee pain. You're not terribly surprised because anything you do that reduces weight reduces knee pain. You could argue that it's that effect alone and nothing else. Although when you decrease fat cells, you decrease inflammation. I'd be interested in knowing if you took people that weren't obese and gave them this agent, would it reduce knee pain? Is there anything in here that surprised you at all?

Elizabeth: In the retatrutide group, they had a reduction of at least 50% in their pain score, but that was compared with 37%, almost 38%, reduction in those in the placebo group. And we've noted this in the past.

Rick: Yeah. Placebo effect. That's why all studies to assess whether a treatment is effective has to be compared to some placebo.

Elizabeth: Right. And of course, we have to note that we see the same side effects that we see with almost everything that's relative to the GLP-1s: nausea, diarrhea, constipation. For the most part, they resolved over time.

Rick: And they're usually associated with dose escalation. For people that are taking a GLP-1, I usually advise them, "Listen, as we increase the dose, we're going to do it slowly and methodically. You may experience these." But as you mentioned, it's usually mild and it will go away if they'll persist with it.

Elizabeth: OK. Still staying in the New England Journal.

Rick: This is the third study that I'm aware of that looks to see whether intensive blood pressure lowering is beneficial in people with type 2 diabetes. It follows on two other studies. One was the ACCORD study, and it showed that intensive blood pressure lowering, trying to get it below 120, was not beneficial. And they followed that with a study in China that did the same thing. And it showed just the opposite effect, primarily by reducing strokes, which are more prevalent in the Chinese population.

This study was done in Brazil, about 9,400 individuals, ethnically diverse. About two-thirds were women that had hypertension, type 2 diabetes, and a high cardiovascular risk. And they randomized them to blood pressure lowering to either get it below 140 versus 120, and followed these individuals over a mean follow-up of 4 years.

There was really no significant difference. So in this ethnically-diverse population, it appears that intensive blood pressure lowering in people with type 2 diabetes really doesn't reduce the occurrence of major cardiovascular events.

Elizabeth: Of course, what we know is that when we attempt these intensive blood pressure-lowering regimens, people object to polypharmacy, and particularly those who already have diabetes and are already taking other meds.

Rick: Yeah. In fact, for the individuals that were taking the less intensive, the median of 2.3 different medications, and those on the more intensive, 3.2. This was so well done. Oftentimes, when you get really intensive therapy, you have more episodes of hypotension and syncope falling out, sometimes fractures. But in this particular study, not. They approached it very methodically. It's just that it just didn't really help.

Elizabeth: Let's turn to the Journal of Medical Virology. I think this might be the first time we've ever actually talked about a study that was in this. They were looking at metformin at the time of COVID-19 infection and the risk of long COVID. And this, they identify as a target trial emulation study. They were looking to evaluate the impact of metformin prescribed at the time of COVID infection on the risk of developing long COVID, and they did it by examining electronic health records.

They conducted a new-user analysis of metformin, prescribed within 6 days of documented infection with COVID, versus experimental controls. And those were prescriptions for fluvoxamine, fluticasone, ivermectin, or montelukast. They had to have, as their inclusion criteria, a clinic visit in the 0 to 6 months and 6 to 12 months before infection. They excluded all folks who had had metformin or control within 12 months, and their primary outcome was long COVID development or death.

The number that they actually came up with was 248 in the metformin and control groups with an average age of 53. They had 4% in the metformin group develop long COVID or death versus 8.5% in the control group, seeming to show that there's a benefit of prescribing metformin when somebody presents with a new COVID infection.

Rick: As you mentioned, this was a different kind of study. It's an emulation trial. Usually, we look at randomized controlled trials as appearing to be the gold standard.

So in this particular case, individuals with COVID, some of whom received metformin, and then the other group, let's give them some medication that is not known to be beneficial with regard to COVID, because there are some in vitro and in vivo studies that suggest there is a benefit of metformin in people that have COVID. So those are the active comparators. They used an antidepressant, an anti-inflammatory, an anti-parasite, and then a respiratory drug. It looked like there was a benefit, a reduction in long COVID symptoms attributed to the metformin, because they didn't see with any of these other medications. So that's how this emulation study was done.

Elizabeth: Based on this study, Rick, would you say that you would, especially as we see different variants emerging, if one emerges that's going to be more severe, would you use metformin in patients with a new diagnosis of COVID?

Rick: That's an important distinction. So first of all, it's a new diagnosis. So they were given this within 6 days of the diagnosis. I would be inclined to offer it to the patient and say, "We're not going to be on this long term, but there is some data that suggests it can reduce the risk." I'll let the patient be involved, but I would not have any qualms about it. There's no harm associated in any of the studies. And again, there's pretty convincing data that it reduces the risk of long COVID symptoms by about 50%. So the answer is yes. Elizabeth, let me take it back to you. Would you take it if I prescribed it?

Elizabeth: Oh, I would take it for sure. I would also note that they used a phrase in this paper that I was completely unfamiliar with. They say, "An experimental control reduces unmeasured confounding, such as the likelihood of engagement with healthcare, immortal time bias, and the placebo effect." I've never heard of an "immortal time bias" before.

Rick: That has to do with when the diagnosis is made, when things are given. I'll give you a great example. If we're looking at how long people live and you have a patient group that's 5 years old, they're likely to live for another 80 years. If you start with people that are in their 80s, they're likely to live 5 years. Is there a mortality difference? It has to do with the time that the patient is enrolled in the trial, not the long-term outcome.
