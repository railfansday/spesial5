Cannabis use disorder is linked to higher odds of suicidal thoughts

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

Adults with cannabis use disorder reported suicidal thoughts far more often than other adults, and the gap held steady for a decade.

Adults with cannabis use disorder had 57% higher odds of reporting serious suicidal thoughts in the past year than adults without the disorder, even after researchers accounted for depression and other factors.

Across U.S. surveys from 2014 through 2023, researchers found no clear evidence that the link grew stronger.

Cannabis use disorder is not the same as using cannabis. It means having trouble controlling the habit even when it is clearly causing harm.

The survey measured it with nine yes-or-no questions about the past year.

Tosin Philip Oyetunji, a doctoral student in public health at the University of California, Merced, led the study with three colleagues there.

The team pooled ten years of the National Survey on Drug Use and Health, which the federal government runs every year. After minors and incomplete answers were set aside, 415,861 adults were left.

Among them, 4.0% met the criteria for the disorder. And 4.6% said that in the past 12 months they had seriously thought about killing themselves.

“What we would most like people to understand is that struggling with cannabis use can go hand in hand with mental health challenges that are not always visible,” Oyetunji said in an interview with Earth.com.

Adults with the disorder had 1.57 times the odds of reporting suicidal thoughts. The range around that figure runs from 1.44 to 1.72.

The comparison is adjusted, which matters more than usual here. The model accounted for depression, serious psychological distress and heavier drinking that met the criteria for alcohol use disorder.

The team also controlled for income, education, insurance, self-rated health, religious attendance and whether a person’s state had legalized recreational cannabis.

So the gap isn’t just depression showing up in a different column. Nobody was followed over time, though, so the survey can’t say which came first.

The team expected the link to strengthen. Cannabis potency has risen sharply since legalization began, and Canadian data had suggested a tightening link.

An earlier national study had also tied cannabis use to suicidal thoughts, plans and attempts.

The researchers found no clear evidence that the link strengthened. In the 2016 and 2022 surveys, the odds ran higher than in the first survey year.

Neither of those two survived the correction the team applied for testing ten years at once. Across the decade, the differences from one survey to the next were too small to rule out chance.

“A public health concern does not have to be getting worse to deserve attention,” Oyetunji said when Earth.com asked why the link had held while the drug changed.

Sidra Goldman-Mellor, a professor of public health at UC Merced and the study’s senior author, put the other half of it plainly.

“People with cannabis use disorder are still at a very substantially increased likelihood of experiencing serious suicidal thoughts, and that’s not something that is widely understood,” she said.

The disorder was not the only measure that showed the pattern. The survey’s criteria changed partway through the decade, so the team ran the analysis again using how often people used cannabis.

Nondaily users had 1.39 times the odds of reporting suicidal thoughts, compared with people who had not used cannabis at all. Daily or near-daily users had 1.65 times the odds.

“This suggests that patterns of cannabis use may also be important to consider when examining mental health, and it raises questions about how the frequency of use relates to suicidal thoughts,” Oyetunji told Earth.com.

The team also split the sample by sex, by age and by race and ethnicity.

Adults with the disorder had higher odds in every one of those groups, and the differences between the groups were too small to call real.

Everyone answered once, so nobody can say which came first. Every answer was also the person’s own account of a year they were remembering.

The study focused on serious suicidal thoughts. The link with cannabis use disorder may differ for suicide plans, attempts or deaths.

The survey also records nothing about what people were using. Potency, product type and whether someone vaped concentrates are all missing, so the study cannot test the potency question it raises.

Studies of high-THC products disagree anyway. A recent review found strong links between high-THC cannabis and the disorder itself or psychosis, and weaker ones for depression and anxiety.

Craving and withdrawal were left out of the disorder measure so the questions would match across years, which probably undercounts it.

The survey excluded people living in institutions and people experiencing homelessness who were outside shelters.

The authors raise one more possibility. As cannabis use spread, the group meeting the criteria may have shifted toward milder cases.

A shift like that would hold the average down even if nothing had changed for the most severe cases.

Two people can give this survey the same answers. In one of them, cannabis use deepened a crisis. In the other, a crisis drove heavier use.

A single round of questions cannot tell them apart. Researchers would have to follow the same people for years, with the potency and the products written down.

Oyetunji is widening the question rather than narrowing it.

“I want to better understand how these risks overlap, what drives them, and where we may be missing opportunities to help people before they experience serious harm,” he said.

Clinics don’t have to wait for that. Someone asking for help with cannabis may be carrying something they have not mentioned, and the authors argue that the right response is to ask.

The full study was published in the journal Journal of Studies on Alcohol and Drugs.
