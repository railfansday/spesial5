The Forever Chemicals Wreaking Havoc on Our Planet Are About to Meet Their Kryptonite

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Your water filter traps forever chemicals but can’t destroy them. This new one can, if we can afford it.

One of the most worrisome side effects of modern life is a class of chemicals called per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances, or PFAS. These man-made compounds show up in everything from non-stick pans to fast-food wrappers. They’re called “forever chemicals” because their carbon-fluorine bonds are so strong that they barely break down. The PFAS family includes nearly 15,000 different compounds, according to the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, but the FDA tests food and animal feed for only a couple dozen of them.

Today, nearly all humans, including newborns, have detectable levels of PFAS in their blood.

One promising approach to combating PFAS is to find a way to break their super-strong chemical bonds, then bake that chemistry into next-generation filters. Currently, filters can trap a variety of PFAS but can’t destroy them, meaning the filters basically just move the toxic chemicals from one place to another. However, a study in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences describes a filter that uses hydrogen to attack carbon-fluorine bonds. When tested against six PFAS of regulatory concern, it worked on all of them.

“PFAS are hard to break down, and normal processes that we use don’t work,” Arizona State University’s Bruce Rittmann, the senior author of the study, said in a press statement. “We showed that this mechanism worked for all six. It’s a universal mechanism.”

Hydrogen is essential for this process because once activated into a reactive form, it drives a process known as “hydrodefluorination,” stripping and replacing the fluorine and forming carbon-hydrogen bonds. To activate that hydrogen, Rittman and his team used palladium nanoparticles that can convert the hydrogen gas into its much-needed reactive form.

“The palladium attracts the PFAS molecule, and the palladium attracts the hydrogen and puts them together,” Rittmann said in a press statement.

As hydrogen and PFAS travel through the membrane, both are attracted to the palladium catalyst. However, hydrogen isn’t simply added to the waste stream—instead, it’s fed directly into the membrane itself. This gives the filter the perfect opportunity to chemically dismantle those troublesome carbon-fluorine bonds. Over two months of continuous operation, the membrane broke apart most of the PFAS it captured. It removed 90 percent of the chemicals and stripped about 83 percent of their fluorine.

That’s a huge advancement for combating the proliferation of PFAS in the natural environment, but a big limitation will be cost. Simply put, palladium isn’t cheap, and Rittman estimates that roughly a kilogram of the stuff costs about $50,000. The new approach could be paired with conventional treatments, which would first concentrate the PFAS into a smaller waste stream that the palladium membrane could then destroy.

“Decades of extensive deployment of PFAS in firefighting foams, oil- and water-repellent surfaces, non-stick cookware, and food-contact materials have created diffuse, chronic inputs into watersheds and drinking-water supplies,” the authors wrote in the study. “Hydrogen-driven hydrodefluorination offers a direct reductive route under comparatively mild conditions.”
