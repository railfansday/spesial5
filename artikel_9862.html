Performance evolution of route lookup on Linux

Tanggal: 2026-11-11

In pre­vi­ous ar­ti­cles, I ex­plained how the Linux ker­nel im­ple­ments IPv4[1] and IPv6[2] rout­ing ta­bles, and I showed the per­for­mance im­prove­ment of route lookups from Linux 2.6.32 to 4.16 for IPv4[3]—in­clud­ing the im­pact of the Melt­down and Spec­tre mit­i­ga­tions[4]—as well as from Linux 2.6.39 to 4.18 for IPv6[5]. This ar­ti­cle up­dates the num­bers through Linux 7.3 and in­ves­ti­gates the per­pe­tra­tor re­spon­si­ble for the per­for­mance re­gressions! 🕵️‍♂️

I ran the fol­low­ing bench­marks on an AMD EPYC 8024P[6], a Zen 4c CPU[7], rented from Scale­way[8], run­ning at 2.4 GHz with CPU boost dis­abled. I com­piled the ker­nels with GCC 10.2: this ver­sion can com­pile older ker­nels as well as the cur­rent ones.

In Linux 4.15, Wei Wang im­proved the lookup per­for­mance by re­plac­ing the read-write lock with an RCU and a spin­lock in com­mit 66f5d6ce53e6[9]. In Linux 4.18, David Ah­ern im­proved the data struc­tures used by route lookup[10]. In Linux 5.9, Brian Vazquez re­moved ex­pen­sive in­di­rect calls in com­mit 55cced4f813b[11].

The ma­jor 200 ns re­gres­sion in­tro­duced in Linux 6.5 wiped out all these im­prove­ments. This ver­sion im­ple­ments a mit­i­ga­tion for “Spec­u­la­tive Re­turn Stack Over­flow[12],” a vul­ner­a­bil­ity af­fect­ing AMD pro­ces­sors, in com­mit fb3bd914b3ec[13]. This mit­i­ga­tion adds about 20 cy­cles on each func­tion re­turn. It was back­ported to older ker­nels (5.10, 5.15, 6.1, and 6.4). To keep the re­sults leg­i­ble, I dis­abled the mit­i­ga­tion for these ver­sions with spec_​rstack_​overflow=​off on the ker­nel com­mand-line.

You can check the vul­ner­a­bil­i­ties im­pact­ing your CPU and their mit­i­ga­tions with lscpu. Some mit­i­ga­tions may de­pend on the mi­crocode or on the firmware.1

The mi­crocode was out of date on my ma­chine as I for­got to in­stall the amd64-microcode pack­age. As it only im­pacts TSA[14], our bench­mark is un­af­fected. Spec­tre v2 mit­i­ga­tion did not en­able au­to­matic IBRS[15], be­cause of SEV-SNP be­ing en­abled in the firmware[16]. With au­to­matic IBRS, the im­pact of SRSO should be smaller. ❦

The 30 ns im­pact of the mit­i­ga­tion for SRSO in Linux 6.5 is smaller than for IPv6 be­cause IPv4 route lookups tra­verse fewer func­tions: around 4 func­tion calls for IPv4 com­pared to 25 for IPv6.

To re­duce the im­pact of mit­i­ga­tions, you can dis­able all of them with mitigations=​off, dis­able only a spe­cific one like with spec_​rstack_​overflow=​off, or pro­tect against only some at­tack vec­tors[23] with mitigations=​auto,no_​user_​kernel, start­ing from Linux 6.17.

The im­pact is also quite dif­fer­ent de­pend­ing on the CPU. For ex­am­ple, on a Xeon W-2295[24], an In­tel desk­top CPU from 2020, the im­pact of the mit­i­ga­tions is only a few cy­cles for IPv4, and less than 3% for IPv6. The main con­trib­u­tor is the mit­i­ga­tion for the In­di­rect Tar­get Se­lec­tion[25] vul­ner­a­bil­ity, which is fixed since the Sap­phire Rapids[26] ar­chi­tecture.

If we set aside the re­gres­sions in­tro­duced by the mit­i­ga­tions against CPU at­tacks[27], the per­for­mance of IPv4 is de­clin­ing slowly over time while IPv6 has im­proved in the past and is cur­rently sta­ble. If rout­ing per­for­mance is im­por­tant, you have sev­eral op­tions: choose a dif­fer­ent CPU like an AMD Zen 5 CPU[28], a fourth gen­er­a­tion Xeon CPU[26], or maybe an ARM Neo­verse V2[29]; dis­able some or all the mit­i­ga­tions; by­pass rout­ing with XDP/eBPF;5 or off­load rout­ing[30] to the net­work card.

This re­quires avoid­ing bpf_​fib_​lookup()[31], which re­lies on the same ker­nel lookup. ❦
