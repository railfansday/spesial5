Tennessee basketball players propose model employment agreement to university leaders

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Tennessee basketball players have proposed a model for a collective bargaining agreement to the university. According to a term sheet obtained by USA TODAY Sports, the proposed CBA addresses issues of compensation, player movement and more.

Three representatives chosen from the men's basketball team − Tyler Lundblade, DeWayne Brown II and Campbell Duncan − created the term sheet with Athletes.org, an advocacy group for college athletes, and shared it with university leadership, including athletic director Danny White.

"We take a lot of pride in being extremely athlete-centric, and listening to our players' interests through Athletes.org has shown that sharing perspectives through meaningful dialogue isn't something to be afraid of," White said in a statement.

"Without athletes, we don't have championships to win, we don't have 102,000 coming into Neyland Stadium. In my conversations with athletes over the years, they want the same things we want."

The player representatives worked with Athletes.org to create the term sheet then shared it with teammates in late September for discussion.

The term sheet is not a binding CBA and does not signal any imminent attempts by UT athletes to unionize, but rather provides a model for what collective bargaining between players and their universities could look like.

"Rather, it offers the clearest picture to date of the issues a CBA could address, the accountability mechanisms that could protect athletes and an institution, and what athletes can achieve when they have a direct role in deciding the terms of the industry they power," Athletes.org said.

Among the proposed terms is a "Universal Athletic Service Agreement" that ties an athlete's compensation to what they do on the field or court, rather than endorsement deals. It would replace the revenue sharing contracts athletes currently agree to with their schools.

Athletes would begin receiving payments at enrollment or their first team activity, whichever comes first and there would be "financial penalties" for schools that don't meet contractual obligations, according to the term sheet. The proposal also outline initiatives for postseason success, individual performance and players who stay at the same school.

The term sheet also outlines a 15-day transfer portal window that would open one week after the national championship game and creates enforceable anti-tampering mechanisms, like an independent grievance process handled by a neutral arbitrator. It also calls for guaranteed compensation for athletes on injured reserve, at least two additional years of health coverage after the end of eligibility and lifetime health coverage for "catastrophic injuries."

"Some people say we're kids, that we're immature or that we don't understand," Brown said. "But we're the ones living this experience, and our opinions matter. We know what we want and how we want to move forward. Working through the collective bargaining process has been great and I hope it's just a stepping stone for more college athletes to replicate our process with their own schools."

Athletes.org has also engaged in conversations with representatives from Tennessee's football program.

"This CBA term sheet shows that with athletes at the table, everyone in college sports can win," said Brandon Copeland, the association's executive director. "If we repeated this process with every Power Four university today, we could solve most of college sports' problems by tomorrow afternoon."
