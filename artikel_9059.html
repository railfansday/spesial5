Faurschou Ends Its Public Exhibition Program as Its New York Space Heads for Demolition

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

When Faurschou New York opened in 2019, the New York Times compared it to major collector-founded institutions like the Hill Art Foundation, the FLAG Art Foundation, and the Brant Foundation. But unlike those institutions, Faurschou New York was not a 501(c)(3) nonprofit. It also never quite lived up to its lofty expectations.

In March 2025, Faurschou, which was founded as a private art museum and art consultancy in Copenhagen in 1986, announced that the New York outpost was closed “for renovations.” It never reopened. The Greenpoint building that housed it was sold in June to a developer, city records show, and the city has approved its demolition. A condominium planned for the site cements what founder and art collector Jens Faurschou himself announced in April: After four decades, Faurschou’s public exhibitions are over. The building at 148-150 Green Street, once home to Faurschou New York and the restaurant Ilis, was sold at the end of June to the developer Investmates for $25.55 million, according to city property records reviewed by ARTnews. The seller, Green Art Holdings LLC, bought it in 2017 for $18 million, according to Commercial Observer. On October 27, the Department of Buildings approved its full demolition, according to city records. Investmates plans a seven-story, 73-unit condominium on the site, Crain’s reported.

In a statement posted to the company’s website, Jens Faurschou said that with the closing of the New York space he was “bringing the public exhibition program to an end.” After 40 years, he wrote, his “direction changes”: He will focus on his family, private dealing, and working with artists, institutions, and collections. The statement does not clarify what will become of the collection, or whether the Copenhagen and Beijing spaces remain operational.

Faurschou staged solo exhibitions of artists from Tracey Emin to Shirin Neshat and Danh Vo, and showed works by Robert Rauschenberg and Louise Bourgeois. Programming at its global locations appeared to have stopped years earlier: Copenhagen last listed an exhibition in 2018 and Beijing in 2021-22, according to the company’s website and exhibition listings.

Its 12,000-square-foot New York space last hosted an exhibition of work by the Chinese artist Ai Weiwei, which ended in February 2025. As of late October 2025, the exhibition appeared to still be installed, visible through the windows. Neither the artist’s studio nor Faurschou responded to a request for comment at that time.

His statement did not address the company’s finances, which have come under scrutiny in recent years. An auditor warned about its accounts, and a court issued a bankruptcy order against Faurschou ApS in November 2025 that was canceled two weeks later.

A degree of mystery surrounds the Danish art dealer, dubbed the “Don Corleone of art” in a 2016 Politiken profile. By his account, his career began in 1984, the year he traveled to New York City and made his first art deal—a few Fluxus works, sold at the suggestion of Art Library founder Knud Pedersen. That same year, he met his future first wife, Luise Faurschou. The two married in 1985 and soon after opened Galleri Faurschou on Store Strandstræde.

According to the profile, the gallery maintained a small but respectable stable of artists—Christian Lemmerz, Nina Sten-Knudsen, Erik A. Frandsen, and Michael Kvium—while also handling blue-chip works. “I sold Warhol,” he recalled. “I sold Poliakoff, I sold Picasso, lots of Picassos. The biggest deal I made, which was the most fun, was around 2004. It was a package of five Picassos and two Mirós.” In 2012, he and Luise relocated the business to Nordhavn, which he continued to run alone following their divorce.

The Faurschou collection underpins his business, but in interviews, he has declined to specify its scope, value, and exact ownership. In 2016, he compared himself to collectors who “have a showroom and a big collection. And sell something from time to time to be able to buy something else.” He claimed to have purchased a monumental work from Robert Rauschenberg, which he said he would part with only for the Museum of Modern Art in New York. (There is no public record that MoMA owns a Rauschenberg that was sold or donated by Jens Faurschou. However, Mirthday Man, a work from the Faurschou collection, was shown in a 2017 Rauschenberg exhibition at MoMA.)

Also listed in his collection are works by Louise Bourgeois, Cai Guo-Qiang, Danh Vo, Shirin Neshat, and Bill Viola, as well as a substantial number of pieces by Ai Weiwei. He told Politiken the value of the collection “depends on whether it’s a good or a bad day,” and that he is an art dealer but that he “no longer” runs a gallery that represents artists and sells their works.

A 2024 article in the Danish-language outlet Børsen described Faurschou’s business model as including a “philanthropic” part, which stages “non-commercial” exhibitions. His philanthropic activities, the report notes, include helping establish the art center and nonprofit organization Copenhagen Contemporary. When he opened his Brooklyn location in 2019, Faurschou told the New York Times that he occasionally sells works from his private collection and does consulting work to finance his free exhibitions. According to Børsen, however, his business is financed by wealthy, unnamed individuals, who also finance and own the art. In 2025, Jyllands-Posten estimated that the collection comprises roughly 400 works.

Faurschou has said that these non-commercial shows have cost him several million DKK over the years. The Politiken profile notes that several of Faurschou’s companies narrowly sidestepped going into the red in 2015, after the sale of several properties in Taastrup. The art dealer said he was “absolutely” generating funds from the collection to remedy the shortfall.

Jyllands-Posten reported in 2025 that with a loss exceeding DKK 25 million ($3.6 million) in 2024, his holding company, Faurschou Holding ApS, had amassed total losses of nearly DKK 174 million ($25.3 million) since its founding in 2012. Company accounts show equity has been negative for six straight years, standing at a deficit of DKK 154 million ($22.4 million) as of 2024. Given these circumstances, the auditor expressed doubts about the value of the art Faurschou has purchased, noting that there was “significant uncertainty about the valuation of the company’s assets.”

The 2023 accounts were similarly fraught. Børsen reported in 2024 that despite his industry renown, “the seasoned art dealer has blood-red accounts, where virtually no values are registered.” Faurschou also had a dispute with an auditor from Deloitte, who at the time questioned his business model. According to the article, the auditor wrote that the company accounts “do not give a true and fair view of the companies’ liabilities, assets and financial position, and in addition, the auditor does not believe in the companies’ future operations.”

The report added: “Because there is no faith in the future operations, the auditor also calls the management report incorrect, as it assumes continued operations.”

This credit history painted a tense backdrop for the company’s brush with bankruptcy. On November 6, 2025, the Probate Court of the Maritime and Commercial Court in Denmark issued a bankruptcy order for Faurschou ApS, only to cancel it on November 20. The cancelation ended the legal process that would have seized the company’s assets and operations, because the legal threshold for bankruptcy was no longer met.

Public records also show legal claims on Faurschou’s own property. Notices in Statstidende say Møns Bank asked a court to auction two properties he owns, a house in Humlebæk and a summer house in Nysted, with sales set for May. As of November 23, the land registry still listed him as the owner of the Humlebæk house. Separately, the registry shows the Danish Tax Administration placed liens on that house on April 23 totaling about DKK 8.5 million, or roughly $1.3 million.

For his part, Faurschou had expressed confidence in the future of his company, telling Børsen in 2024: “I have tried to explain to the accountant why I see it differently than he does, but I absolutely cannot blame him for what he writes, because the requirements for an accountant are much higher today than they were before.” He said the company was working on “large and complex projects,” the details of which he could not share at the time. He added that, alongside the “philanthropic” part of his business, there was an “income-generating” side, where he acted as a broker for major art dealers and facilitated sales from the company’s collection. His April statement does not indicate whether his plans to focus on private dealing include that brokerage and those collection sales.

Reports have left unclear the exact nature of Faurschou’s operations, and the terms used to describe it have shifted over the years between “foundation” and “private museum.”

“You call [Faurschou] a foundation, but it’s not a trust, is it,” Politiken asked. Faurschou replied, “No, but neither is the Tryg Foundation,” referencing the charitable arm of TryghedsGruppen, a Danish holding company that is the largest shareholder of the insurance company Tryg. He confirmed that his business is a limited company and explained that the use of the word “foundation” signals that the art on view is not for sale. Visitors “expect to come into a place where there [are] no sales” being conducted, he said, adding that this also ensured employees at the front desk were not asked about the price list.

When pressed further, he stressed that at least the Copenhagen operation “absolutely” was philanthropic, but did not elaborate on the specifics of the philanthropy. “It’s just that not many people take advantage of the philanthropic offer,” he said.

Faurschou launched its Greenpoint, Brooklyn, space in 2019 to great fanfare. Mads Refslund, the Scandinavian chef who cofounded Copenhagen’s Noma with René Redzepi, opened Ilis New York in a space attached to Faurschou. (Refslund is reportedly close with Jens Faurschou and his wife, Marsha, with one source saying he contributed to their wedding menu.) The opening of Ilis, packed with art world regulars, was similarly tasteful: employees’ uniforms were designed by Camilla Staerk and Lady and Butler’s founder Eli Caner. The restaurant closed in May 2026, citing the building’s sale; Refslund told Eater that he and a group of investors had tried to buy the building, but were outbid by a developer.

In his closing remark, Jens Faurschou described the whole venture, across four cities, as a “program of cross-cultural dialogue.”
