Azaleas and rhododendrons paint the spring garden in gorgeous shades of pink, red, orange, and other vibrant colors. But the work of protecting those future flowers starts now. Fall care before winter is essential for keeping these shrubs healthy and ready to bloom abundantly in spring.

Fall azalea care and rhododendron care are all about preparing them for winter dormancy, maintaining healthy roots, and protecting flower buds. Did you know that these shrubs form their buds in summer? Next year’s flowers are already set in buds, so now is the time to preserve and protect them.

Azaleas and rhododendrons are very similar plants with similar care needs in fall. However, keep in mind that there are deciduous and evergreen types with slightly different needs. We’ll point these out here, along with the essential tasks you should be tackling this fall for all your Rhododendron species.

Fall is not the time for heavy annual pruning of rhododendrons or azaleas. Most spring-blooming members of this plant flower on old growth. This means they set buds over the summer. Trimming too hard in the fall will prune away many of the buds that become next spring’s flowers.

The best time for heavy shaping or pruning is right after spring flowering. But this doesn’t mean you can’t or shouldn’t make any cuts in fall. Inspect your shrubs now for dead, damaged, or diseased branches. Remove these using sharp, clean bypass pruners, such as the Felco 8, available on Amazon, which can cut through woody stems up to 1 inch (2.5 cm) thick. For larger branches, switch to loppers or a pruning saw.

Fertilizing late in the season can trigger new growth that only gets damaged in winter conditions. That damage can introduce disease. It’s also a waste of energy that should be going into keeping roots healthy over winter.

Most fertilizing for azaleas and rhododendrons takes place in spring, after flowering. At that time, you can use a product formulated specifically for these shrubs or for any acid-loving shrub. Also, consider the fact that your shrubs might not need feeding at all if they are healthy or thriving.

If you suspect your plants need fertilizer, a soil test is the best way to determine their nutrient needs. The MySoil Soil Test Kit measures both soil pH and nutrient levels, helping you identify any deficiencies before deciding whether to feed your shrubs in spring.

You should keep watering your azaleas and rhododendrons right up until the ground freezes, especially if you’re having a dry autumn. Ideally, the ground will be moist before real winter weather sets in. Check the soil regularly using a moisture meter, such as the Yamron 4-in-1, to see whether water is reaching the root zone. Provide a deep soaking as needed, including one as late in the season as possible before the ground freezes.

This matters because many azaleas and rhododendrons are evergreen, making them susceptible to desiccation, or drying out, over winter. Cold, dry winds leech moisture from the leaves, but roots in frozen ground can’t take up any water. This can result in curled, brown leaves and even damaged buds. Ensuring there is moisture in the ground before winter is less important for deciduous types.

Mulch insulates the soil and roots of your flowering shrubs. It helps regulate soil temperature, retains more moisture around the roots, and prevents the root damage caused by soil heaving that occurs during cycles of freezing and thawing.

Organic mulches such as shredded pine bark or partially decomposed, shredded leaves are ideal for mulching azaleas and rhododendrons. If you don't have suitable materials to hand, Garden Pro's shredded pine bark mulch from Home Depot is a good alternative. Layer the mulch around the root zones of shrubs to a depth of 2 to 3 inches (5–8 cm). Keep it a few inches away from stems to prevent trapping moisture that can encourage rot and disease.

Moist soil is important, especially for evergreen shrubs, but too much moisture can also be damaging. Poorly drained soil can lead to root rot and fungal diseases. Eventually, these diseases can kill shrubs.

Look for signs your shrubs are getting waterlogged: standing water around plants, soggy soil long after rainfall, and wilting or yellowing foliage even when the plants have plenty of water. It’s important to try to remediate soggy conditions. You can clear off compacted mulch and other plant debris, divert runoff from other areas, or regrade the surrounding soil, for instance. If the problem isn’t easily fixed, consider relocating the plant in spring.

Evergreen azaleas and rhododendrons add beautiful color and texture to the winter garden, but they are also more vulnerable to winter damage. Winter burn happens when the leaves lose moisture faster than the roots can draw up water to replace it.

Cold and dry winter wind is a primary cause of winter burn on evergreen shrubs, so creating a windbreak can protect them from much of the damage. Set up a barrier on the side of the shrubs that get hit by winter wind, using natural burlap garden fabric, like this from Home Depot, attached to wooden stakes.

Don’t let the barrier actually touch the leaves. They need some airflow to stay healthy. Young shrubs are more likely to need this kind of winter protection. Established shrubs in more sheltered areas are probably fine without it.

Finally, it’s a good idea to look over your shrubs for any signs of problems caused by common azalea pests or diseases. Managing these issues now can prevent them from carrying over into next year’s growing season. Look for stippling or black specks on leaves, which can indicate lace bugs, a common pest of azaleas and rhododendrons. A 10x illuminated magnifying loupe, like this Carson loupe from Amazon, can help you inspect the undersides of leaves, where these tiny insects and their dark droppings are often found.

An unusual number of dying branches, persistent wilting, and many or unusual leaf spots can indicate fungal diseases and root rot. Keep in mind that some spots are normal and that leaf drop is expected in deciduous varieties.

If you do see issues or unusual damage, remove diseased plant material and dispose of it. If issues persist or you don’t know how to identify or treat a particular pest, check with your local extension office for advice.

Fall azalea and rhododendron care isn’t extensive, but it will help you protect next year’s flowers, safeguard vulnerable foliage, and keep roots healthy. Simple steps now help your shrubs enter winter in better condition and improve the chances of a healthy, colorful spring display.
