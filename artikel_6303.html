Women who used menopause hormone therapy had less brain vessel damage later in life

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

Women who had used menopause hormone therapy showed fewer signs of damage to small brain blood vessels at 70.

Estrogen helps regulate the menstrual cycle and supports pregnancy, but it also helps keep blood vessels healthy, including those in the brain.

That second job may still matter decades after menopause.

Women who had used menopause hormone therapy showed fewer signs of damage to the brain’s smallest blood vessels at age 70.

Women whose menopause came later showed a similar but weaker pattern.

Damage to these tiny vessels becomes more common with age, and it’s linked to a higher chance of stroke and dementia.

Giulia Lorenzon, now at the Yale School of Medicine, and Anna Marseglia of Karolinska Institutet in Sweden led the work.

The team compared each woman’s hormone history with her brain scans but never gave anyone hormones, so the study can show links but can’t prove cause and effect.

Doctors use the word cerebrovascular to describe the brain’s blood vessels.

“Our results suggest that hormonal exposure across different periods of a woman’s life may have cumulative effects also on the cerebrovascular health in later life,” said Marseglia, one of the study’s senior authors.

“The findings suggest that the brain’s blood vessels may play an important role in the link between estrogen and brain aging.”

The women all took part in the Gothenburg H70 Birth Cohort Studies, a long-running study of aging in Gothenburg, Sweden. Between 2014 and 2016, 352 women born in 1944 had MRI brain scans at about age 70.

None had dementia. On a common 30-point test of memory and thinking, the women averaged just over 29.

Staff asked each woman about her reproductive history and used her answers to work out her reproductive span, the number of years between her first period and menopause.

A woman whose first period came at age 13 and whose menopause came at age 50, for example, had a span of 37 years. That’s close to the group’s average.

The ovaries make most of a woman’s estrogen. For women who had both ovaries removed, the researchers used their age at surgery to calculate their reproductive span.

Most of the women had also used hormones. About 73% had taken birth control pills, and about 61% had used menopause hormone therapy.

That level of hormone use fits Swedish history, the authors wrote. The pill caught on in the mid-1960s, and hormone therapy use rose sharply in the 1990s, as these women reached menopause.

The brain depends on a dense network of small blood vessels. Damage to them is called small vessel disease, and it often causes no symptoms, so many people learn they have it only from a brain scan.

Radiologists checked each scan for four common signs. Large bright patches in the brain’s white matter can mark areas where blood supply has suffered.

Lacunes are small holes often left behind by tiny strokes, and microbleeds are pinpoint spots of old bleeding.

The fourth sign is widened fluid-filled spaces around the vessels themselves.

Each woman got one point for each sign that passed a set threshold. About 59% of the women scored zero, and 12% scored two or more. No one scored four.

The team also measured the white matter itself, the bundles of nerve fibers that carry signals between brain areas.

In healthy white matter, water tends to move along nerve fibers rather than across them. The scans measured this movement to assess the tissue’s structure.

Women with more years between their first period and menopause were less likely to score two or more. Each extra year was tied to about 8% lower odds of that higher score.

The team found the same pattern after accounting for education, pregnancies, and breastfeeding.

The age at menopause mattered more than the age at a first period. A later menopause was tied to a lower score and to fewer lacunes.

Still, this was one of the weaker results. When researchers run dozens of comparisons, a few can look real by luck alone, so the team applied a stricter test that allows for that.

The reproductive span link weakened under that test, and it weakened again when the team left out the 31 women who’d had a stroke or a mini-stroke.

Women who had used menopause hormone therapy had less than half the odds of scoring two or more, compared with women who never had. Their white matter also looked more orderly.

Both of those links passed the stricter test. They held after the team accounted for high blood pressure, heart disease, and diabetes, and when women with a past stroke were left out.

Birth control pill use was linked to more orderly white matter but not to a lower score, and that white matter link faded under the stricter test.

Women who had used birth control pills when younger and menopause hormone therapy later had the most favorable scans.

Compared with women who had used neither, they had about a third of the odds of scoring two or more.

The researchers found no clear interaction between birth control pill use and menopause hormone therapy.

They described the combined pattern as preliminary and called for studies in more varied groups of women.

Women make up about two-thirds of dementia cases. Earlier studies of hormone therapy and dementia have reached opposite conclusions, with some finding lower risk and others higher.

So the team also measured four areas of the brain that tend to thin early in Alzheimer’s disease. Overall, none of the hormone measures was linked to the thickness of those areas.

One exception involved a version of a gene called APOE. Its ε4 version raises the risk of Alzheimer’s, and about a third of the women carried at least one copy.

Among those carriers, hormone therapy users had thicker tissue in the Alzheimer’s-prone areas than nonusers. That result didn’t pass the stricter test, and the authors wrote that it should be read with caution.

Among the 205 women scanned again five years later, hormone history made no clear difference to how fast the bright patches grew or the Alzheimer’s-prone areas thinned.

Hormone therapy users had a hint of slower growth in the bright patches, but it wasn’t strong enough to rule out chance.

To the authors, the split between these results suggests that estrogen’s link to brain aging in these women may mostly involve the blood vessels.

The women recalled their hormone use decades later, and researchers recorded whether they had used birth control pills or menopause hormone therapy.

The study didn’t capture the products, doses, or treatment start dates.

“Furthermore, the study is an observational study, and we cannot therefore determine cause and effect,” Lorenzon said. “The results cannot therefore be used as a basis for recommending hormone therapy for preventive purposes.”

The volunteers were also a healthy, well-educated group from one city.

Among the women with white matter measurements, only 46 had used neither the pill nor hormone therapy, so that comparison group was small.

Some earlier research suggests hormone therapy may help the brain most when it starts close to menopause.

The team couldn’t test that idea, because the study didn’t record when each woman began.

Nor did the researchers check whether healthier-looking vessels made any difference to memory or thinking.

Answering that will take studies that record exactly which hormones women took and when, then follow their thinking skills along with their brain scans.

The full study was published in the journal Neurology.
