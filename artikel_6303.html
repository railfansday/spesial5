Washing your Thanksgiving turkey spreads germs around the kitchen, but a USDA study shows skipping it is also a bad idea

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Washing a Thanksgiving turkey spreads germs, the USDA says. But its 2019 test-kitchen study found the sink and poorly washed hands mattered more than splashes.

Many families rinse the Thanksgiving turkey in the kitchen sink before it goes in the oven, because that is how a parent or grandparent always did it. The U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA) has been telling people for years to stop.

“USDA research has found that washing or rinsing meat or poultry increases the risk for cross-contamination in the kitchen,” the agency’s Food Safety and Inspection Service (FSIS) says. Cooking the bird to the right temperature kills the germs, so washing it is “risky and not necessary for safety.”

The research behind that advice holds a twist, though. The agency’s key experiment did not use turkeys.

When USDA-funded scientists filmed 300 home cooks preparing chicken thighs in test kitchens, many of the people who skipped washing their chicken still ended up with its bacteria in their salads. Their sinks and their hands spread it anyway.

The study was carried out for FSIS by RTI International and North Carolina State University (NCSU), and its final report was dated August 20, 2019. It was the second round of a five-year FSIS consumer food safety research project.

The team set up eight test kitchens in the Raleigh-Durham area and in Lillington, a rural town in North Carolina. Every participant had said they wash or rinse raw poultry when cooking at home.

Before their appointments, 142 of them received three emails with USDA food safety messages. One said not to wash poultry and linked to a USDA video; one recommended separate cutting boards for raw meat and ready-to-eat food; and one was a USDA “Clean” infographic about not washing poultry and about washing hands and kitchen surfaces.

The other 158 participants received nothing.

In the kitchen, six cameras recorded every move. Each person cooked chicken thighs and made a mixed green salad.

What they did not know was that the chicken had been spiked with a harmless, traceable strain of E. coli called DH-5 alpha.

After the meal and the cleanup, the researchers swabbed the kitchen surfaces, tested the lettuce and counted where the marker bacteria had ended up.

The messages changed what people did with the chicken. In the group that got the emails, 93% did not wash the chicken, compared with 39% in the group that did not.

Those who did wash it mostly rinsed it in the sink rather than submerging it. Asked why they wash chicken at home, 30% said they wanted to remove blood or slime, and 19% said it was how a family member did it.

Two-thirds of the participants who got the emails, 66%, said the messages had changed how they cooked that day.

Then came the lab results. Lettuce from the salads carried the marker bacteria in 26% of the cooks who had washed their chicken and in 20% of those who had not. Washing made things somewhat worse, but skipping it was far from a fix.

The researchers suspect hands were an important factor. Handwashing was covered in one of the emails, but it did not change how participants washed their hands.

Of all the handwashing that should have happened before or during the meal, only 2% included every step the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention recommends. The most common mistake was not rubbing hands with soap for at least 20 seconds.

The report also found high levels of the marker in the sink. Microbes that reach the sink from the chicken, its packaging or contaminated hands “are a larger cause for concern than splashing contaminated chicken fluids onto the counter,” the authors wrote.

A sink that is not cleaned properly can pass germs to whatever is rinsed in it next, such as lettuce or vegetables. Researchers have warned before that the sink deserves as much attention as your hands.

When the study was published in the Journal of Food Protection in 2022, Ellen Shumaker, its corresponding author at NC State, summed it up.

“Washing the chicken is still not a good idea, but this study demonstrates the need to focus on preventing contamination of sinks and emphasizing the importance of hand-washing and cleaning and sanitizing surfaces,” she said.

Another finding: only 47% of all participants used a food thermometer on at least one chicken thigh.

In a release on November 13, the USDA reminded families that a food thermometer is the only way to know a turkey is done.

The bird is safe at 165°F, checked in three places: the thickest part of the breast, the innermost part of the wing and the innermost part of the thigh. Stuffing is best cooked separately, and if it goes inside the turkey, its center must also reach 165°F.

For a frozen turkey, the agency says never to thaw it on the counter. In the refrigerator, allow about 24 hours for every 4 to 5 pounds.

In cold water, allow about 30 minutes per pound and change the water every 30 minutes. A fresh turkey should be bought one to two days before cooking.

Washing raw meat or poultry is not recommended, according to FSIS, and soap or detergent should never be used on it. Rinsing in salt water, vinegar or lemon juice does not make it safer either.

Kitchen surfaces should be cleaned first with hot, soapy water and then sanitized. Germs can linger: Campylobacter can survive in a kitchen for up to 4 hours and Salmonella for up to 32 hours, the agency says.

Leftovers should go in the refrigerator within two hours. They keep for up to four days and should be reheated to 165°F.

Every year, USDA food safety specialists answer thousands of Thanksgiving questions through the agency’s Meat and Poultry Hotline. It is normally open Monday through Friday from 10 a.m. to 6 p.m. Eastern Time.

On Thanksgiving Day, November 26, 2026, it will be open from 8 a.m. to 2 p.m. Eastern Time at 1-888-MPHotline (1-888-674-6854). Questions can also be sent by email to MPHotline@usda.gov.
