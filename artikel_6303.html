El Niño’s global death toll hides a troubling imbalance

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Very strong El Niño years are linked to more heat deaths worldwide, with poorer countries bearing a disproportionate share of the burden.

Very strong El Niño years are associated with an estimated 107,000 additional heat-related deaths and 113,000 fewer cold-related deaths annually, compared with neutral conditions.

Globally, those numbers nearly cancel out, but they shouldn’t reassure anyone.

The people dying from heat and the people spared from cold are not the same people.

That’s the core finding of a new study led by the Barcelona Institute for Global Health (ISGlobal).

The research team, coordinated by Joan Ballester Claramunt, analyzed climate and mortality data from 1,667 regions in 125 countries spanning 1980 to 2023.

This period includes three particularly brutal El Niño episodes (1982–1983, 1997–1998, and 2015–2016).

They then extrapolated those relationships to 3,331 regions in 189 countries, covering more than eight billion people.

This is the first study to put global and regional numbers on the link between El Niño intensity and temperature-related deaths.

El Niño–Southern Oscillation is a natural cycle. During El Niño, unusually warm surface waters release heat into the atmosphere, reshuffling weather patterns across the planet.

Temperatures rise in some regions, fall in others. The global average ticks up. But the health consequences don’t distribute evenly.

During very strong El Niño years, the largest jumps in heat-related mortality hit South America (76% increase), Small Island Developing States (66%), sub-Saharan Africa (54%), and South and Southeast Asia (43%).

Many of these areas include tropical populations where additional warming can increase the risk of dangerous heat exposure.

Meanwhile, the cold-related mortality reductions tend to benefit higher-latitude, wealthier countries with better infrastructure and healthcare systems.

The inequality gets sharper when you sort by development. Countries with low or medium scores on the Human Development Index make up 42% of the world’s population.

They account for nearly 68% of the estimated increase in heat-related deaths during a very strong El Niño.

The medium-HDI group alone accounted for an estimated 61,863 additional heat-related deaths annually – 58% of the global increase.

These are countries with growing cities, limited air conditioning, and fragile health systems.

“The fact that these two changes largely offset each other globally does not mean that El Niño is beneficial to health,” said lead author Natalia Shartova from ISGlobal.

“Combined heat- and cold-related mortality increases in countries with fewer resources and, in an increasingly warm climate, future episodes could exacerbate these inequalities.”

“Furthermore, El Niño’s effects extend beyond temperature anomalies to include extreme weather events, such as floods and periods of drought.”

Here’s the frustrating part. El Niño is one of the most predictable climate patterns on the planet.

Meteorologists can forecast El Niño several months ahead, giving governments time to prepare for potential health impacts.

That makes it fundamentally different from a surprise earthquake or an unexpected flood.

The mortality toll it brings is, in principle, something governments could prepare for.

“Our findings identify the regions and seasons most affected by El Niño and, therefore, where health preparedness could be most valuable,” Ballester explained.

“This could support coordinated action plans involving health authorities, region-specific warnings and targeted interventions for populations at greatest risk.”

NOAA forecasts indicate that the ongoing El Niño is likely to remain strong to very strong through early 2027.

The authors note that their estimates come from retrospective data and can’t be used to directly predict mortality for the ongoing event.

But the map of vulnerability they’ve drawn is clear enough: South America, sub-Saharan Africa, small island nations, South and Southeast Asia.

The places that get hit hardest are the places least equipped to absorb the blow.

Mortality records weren’t available for one-third of the countries in the analysis – mostly under-resourced nations.

Estimates for most low-HDI countries and much of sub-Saharan Africa were built from models trained on data from other regions, adjusted for local climate and socioeconomic conditions.

That’s the best available approach, but it means the numbers for the most vulnerable places carry the most uncertainty.

The true toll in those regions could be higher or lower. Either way, the gap in data mirrors the gap in protection.

El Niño has always been part of Earth’s climate system. It isn’t new. But it’s playing out against a background that is.

Global temperatures are rising and baseline heat exposure is climbing.

A strong El Niño episode layered on top of an already warming planet compounds risk in places with little adaptive capacity.

The near-cancellation of additional heat deaths and fewer cold deaths in the global totals masks an uneven burden. In the regions facing the largest increases, there is little comfort in that apparent balance.

The study is published in the journal Nature Medicine.
