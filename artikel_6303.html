Why a NASA spacecraft bound for Jupiter is on its way back to Earth

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

NASA’s Europa Clipper will fly past Earth on December 3 to gain speed and test its instruments before studying Jupiter’s icy moon Europa.

More than two years after leaving Earth, a spacecraft headed for Jupiter is about to pass within roughly 2,000 miles of home.

It has already traveled as far as Mars. Now it needs a close encounter with the planet it launched from to gain enough speed for the next leg of its journey.

NASA’s Europa Clipper is scheduled to make its closest approach west of Africa at 3:14 p.m. EST on December 3.

While Earth’s gravity bends its flight path, all nine science instruments will collect data.

For the scientists who will eventually study Jupiter’s icy moon Europa, the pass offers a chance to check how their instruments measure a world they already know.

Even after a spacecraft leaves Earth, it continues to travel around the Sun. Its speed and direction determine the shape of that orbit and how far from the Sun it can reach.

A close pass by a planet can change both, saving propellant that the spacecraft would otherwise need to make a similar maneuver with its engines.

As Clipper approaches Earth, gravity pulls the spacecraft toward the planet and curves its path. Earth is moving around the Sun the whole time.

The approach is timed so that this encounter transfers a tiny share of Earth’s orbital energy to Clipper, sending the spacecraft away at a higher speed around the Sun.

The geometry can also work in the opposite direction. When Clipper passed about 550 miles above Mars in March 2025, that encounter slowed the spacecraft and reshaped its orbit.

The new path brought it back toward Earth for the December flyby. By the time Clipper reaches Jupiter in April 2030, it will have covered about 1.8 billion miles.

On its way past Earth, Clipper will measure a magnetic field that scientists have spent decades mapping.

Engineers can compare those measurements with existing data to check the spacecraft’s magnetometer and refine its calibration.

At Europa, the same instrument will help scientists look for an ocean they can’t see.

The search depends on how saltwater responds to a changing magnetic field.

As Europa moves through Jupiter’s magnetic field, the field can drive electrical currents in a conductive ocean beneath the ice. Those currents would produce a magnetic signal of their own.

By measuring how that signal changes over repeated flybys, researchers hope to confirm the ocean is there and estimate its depth and salinity.

Clipper’s infrared camera will scan Earth every eight hours as the spacecraft approaches.

At Europa, it will map surface temperatures and look for areas that cool more slowly than their surroundings.

Those measurements can help scientists distinguish differences in the surface material and investigate whether any unusually warm spots point to activity below.

Comparing Clipper’s readings of Earth with observations from other spacecraft gives the team practice working with measurements taken from different vantage points.

Another gravity assist awaits Clipper at Jupiter. A close pass by Ganymede, the largest of Jupiter’s moons, will slow the spacecraft before it fires its engines to enter orbit around the planet.

From there, Clipper is set to make 49 flybys of Europa, some as low as 16 miles above the surface.

Each pass will take it over a different stretch of terrain, allowing scientists to piece together a picture of nearly the entire moon.

Europa’s surface is crossed by cracks and ridges. Some of the most revealing observations could come from looking at the same feature in several ways.

A camera can show where a ridge cuts across the ice, while temperature readings can reveal how quickly the surrounding ground cools.

Ice-penetrating radar can add a view beneath the surface, where echoes may reveal layers or pockets of liquid water.

Together with measurements of the moon’s composition, those observations could help scientists work out whether material moves between Europa’s surface and its ocean.

That exchange matters because liquid water is only one ingredient of a habitable environment. Life also needs a source of energy and chemicals it can use.

It will take years for Clipper to make those observations at Europa. On December 3, the team will start with a closer and more familiar target.

As the spacecraft heads outward again, it will carry a fresh set of measurements from Earth that will help scientists interpret what they eventually find beneath another world’s ice.
