Prickly pear cacti generate electricity, and sunlight changes their voltage

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

Prickly pear cacti generate a small voltage that varies with sunlight, and researchers are exploring whether it could power field sensors.

In a California greenhouse, a row of prickly pear cacti is quietly generating electricity. A single stem can produce about half a volt.

When eight stems were wired together, they reached 4.5 volts, three times the voltage of a standard AA battery.

The trick depends on the unusual way cacti photosynthesize. They store up acid overnight and use it up in daylight.

That turns each stem into something like a small living solar panel.

The work was led by the University of California, Santa Cruz (UCSC).

Most plants take in carbon dioxide during the day. They open tiny pores in their leaves, let the gas in and use sunlight to turn it into sugar.

The downside is that water escapes through those open pores. In a desert, that’s a costly trade.

Prickly pears and some of their relatives work around this problem. Aloe, pineapple, and dragon fruit use a system called crassulacean acid metabolism, or CAM for short.

These plants keep their pores closed in the heat of the day and open them in the cool of the night. They pull in carbon dioxide while it’s dark and store it as malic acid, the same acid that makes green apples sour.

When the sun comes up, they break the acid down and finish making sugar.

The cactus’s shape provides the second part of the trick. Prickly pear stems are thick and opaque, another way of holding on to water, so photosynthesis happens only in the outer two millimeters or so.

When morning light hits one side of a stem, that side starts using up its stored acid. The shaded side keeps its acid.

The result is a difference in acidity across the stem, from one face to the other. That difference creates a voltage, and the researchers tap into it with a wired electrode inserted into the plant.

In effect, the cactus charges overnight and releases the energy during the day.

The idea came from Michael Loik, a professor of environmental studies who has studied cactus physiology and ecology for about 40 years.

“I’ve been obsessed with the plant since forever, it seems,” Loik said.

He suspected the cacti could produce electricity. He and Charlie Chesney, then a PhD student in environmental studies, set out to prove it and measure it.

They used cacti in field plots at the UCSC Farm, in greenhouses, and in the lab. Chesney recruited a large group of undergraduates from engineering, the sciences, and the social sciences, and seven of them are now authors on the paper.

“The undergraduate students told me they liked this project so much because it felt whimsical, and like a light-hearted approach to something that is so serious,” said Chesney, who led the project.

“It was a fun way to explore climate solutions that put creativity first.”

When researchers wired eight stems together, they measured 4.5 volts, three times the nominal voltage of a standard AA battery.

The researchers are exploring whether prickly pears could power low-energy field sensors. The voltage may also help growers monitor how the plants respond to changing conditions.

Chesney has since graduated. Colleen Josephson, an assistant professor of electrical and computer engineering, is continuing the work with her PhD student John Madden, and they are looking for ways to strengthen the signal.

The team also wanted to know how weather and growing conditions affect the voltage. The cacti behaved very much like solar panels.

Voltage was much higher in summer than in winter, and through the day it tracked the amount of sunlight.

“Just how closely the voltage aligned with the sunlight conditions was really surprising,” Chesney said.

“Comparing winter to summer voltage, the voltage really follows the same pattern of the amount of sunlight throughout the day.”

There is an upper limit. Too much sunlight and the cactus goes into self-protection mode.

“If it’s too high, then the cactus will start trying to protect itself from a sunburn, which will also decrease the voltage,” Chesney said. “There’s a balance, a mid-range of light that causes the voltage to be higher.”

Then came the drought test. The team stopped watering some cacti for 15 months.

The plants survived and kept photosynthesizing. Sometimes, they even produced more voltage than cacti that had been watered normally.

“This was a really surprising result, but also showed how resilient a cactus is – it could still produce electricity even when it seems, to us, like it’s near death,” Chesney said.

A separate experiment led by undergraduate Erin Langness looked at nitrogen, a common fertilizer ingredient.

Nitrogen increased the cacti’s store of malic acid, so they kept photosynthesizing and producing voltage later into the day.

The team isn’t trying to run homes on cacti. They have something smaller in mind.

The researchers are exploring whether prickly pears could power low-energy field sensors.

As climate change puts more pressure on crops, farmers increasingly use low-power sensors to monitor their fields.

The voltage may also help growers monitor how the plants respond to changing conditions.

That could be useful on farms that grow prickly pears for their edible pads, called nopales, and their fruit, called tunas.

“There are many commercial applications of cacti where monitoring their health using a low-power system like ours, that is just operating off on the side and is self-powered,” Loik said.

“This could be a really interesting avenue to pursue. It could be connected to a plant that acts as sentinel, measuring conditions and sending it back to the base station at the farmer’s office.”

Other CAM plants, such as agave and pineapple, might work too. The researchers note, however, that some of them may not tolerate the wires as well as the prickly pear does.

The study is published in the journal Plants, People, Planet.
