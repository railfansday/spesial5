The Washington Post Nears Breakeven After ‘Hundreds and Hundreds of Millions’ in Losses

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

This story was originally published in On Background with Mark Stenberg, a free, weekly newsletter that explores the key themes shaping the media industry. You can sign up for it here.

After years of steep losses, layoffs, and leadership churn, The Washington Post is within striking distance of breaking even financially, according to executive editor Matt Murray.

In a conversation at the Web Summit conference in Lisbon, Murray argued that the publisher is poised to turn a corner in 2027. While that won’t be easy, The Post has recently begun rebuilding its commercial momentum, stacking up a handful of revenue wins alongside a string of editorial scoops.

In the third quarter, direct advertising sales at The Post notched their strongest year-over-year growth since 2021, while programmatic revenue was up 48% year over year as of September, according to a company spokesperson.

Meanwhile, the newsroom is hiring again, adding James Politi from the Financial Times, Nancy Cook from Bloomberg, and Ian Ward from Politico, among other additions.

The Post is also relaunching its Ben Bradlee award, an internal designation that honors the work of an outstanding reporter. On Tuesday, the award will go to Hannah Natanson, whose home the FBI raided in January, according to Murray. The Post will also be recognizing Heidi Levine, the photojournalist who was attacked by Israeli settlers in the occupied West Bank last month.

In the Q&A below, Murray reflects on five years of turmoil at The Post, the first few months of the D’Onofrio era, and his plan to own post-Trump Washington.

This interview has been edited for length and clarity.

Mark Stenberg: How did election night go?

Matt Murray: We had a good night, and we wanted and needed that. This whole year has been about getting the layoffs behind us, but more than that, getting five years of turmoil behind us. The layoffs were terrible, but they were a kind of climax to a long stretch of buyouts and staff turnover. We hoped they would punctuate it, and that seems to be what happened. The rest of the year has been about stability, core coverage, and breaking scoops.

Mark: You were recruited to The Post by Will Lewis, who left in February after much controversy. How does the leadership style of his successor, Jeff D’Onofrio, stack up?

Matt: I’m cautious here. Will is a friend and somebody I’ve worked with for a long, long time, so I don’t want to make too much of a contrast. But we had some deep-seated challenges in how we were organized and how we understood our audience. We didn’t have great data. When I arrived two and a half years ago, too much of the newsroom was still oriented toward print. We had more foundational work to do than people realized. Jeff brings a low-drama, data-oriented focus on fundamentals. He’s very calm. He’s about the journalism, but he knows he’s not a journalist. He’s a business guy, and he relies on us. We haven’t had any clashes about that.

Matt: I’ll be honest. We had years of losses, and steepening losses. That’s the truth. We’re a private company and don’t share the figures, but it was hundreds and hundreds of millions of dollars. Now we’re within striking distance of break-even, and I expect next year will be our best year in seven years. We’ve done the right-sizing we needed, which was tough.

FOR Scoops, Sharp Analysis, and Insider Context

Mark: Layoffs might reduce costs, but where do you see growth coming from?

Matt: The Post has got to be about Washington, and I didn’t think we were dominant enough there in reach and comprehensiveness. We’ve had real successes this year: the ballroom, the Kennedy Center, the monuments. But how do we own post-Trump Washington? The whole city has been oriented to Trump for 10 years, and he’s still the center of the action. But last week was about millennials and Gen Z coming to the fore. I’m an older guy, and I’m trying to understand the world through their eyes. This is the first time in my lifetime that the boomers are receding and the next generations are rising. That’s the audience we want for news.

Mark: What editorial changes are you making that will help drive new business?

Matt: One thing we’re trying to do better is understand franchises. We suspended our daily podcast because costs overran the revenue, but now we can sell on the ad side, including through a partnership with Acast. The Post talked for years about [advice columnist] Carolyn Hax having a podcast. She has one now, and it’s taking off quickly. Personal finance reporter Michelle Singletary has one too. We’re reviving a version of the daily podcast, and we have at least half a dozen people for whom we can find that sweet spot of newsletter, podcast, and writing.

Mark: People poke at The Post for not acquiring properties the way The Times does, which has recently set its sights on Letterboxd. How do you see that?

Matt: The Times deserves enormous credit for how they reoriented the entire company and how it interacted with the newsroom. It took them many years. In hindsight, it seems inevitable, but it wasn’t. Our company struggled during that decade. We had successes in the more open internet age, like Facebook partnerships, and of course we had a Trump bump. We went from something like 35,000 subscriptions before Trump to 3 million. In the long run, we probably need our version of that model, with the journalism at the center. But we’re still getting the Post itself where it needs to be.

AP Up (EXCLUSIVE): The Associated Press had a big week last week. In addition to penning a content licensing agreement with Anthropic, the first of its kind for the artificial intelligence firm, the publisher also grew elections revenue by more than 80% compared to the 2022 midterms, according to the company. The AP saw its highest traffic since the 2024 election, with over 23 million pageviews, a sixfold increase over election night in 2022.

In our conversation, Dreyfus shared a number of surprising elements, including the unlikely way in which the OpenAI deal originated, what Le Monde did with the licensing revenue it gained from the deal, and why it has avoided the fallout from Google Zero.

Mark Stenberg: Le Monde was the first French publisher to sign with OpenAI. How did that happen?

Louis Dreyfus: In fall 2023, I saw that Sam Altman was speaking at Station F, so I went. When they took questions from the room, I raised my hand and said: “I’m the CEO of Le Monde. Seventy-five percent of my revenue comes from readers. You need reliable data, and you give it to users for free. If you want Le Monde to keep publishing reliable news, we need to negotiate.” He said he knew Le Monde and would be happy to. Three months later, we had a deal.

Mark: What have the partnerships actually delivered?

Louis: First, a significant and sustainable revenue stream at a time when advertising in Western Europe is slow. Second, a new audience, as the readers click through far more than people coming from Facebook, and they convert to subscriptions. And I decided to redistribute a share of the licensing revenue from the deal to the newsroom, so every journalist at Le Monde gets an annual bonus from it.

Mark: Anthropic has largely avoided these kinds of deals. Have you spoken with them?

Louis: Our last conversation was almost a year ago. They said it was too early and that their focus was B2B, not general news. I believe that will change, though, as they prepare to go public and investors will ask whether their data is stolen or legitimate. The more surprising holdout is Mistral, a French unicorn backed by the French government that refuses to sign a licensing deal.

Mark: Google rolled out AI Overviews in France this summer. What impact have you seen?

Louis: It launched in mid-July, and we haven’t seen a drop in audience. I was in London last week, and British publishers said the same about general news. They lost traffic on evergreen content, food, automotive, and consumer electronics.
