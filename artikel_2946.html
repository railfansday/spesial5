How To Make AI-Assisted Evaluation A Reliable Part Of Your Evals

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Chhaya Methani is an AI/ML leader driving enterprise innovation with expertise in AI, recommendation systems and evaluation frameworks.

​As enterprises move AI agents into production, AI-assisted evaluation has become the default way to measure quality at scale. Its most common form is LLM-as-a-judge, one model scoring another model’s output. Human review is slow and expensive, while a judge model can grade thousands of responses overnight.

In my experience, the challenge is not configuring an LLM judge but establishing what its scores mean and whether stakeholders trust them enough to act. Judges can favor familiar model outputs, reward verbosity or respond differently to answer order. Without accounting for these biases, your evals can give you false confidence instead of a clear signal.

Here, I share four lessons on making the judge work within a scalable eval harness. My previous article explained why enterprise AI needs that harness to catch regressions before production.

Many teams start with one large judge that scores every aspect of quality at once. I recommend starting smaller. Pick the simplest version that answers a question you care about, such as whether the agent completed a specific task, and understand what that score captures and what it misses.

Simple metrics are easier to validate and explain. Composite scores can change for many reasons, making it hard to distinguish product improvements from changes in the judge’s assessment.

In my experience, as our agents took on longer tasks, reviewing their trajectories became much harder. Trajectories still matter, but we shifted our focus to two questions: What results did the agent achieve, and did it hit the required milestones along the way?

Checking outcomes sometimes caught an agent reporting a step it never ran, something trajectory review could miss. We could confirm that agents had actually changed the underlying datasets and had used the right authenticated tools to do it. We ended up with a mix of dataset tests and rubrics checked by the judge.

LLM judges will make mistakes. The goal is to build a flywheel: Product failures show up in evals, judge errors show up in human review and both feed improvements back into the product and the eval. As your understanding grows, add dimensions, finer-grained scales and harder scenarios. ​

Judges need clear instructions. A common pattern I see is a rubric that asks the model to rate responses from one to five for “helpfulness” or “accuracy,” with no guidance beyond “One is poor and five is excellent.” The judge fills the gaps with its own interpretation, and that interpretation can change from run to run.

Narrow the definition as much as you can. Instead of describing a one as “bad,” describe the specific ways an agent gets it wrong. For an agent answering policy questions, a one could mean it cited the wrong policy or year, a three could mean it found the right policy but missed an exception, and a five could mean the answer was correct and cited its source.

Where possible, break broad dimensions into questions you can check: Did the agent call the right tool? Did it use the correct date range? Did it make unsupported claims?

Ideally, each rubric item is a true-or-false check with clear criteria for what counts as true. In my team’s work, this change significantly improved agreement among our human annotators, as measured by Cohen’s kappa, which corrects for agreement by chance. If trained annotators can’t agree on a criterion, a judge model won’t either.

Specific criteria make scores consistent and explainable. When a score drops, you know which behavior caused it.

Before relying on a judge, check how often it agrees with your human annotators. Have annotators label a representative sample, run the judge on the same sample and compare the results. Overall agreement is a starting point, but the real value is in the disagreements.

Annotator expertise matters just as much. With external annotation vendors, subject matter experts are a must-have. A generalist can tell whether a response reads well, but only a domain expert can tell whether a policy exception or a data change is correct. Without that expertise, you risk aligning your judge to labels that are wrong.

Train annotators with a typical data science workflow, including documented guidelines, representative examples and edge cases, and an independently labeled pilot. With domain experts, review disagreements, refine guidelines and recalibrate before scaling. Use expert-reviewed reference examples for ongoing quality checks and version guidelines to keep labels traceable as the task evolves.

Look for the slices where the judge gets it wrong. These errors are rarely random. A judge may be too lenient on responses that sound confident, struggle with domain terminology or miss errors buried in long multistep traces. Update the rubric, add examples or split the criterion and then measure agreement again.

Recheck alignment whenever you change the judge prompt, swap the judge model or launch a new scenario.

Without trust, metrics don’t drive decisions. I’ve seen teams review dashboards weekly yet change nothing. Then stakeholders doubt the scores and fall back on demos and anecdotes.

Before a metric goes live, align product, engineering and business stakeholders on what each value means and what happens in response. For example:

• Release Gates: What score, or what drop from the baseline, blocks a release?

• Investment Priorities: Which failing slice gets engineering time next cycle?

• Escalation: What regression on high-risk scenarios triggers review before shipping?

Reliability matters here, too. Run the judge several times on the same data to measure its variance, so noise isn’t mistaken for a real change. A metric that can’t distinguish a regression from noise shouldn’t gate a release.

AI-assisted evaluation scales review, not the work of defining quality. Start with clear instructions and a narrow, trusted metric, then add complexity as alignment and process mature. Evals that evolve with the product drive decisions rather than remain limited to reporting.
