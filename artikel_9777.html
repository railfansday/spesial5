Nvidia without Jensen Huang: the resignation, the earnings and the GPU/CUDA concentration risk

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

Posted on Nov 21 • Originally published at programero.blogspot.com

This post was originally published in Portuguese on my blog: Nvidia sem Jensen Huang: renúncia, balanço e risco de concentração em GPU/CUDA.

In 33 years of history, Nvidia has had only one CEO. Last Thursday, November 12, that ended: Jensen Huang resigned as chairman, CEO and board member, effective immediately. The CFO, Colette Kress, took over as interim CEO while the board searches for a successor. Harvey Jones, on the board since 1993, became the first chair of the board in the company's history.

The official announcement gives no reason. What is known comes from the press. The New York Times and the Financial Times reported in the previous weeks that Huang had a relationship with an executive who, according to the reports, was the target of a Chinese intelligence operation, and the board hired an independent investigation. The day before the resignation, the New York Times added that there was a second relationship, with a vice president who reported directly to him, and the Wall Street Journal said the board was preparing to remove him. Nvidia didn't confirm these reports, so it's worth treating them as reporting, not settled fact.

The stock fell more than 10% at the open that day. Five days later, Nvidia released results for the quarter ended in October, the first without Huang: revenue of US$ 115.8 billion, up 103% year over year, with US$ 107.6 billion coming from data center. Guidance for the next quarter is US$ 130 billion, without counting any data center compute sales to China. In other words, the machine keeps running.

First, the obvious: nothing breaks today. CUDA still compiles, the published product roadmap still stands, and the Rubin platform is already shipping. Analysts quoted in the press say much of the next two years is already contracted and designed. If your training and inference pipeline is stable, the CEO's resignation is no reason to touch it.

The more interesting point is a different one. Analysts described Nvidia as a company built around one person, with no named successor, where many decisions about pace and priorities went through him. That matters because almost everyone building AI today depends on this company: clouds, startups, in-house data teams. A consultancy cited by CIO recommended exactly this to technology leaders: don't overreact, but use the moment to review concentration risk in AI infrastructure suppliers.

For developers, the message is more concrete. According to Nvidia's own annual report, more than 7.5 million developers use CUDA and its tools. It's a huge ecosystem, and that coupling is exactly what makes switching vendors expensive. I see no reason to migrate anything because of a governance crisis. But I do see a reason to ask a question almost nobody asks until they have to: if tomorrow I had to run this on other hardware, how much of my code is glued to a specific API and how much sits behind a layer I control? Finding out the answer costs an afternoon. Finding out at the wrong time costs a quarter.

There's a parallel with our day-to-day that seems more useful to me than the gossip. Every team knows the bus factor, that person who, if they leave, takes with them the knowledge nobody documented. Nvidia is worth trillions and had the same problem, just at industry scale. The successor most cited by analysts is Ian Buck, the engineer who helped create CUDA and now runs the data center business, and other outside names, like Arm's CEO, show up on the lists. If the pick is a technical profile from inside, the roadmap should stay as it is. If someone comes from outside, the conversation changes.

I also think the story has an uncomfortable side for the whole industry: an executive's personal life became a national security issue and an AI supply chain issue at the same time. When a technology's infrastructure depends this much on one company, any fragility of theirs becomes everyone's fragility. It's worth watching the succession process over the next few weeks, because it will say more about the platform's future than any headline about the resignation.
