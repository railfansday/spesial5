As I write this, I am sitting with my companion in the lovely, glass-walled Explorers' Lounge atop the Viking ocean ship Viking Sky, watching the coast of Spain scroll by.

The setting is, in a word, refined. The Scandinavian-influenced decor is upscale and residential, the music classy (for the last half hour, a classical pianist and violinist have been playing), the service attentive.

Like all the spaces on Viking ocean ships, it is a venue custom-designed for people like me, in a way you won't quite find anywhere else at sea.

And by people like me, I mean people of a certain age, as they say. Or even older.

At 57, my companion and I are on the younger side among passengers. Most are in their 60s, 70s and 80s. And that's not by accident.

Some cruise lines try to be all things to all people, offering onboard venues and activities designed to appeal to every possible age group and interest. Viking isn't one of them.

Since its founding just 29 years ago, the upscale cruise brand has carved out a niche (and had huge success), catering specifically to a certain type of older traveler, not just through the design of its ships, but through the curated experiences it offers both on board and ashore.

For this subset of travelers, as I am seeing this week on a seven-night "Iconic Western Mediterranean" sailing from Barcelona to Civitavecchia, Italy (the port for Rome), and have seen on many Viking voyages before, Viking has crafted what just might be — in my opinion, at least — the ultimate cruising experience.

Regular readers know that I cruise with a wide range of brands every year and, as I've written before, I find many of them to offer wonderful experiences. But I've long had a particular fondness for Viking — even before I finally aged into its target 55-plus demographic.

More than anything else, that's because it's one of the rare lines that makes the destinations it visits the focus of the experience, something that not only matches my personal tastes in travel but also resonates particularly with older travelers.

Unlike the cruises offered by the big megaship lines, which are more family-focused, these aren't trips that revolve heavily around a resort-like onboard experience (think sprawling pools and water parks, amusement rides and bustling entertainment zones), with the destination being almost an afterthought. They are more oriented toward what you will experience off the ship.

The former is often what you want when you're younger, when your vacation time is short, and you just want to relax and play a little after months of working, or spend quality time with the kids. Cultural immersion and sightseeing often aren't the top priority.

But that often changes as you get older and finally have the time (and money) to see all the places you didn't have time to visit while raising kids and establishing careers in your younger years. Viking's sailings are perfectly designed for the latter.

Something that I have always loved about Viking is that its ships often take it slow when sailing around a region, staying in ports longer than is typical and spending less time at sea moving from place to place. That, in turn, gives passengers more time to explore during stops than is typical on cruises.

On this seven-night Iconic Western Mediterranean sailing, for instance, we are spending two full days in both Barcelona, where the cruise begins, and Livorno, Italy (the port for Florence and Pisa) — an unusual number of overnight stays for such a short voyage.

In another port along the way, Sete, France, we remain docked until 8 p.m., late enough for passengers to head into town in the early evening for one of its famous octopus pies and a local Picpoul wine.

We can do this because we never travel more than 200 miles between ports. From Sete to the next stop, Marseille, for instance, is just 82 miles.

Another thing I love about Viking is how it helps passengers get to know each place they visit. In contrast to most other ocean lines, Viking includes a guided tour in every port at no extra charge.

This is something that even some of its highest-end ocean cruise rivals, such as Seabourn, Crystal and Explora Journeys, don't do.

During our overnight stay in Barcelona, this means an included walk with a local guide around the city's most iconic sites, Antoni Gaudí's surreal Basilica de la Sagrada Familia, and the nearby Gothic Quarter.

The next day, in tiny Sete (pop. 45,000), it means wandering with a guide through the charming, restaurant-lined canals from which the town gets its "Venice of the Languedoc" nickname to a bustling central market, where some of us slurp down piles of local oysters.

In Livorno, it means heading to nearby Pisa, where we join the throngs pretending to hold up the city's famous leaning tower for silly pictures while learning from a guide about the 850-year saga of engineering miscalculations that left it atilt.

There are also more in-depth tours available for an extra charge. On one of our two days in Livorno, my companion and I signed up for an all-day, $209-per-person tour to Florence that got us to many of its most iconic sites, including the soaring Duomo.

But the bottom line is that everywhere we go, Viking is helping us to see the amazing things that we have taken the trip to see, from UNESCO World Heritage-listed monuments to celebrated museums and cultural institutions, in the easiest, non-nickel-and-diming sort of way.

And then there's the lovely, grown-up design and serene, nothing-too-loud-or-flashy atmosphere of Viking ocean ships, which is just what you want when you're heading into your later years.

It's not just the Explorers' Lounge that is my happy place when I sail on one of these ships (at last count, there were 13, all essentially the same), but really the entirety of the vessel.

From the easy flow of interior spaces to the understated sophistication of the Scandinavian-influenced decor, the look is unified, refined and residential in a way that is rare in the cruise industry.

Or, to put another way, it's the elegant and comfortable second home that people my age and older have always wanted, except that it isn't fixed in one place.

At the core of the public areas on each vessel is a central atrium-like space that, in contrast to what you find on many ships, is an oasis of calm and civility. Called The Living Room, it is just that, a living room (albeit a big one), with comfortable and cozy seating areas, residential in their feel, that are perfect for conversation over coffee and pastries from the cafe in one corner.

I love just sitting in this space, sipping a cappuccino, reading one of the books on art and design in the small library in another corner, or listening to the classical pianist who sometimes plays the Steinway at the foot of the room's grand staircase.

That staircase leads up to a second level, where additional seating areas feature high-tech tables for playing digital versions of chess, mahjong and other games. I've spent hours up there on Viking sailings with my companion and newfound friends.

The Living Room is one of two main gathering spots on every Viking ocean ship, the other being the two-deck-high Explorers' Lounge, with its soaring glass walls that make the passing scenery the "art." Also residential in feel, it is where I always seem to find myself on any given day, soaking in the beauty on display through the glass — a coffee in my hand if it's early, a cocktail later in the day.

That is, if I can tear myself away from the tranquility of my room. The cabins on Viking ocean ships are triumphs of cozy and comfortable Scandinavian design (the Norwegians have a word, hygge, for how this makes you feel), with elegant touches big and small (I love the heated floors and anti-fog mirrors in the bathrooms) that make you never want to leave.

Viking vessels are also notably small when compared to many of the giant ships debuting these days, which creates its own kind of elegance. In an era of ever-bigger megaships that can hold as many as 7,000 people, Viking decided to keep its vessels human-sized. No matter where you are on board, you never feel like you're traveling with a crowd.

Viking's focus on the destinations its ships visit goes beyond what it offers during port stops. It also infuses everything you experience on board.

I love that the activity schedule often revolves heavily around what the line calls "cultural enrichment" — lectures by experts on topics related to the places its ships visit, as well as cultural and culinary offerings that often have a local tie-in.

On our voyage across the Western Mediterranean, for instance, a historian gives us broad overviews of the two main countries that we are visiting, France and Italy, during lectures in the theater, covering everything from Roman times up through the Second World War. Just before stops in France and Monaco, another expert regales us with tales of iconic French artists such as Claude Monet and the backstory of the Monaco Grand Prix.

During dinners in the main dining room, there's always an array of local "destination menu" items that ties to the places we are visiting — a Spanish red bean soup appetizer, grilled lemon marinated chicken main course and rice milk pudding while we are docked in Barcelona, for instance.

There is musical entertainment, too, that sometimes has a local tie-in. During our overnight in Barcelona, local flamenco dancers come on board to perform in the ship's theater. But it's notably not the sort of overly loud and flashy musical entertainment you often get on cruises — and aimed at an older crowd.

Instead of raunchy comedians and ear-splitting, strobe-light-flashing musical extravaganzas, our sailing offers a less frenetic lineup of theater performances, ranging from the ship's vocalists singing ABBA songs to Broadway show tunes. On the night we're docked in Livorno, a local tenor comes on board to sing opera.

Just steps away from the theater, at the intimate Torshaven nightclub, there's often an acoustic guitarist playing in the evening, or a vocal duo with a small band.

Another entertainment highlight on my sailing has been a pianist playing traditional Norwegian music in The Living Room in the early evenings while works by the Norwegian artist Edvard Munch flash across a large digital screen. Viking calls these Munch Moments, and they're about as classy as can be.

Up in the Explorers' Lounge, meanwhile, evenings typically bring live music from a guitarist, a pianist or a classical pianist-and-violinist duo. But it's live music played softly enough that you can still hold a conversation.

Other daily entertainment includes an elaborate afternoon tea in the glass-enclosed Wintergarden atop the ship, accompanied by classical music.

What Viking ships don't offer — and, for many of its older customers, this is a significant part of the appeal — are any onboard amusements that would draw families, such as waterslides or go-kart tracks. They're not those sorts of ships.

In fact, the line doesn't even allow children under 18 on its ships. It's one of the only major cruise brands in the world with such a rule.

Viking ships also don't have casinos, which is unusual. They're not those sorts of ships, either.

It's a calm, quiet and cultured sort of vibe.

Travel with Viking is also travel made easy — in a way that matters a lot more as you get older. I know it makes a big difference to me.

It starts with little design touches that make it easier if you are, say, experiencing declining eyesight or becoming less sure-footed.

I hate to admit this, but I sometimes have trouble now reading the small print on toiletries in many hotels — even the fanciest ones. If you're my age or older, you know what I'm talking about. That tiny scribble might say "body wash," "shampoo," or "body lotion" — who knows. It's a toiletries lottery until you squeeze it out.

On Viking ships, by contrast, all the toiletries come in custom-designed containers with oversized, clear type. Even without my reading glasses, I can see what it is.

As someone who is increasingly struggling to read small type in low light (as is typical for people my age), I also truly appreciate the high-lumen reading lamps built right into the headboards of cabin beds. That is Viking knowing its customer. I also love the ring-lit or backlit mirrors in bathrooms that make grooming (or putting on jewelry) easier for those with changing vision. And I'm a big fan of the easy-to-understand television remote controls that have just a handful of big, easy-to-understand buttons.

The latter is something I wish more hospitality brands would do, not just for older travelers but travelers of all ages. Even in my 30s and 40s, I found the typical button-jammed television remotes maddening.

There are small things, maybe. And Viking doesn't call them out. Nobody likes to be reminded that they're getting older. But they're all part of the brand's deliberate focus on older travelers.

Other nods to older customers include a deliberately relaxed pace for walking tours and relatively low solo fares (since many older travelers travel solo).

It's a "travel made easy" mindset that also extends to ease in booking.

Viking fares are notably mostly inclusive, with a lot bundled into the price, in keeping with a "no nickel-and-diming" philosophy. Not only are your accommodations included in the fare, but your meals (including wine, beer and soft drinks) are and well.

Some higher-level cabins also come with complimentary laundry service and a premium beverage package. Little things like shipboard WiFi are also included for all.

As noted above, you also get guided tours in every port you visit, and Viking will also book your airport transfers if you're booking international flights through Viking. In addition, it offers a wide and growing range of add-on pre- and post-cruise land tours and hotel stays that can round out your trip.

Taken together, it makes for about as easy-to-book a trip as can be, one where you set it all up with one call to a travel agent or Viking directly, or with a few clicks on the website, and then basically don't have to plan (or pay for) a thing from the moment you leave the house. You just show up.

As you get older, this sort of simplified travel planning becomes more important.

Viking is a brand that is sometimes misunderstood by would-be cruisers. It's not for everyone — and that's by design. Family travelers with young kids, for instance, aren't even welcome on the line's ships. But it's a wonderful line for a certain type of older traveler who wants to explore the world in their later years and do it in comfort.

Viking is a cruise company that, in many ways big and small, has created the perfect vacation experience for people around my age (57) and older — particularly retirees in their 60s, 70s and 80s. It's a brand with a particular focus on the destinations its ships visit, learning and cultural enrichment, and onboard elegance and ambiance that are perfect for an older crowd.
