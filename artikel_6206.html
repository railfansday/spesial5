I’m a Neurosurgeon. My Near-Death Experience Showed Me ‘Universal Consciousness’ for the First Time.

Tanggal: 2026-11-15

By comparison, it makes our material world feel “murky.”

Here’s what you’ll learn when you read this story:

When I earned my medical degree at Duke University, I was taught that consciousness is created out of chemical reactions occurring within the brain, a foundational assumption of scientific materialism, or physicalism—the notion that only the physical exists. After eleven years of rigorous training, I practiced as a neurosurgeon for more than two decades, including 15 years teaching and operating at Harvard Medical School.

I performed surgical procedures that took patients from extreme discomfort to full relief by releasing a nerve blockage or removing a tumor. I could stimulate the brain in awake patients and affect their mental experience, which initially seemed to support my physicalist worldview. At the same time, patients deeply comatose from brain maladies would sometimes become fully alert right before dying. I couldn’t explain these phenomena, and so I dismissed them. That is, until November 2008, when a severe case of bacterial meningitis caused me to spend seven days in deep coma—and later reshaped my view of the universe.

Over three hours, I went from back pain and headache to epileptic fits and coma, as E. coli bacteria devoured my neocortex, or the outer surface of the brain, the part that is most responsible for human mentation. Healthcare professionals use the Glasgow Coma Scale to measure decreases in consciousness, with scores less than 9 indicating deep coma. A corpse would score a 3, the lowest grade on the scale, and my scores were 6 to 7 during most of that week—the doctor was out! Lumbar puncture revealed my cerebrospinal fluid to be filled with pus under pressure, and the glucose level in that fluid was only 1 mg/dl (normally 80-90, with severe bacterial meningitis occasionally as low as 20). CT and MRI scans revealed my entire neocortex was involved, meaning there was no physical functioning brain left to produce a dream or hallucination.

By the tenets of modern neuroscience, my awareness should have been zero. Without a functioning neocortex, my brain was incapable of producing a simple thought or image—and yet, I lived through a vivid, richly-detailed, hyper-real event, by comparison making this material world appear murky and dreamlike. I was unbound by space and time, immersed in universal consciousness, and wrapped in unconditional love. I had visions revealing structure to the non-physical realm that fully relates to and intertwines with our lives here on earth.

Back in the hospital, my prospects for survival were down to 2 percent when, on day seven, I startled everyone as I began to awaken. My doctors were even more surprised when I fully recovered over about two months. As my medical knowledge returned, I reviewed my records and scans, and discussed them with my doctors, trying to make sense of it all. I searched for a physicalist explanation, yet the case was unyielding—no conventional explanation could suffice. I was forced to acknowledge that an expansive model of reality was required to explain my experience.

When my case records were peer-reviewed before publication in the Journal of Nervous and Mental Disease, the editors asked the authors to explain my unprecedented recovery. They replied that my miraculous recovery occurred because of my spiritual journey, knowing of similar extraordinary recoveries in the near-death literature. Due to techniques of cardiac resuscitation developed in the 1960s, millions of others have escaped the shackles of the physical brain and body, and reported similar near-death experiences (NDEs) of connection with a non-physical realm. Those descriptions have been categorized and evaluated using Greyson’s NDE Scale (developed by UVA psychiatrist Bruce Greyson), which quantifies the cognitive, affective, paranormal, and transcendental aspects of the experiences.

The vast evidence from these unusual experiences has convinced many former physicalists that their model is outdated. A more sufficient explanation is that the brain serves as a transceiver or antenna for tuning in to a larger, collective consciousness. Renowned Harvard psychologist William James, French philosopher Henri Bergson, and English writer Aldous Huxley, all postulated this “reducing valve” role for the brain in the early 20th century, noting that a shift in the primary physicalist assumption was required to explain these phenomena.

With a growing abundance of data, it is clearer that consciousness is fundamental and influences physical matter from the top down, rather than the bottom-up model of physicalism, where consciousness arises from the brain. Spontaneous remissions and the placebo effect confirm the power of the mind over matter and validate the observer effect seen in quantum mechanics experiments.

The “hard problem of consciousness” remains unsolved because its underlying premise is false.

I believe merging first-person subjective inquiry with rigorous empirical science will take us farther in knowing the true nature of our consensus reality.

To that point, humanity will thrive when science finally acknowledges that we are interconnected through love and part of one larger consciousness, with free will to become the best version of ourselves, both as individuals and collectively. The golden rule is woven directly into the fabric of the universe, providing a moral compass that shows that, ultimately, what matters most is how we treat each other.
