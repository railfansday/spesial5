What Makes The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time So Great Anyway?

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Is it just nostalgia, or is there something else?

Is it just nostalgia, or is there something else?

Spend even a little time online in gaming circles, and you'll inevitably come across claims that The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time is one of the greatest games ever made. Those claims resurfaced with greater frequency with the announcement of the Nintendo Switch 2 Ocarina of Time remake and subsequent warm reception from critics. The reaction from those I know who played for the first time and enjoyed it has generally been "yes, it really is one of the greats." What there's been less of, however, is a concerted effort to explain what exactly makes it deserving of that praise. Make no mistake, it does deserve it — and not just for reasons of nostalgia. That’s because Ocarina of Time anticipated some of gaming's biggest trends, and now that those trends have spiralled out of control, the remake serves as a blueprint for good game design moving forward.

Like most video games whose stories endure, Ocarina of Time blends elements of classic narrative styles. It's a fairy tale tinged with tragedy, a coming-of-age tale, and a remix of classic archetypes all mashed up together, and it's one of the few games that uses its game-ness to help tell that story. The landscape of Link's adult life changes dramatically, in obvious ways like all his friends scattering and various tragedies befalling Hyrule's people, but also in more mundane ways that drive a sharp distinction between past and present.

Ghosts haunt Hyrule field, and new, stronger monsters lurk in places that young Link once played in safely, without a worry. Grown Link can ride a horse. He wears clothes his child self would never have managed, uses tools too big for a kid, and is a master of combat arts. Liked the slingshot? Tough luck, buddy. That's for kids. Adults use real projectile weapons. These tools and the addition of a horse to Link's circle of friends give him access to new parts of Hyrule he never could've visited as a child — partly for reasons of geography, but also for the simple fact that he wouldn't have stood a chance against an angry Gerudo in his younger days.

These changes create a satisfying sense of progression, sure, but they're also one of the only examples of a coming-of-age story told through gameplay. Normally, such tales get their message across through some personal ordeal, or cumulative learning, or a big epiphany. But Link has a limited personality, so those methods wouldn’t work here. Instead, coming of age for Link means literally navigating the landscape of adulthood and adjusting to the new opportunities and limitations it presents, while the memory of childhood is never allowed to fade. No other game combined its storytelling and interactive elements in such a way when Ocarina of Time first launched. Few have done it since, and none as successfully.

But Ocarina of Time was ahead of its time in more than just storytelling. It sits in the Goldilocks Zone between linearity and open-endedness. Ocarina won't hold your hand, but its relatively small size and contained nature mean you're bound to eventually work out the right path in a dungeon or the answer to a riddle on your own. It also means you're bound to run across some of its less immediately obvious side quests simply by chance, just from talking to people or investigating peculiarities in the environment. The lack of signposting creates an emergent feel that puts Ocarina closer to the likes of Disco Elysium than it does other action-adventure games of the '90s and, admittedly, most other rigidly designed modern games that fear you'll miss a side quest or line of dialogue if you aren't told where to look.

Ocarina’s rewards are usually small and limited, or sometimes just plain goofy. The tasks they're associated with demand comparatively little time, and the effort involved is effort engaging with the world — exploring out-of-the-way places, speaking with people, piecing together vague environmental clues — not clearing huge challenges or gathering dozens of unnecessary collectibles.

You can sell four masks from the vendor in Hyrule Castle Town and get a fifth fun-but-impractical mask for your trouble, without feeling like that reward massively disrespects your time. It's fine that the hole in the ground on Death Mountain only houses a cow (questionable from an animal ethics standpoint, perhaps, but fine otherwise) because it took you under one minute to reach this point from elsewhere on the map. The existence of a secret underground cow is reward enough in itself. The practical side of it, some restorative milk for an empty bottle, is a nice bonus. Nintendo built Ocarina of Time around curiosity and a drive for exploration. It was the pinnacle of open-ended design at the time, and a philosophy driven by fun over content is something modern game developers are only just gradually rediscovering now.

The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time is timeless. It's a standout example of creativity in a contained scope and a rare instance where video games embrace what distinguishes them from other mediums and puts it to good use. Nostalgia might encourage fond feelings toward it, but it has a lot to offer beyond that and should serve as a guidepost for good game development moving forward.

On Saturday nights, I’d take my Nintendo 64 cartridge upstairs to Mémère’s apartment. She always knew where I needed to go next.
