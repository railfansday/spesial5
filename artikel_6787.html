This Forgotten ’90s Comedy Is the Most Demented Thanksgiving Movie Ever

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Ed O’Neill and Ethan Embry go on the road in Dutch but end up in some bizarre and disturbing places.

Let’s start off by acknowledging that the late John Hughes wrote and directed the greatest of all Thanksgiving movies with Planes, Trains and Automobiles. There are definitely other great ones, but … Dutch is a different kind of creature. This is a Thanksgiving family comedy in which a grown man fights, kidnaps, and abandons a child. Occasionally he holds him at gunpoint. It’s a rotten child but a kid nonetheless. The movie still made me laugh, but it was mostly in disbelief.

This road-trip saga that Hughes penned and produced in 1991 is by the numbers in most ways but excels in its brazen wrongness, even by the free-range standards of Generation X. I come here to praise this messy movie, not to bury it. It’s a relic from when films could be wilder, shaggier, and more shocking than the rigidly tame family comedies of today. Dutch is many things, but it is not refined to baby-food levels of inoffensive smoothness.

Hughes didn’t direct this one, handing off those duties to Peter Faiman, who had worked extensively in Australian TV and only made one other feature film: 1986’s Crocodile Dundee. I can’t help but feel this was an experiment gone wrong, or maybe a first draft that needed another pass from Hughes. It’s built out of leftovers from better road-trip movies, and like any Frankenstein’s monster, this one runs straight into a wall and ultimately falls apart.

But if you want something different for your Thanksgiving watch, go find this one. Fair warning: It’s not streaming anywhere in the United States, so you’ll have to seek it out on a sketchy Russian bootleg site that makes it look like the footage was ripped from a worn-down VHS.

Dutch stars Ed O’Neill as the title character, a blue-collar guy sent to pick up his girlfriend’s blue-blooded son from a Georgia boarding school and deliver him to Chicago in time for the holiday feast. The not-so-surprising catch is that the kid (played by a 13-year-old Ethan Embry) mercilessly despises Dutch, and honestly the feeling is mutual.

Doyle is one of the most hateful kids in comedy history. Even the ginger demon of Problem Child was more cartoonishly mischievous than sociopathic. Embry, who was then credited as Ethan Randall, isn’t playing a troublemaking scamp like the kid in O. Henry’s “The Ransom of Red Chief” or Hughes’s own Home Alone. He’s a smug sociopath with a violent mean streak.

We learn that Dutch is a salt-of-the-earth cousin to O’Neill’s uncouth Al Bundy from Married with Children because he flicks the caviar off his cracker at a fancy party. (“I make it a rule never to eat anything a fish deposits in a riverbed.”) Okay, fair.

Compare that to how we meet Doyle: Another young boy in his boarding school comes to his room to say he has a call on the dormitory’s community phone line. “Someone from any sort of decent background would knock,” Embry’s character hisses. He then corrects the kid’s vocabulary and threatens to report him to the school administrators for mild cursing. So he’s a snob, a prick, and a rat. But even this is within the bounds of film comedy. You could picture an older version of him as a stuck-up villain in Caddyshack or Animal House.

Then there’s another, even more disturbing phone scene when Doyle gets the bad news that his rich father won’t be helping him get out of this road trip with Dutch. He hangs up and stalks past an elderly janitor who is sweeping the hallway, silently kicking over the garbage can to scatter trash across the helpless old man’s feet. There’s no joke. He’s just a son of a bitch.

Doyle would fit in great with the Children of the Corn, except he’d scowl at their shabby farm clothes.

Obviously this kid is going to get some form of comeuppance. When Dutch shows up at his dorm room to start the trip, Doyle beats him with a golf club and shoots him in the testicles with a BB gun. Something about this is much more violent and visceral than the Looney Tunes–style pratfalls and clobberings of Home Alone.

They have a fistfight in the tiny room, and Dutch seizes the weapon. Does he toss it away or break it? No—he uses it himself to hold the kid at gunpoint. We have just crossed beyond the boundaries of comedy, never to return. Dutch then ties the boy up, lashes him to a hockey stick for easier carrying, and stuffs underwear in his mouth to muffle his cries.

You know a movie is going off the rails when watching it makes you feel like you should be testifying against the characters as a witness for the prosecution.

So begins the drive, with Dutch and Doyle doing their best to annoy each other verbally. Then comes a bizarre scene in which Dutch stops for fireworks for some reason and sets them off in a snowy field while the kid watches from the car. I suppose he’s trying to inspire a little playfulness in the boy, but it just seems like Dutch has lost it and is indulging a pyromaniac streak.

It goes wrong and he sets his coat on fire, which makes it harder to understand why he loses his temper when the boy flicks the butt of his lit cigar at him. The man just went mano a mano with a whizzing fireworks spinner, but this is what lights his fuse?

Dutch does the reasonable thing. He abandons the boy in the snowy night and tells him to walk the rest of the way to the motel. “About 50 miles down the road!” Fifty miles? Okay, Dutch, I believe we’ve added another felony to the charges.

This isn’t one of those moments where the grown-up pulls over just beyond the tree line and waits for him. Dutch actually goes to the hotel, although he occasionally puts down his paperback and checks the window to see if the boy has made it there alive. When Doyle finally does show up, shivering and wet, Dutch says, “Here’s something you should know about me. I don’t fool around. You piss me off, I react.”

Uncle Buck certainly had its off-color and irresponsible moments, but even that havoc-prone babysitter might have called Child Protective Services over this situation.

Doyle responds by stealing Dutch’s car keys while he sleeps and parking the sedan sideways in the snowy road, where it is slammed into by an 18-wheeler. This is a comedy, so the hapless truck driver is not killed on impact.

Faiman shoots all of this with too much gritty realism. Maybe it would work if it was all a little zanier and over the top (think of Vacation), but Dutch just plays less like a series of set pieces and more like an array of crime scenes. They don’t call anyone for help because Dutch doesn’t want to admit to his girlfriend how badly things are going, so they hitchhike the rest of the way and stay in a homeless shelter, where the rotten kid learns that a lot of people have it much worse than he does.

At one point, they are picked up by a pair of pretty young women who are driving home from a job at the Superdome in New Orleans. “We’re call girls,” one of them explains. Hoo boy. Here we go.

But thankfully—mercifully—Dutch goes no further than showing the kid sleeping in the backseat with his head resting on the chest of one of the women. Not great, but it could be worse. (Worse is when Dutch tries to bond with the child over a deck of naked playing cards.)

Even Dutch seems to recognize that “prostitute road trip” probably isn’t the ideal situation for a young boy. But he explains it in the most vulgar way possible: “You and I are riding in the backseat from now on, because what that missile twister can teach you, you don’t need to learn until you’re in prison.” Then the ladies drive off without them, taking Dutch’s wallet and leaving the pair stranded again.

They spend the next leg of their trip riding in the open concrete pipes in the back of a long-haul truck, but they fall asleep and wake up in a trailer depot where they are roughed up by two security guards. Doyle responds by drawing his BB gun on them and making them believe he is going to blow their heads off.

This same joke was deployed by Chevy Chase in the Hughes-scripted National Lampoon’s Vacation (“Are you kidding, this is a Magnum P.I.”) but here, again, it just plays as psychotic violence rather than absurd humor. “The kid is crazy,” Dutch tells the men as they beg for their lives.

By the time the duo finally get home for the Thanksgiving meal, the hostility between Dutch and the kid has softened into a grudging respect. I know: Surprise! Back at the mother’s house, Dutch physically assaults the boy’s callous father, leaving a mark with his pinkie ring on the jerk’s forehead. Instead of an assault charge, it all ends happily ever after with a callback to an earlier gag: Dutch pulls the BB pistol from his pocket and says it’s time to get the revenge he promised he would one day seek. As the boy’s smile fades, Dutch aims the gun at him—and us (!!!)—as the film cuts to the credits.

Oh, Dutch … you dangerously inappropriate maniac. Don’t ever change. And, well, he doesn’t, although the kid goes through the expected metamorphosis to become a more sensitive and understanding human.

They don’t make ’em like this anymore, as the saying goes, and I actually think that’s a pity. Dutch isn’t all that funny, but neither is toothless humor that’s overly safe. I’d gladly tolerate a few misfires like this if it meant more truly unhinged comedies like we had in the ’80s, ’90s, and early 2000s. Dutch stands as a throwback to when small-scale comedies weren’t afraid to take big swings—even if they occasionally landed against someone’s skull or testicles.
