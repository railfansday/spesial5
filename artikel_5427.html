Blockbuster games now earn and cost like big films, and 10 forces explain why

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Why video game budgets have exploded, from Grand Theft Auto V to Marvel's Spider-Man 2 and Concord, explained through 10 cost drivers shaping how blockbuster games get made

Grand Theft Auto V passed $1 billion in sales within three days of its September 2013 release, according to publisher Take-Two Interactive $TTWO. Returns on that scale give publishers a reason to spend heavily on the next potential hit.

The spending behind those hits is harder to see. Most publishers do not disclose what an individual game costs to make. Public figures are scarce. One of the clearest comes from leaked Insomniac Games documents. Press reports on those files put the cost of the 2023 game Marvel's Spider-Man 2 at $315 million. That number comes from the leak, not from a company announcement.

Explaining why video game budgets have exploded means looking at several pressures at once. Hardware is one. The PlayStation 5 and Xbox Series X $TWTR both launched in November 2020. Each new console generation asks studios for more detailed art, animation and environments. People are another. Big games depend on actors for motion capture and voice work. Video game performers represented by SAG-AFTRA began a strike on July 26, 2024.

Release gaps show how long the work can take. Rockstar Games released Red Dead Redemption 2 in October 2018, eight years after the first Red Dead Redemption arrived in May 2010. Every year in production adds to the bill.

Marketing is a separate expense on top of development. Publishers rarely say what they spend on it.

The companies behind games have grown larger as well. Microsoft $MSFT announced its $68.7 billion deal for Activision $ATVI Blizzard in January 2022 and closed it in October 2023. Prices have moved too. A $60 price was standard for new games for many years. Nintendo priced Mario Kart World at $79.99 when it announced the game in April 2025.

Higher spending also raises the cost of failure. Firewalk Studios released the live-service shooter Concord on August 23, 2024. Sony $SONY pulled the game on September 6, 2024, and closed Firewalk in October 2024.

The 10 slides below cover a selection of the forces behind modern game budgets. They end with Larian Studios' Baldur's Gate 3, which won Game of the Year at the 2023 Game Awards, as a different route to a hit.

Grand Theft Auto V showed how much money one blockbuster game can make in its first days. Rockstar Games released it in September 2013. Its parent company, Take-Two Interactive $TTWO, reported that the game brought in $800 million in sales on its first day and passed $1 billion within three days.

Those totals came from the publisher itself. That matters in an industry where hard numbers are scarce. Publishers often keep detailed sales data private. A confirmed launch figure gives outsiders one of the few clear public reference points for what a top-tier release can earn.

The game arrived on PlayStation 3 and Xbox 360. Rockstar North, the Edinburgh studio that has long led the series, built it around three playable protagonists named Michael, Franklin and Trevor. Players could switch among them during the story. The plot was structured around how their lives overlapped.

The setting was Los Santos, a fictional city modeled on Los Angeles. Around it sat the countryside, desert and coastline of the fictional state of San Andreas. Filling a map of that size with streets, buildings, traffic and characters is the kind of work that demands large teams of artists, designers, writers and engineers.

The launch gave the industry a public benchmark. Publishers planning their own flagship projects now had a confirmed example of how large and how fast the return on a major bet could be.

That payoff is part of the logic behind larger spending. When a single title can bring in that much revenue so quickly, the argument for funding bigger teams and more detailed worlds becomes easier to make inside a publisher.

Rockstar also kept selling the same game for years. It later released versions for PlayStation 4, Xbox One and PC. It then released versions for PlayStation 5 and Xbox Series X $TWTR and Series S. Each release extended the commercial life of a game first sold in 2013.

Red Dead Redemption 2 came out in October 2018, eight years after the original Red Dead Redemption launched in May 2010. Long gaps between major releases are one sign of how long blockbuster production can run.

Rockstar Games made both games. Between the two Red Dead launches, the studio also released Grand Theft Auto V.

A long production cycle raises a budget in a direct way. A studio pays its staff for every year a game is in development. Those salaries build up before a single copy is sold. A project that runs for many years has to recover all of that spending after release.

Time adds costs beyond payroll, too. Office space, software licenses and equipment are paid for across the full life of a project. A studio also carries the risk that player tastes or technology will shift before the game is finished.

Long cycles create a trade-off as well. A studio that spends years on each game releases fewer games. Each release then has to cover a larger share of the studio's costs.

The gap shapes the series itself. Without a new release, a franchise has no fresh launch to drive a wave of sales. Players who finished the first game have to wait for the story to continue. The publisher has to keep interest in the name alive across that wait.

Take-Two Interactive $TTWO, Rockstar's parent company, reported that Red Dead Redemption 2 earned $725 million in its first three days on sale. That opening came after a stretch of years in which the series had no new main entry to sell.

The $725 million figure measures sales, not profit. Take-Two did not report a production cost alongside it. The number shows how quickly a long project can bring money in. It does not show how much of that money remained after years of spending.

Leaked Insomniac Games documents, as reported by the press, put the cost of making Marvel's Spider-Man 2 at $315 million. The 2023 game's budget became public through that leak, not through an announcement by the studio or its publisher.

A leaked internal figure is a reported cost, not a confirmed one. It shows the scale of one project. It does not show how the money was counted, which expenses it covers or how it was spread across the production.

Those gaps matter when the number is set beside other games. Two studios can define a budget in different ways. One might include years of salaries, outside contractors and licensing. Another might leave some of those items out. The figure also leaves open whether marketing falls inside the total. Without the underlying accounting, a single total cannot be lined up cleanly against another project's cost.

Hard budget numbers rarely surface in the games business. Sales milestones often get public announcements. Production spending usually stays inside the company.

That leaves public discussion of rising costs resting on a small set of data points. A single document from inside one studio can carry a lot of weight in that discussion. The Spider-Man 2 number became one of those reference points after the press reported on the leaked Insomniac Games files. For readers trying to gauge how expensive blockbuster games have become, it offers a rare concrete number tied to a named title.

The game itself also shapes what the figure represents. Marvel's Spider-Man 2 is a sequel built around a licensed Marvel character. The total therefore belongs to a franchise project, not a new property built from scratch.

The $315 million covers one title. It is not a figure for a studio's annual spending or for a publisher's full slate of games.

Without confirmation from the company, the $315 million remains a reported budget. It rests on the leaked documents and the press coverage of them, not on an official disclosure.

The PlayStation 5 and Xbox Series X $TWTR launched in November 2020, and both were built to run games at 4K resolution. Higher resolution raises the level of detail that every character, building and texture must hold up to, and artists have to create that detail.

A 4K image measures 3,840 by 2,160 pixels. That is four times the pixel count of a 1080p image, which measures 1,920 by 1,080 pixels. On a large television at that resolution, a blurry texture or a blocky, low-polygon object is easier to spot. Art teams respond by building assets with finer geometry and higher-resolution textures.

That work does not scale automatically. A rock, a jacket or a car door still has to be modeled, textured, lit and checked. When the target resolution rises, each of those steps involves more data and more review. Each object also needs several versions at different levels of detail so it can render efficiently at a distance. Large open-world games contain many thousands of such objects.

Both consoles also added hardware support for ray tracing. The technique simulates how light bounces, reflects and casts shadows. It can make scenes look more physically accurate. It also gives developers another lighting system to build, test and tune alongside traditional methods.

Fast solid-state drives are standard on both machines. They let games stream large amounts of data quickly, which cuts down on loading screens. Faster streaming also lets designers place denser, more detailed environments in front of the player. More detailed environments mean more assets to produce.

In the years after the 2020 launch, many studios shipped games on two console generations at once. Building for both the older PlayStation 4 and Xbox One and the newer machines meant supporting different performance targets within a single project. Each version needed its own optimization and testing.

None of this adds up to a single published price tag. Publishers rarely break out how much of a budget goes to art or graphics work. What the hardware shift did change is the baseline of detail a blockbuster release has to meet.

Motion capture and voice acting make blockbuster games depend on large casts of paid performers, specialized capture stages and long recording schedules. That labor was at the center of the strike SAG-AFTRA video game performers began in 2024.

Performance capture records an actor's body, face and voice together. Performers wear tight suits dotted with reflective markers. A ring of cameras around the stage tracks those markers as the actors move through a scene.

Facial work adds another layer. Head-mounted cameras pointed at the performer's face record expressions in detail. Animators later map that data onto a digital character so its face matches the original performance.

The raw capture is only the starting point. Animators and technical artists clean up the recorded motion, correct errors and fit it to each character model. A single cinematic scene can pass through several departments before it reaches the finished game.

Naughty Dog's The Last of Us, released in 2013, relied heavily on this kind of work. Troy Baker and Ashley Johnson played Joel and Ellie on a capture stage, supplying both movement and voice. Their performances shaped the characters as players see and hear them.

Casting extends well beyond the lead roles. Large productions hire stunt performers for fights and action sequences. They also cast separate voice actors for supporting characters, enemies and crowds.

Voice work grows with the size of the script. Open-world and role-playing games can contain many thousands of recorded lines across main stories, side quests and background chatter. Publishers that release a game in several languages often record new voice tracks for each market. That repeats much of the casting and studio work.

The SAG-AFTRA strike centered on protections against artificial intelligence tools that could replicate performers' voices and likenesses, alongside compensation. The walkout fell under the union's Interactive Media Agreement, which covers work for major game companies.

Performer costs rise with the scope of a production. Every added scene, language or retake means more hours on a capture stage, more time in a recording booth and more animation work afterward.

Marketing is a separate cost from development for big-budget AAA games, and it sits on top of what a studio spends building the game. A publisher rarely reveals how much it spends promoting one title. As a result, the full cost of most games stays out of public view.

A development budget pays for the people, tools and technology that make the game. A marketing budget pays for making sure buyers know the game exists by launch day. That work includes cinematic trailers, gameplay reveals, paid advertising, retail promotion and partnerships with streamers and video creators.

For a blockbuster release, a campaign can begin long before the game ships. A first teaser may appear years ahead of launch. The publisher then has to keep attention alive through later trailers, preview events and preorder pushes. Each stage carries its own production and media-buying costs.

Live showcases are one common stage for that work. The Game Awards and Summer Game Fest both feature world premieres of new game trailers. Platform holders run their own broadcasts as well, including Sony $SONY's State of Play and Nintendo Direct. A reveal at one of these events puts new footage in front of a large live audience at a single moment.

Platform holders also sign marketing agreements with publishers. These deals can attach a console brand to a game's advertising or give one platform's players early or exclusive content. Such arrangements add another party to the campaign. Their terms are rarely made public.

The timing matters because big-budget games depend on strong opening sales. A publisher that has funded years of development needs a large audience ready to buy as soon as the game appears. Marketing is how it builds that audience in advance.

Publicly traded publishers such as Electronic Arts $EA and Take-Two Interactive $TTWO report marketing spending in their financial filings. They report it as a company-wide expense rather than game by game. That makes it hard to set one title's promotional spending against its development cost. Any specific ratio between the two is a guess unless a company discloses it.

A reported game budget, when one surfaces at all, may leave out much of what a publisher spent to bring that game to market.

Microsoft $MSFT agreed to pay $68.7 billion for Activision $ATVI Blizzard, a sign of how much money now surrounds the video game business. The company announced the deal in January 2022 and completed it in October 2023.

The figure is the price of a whole publisher, not the cost of making any one game. The money bought a company with many studios, a catalog of existing titles and the rights to future releases. Reading it as a game budget would misread what the number measures.

Those holdings include some of the largest franchises in the business. Activision publishes Call of Duty. Blizzard Entertainment makes World of Warcraft, Diablo and Overwatch. King, the mobile arm, makes Candy Crush Saga. Each of those series has its own studios and release schedule.

The purchase did not stand alone. Microsoft had already bought ZeniMax Media, the parent of Bethesda Softworks, for $7.5 billion in a deal that closed in March 2021. Bethesda's franchises include The Elder Scrolls and Fallout. Together, the two acquisitions moved several major franchises under one owner. Microsoft also runs the Xbox console business and the Game Pass subscription service. Both draw on the games its studios produce.

The gap between announcement and completion was about 21 months. Regulators examined the deal during that period. The U.S. Federal Trade Commission sued to block it in December 2022. The U.K. Competition and Markets Authority blocked the original version in April 2023. Microsoft then restructured the deal. It sold the cloud streaming rights to Activision Blizzard games outside the European Economic Area to Ubisoft. The CMA cleared the revised version in October 2023.

The acquisition is a story about consolidation. Fewer, larger owners now hold more of the studios and franchises behind big-budget games. The most expensive productions increasingly sit inside companies large enough to fund them. Microsoft's purchase of Activision Blizzard is one of the clearest examples of that concentration.

Concord shows how quickly a live-service bet can come apart. Sony $SONY's Firewalk Studios released the game on August 23, 2024, and Sony pulled it on September 6, 2024. Sony then closed Firewalk Studios in October 2024.

The game was pulled 14 days after its release date. That left the development spending behind Concord a two-week window to start earning money back.

A live-service game is designed to run for years rather than ship once and be finished. Publishers plan seasons of new content, such as maps, characters and cosmetic items, to keep players returning and spending. The model ties revenue to an active player base over a long period, not to a single sale at launch.

That structure changes the cost math. A traditional single-player game spends most of its budget before release. A live-service game also needs servers, a team that keeps producing updates, and staff for balance changes and player support after launch. Those costs start on day one, whether or not players show up.

Multiplayer design adds another pressure. Matchmaking depends on enough people being online at the same time. When a player base is small, queues get longer and matches are harder to fill. That can push the remaining players away.

Live-service titles also compete for a limited resource: player time. A game built around seasonal play asks people to commit for months. Many players already split that time among online games they have played for years.

The result of a failed launch is lopsided. A single-player game that sells poorly still keeps the revenue from the copies it sold. A live-service game that loses its audience may no longer be worth running at all. Taking it offline means the planned years of post-launch income never arrive.

For a publisher, the loss from a case like Concord covers more than the money spent building the game. It also includes the team behind it. In Concord's case, that meant the end of Firewalk Studios as well.

For many years, $60 was the standard price for a new console game in the U.S. That ceiling has since moved higher. Nintendo priced Mario Kart World at $79.99 when it announced the game in April 2025.

The sticker price of a new release is one of the few levers a publisher controls directly. Revenue from a full-price game comes down to two numbers: how many copies sell and what each copy costs. When development spending rises, a publisher can try to sell more copies, charge more for each copy or do both.

The $60 standard held for a long stretch in which production work grew much larger. Across those years, one base price had to cover projects with bigger teams, longer schedules and more detailed art. Inflation also eroded what a fixed $60 was worth over time.

Publishers found other ways to raise what each player spends while the base price stayed put. Deluxe and collector's editions sell the same game at higher prices, usually bundled with extra content. Downloadable expansions and in-game purchases bring in money after launch. These options let the headline price stay fixed while the total a player could spend kept climbing.

The $70 price point moved part of that burden back onto the base game. At $70, each standard copy brings in $10 more than at $60, before retailers and platform holders take their share. A buyer who wants only the standard edition now pays more up front than under the old norm.

Mario Kart World pushed the figure further. Its $79.99 price is $19.99 above the long-running $60 standard.

A higher price raises revenue per copy only if enough players still buy at that level. The extra amount applies to every copy sold, so the gain grows with a game's sales. Publishers rarely disclose how sales respond to each increase. That makes it hard to measure from the outside how much of a game's budget a price change actually recovers.

Baldur's Gate 3, released by Larian Studios in August 2023, won Game of the Year at the 2023 Game Awards. Larian has not disclosed what the game cost to make.

That missing figure limits any direct comparison with blockbuster spending. What can be compared is how the project was organized and paid for along the way.

Larian is a Belgian developer headquartered in Ghent. It is not owned by Sony $SONY, Microsoft $MSFT, Nintendo or Take-Two Interactive $TTWO. Larian published Baldur's Gate 3 itself rather than handing release decisions to an outside publisher.

The studio built the game on an existing foundation. Baldur's Gate 3 adapts the fifth edition rules of Dungeons & Dragons under a license from Wizards of the Coast. It is a turn-based, party-based role-playing game. Larian had already worked in that genre with Divinity: Original Sin and Divinity: Original Sin 2.

Larian also developed the game in public for years. Baldur's Gate 3 entered paid early access on PC in October 2020, nearly three years before the full launch. Early buyers could play an opening portion of the game and send feedback while work continued. That arrangement brought in revenue and player data well before the finished version shipped.

The full release arrived on PC in August 2023. A PlayStation 5 version followed in September 2023, and an Xbox Series X $TWTR|S version came in December 2023.

The Game Awards win came against nominees from some of the industry's largest companies. The 2023 Game of the Year field included Marvel's Spider-Man 2 from Sony's Insomniac Games and The Legend of Zelda: Tears of the Kingdom from Nintendo. Capcom's Resident Evil 4, Remedy Entertainment's Alan Wake 2 and Nintendo's Super Mario Bros. Wonder were also nominated.

The case does not show that blockbuster games can be made cheaply. It shows another route to Game of the Year: a long public development period, a licensed rule set, prior genre experience and self-publishing.
