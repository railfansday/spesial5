Wife of Trump ally Todd Blanche promoted treatments with unproven medical claims

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Kristine Blanche, named to US health advisory role, offered purported treatments for Epstein-Barr virus and HPV

Attorney general Todd Blanche’s wife, Kristine Blanche, who was appointed earlier this year to a US government advisory board on alternative medicine, has a website on which she marketed dietary supplements and treatments with unproven medical and health claims.

Blanche is a licensed physician assistant who says she has a PhD in breast thermography from the University of Science, Arts and Technology in Montserrat, a British territory in the Caribbean. She is also an advocate for alternative medical cures and holistic health.

Her site also links to a page called “Dr Blanche’s Dispensary”, where patients can buy supplement pills she recommends. She suggested that one product, a liquid herbal compound, could be used to treat the Epstein-Barr virus, although she misspelled “Epstein”.

There is no medication approved by the FDA to treat or cure Epstein-Barr, and the product itself does not claim to treat the virus.

Blanche also listed five supplements in her store, priced up to $63 a bottle, which she said can help treat human papillomavirus (HPV).

Another treatment Blanche offers, priced online at $595, claims to help men “boost” their testosterone levels and lose weight.

The day after the Guardian emailed questions to Blanche about the supplements she offered, the products related to purported treatments for Epstein-Barr and HPV disappeared from her website. Her online store carries a disclaimer that “these statements have not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration. These products are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.”

Peter Lurie, the executive director of the Center for Science in the Public Interest, said that Blanche’s website highlights gaps in regulations of claims made by providers. “That leaves an opening for some to exploit,” he said. “It is not in the interest of American patients, but it is not clearly illegal.”

Blanche wrote to the Guardian that she has “a PhD and an MD degree”, and said she complies “with all mandatory FDA guidelines. Any publication to the contrary is false and defamatory.”

She said that while she has a medical degree from the university in Montserrat, she has never been a licensed physician in the US. She has previously practiced as a physician assistant.

In April, Blanche was appointed to the advisory council of the federal National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH). The center, which has a $170m annual budget, supports research on alternative and holistic medicines and practices.

“It absolutely concerns me,” Lurie said of her appointment. “I have no confidence that the attorney general’s wife, with a financial interest in the kind of products this advisory committee concerns itself with, would be appropriate on the committee.”

In response to questions, a National Institutes of Health (NIH) spokesperson emailed that “the Trump administration is bringing the best and brightest into HHS”, adding: “Dr Kristine Blanche was appointed through the established appointment process based on her work and background in integrative health. Her experience provides a relevant foundation for service and a valuable perspective to inform the council’s work, in compliance with the charter.”

Iris International, which invests in medical companies, also hired Blanche this year as its chief medical officer. A spokesperson for the company wrote that Blanche “reports to the CEO and provides a view on emerging healthcare businesses in the market”.

It is unclear how much business Blanche does on her website, which appears to have been active for over a decade.

In Todd Blanche’s 2025 and 2026 financial disclosures, his wife’s income from “Integrative Healing Center” is not large, listed at just between $1,000 and $15,000.

Her “Man Up” cleanse and “Girlfriend” cleanse – each priced at almost $600 – are still available, and include detoxes, shakes, videos and dietary supplements.

“Lose weight, boost your testosterone & ditch the toxins. All while eating real food,” the Man Up program promises.

“I’m going to share with you how you can turn back your body into that place it was before. I’ve had patients lose 40 pounds,” Blanche said in a video sales pitch on YouTube. “It’s not acceptable to you if you’re not really feeling great.”

She has praised the use of a type of alternative diagnostic imaging technology called “thermography” – which detects heat variations in the body – and in a podcast has said it can predict potential risks years before mammograms. Her website says her PhD thesis was about “breast thermography”.

The FDA has approved the use of thermography if it is used with mammography, but has warned it cannot be a substitute.

The Blanches moved to Florida in 2024 and the website says she has a virtual practice in Palm Beach Gardens, where the couple live, not far from Mar-a-Lago. Her PA license lists her home address.

Last month, Blanche appeared on a little-watched podcast called I Choose Health, run by Stefan Lessie, a New York City-based personal trainer who posts inspirational and educational interviews with doctors and health activists.

Lessie has a tiny audience of about 20 to 30 listeners a week on Spotify. He said he received an unsolicited email last month from a PR executive explaining that “my client Kristine Blanche would resonate with your audience”.

Lessie interviewed Blanche for an hour, as she discussed her practice and her philosophy of medicine. “A lot of women come to me after they’ve been to many doctors over several years of suffering,” she told him.

“I love that the Maha movement is out there,” Blanche said, referring to Robert F Kennedy Jr’s Make America Healthy Again movement. “Trying to educate moms on, you know, food dyes are bad.”

Lessie said he did not know who Blanche was. “I was doing some research after the interview, and I saw her husband, and I was like: ‘He’s the US attorney general!’ And I was like, ‘Wow!’”

Blanche has received little, if any, public attention. A Stat News article in April reported her appointment to the federal advisory council.

The former head of the NCCIH, Dr Helene Langevin, a professor at the University of Vermont, said the advisory council role is “important” because members review every research grant decision made by independent panels.

Langevin, who retired in September 2025, declined to discuss specifics about Blanche. “I can’t comment on the qualifications of any advisory board member,” she said.
