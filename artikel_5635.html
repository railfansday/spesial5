314 Action Celebrates Its Victories in Getting Docs, Other Clinicians Elected

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

— "Things went even better than expected," said Shaughnessy Naughton, the group's founder

The midterm results earlier this month included the election of several physicians and other healthcare professionals to federal and statewide seats, especially on the Democratic side. MedPage Today Washington Editor Joyce Frieden spoke with Shaughnessy Naughton, founder and president of 314 Action, about the organization's role in helping to get some of these clinicians -- as well as scientists -- elected to office. The conversation, which was conducted over the phone with a public relations person present, has been edited for length and clarity.

How did your organization do in terms of seeing its chosen candidates elected?

Things went even better than expected. We are the only organization to elect Democratic scientists and doctors to all levels of government -- 14 members to the House, including 10 who took seats from Republicans -- two new doctors to the Senate, which hasn't had Democratic doctors serving in close to 60 years; and a Democratic doctor as governor in Ohio, the first woman doctor-governor in the country.

There's a couple of arms to that answer. First, we recruited and invested early in quality candidates. Additionally, this was a healthcare election and our candidates leaned into their backgrounds as doctors and as problem-solvers to present a clear contrast with their opponents that have overseen, particularly in the federal races, the slashing of Medicaid, the elimination of the [Affordable Care Act Enhanced Premium Tax Credit], the assault on rural health, and on and on.

Then you couple that with [HHS Secretary] Robert F. Kennedy Jr. -- the vaccine delusionist -- overseeing HHS during one health crisis after another, leading to preventable deaths from measles and preventable diseases. So I think all of that compounded to really create a lane that voters saw as a viable and important alternative to what they currently had.

Annie Andrews, MD, [D-S.C. senator-elect] is at the top of that list. South Carolina hasn't elected a Democrat statewide, I think, in over 20 years. It is very hard to change people's voting patterns, but it does speak to the strength of her message and her candidacy that she was able to not only get elected, but she brought along a congressional race in South Carolina -- one that flipped to Democrat -- and 10 statehouse seats. Wow!

We are really looking forward to what she can accomplish in the Senate. I was having coffee this morning with a senator who had just met her at orientation this week, and he said he told her to "Lean in and make sure your voice is being heard because we really need you right now." I think that that is true coming from the senator's mouth, but I think it's also true coming from the voters' mouth.

You mentioned the vaccine issue and the Affordable Care Act tax credits. What other healthcare issues are you hoping to see acted on in the new Congress?

We were promised [by the current administration and Congress] to see a reduction in healthcare costs, and we've seen the exact opposite. Premiums continue to skyrocket. Out-of-pocket costs and deductibles just keep going up, and there doesn't seem to be much relief on prescription drug prices either.

This is at a time when we're having record numbers of foodborne illness outbreaks and measles deaths and spread. The threshold for herd immunity is 95% for measles, and most kindergarteners' classes are below that at this point. So it really does call to have strong health leaders leading with facts and evidence and their values to protect Americans' health and make healthcare more affordable.

We are looking for action on healthcare affordability and accessibility, and holding the administration accountable for what, to say the least, have been questionable choices such as the approval of candy-flavored vapes, so we do expect that Congress should be able to deliver on both.

Even though they don't have enough votes to override a presidential veto?

What happens remains to be seen. The moves from the administration do not [suggest] that they want to work with Democrats, but the remaining Republicans in the House still have elections in 2 years, and the American people spoke loud and clear that they want relief from high prices across the board.

Can you explain more about your organization's role? I understand you spent more than $30 million this cycle. Do you recruit as well as contribute financially?

Yes, we are involved in recruiting. One of the things we're seeing bearing fruit is an investment at the state and local level. One of the people we endorsed in the 2018 cycle was [microbiologist] Jasmine Clark, PhD. She ran in a Republican district in the Georgia statehouse and flipped the seat. This year she stepped up to run for a congressional seat, and won.

We work with campaigns, helping them put together their campaign strategy, a financing plan, and consulting teams. We find staff and work at the grassroots level with them. Often our campaign staff will be in the district with them when they launch, and our communications staff works with them on media training.

We have individual donors; this cycle we had 150,000 individual contributors. Their first donation average is $10; the lifetime average is $17. People usually donate three times a year and many have been donating to us for 3, 4, or 5 years. We do a lot of grassroots fundraising, and we also have been the beneficiary of [former New York City Mayor] Michael Bloomberg's generosity this cycle, which makes a lot of sense because he is someone invested in public health and public health education.

We have candidates fill out a questionnaire, which is on our website. And we are already thinking about 2028 -- even 2027 has some local elections [of interest]. We need to play everywhere. When you run credible candidates, they can resonate and make a difference and get elected.

What is your background and how did you end up starting 314 Action?

I was a chemist and worked in breast cancer research and drug discovery, and I worked on creating an antibiotic to combat superbugs. In 2014, I became concerned by the antiscience rhetoric coming out of so many politicians, so I decided to run for Congress in Bucks County, Pennsylvania, where I'm from. But I didn't have 314 Action and was locked out of the [standard] donor network, so I started cold-calling chemists and making my case and building out a network from there.

Ultimately, I didn't win, but I learned a lot in the process and part of what I learned is that it's very hard to break into politics when you don't have that traditional political background. And so I founded 314 Action to break down those barriers, to recruit, train, and elect people with scientific backgrounds, with the thinking that there isn't an issue that doesn't benefit by having data-driven analytical thinkers actually at the governing table.
