When Helping Hurts

Can helping another person do more harm than good? That depends on how we define “help.” When we undermine another person’s autonomy, confidence, independence, freedom, growth, or power, we are not helping—we may be doing more harm than good. Real help implies doing what is best for another’s growth and development, not what is best for the person who wants to be helpful.

When we see someone facing a challenge or asking for help, the desire to help can be a natural impulse. But when someone asks for help, do they want to change? If you notice your child isn't following through on school responsibilities, what does it mean to help your child? Should we step in and help a co-worker behind on their work?

As you reflect on your own experiences helping others or being offered help, you already know helping isn't straightforward. Not everyone who asks for help is ready for change, and not everyone who appears to need help wants it. In healthcare, assessing readiness for change is key to improving outcomes. When our efforts to help don't match a person’s perception of their need for help or readiness for change, our help is not well received. This principle is similar in the home; we know that parental overinvolvement undermines students' academic success.

There is a difference between helping and falling into the helper role. When we fall into the helper role, we are overly invested in the outcome of what we attempt to accomplish. We need people to follow our helpful advice to feel good about ourselves. When we are in the helper role rather than actually helping, we find purpose by getting others to change.

A key sign we are caught in the helper role is believing the person we are helping is powerless to help themselves. They need us to remind, motivate, and help them change.

How many times do we get hooked by that roommate or colleague who regularly leaves their empty food container on the counter, only two feet from the garbage can? If we feel compelled to help, we might first mention the container and nicely ask, “Are you done with that carry-out box on the counter?” The offending person knows we are not asking a question but making a complaint. But that is how the game progresses, and the person plays along. The response might be, “Oh yeah, thanks! I will get to it.” Several hours or days later, nothing changes. Then we get upset, throw out the box, and think we are helping by taking care of it ourselves.

We know that helping undermines growth when the person we are helping becomes dependent on us—they never learn to throw out their own garbage. The person being helped misses the opportunity to face challenges and solve problems on their own, undermining their autonomy, agency, and confidence.

In healthcare, medical providers need to avoid falling into a predictable game that occurs when we dispense helpful advice about lifestyle changes, exercise, diet, and stress management.

Imagine a conversation between a physical therapist and a patient finishing up several weeks of therapy related to a motor vehicle accident. The patient asks, “What can I do to continue my progress when I am at home?” This seems like a straightforward request. Without checking the person’s readiness for change, the therapist, wanting to be helpful, suggests that the patient continue gentle rehabilitation exercises in a swimming pool.

This patient might not be ready for change, as we will see in their reply. The patient responds, “Yes, that is a good idea, but I don’t have money for an expensive fitness gym membership.” The therapist then reminds the patient they can easily use the nearby hotel pool for only $5 per use. Again, the patient responds, “Yes, but getting changed at the hotel into a swimsuit, showering, and getting dressed again takes too much time.” The classic yes-but game has begun, and the therapist would be better off changing tactics.

Not sensing a game being played, the therapist reminds the patient that if they start with simple exercises in the pool, they would eventually be able to use swimming as their strengthening and conditioning program. The therapist then emphasizes that an extended conditioning program will be necessary for full recovery for this patient—something that will require real commitment. The patient then says, “Yes, but if the water in the pool is not at least 74 degrees, then it will be too cold. Cold water flares up my pain. I can’t take that risk.” This response is like saying, “Checkmate!” in the yes-but game.

Cultivating self-awareness in our interactions with others is important, especially when we are giving advice. If our goals for others and our desire to see them change do not match their ability to change, we may be setting a person up to see themselves as unable to change. I have talked with many patients with chronic pain who have been strongly encouraged by their medical doctor to start a running program to manage pain. As you might guess, not too many people are ready for that level of change and feel defeated by their inability to follow medical advice.

Start by asking questions about steps they have taken in the past to change, problems they have encountered with change, and a few small goals they believe they can reach now. This approach will not only better assess their readiness for change but also build a better connection with the person you are helping, which we know is a key ingredient of lasting change.
