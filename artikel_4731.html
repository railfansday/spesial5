Is Social Media Killing Conversation and Connection?

In a world of increasingly virtual interaction, not face-to-face, it is reasonable to wonder whether the time we spend in the digital world is affecting how we communicate in the real one. Does this time online hinder how connected and engaged we are in our everyday lives?

One recent widely reported study (Pfeifer and Mehl, 2026) compared data on words spoken by more than 2000 speakers, ages 10 to 94, drawn from 22 studies over 14 years (2005-2019).

Overall, during the period studied, they found a consistent annual decline in word count—dropping about 338 words per year. As a group, those under 25 showed the greatest decline, losing about 458 words per year, though speakers over 25 showed the same downward trend.

The researchers suggest this small but consistent drop in spoken interaction for both young and old likely resulted from the rise of social media and structural changes in how society “congregates and communicates.”

With modern society facing what the U.S. Surgeon General calls an “epidemic of loneliness,” Pfeifer and Mehl wonder whether this loss of words might contribute to the problem. They also wonder whether online conversations, if they replace face-to-face ones, can provide the same social benefits.

Certainly, the rise of social media and the time people now spend engaging with it would help explain the drop in spoken words, particularly among younger speakers.

But given the variety of engagement social media offers, from viewing posts and content to interactive back-and-forth chats among friends, time spent online may sometimes remove opportunities for interaction; in other cases, spoken conversation may be replaced with online ones.

People who spend a couple of hours scrolling TikTok videos spend more time alone and less time conversing with others. In contrast, apps designed to encourage direct or group messaging (like Snapchat) may provide some of the same social benefits as real-life conversation.

As we increasingly shop online, work from home, and spend more time on a screen and less time chatting with a neighbor over the fence, we may not have the same potential for conversational interaction that we did in the past. What may have really changed is not our desire for connection but the casual in-person situations that give rise to it.

For instance, using American Time Survey results from 2003-2019, the World Happiness Report finds that Americans spend more time eating alone than they did at the beginning of the 21st century, particularly younger people. Likewise, using the same data, other research has found more social isolation and less social connection starting well before the COVID pandemic.

Young people, in particular, report spending more time alone now. More time alone, though, doesn't necessarily mean they are not socially engaged. In fact, their attraction to social media may not be the cause but a symptom of this greater real-world isolation.

Research comparing the benefits of digital versus face-to-face contact has found that social interaction of any type, be it in person or online, increases well-being, particularly when it is with close friends; however, digital communication by itself (without real-world interaction) contributes less to well-being than in-person contact.

Research also suggests that not all online interactions are the same in terms of replacing opportunities for real-life social interaction or in terms of the negative or positive impact on the development of social skills.

For instance, a longitudinal study tracked social media use and time spent offline among Norwegian kids as they aged from 10 to 18. They found that children who spent more time posting, commenting, and liking on social media subsequently spent more time offline with their friends.

This may suggest overlap in friendship groups, both online and offline, with online interaction reinforcing rather than replacing real-world interaction, aligning with the finding that digital interaction that includes real-life connections is more beneficial.

The Norway study also found that, among kids with more social anxiety, greater social media use negatively affected the development of social skills; for those without anxiety, there was no positive or negative effect on later social skills.

Another study tracked survey responses at identical ages across four generations of American adolescents. Researchers found a “robust” decline in how often adolescents reported meeting in person with their friends, particularly after 2010, notably a time when smartphones became more widespread.

While reported time spent with friends declined overall, this did not necessarily happen at the individual level. Like the Norway study, they found that kids who spent more time with friends also reported spending more time on social media. But, highlighting a greater risk to well-being for less extroverted kids, teens with the lowest in-person interaction and high social media use reported the greatest loneliness.

These findings suggest that spending a lot of time on social media is not necessarily detrimental to, and may even enhance, face-to-face relationships. It depends on how socially anxious kids are and how active their in-person support and friendship networks are.

The takeaway is that social media is not—unavoidably—a conversation killer. While research suggests the time we spend conversing face to face has been decreasing—especially for younger speakers—meaningful social connections can still be made in the digital world. But ideally, these should enhance, not replace, strong face-to-face relationships.
