Bob Hope at Home: 10 Photos of the Comic’s Life Behind the Curtain

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Though Bob Hope was born in England, he fell in love with the USA. “Sometimes I do my best to hide it, but I’ve got a real crush on America,” he wrote in his 1990 memoir, Don’t Shoot, It’s Only Me. “Nobody ever had a girl like her. Nobody ever gave me as much affection, as much honor, or as much real estate.”

Born Leslie Townes Hope in Eltham, London, on May 29, 1903, the future comedian and actor came from humble beginnings. His family had little money and moved often throughout his childhood. After he found success on stage and it came time to raise his own brood, Hope put down roots in California. Across Toluca Lake and Palm Springs, the star’s homes offered a place to escape the limelight, spend time with his wife and four kids, entertain very famous guests, and indulge in his love of golf.

Below, we’ve rounded up 10 photos of Hope at home, from his early family life to the residence where he died at age 100.

Hope (bottom right) at the family estate in Cleveland. The comic’s parents William Henry Hope and Avis Townes Hope are on the porch in the background. Hope was 20 years old at the time of the picture.

Hope and his family moved from London to the United States when he was four. In this 1923 photo, he’s pictured with friends and members of the Hope clan at their Cleveland home. Because money was tight, the family took in boarders to supplement Hope’s father’s income, according to the Library of Congress. In his 1954 memoir, Have Tux, Will Travel, Hope recalls his early days in Cleveland. “The first place we lived by ourselves was Standiforth Court,” he wrote. “We were always on the move. We lived at 2029 Euclid. After that we moved behind the Cleveland Express barns…. It was a long walk between the barns and our house. And if we got home after dark, it was scary…. Next we lived at 1925 East 105th, then at 1913, then at 2029—all on Euclid. We didn’t move very far, only to bigger houses which had more room for roomers.”

Hope and his wife Dolores at home in the 1940s.

Hope married Dolores DeFina in 1934, and the couple adopted their first child, a daughter named Linda, in 1939. That same year, the comic bought a five-acre former walnut grove in Los Angeles’s Toluca Lake neighborhood at 10346 Moorpark Street. He transformed the property into a lavish French Revival estate, featuring a 14,876-square-foot main house with eight bedrooms and eight bathrooms; a two-bedroom, two-bathroom guesthouse; a staff residence; and a pool.

Hope and his grandfather catch up over tea.

In this 1943 photo, Hope is pictured at home with his “Grandpop.” As a young boy in London, Hope lived with his family in a house his grandfather had built. “Grandpop had built a row of houses in Eltham. We lived in one of them. They were put together like stone battleships,” he writes in Have Tux, Will Travel. After moving to the United States, his grandfather went on to build churches in Cleveland and Detroit. Hope reunited with him during a return trip to England in 1939—the star’s first visit since moving across the pond as a child. He recalls the occasion in his memoir, writing that “It wasn’t until I went back to England in 1939 that I got to know [Grandpop]. It was the first time I’d been there since I was four. The English papers reported: ‘One-time shoeshine boy and ironmonger’s assistant comes home.’ The English are supposed to be conservative and not given to exaggeration, but they hooked one there. How could I have been a shoeshine boy or ironmonger’s assistant when I was only four when I left there?”

Back in California, Hope and his wife continued to grow their family. They adopted three more children: Anthony, born in 1940, and twins William and Eleanora, born in 1946. In this circa 1940s photo, the family of six is pictured at home. In Have Tux, Will Travel, Hope reflects on the choice to adopt. “No matter how strongly I feel about the word ‘family,’ Dolores feels even more deeply about it,” he writes. “When we found out that she couldn’t bear children, she began to bend my ear about adoption.” They welcomed their children through The Cradle, an adoption agency in Evanston, Illinois. “Of the five thousand, four hundred and nine babies that The Cradle has placed, there are eight Cradle babies out here in Hollywood,” he continues. “The Hopes are proud to be Cradle parents. I wish I could really tell you what The Cradle means to me here in Hollywood, where all day long the talk is box-office and autographs and premieres and who’ll get the Oscar. ‘The town of make-believe,’ they call it, but don’t get the idea everything is make-believe in Hollywood. There's nothing make-believe about our children. They are real.”

Hope enjoys some family time in the living room with Dolores and two of their children.

Hope’s Toluca Lake estate was more than a family retreat; it was also a gathering place for celebrities and politicians, including Bing Crosby and Richard Nixon. When the property went on the market for $27.5 million in 2013, listing agent Jade Mills said, “[Hope’s] daughter, Linda Hope, would often peek down the grand staircase to see who was coming. She once spoke to Lucie Arnaz and Maria Cooper about doing a book about watching celebrities come and go called ‘Seen From the Landing.’”

The home was “always impressively staffed,” Mills continued. “A full-time cook, butler, estate manager, and two housekeepers manned the property on a daily basis, while additional staff was hired for large receptions.”

Hope, Dolores, and their four kids pose at home in 1958.

In this 1958 photo, Hope and the family pose for a photo on the stairs. In Have Tux, Will Travel, the comic gushes about his wife as a mother. “Dolores has a wise and loving touch with our children,” he writes. “I’m lost in admiration of the job she has done with them, and with the job she’s done with keeping me in line. A lot of children whose fathers are in show business grow up too precocious, too wise, too fresh, too unfunny. That’s not true of our four. Dolores sees to that. She also sees to it that they’re having a devout rearing.”

The family is shown here opening Christmas presents in 1964.

Hope was determined to give his children space at home. “When our kids began to grow up, we fixed up a play room for them and gave them rooms of their own,” he writes in Have Tux, Will Travel. “While we were at it, Dolores and I decided to redo the whole house. We tore out one entire downstairs wall and put in glass, so no matter where we are, we can look out at the garden and enjoy the California sun—if it shows up.”

While his home workspace was connected to the residence, it was its own separate world. “My office adjoins my house,” he writes. “It’s a different project, separate and distinct from our house. In fact, it’s on a different property. It was put up (and is owned) by Hope Enterprises. But it’s handy. The people I want to see on business can walk in and out without disturbing anybody…. It’s a nice set-up.”

Hope was an avid golfer in his free time.

Hope was an avid golfer and built a one-hole golf course at his home in Toluca Lake, where he’s pictured here in this 1990-era photo. He once even said that he considered golf to be his real job. “Golf is my profession,” he said, per ESPN. “Entertainment is just a sideline. I tell jokes to pay my greens fees.” The outlet further reported that he played more than 2,000 courses all over the world and golfed with everyone from Crosby to Dwight Eisenhower and Bill Clinton. “I’ve played all over the world, which means there isn’t a country with a course in which I haven’t three-putted,” Hope said.

Hope and Dolores at home in Palm Springs.

While Toluca Lake served as the family’s primary residence, Hope’s most iconic home was arguably his Palm Springs estate, where he and Dolores are pictured here in 1991. Designed by famed architect John Lautner, the 24,000-square-foot estate featured 10 bedrooms, dramatic arches, and a statement curved roof. The futuristic design closely resembled a spaceship. Hope once joked, “Well, at least when they come down from Mars they’ll know where to go.”

The home’s construction, however, was plagued by setbacks. According to the Los Angeles Times, when building began in the early ’70s, the structure caught on fire and burned down. The project was subsequently delayed for five years, during which time the couple’s interior designer, Arthur Elrod, was killed in a car accident. Dolores then took over the design duties. Lautner’s protégé Helena Arahuete recalled Dolores’s vision: “She wanted everything very ornamented, very rich—you know, gold and pink and black and mirrors everywhere.” Inspired by her friends Walter and Leonore Annenberg’s home, which had a pink roof, Dolores requested that their curved roof be covered in pink terra-cotta gunite.

In 1991, at his Palm Springs pad, Hope hosted “Bob Hope’s Yellow Ribbon Party,” a homecoming celebration for U.S. troops. The soiree included celebrity guest appearances by Brooke Shields, Patti LaBelle, Jimmy Stewart, and former President Gerald Ford and his wife, Betty.

Hope died at home in Toluca Lake in 2003 at the age of 100. The estate remained in the family until Dolores’s death in 2011, after which it was listed for $50 million. LA businessman Ron Burkle purchased the star’s Palm Springs pad in 2016, and two years later, added Hope’s Toluca Lake residence to his real estate portfolio.
