Feeling the Pain of Others

Posted November 19, 2026 | Reviewed by Lybi Ma

“Are you sure your work hasn’t gotten to you?” he asked. We were visiting our family in the Caribbean. We had hiked to the top of the cliffs on a small island, and for a moment the entire family stood quietly together, marveling at the sea. It was an exquisite sight—turquoise water as far as you could see, and a vast, cloudless sky. As we reached the edge of the cliffs, my first thought was, This is unbelievably beautiful. My second thought was, I wonder how many people have killed themselves by jumping off these cliffs.

I assumed everyone thought this; I asked my question out loud. My stepfather-in-law turned to me slowly and asked his question with such sincerity that I finally understood: My work had gotten to me. I didn’t even tell him the rest of my thoughts: Where would the helicopter land? Could it land on the beach, or would it land on this cliff and then they’d belay down? Is there even a Level 1 trauma center in the Caribbean? Would they have to go to Miami? Would they stop you in customs? It was quite a list.

I had always considered myself self-aware, but this was the first time I saw my work had transformed my engagement with the world.

My jobs had brought me into intimate contact with people who were living close to or experiencing different types of acute trauma. I worked in confidential locations and at large institutions. I worked days, evenings, and graveyard shifts. I worked in local communities, throughout the country, and the world.

Over time, friends, family, and even clients said I needed a vacation. Otherwise, stop taking everything so seriously. But I could not hear them; I was doing God’s work. They could either step up and help me or step off. I did not need to reflect on myself. I was arrogant, self-righteous, and impassioned, perhaps to the point of selective blindness. I was convinced that I was just fine.

After so many stories of abuse, death, and tragedies, including missing children, deported loved ones, climate disasters, and other nightmares, I had absorbed and accumulated other people’s pain to the point that it had become part of me, and my view of the world had changed. But I finally understood that this exposure to other people’s overwhelm had fundamentally changed me. I did the work for a long time with little ability to integrate my experiences emotionally, cognitively, physically, or spiritually. I suffered from compassion fatigue.

Rather than staying in touch with the heart that was breaking, I started building walls. In my case, this meant I became increasingly cocky. I had no access to humility, which we all need when honestly engaging our own internal process. Rather than acknowledge my own pain and helplessness in the face of things I could not control, I raged at the possible external causes. I sharpened my critique of systems and society. I became more dogmatic, opinionated, and intolerant of other people’s views. It never occurred to me that my anger might in part be functioning as a shield or that I was warding off anguish. A widely held belief: If you’re tough enough, cool enough, and committed enough, you’ll keep on keeping on. You’ll suck it up.

Instead, I began the long haul of making change. I acknowledged the effects of other people’s pain; I made room for my own internal process. Over the years, I gradually let go. This fatigue takes a personal and professional toll.

We are slow to recognize such fatigue as a broad social concern rather than an isolated individual issue—through a job, daily life, or caregiving. It also goes beyond exposure to human suffering; other species and the planet are also in pain.

I needed a new framework that allowed me to bear witness to suffering without surrendering my ability to live fully—I call the concept trauma stewardship. This refers to the entire conversation: How we are affected over time, how we make sense of, learn from, and ultimately transform, individually and collectively.

In stewardship, we are entrusted with people’s stories and lives, animals’ well-being, and our planet’s health. We understand that this is an honor as well as a responsibility. As stewards, we make room for and honor others’ hardship and suffering, yet we do not assume their pain as our own.

The essayist E. B. White once wrote that the early American author, naturalist, and philosopher Henry Thoreau had perhaps been “torn by two powerful and opposing drives—the desire to enjoy the world, and the urge to set the world straight.”
