What Kids Won't Tell Their Parents

November is a good month to be a child in the Gulf. The heat has broken, and families are outside in the evenings for the first time since spring. It is also the point in the school year when the novelty has worn off. Friendship groups have settled, and the first-term report cards are a few weeks away.

For many parents, this is when “How was school?” starts to get the same one-word answer every afternoon: "Fine."

Here in the Gulf, that answer can cover a lot. Our children attend demanding schools in a competitive environment. Their friendship groups shift every year as families arrive and leave, and many of them have been the new child more than once. They are navigating friendship politics and the pressure to do well, and much of it never comes up at home.

This is not unusual, and it is not a sign that something is wrong. Keeping some things private is a normal part of growing up. The more useful question is what leads children to open up.

Two influential studies by Kerr and Stattin (2000) examined how parents come to know about their children’s lives. The common assumption was that parents who ask more questions and keep closer track know more. Instead, the researchers found that parents’ knowledge came mostly from what their children chose to tell them. Spontaneous disclosure, rather than tracking or questioning, predicted how much parents knew, and it was linked to better adjustment in the children themselves.

We cannot question our way into our children’s inner lives. We can only make it more likely that they will let us in.

In our part of the world, there is an additional layer. In my research with Arab families, honor and reputation came up again and again as issues that mattered to young people and their parents, and they were not well captured by the Western measures of family conflict available to us (Rasmi, Daly, & Chuang, 2014). Children in our communities learn early that some things reflect on the whole family and that awareness makes them more careful about what they bring home.

Younger children often do not have the words yet. A 9-year-old who feels left out at school rarely says so. Instead, her parents hear about her stomach ache on a Sunday evening.

One finding from my own work has stayed with me. When we asked young Arab Canadians how they handled disagreements with their parents, the most common approach was to oblige (i.e., to go along with what their parents wanted). On the surface, this looked like the least conflict of all. However, we found, in interviews with emerging adults, that obliging took two distinct forms: Some young people genuinely deferred to their parents, whereas others said yes to their parents’ faces and then quietly did what they had planned anyway (Rasmi, Daly, & Chuang, 2014).

That second group is the one parents need to think about. A child who has learned that disagreement is not safe at home keeps disagreeing but stops telling you about it.

In a follow-up study, we interviewed young people about their relationships with their parents and found that the quality of the relationship shaped almost everything else (Rasmi, Chuang, & Hennig, 2017). Young people talked through their differences when the relationship was close, trusting, and marked by open communication. On the other hand, they withheld information to avoid conflict when they perceived the relationship to be distant or strictly hierarchical. In our quantitative research, a stronger parent–child relationship was also associated with less conflict at home (Rasmi, Chuang, & Hennig, 2015).

Smetana’s research points the same way. Adolescents disclose more to parents they experience as trusting and accepting. Children talk to the parents they feel safe with. That was true of the families I studied in Canada, and it is true of the families we see in Dubai.

Ask smaller questions. “How was your day?” invites one word. “Who did you sit with at lunch?” gives your child somewhere to start.

Talk side by side. Children share more when they are not being looked at directly, and this is the season for it. A walk on the beach after dinner gives you an hour with no screens and no eye contact. Bedtime works, too, when the lights are low.

Watch your reaction. Tilton-Weaver and colleagues (2010) found that when children met negative reactions after sharing something, they shared less afterward. Our response to the lost hoodie tells our children how we will respond to the bigger things.

Make disagreement safe. If your child can tell you they disagree and the conversation stays respectful, they are far more likely to tell you what is actually going on. You can hold your position and still let them voice theirs.

Let silence do some work. Children often circle a difficult topic before they land on it, and if we jump in too soon, they tend to retreat.

Notice what their body is saying. Stomach aches with no medical explanation can be a sign of something unsaid. So can a change in sleep or pulling away from things they used to enjoy. Several together, or any lasting more than a couple of weeks, are worth a gentle conversation and, if needed, a professional.

A child who keeps some things private while otherwise seeming like themselves is doing something healthy. A child who becomes withdrawn or unlike themselves alongside the silence may need more support. The goal is not to know everything, but to be the person your child comes to when it matters.

My colleagues Natalie Maaitah and Valen Valentine and I discussed this on a recent episode of our podcast, Thrive With Us. One idea stayed with me: Connection has to come before correction. Our children only tell us when they have learned that we are safe to tell.

November 20 is World Children’s Day, and its theme, in one form or another, is always listening to children. The cooler months give us more time together than any other part of the year. Some of it can go to smaller questions and to listening to the answers.
