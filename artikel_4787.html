How Books Can Boost Emotional Intelligence

Reading to your child can not only improve their early literacy skills, which are important for later academic achievement, but it can also boost their emotional intelligence.

Choosing the right books to read with your child can build emotional intelligence, teaching them about feelings, helping them to become more aware of their thoughts, and modeling important skills for managing big emotions. When characters in the story experience the daily challenges and accompanying feelings that your child faces, they receive validation and empathy in the pages of the book, and in your warm presence as you read to them a story that feels personal.

Author Sandra V. Feder has worked to intentionally bring children’s emotions to the page through her Emotions and Me series of books, tackling big feelings that kids experience like loneliness, anger, and shyness, so that children can feel seen and validated, and build emotional intelligence for the future.

Q: Share a bit about your background and what inspired you to write the Emotions and Me series of picture books.

Sandra V. Feder: I’ve been a writer my whole life, from an early career in print journalism to becoming a children’s book author, so I am always thinking about how we use words. When I had children of my own, it seemed natural to talk about words we use to describe feelings, because all kids have lots of big feelings.

My inspiration for the Emotions and Me series, which includes Angry Me, Peaceful Me, Shy Me, and Lonely Me, was my youngest daughter’s anger. Trying to understand what she was feeling led me to explore situations, whether at home, school, or out in the world, that contributed to her anger. I chose to write this series in relatable first-person voices to validate children’s feelings and frustrations. Each book also offers ways children can help soothe themselves and learn strategies to help with emotions.

Q: What role can books play in helping children to learn about emotions and self-regulation?

Sandra V. Feder: Books can be hugely important in a child’s understanding of their own emotional life, including how to explain and manage feelings. When young children see their experiences reflected in books, those experiences become validated and normalized, helping them feel less alone in navigating whatever it is they’re feeling.

Each of the books in the series deals with a different emotion and describes familiar scenarios when a child might experience that emotion. The books give children agency—there’s a point in each book where the child tries to find solutions. Those might include talking to a friend, asking for help, or putting words to the feeling even though it seems like no one will understand.

Picture books are wonderful conversation starters because they’re meant to be read together. That opens the door for meaningful discussions where the adult can ask the child guiding questions, allowing them both to share their experiences with similar feelings.

Q: From your experience as a parent and children’s book author, what three things can parents do to help children with their big feelings?

Sandra V. Feder: The most important thing is to talk about feelings. You have to be able to name it to tame it. Helping your children put words to their feelings is a gift. In Lonely Me, there is a scene where the child is feeling ignored by an older sibling on his phone. The lonely character remarks, “When I feel lonely, sometimes it helps to ask for attention,” and then adds, “No more screens!” Those simple words really empower a child. When my oldest daughter was young, and I was focused on her two younger sisters, I taught her to say, “I need a little attention.” I didn’t want her to fall apart just to get what she needed. In our current times, we’ve all felt the sting of someone paying more attention to a screen than to us. Giving our children the gift of powerful words and of our attention is vitally important.

Parents can also help their children simply by listening. All too often, adults brush aside a child’s feeling because it’s coming at an inconvenient time, like when we’re rushing out the door for school or trying to wind down at bedtime. But it’s important, even if we can’t do it right then, to circle back and make time to understand our children’s feelings. Sometimes what we see as anger might really be frustration, fatigue, or hurt feelings. Each of those requires a different response and a different path for regulation.

Finally, parents shouldn’t be afraid to share their own feelings. When children see that it’s okay to have strong emotions, it’s a powerful way to begin teaching them how to regulate those feelings. Explaining how we handle our own big feelings, like taking deep breaths when we’re angry or reaching out to friends when we’re feeling lonely, can be lovely teaching moments and allow parents to lead by example.

Q: How did you choose the emotions to focus on in the Emotions and Me series? How do loneliness, anger, peacefulness, and feeling shy resonate deeply with young readers?

Sandra V. Feder: I started with Angry Me, and I felt strongly that the main character be a girl, because girls and women often get the message that they shouldn’t feel anger. Angry Me has resonated very deeply with young readers, precisely because it explores such a universal emotion.

While I was working on that book, I realized I wanted to do a book that showed the opposite emotion, and I landed on peaceful. In Peaceful Me, the boy starts out talking about how he likes feeling peaceful and all the situations and places he does. But when a friend does something to jolt him out of that peaceful feeling, he shares the ways he tries to get back to it, like finding a hug, taking deep breaths, and remembering things that make him happy.

Tackling shyness came about because one of my own children had shy moments, even around people she knew well. In that story and in Angry Me, I didn’t want to imply that the feeling was wrong; rather, I wanted to acknowledge that it’s just one part of who we are. We can talk about it and learn ways to handle that shyness. It’s another feeling so many children experience, but it doesn’t need to be a defining or forever feeling.

Lonely Me, the newest book in the series, was inspired by my concern over the epidemic of loneliness in the United States and in other places around the world, which deeply affects our youth. Loneliness can arise from feeling different, experiencing loss, or when there’s a lack of meaningful connection, and the story provides ways to make connections and soothe that loneliness.

Q: How can parents lay a strong foundation for the next generation’s mental health and well-being?

Sandra V. Feder: Children’s mental health is vitally important. As they grow, the problems they confront—from social situations to challenges at school and at home—grow, too. Giving our children the tools they need to understand and talk about their feelings, and to begin to self-manage those feelings, sets them up for success.

My books promote social-emotional learning, including naming feelings, empathy, self-management, and relationship building. All of these are skills children need now and in the future.

Q: What do you hope young readers take away from spending time with your books?

Sandra V. Feder: I hope children feel seen in my books. I want them to learn new ways to talk about their feelings, to understand what might be underlying or causing those feelings, and to learn new tools for handling those feelings.

I hope my books help children appreciate the richness of their emotional lives, discover words to describe their feelings, and develop greater empathy for the emotional journeys of others.
