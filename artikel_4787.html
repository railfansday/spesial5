Sight Loss Does Not Make Someone an Incomplete Person

Nadine, a research participant in a study on blindness, said that using a white cane in public often made her the center of attention. She explained: “…it’s difficult because sometimes you don’t want everyone to look at you when you walk into a room. Sometimes you just want to sneak in and go … I just want to blend in” (Konrad, 2021).

The white cane attracts the attention of sighted people because it signifies blindness, which evokes their fear about their own vulnerability and their curiosity about people they may rarely encounter in daily life (Paterson, 2013; Garland-Thomson, 2006).

The irony is that this attention-grabbing symbol of blindness often obscures the individuality of those using it, thereby transforming unique cane users into faceless ciphers of disability. As one blind woman put it: “I'm invisible. …Even in a room full of 100 people, I feel like I'm not seen” (Szilva, 2025, p. 63). The person disappears behind the white cane because, in the eyes of others, their disability is the essence of who they are. In fact, others tend to assume that the blind person’s disability explains their personality, motives, and behaviors (Nario-Redmond, 2020).

In this post, I will explore how the use of culturally acquired assumptions to interpret and evaluate the behaviors of visually impaired adults complicates how they view themselves and how they respond to sight loss.

The onset of sight loss most often occurs in middle age or after (Brinks et al., 2019; NHS England, 2024). The radical changes brought about by sight loss usually trigger an extended period of depression and anxiety, as people gradually make the journey from sightedness to blindness (Nyman et al., 2012). To forestall fully arriving at that destination, they may try to pass as sighted for as long as possible (Thurston, 2024).

Eventually, most people experiencing sight loss face reality, and some take advantage of services that teach the nonvisual skills needed to function effectively in their daily lives, despite limited availability (Kaldenberg et al., 2023). Learning to navigate the world with a white cane is perhaps the nonvisual skill most closely linked to feelings of shame, inferiority, and loss of identity (Berndtsson, 2025). When Alice began to use a cane, she described herself as almost subhuman:

When you have a disability, you’re not a whole person.…[That’s] what gives you this nagging feeling that somehow you don’t have the same human dignity, you’ve become a little less, a little lower, a little… well, you’re not a whole, complete person. (Berndtsson, 2025).

What might Alice have meant when she stated that she is not “a whole, complete person”? She likely was referring to what her culture has taught her about blindness and blind people. In childhood, we are exposed to and begin to learn stereotypic beliefs about a variety of social groups, including racial, gender, and disability groups (Devine, 1989; Farago et al., 2019).

People experiencing sight loss and other disabling impairments often view themselves and are viewed by others through the lens of cultural stereotypes that mark them as inferior and defective (Nario-Redmond, 2010, 2020; Ricker, 2023). The white cane is a powerful symbol of blindness that reminds its users of these stereotypic beliefs. The stereotypes characterize people with severe visual impairments as dependent, incompetent, and helpless.

A person is said to have “internalized stigma” when they have learned the stereotypes and use them to evaluate themselves (see Goffman, 1963). These stereotypic beliefs emphasize dependency, which suggests what might be meant by not being “a whole, complete person.” The majority of cultures in Western societies promote an ideal that may be called the “individualistic self” (Cushman, 1995; Markus & Kitayama, 1991).

This ideal views the self as an internal center of agency: the maker and implementer of decisions that require no help from others. The individualistic self consists of a unique combination of elements that underlie our actions: traits, abilities, values, preferences, and desires.

The self is considered to be “bounded”: It is contained within the mind/body, and is presumed to be the primary determinant of our actions. The self, in essence, is assumed to stand alone in choosing goals and directing actions to achieve them. Thus, in this view, a whole, complete person exhibits total self-sufficiency, self-reliance, and autonomy.

Alice is not alone in thinking that reduced independence, understood as a reduced ability to perform tasks without help, somehow reduces one’s worth and dignity. A participant in another study lamented his lost independence: “I was independent all my life, I've done everything by myself … I now have to rely on other people to help in many, many ways” (cited in Enoch et al., 2025, p. 1121).

Some researchers and disability scholars argue for a shift towards encouraging interdependence rather than independence as a goal for adapting well to blindness and low vision.

Kenneth Jernigan, who served for years as the president of the National Federation of the Blind (NFB), gave a speech in 1993 in which he explained to some student critics why he used a sighted guide at the 1992 NFB convention. He stated that, when his mind was occupied with planning and completing tasks, depending on someone to guide him allowed him to concentrate on important matters.

Eva Feder Kittay, a philosopher of disability ethics, has questioned the ideal of the independent self. She has argued that no one is truly independent. Everyone, whether they are disabled or nondisabled, relies on “invisible” networks of supportive relationships that help them with daily tasks (Gesser & Fietz, 2021).

In his 1993 address, Jernigan provided an example that illustrated the importance of this publicly invisible support system in the lives of people who would seem to embody the cultural ideal of the independent, self-reliant person. Lamar Alexander, the Secretary of Education, spoke at the previous year’s convention. Alexander had an assistant arrive a day ahead of time to take care of arrangements. He picked Alexander up at the airport, accompanied him to the meeting room, walked with him to the stage, met him after the talk, and drove him to the airport. Alexander was fully capable of doing all those things without help, but he just did not have the time. Jernigan mused: “If the Secretary had been blind, I wonder if somebody would have said, ‘Just look! He's not independent. He has to have a sighted person with him at all times.’”

For Kittay, needing assistance does not make us incomplete humans or unworthy of human dignity. Needing assistance and giving assistance to others is what being human is all about: They are central features of the human condition.
