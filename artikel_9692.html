Crypto spent years building around regulation. In 2026, more founders are building with it in mind from day one.

There are practical reasons for that. Regulatory status can determine which customers a company can serve, which activities it can legally perform, and how banks, payment providers, liquidity partners and institutional clients assess the business. For some companies, authorization is the price of entering a target market. For others, a lighter registration may be enough for the activities they actually perform.

The difficult part is that there is no universal “crypto license.” MiCA authorization in the EU, Canadian MSB registration and offshore VASP regimes solve different regulatory problems and come with different permissions, costs and obligations. The US does not even offer a single authorization that neatly fits the label.

Choosing between these regimes starts with the business. The right regulatory setup depends on what the company does, where its customers are, which counterparties it needs to work with, and how it plans to grow. Those factors are more useful starting points than headline costs or promises of a fast license.

“Crypto company” tells a regulator very little.

An exchange, custodian, broker, transfer provider, stablecoin issuer and trading platform may face very different requirements. Before choosing a jurisdiction, define which activities the business actually needs to perform and which of them require authorization or registration.

That distinction matters because regulatory frameworks do not all cover the same activities. A regime that works perfectly well for one business model may not give another company the permissions it needs.

So “How much does a crypto license cost?” is already the wrong first question. Start with: “What activities do we need permission to perform?”

Next comes market access. A regulatory status that works in one jurisdiction does not automatically give a company the right to serve customers somewhere else.

If the EU is a core market, for example, MiCA matters because an authorized crypto-asset service provider can use its cross-border framework to provide covered services across Member States. A registration elsewhere may satisfy AML requirements without providing equivalent access to EU customers.

A cheap regulatory setup stops looking cheap if it excludes your most important customers.

Regulatory approval has more than one audience. Customers matter, but so do banks, payment providers, liquidity partners and institutional clients. Each runs its own due-diligence process, and regulatory status is only one part of it.

That makes speed and price poor measures on their own. An authorization that your key counterparties won't accept has limited practical value. Or, put less politely: regulatory paperwork nobody important accepts is expensive wall art.

Application fees make nice comparison tables. They rarely tell you what running a regulated business will actually cost.

Capital requirements, compliance staff, reporting, audits, local presence, technology controls and ongoing supervision can matter far more than the fee paid when an application is filed. A free registration process does not mean a free compliance program, just as a modest government fee says little about the total cost of maintaining authorization.

A regulatory setup that works for an early-stage company can become awkward once the company adds products or enters new markets. That does not mean every startup should begin with the most expensive authorization available. It means the next move should be part of today's decision.

Can the authorization cover additional activities? Does it provide cross-border access? Would entering your next major market require another entity and another regulatory process? Would customers or contracts have to migrate?

The cheapest jurisdiction today can be an expensive jurisdiction to leave tomorrow.

The EU and Canada make the problem easy to see. Both regulate businesses dealing in crypto assets, but the regulatory status they offer serves very different purposes.

MiCA created a common authorization framework for crypto-asset service providers across the EU.

Permanent minimum capital starts at €50,000 for Class 1 services such as execution, transfer and advice, rises to €125,000 when custody or exchange services are included, and reaches €150,000 for operators of crypto-asset trading platforms.

An authorized CASP can use MiCA's cross-border notification process to provide services covered by its authorization in other EU Member States, rather than seeking a separate CASP authorization in each one.

For a business with the EU at the center of its expansion plans, that cross-border access matters. But so does the scope of the authorization itself. MiCA comes with prudential, organizational and conduct requirements, and an authorization specifies which crypto-asset services the provider may offer.

In other words, “we have a MiCA license” only tells you part of the story. What does the company actually have permission to do?

Businesses that qualify as money services businesses or foreign money services businesses and deal in virtual currency may have to register with FINTRAC. Foreign businesses can fall within the regime when they direct covered services at customers in Canada even without a place of business there.

FINTRAC charges no registration fee. Put next to MiCA's capital and compliance requirements, that can look like an obvious bargain. It isn't a like-for-like comparison.

FINTRAC registration brings a business into Canada's anti-money-laundering and anti-terrorist-financing framework. It is not blanket authorization to offer every type of crypto product or service to Canadian customers.

That distinction is worth remembering whenever a jurisdiction is advertised as offering a “fast crypto license.” A license to do what?

“Offshore crypto license” used to suggest a fairly simple trade-off: less access to major regulated markets in return for lower costs and lighter supervision. That shorthand is getting harder to defend.

Take Seychelles. Under its Virtual Asset Service Providers Act, the Financial Services Authority licenses four categories of activity: wallet services, virtual asset exchanges, broking and virtual asset investment services.

The fees are substantial enough to challenge the old stereotype. Applying for a VASP license costs SCR 75,000. Once licensed, a provider pays an annual base fee of SCR 75,000 plus an activity-specific fee: SCR 375,000 for an exchange, for example, or SCR 300,000 for a wallet provider.

Licenses are perpetual unless suspended, revoked or surrendered, but staying licensed requires annual fees and ongoing compliance.

The British Virgin Islands has its own VASP framework. Application fees are $10,000 for an exchange or custody provider and $5,000 for other VASP activities, with separate initial registration and annual renewal fees. Applicants also need an authorized representative, and businesses applying across several categories pay the relevant application fee for each.

None of these figures represents the full cost of running a regulated crypto business. Legal work, compliance staff, corporate substance, technology and other ongoing obligations sit outside the application or registration fee. “Offshore” tells you where a company is regulated. It tells you increasingly little about how lightly it is regulated.

There is another factor to consider in the BVI. As of June 2026, the jurisdiction remains on the Financial Action Task Force's list of jurisdictions under increased monitoring. That does not make BVI businesses automatically high-risk or prevent them from operating, but counterparties may take the jurisdiction's FATF status into account in their own risk assessments.

So a low headline fee can coexist with meaningful regulatory obligations and with considerations that never appear in a “cheapest crypto licenses” ranking.

Georgia is worth watching in 2026 for a simple reason: its regulatory framework is changing in real time.

Virtual asset service providers have been subject to mandatory registration with the National Bank of Georgia since 2023, including AML/CFT supervision and fit-and-proper requirements. Then, on September 8, 2026, the National Bank expanded the framework.

The updated rules introduce minimum supervisory capital based on activity: GEL 150,000 for exchange or transfer services, GEL 350,000 for trading-platform administration, and GEL 250,000 for other VASP services.

Stablecoin issuance is treated separately. For an initial coin offering of a stablecoin, supervisory capital starts at GEL 500,000 and increases as the value of reserve assets grows. Providers offering more than one virtual-asset service must maintain the highest minimum applicable to their activities.

Existing registered VASPs have until September 1, 2027 to meet the new capital requirements, while new risk-management requirements take effect for them on July 1, 2027.

Static jurisdiction rankings have a short shelf life. A founder comparing options at the beginning of 2026 would have been looking at a different cost and compliance structure. By September, the direction of travel had changed.

Georgia remains fundamentally different from MiCA: registration with the National Bank does not provide MiCA's cross-border access to EU markets. What it offers is a supervised domestic framework that is becoming more formalized. If your business model depends on a jurisdiction staying unusually light-touch for the next five years, that assumption belongs in the risk model.

The United States presents a different problem. There is no single authorization that covers the entire crypto industry. The regulatory path depends on the product, the activity and, in some cases, the state.

Money transmission can involve state licensing. New York has its own BitLicense framework. The GENIUS Act established a federal regulatory framework for payment stablecoins, although the framework is not yet fully in effect. Companies pursuing regulated custody or banking models may also deal with the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency. Securities-related activities can bring another regulatory layer into play.

And the map is still changing. In 2026, the OCC continues to receive licensing applications from companies planning to offer digital-asset products and services, including applications for national trust bank charters.

For founders, the lesson is simple: define the product before looking for the license. A stablecoin issuer, an institutional custodian and a retail exchange may all call themselves crypto companies while facing very different regulatory paths.

So a comparison table with a single row for “US crypto license” is already hiding the most important part of the answer.

As of September 2026, businesses carrying out cryptoasset activities that fall within the Money Laundering Regulations must still register with the FCA before starting those activities in the UK. But the country is already moving toward a broader authorization regime under the Financial Services and Markets Act.

The FCA's application gateway opens on September 30, 2026 and closes on February 28, 2027. The new regime is scheduled to come into force on October 25, 2027. Existing MLR registration does not automatically become FSMA authorization. Firms carrying out activities covered by the new regime will need the relevant FCA authorization or permissions.

Timing matters here because firms that apply during the application period can benefit from saving provisions if the FCA has not finished deciding their application before the new regime begins. That can allow them to continue providing cryptoasset services while the application is being determined.

So a company evaluating the UK in 2025, September 2026 and October 2027 is not really evaluating the same regulatory environment. The same problem appears elsewhere. A jurisdiction can look inexpensive because new capital requirements have been announced but are not yet binding on existing providers. Another can look simple because a transitional regime is still open.

Any serious licensing comparison needs a date attached to it. Regulation does not stand still while you choose where to operate.

Strip away the “crypto-friendly country” rankings and the comparison becomes less exciting but much more useful.

Put the same factors side by side for every jurisdiction: market access, permitted activities, prudential requirements, regulatory fees, ongoing compliance, counterparty compatibility and expansion path.

That may be an unsatisfying answer, but it is a useful one.

MiCA offers a route to cross-border market access within the EU, alongside prudential, organizational and conduct requirements. Canada shows why AML registration should not be confused with broad market authorization. Seychelles and the BVI show how far the offshore model has moved from the old light-touch stereotype.

Georgia demonstrates how quickly a comparatively lean framework can become more formalized. The US shows why some markets cannot meaningfully be reduced to a single crypto license. And the UK, right now, is a reminder that the date of the comparison can matter almost as much as the jurisdiction.

None of this produces a satisfying top-five ranking. A license is infrastructure. Choose it for the business you intend to build on top of it, not for the certificate that is easiest to put on the wall.
