Can You Be Too Successful to Fire? Market Basket's Board Said No

Strong operating results did not settle Market Basket's boardroom fight. On April 20, 2026, Vice Chancellor J. Travis Laster of the Delaware Court of Chancery upheld the suspension and subsequent firing of longtime president and CEO Arthur T. Demoulas, while acknowledging the company's operating success (DSM HoldCo). The central question was whether the directors acted in good faith in exercising their authority, not whether firing Demoulas was the best business choice.

The ruling separates authority to remove a CEO from the wisdom of that decision. This article and this series draw a broader governance lesson from that distinction: a board's job is to protect the company's enduring character and accountability, not just referee its machinery. The three-day trial addressed the narrower question of whether these directors acted lawfully (Moran; DSM HoldCo).

The business judgment rule limits judicial second-guessing when it applies. In this case, Demoulas had to prove that a majority of the directors acted in bad faith. The court found that he failed to do so (DSM HoldCo). Laster took the case under submission on February 25, 2026, and issued his opinion on April 20. The outcome established that Demoulas had not met that burden, rather than that the board had chosen wisely. Honesty alone does not capture the rule's full requirements.

Market Basket's public story centers on low prices and loyal cashiers. In 2014, workers shut down stores for weeks to demand Demoulas's reinstatement after an earlier ouster by his cousins. The walkout made the chain a folk-hero story and ended in a $1.6 billion buyout that put Demoulas and his three sisters in sole control of the company (Chesto). Eleven years later, the boardroom fight pitted him against the sisters who had once helped bring him back. That history explains the public stakes; customer loyalty alone did not resolve the legal question.

Demoulas grew Market Basket into an operation generating roughly $8 billion in annual revenue and employing more than 30,000 people (Chesto). By any ordinary measure, he did his job well, and the court did not dispute that. Laster wrote that Demoulas "proved to be an excellent operator, but an imperious leader," adding that strong performance is "not the only dimension of a CEO's job" (DSM HoldCo).

The board's concerns extended beyond operating results. In August 2024, it gave Demoulas a five-item "Issues List" covering budgets, capital expenditure approvals, and succession, including rejecting automatic succession by his children. The opinion describes his failure to resolve those issues (DSM HoldCo). Reports of a possible walkout and boycott heightened the directors' concerns in spring 2025. They suspended him in May pending an outside investigation by Quinn Emanuel, then terminated him in September after unsuccessful mediation (DSM HoldCo; Solis). The investigation's conclusions supported the board's position. Those investigative conclusions and the court's findings represent separate parts of the record.

Two companies, DSM HoldCo and Demoulas Super Markets, brought the Section 225 action with directors Jay Hachigian, Steven Collins, and Michael Keyes to confirm the validity of Demoulas's removal. Demoulas counterclaimed that the directors breached their fiduciary duties by suspending and terminating him. He sought reinstatement as president and CEO (DSM HoldCo; Pelletiere and Boston 25 News Staff). At trial, he had to prove that a majority of the directors had acted in bad faith. He did not meet that burden. Laster rejected the claim that the directors were beholden to the sisters and found a rational basis for their concern about a repeat of 2014. The court upheld the removal (DSM HoldCo).

This case turns on a distinction between operating performance and governance cooperation, rather than a single verdict on a CEO. The two dimensions ask different questions. Collapsing them into one obscures what the court decided.

Operating performance covers revenue, growth, and customer loyalty, familiar subjects in shareholder letters. Governance cooperation asks different questions: whether management gives directors transparent information, plans succession honestly, and accepts that a board's authority to ask questions does not shrink as the business grows. Strong results and legitimate oversight address distinct responsibilities; neither cancels the other. The board, in turn, must exercise its authority for the company, not merely demand obedience.

Could the directors act independently of a powerful executive and obtain the information they needed to govern? Those are the two questions in the series' Independence and Information framework. In this dispute, their documentation began with unresolved issues in 2024 and continued through the May 2025 suspension pending investigation, the investigation itself, and unsuccessful mediation before termination. The court upheld their exercise of judgment on that record (DSM HoldCo). The documents supported the decision as evidence; the obligation to act in the company's interests remained.

The independence question starts with the family dispute. Outside status alone cannot establish a director's independence. Selection by one side of the family, by contrast, does not by itself prove bad faith. What matters is whether the directors exercised judgment for the company rather than serving a faction. The court rejected Demoulas's challenge on the record before it (DSM HoldCo).

None of this settles what Market Basket's culture or legacy required. Demoulas led a business with substantial employee and customer loyalty, and his supporters argue that the hands-on style they associate with "Artie T." helped sustain that success across roughly ninety stores. A spokesperson for Demoulas acknowledged that Delaware's deference to directors imposed high hurdles, but pointed to strong performance as evidence the company did not need fixing (Pelletiere and Boston 25 News Staff). That argument deserves a hearing. It is not a frivolous one.

The strongest objection concerns what removal would mean for the business. The directors could have authority to act without proving that firing Demoulas would preserve Market Basket's advantages better than a workable compromise. Strong results, meanwhile, could not put the CEO beyond oversight. The ruling resolves the legal challenge. It cannot show how the company would have fared under a different arrangement.

The unresolved business question leaves responsibilities intact on both sides. Strong operating results do not erase a board's oversight role. The board must exercise authority over management for the company, rather than treat control as an end in itself. Successful operations and legitimate oversight must coexist.

Section 225 supplied a focused procedure for determining whether Demoulas's removal was valid. The case moved from the September 2025 filing to the April 20, 2026 post-trial opinion in about seven months (DSM HoldCo; Vinson & Elkins). That pace gave this particular leadership dispute a prompt answer. The record also extended beyond the termination meeting: unresolved governance requests, an outside investigation, and unsuccessful mediation preceded the firing.

Ten days after the ruling, Market Basket named a former teenage bagger as its new president. Chuck Casassa, the operations director, joined the chain at fourteen in 1976. The April 30, 2026 announcement also marked the retirement of interim CEO Don Mulligan after more than four decades with the company, including 27 years as CFO (Riley). Separately, the board said the company was not for sale, would remain family-owned, and expected to work productively with Demoulas as a shareholder (Pelletiere and Boston 25 News Staff). Those statements describe the board's intentions. The long-term effects of its decision remain an open question.

The ruling gives family-company executives a clear answer to a recurring governance question: strong operating results do not put management beyond legitimate oversight. Performance is part of the job, not the whole job. Accountability matters too. Directors have a corresponding responsibility to exercise their authority on behalf of the company.
