What it takes to pivot

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

There comes a time when many companies need to pivot. Maybe you want to expand out of your core category into a new one, or maybe you’re making a complete 180. When I joined You.com as COO in January this year, we decided to pivot by consolidating our product and business units around one core offering. Frankly, that offering had always been the heart of the company. This decision came down to evaluating three dimensions of the business: people, products, and customers.

From first-hand experience, I can tell you that how you approach a pivot’s first 90 days can make or break your business. When done ineffectively, it can leave employees and customers uncertain. When done well, you can bring both of those groups on board, united under a new vision.

Here are three tips for how to have a successful company pivot, from someone who recently navigated it.

1. ARRIVE WITH A THESIS, THEN TRY TO BREAK IT

Whether you’re coming into a company for the first time like I did, or you’re a long-time team member, you should always be evaluating its strengths and weaknesses to inform the best next steps to take. I wasn’t trying to shake things up for the sake of change. Rather, I had a series of conversations with the founder and CEO before I even stepped foot in the door that shaped my initial vision and plan for the company’s future. I developed an initial thesis for how the company should move forward. But rather than immediately implementing my ideas, I used the first 30 days to test it, finding its gaps and strengths. When exploring a pivot, keep an open mind and gauge buy-in before making any major changes.

I spent my first 30 days diving into the people, product, and customers. I took dozens of one-on-one meetings with employees from all departments and levels, spent enough hands-on time with the products to understand them, and also spoke with dozens of customers about what they liked about the products or how they would change them.

I looked at the company the way an investor would from the outside. I asked questions like: Where are we best positioned to win short-term and long-term? Where do we have true competitive advantage? Where is the best market opportunity? Where are our best win rates? Where is our biggest pipeline? Where is our best retention? Even if these answers differ from your original ideas, ego can’t change the data. These efforts validated my initial thesis and gave me the data and examples to commit to the pivot.

2. PIVOT AROUND A NORTH STAR

Teams are much more receptive in a return to the mission or reinforcement of the mission rather than a reinvention. You want to frame your pivot around a central anchor point, informed by the data. Another way to think of it is to clarify your North Star and pinpoint the business’s strengths and barriers to success.  

At You.com, we had success because like most companies, we made things that customers were willing to pay for. We also went out of our way to find solutions to specific problems they were facing. This is all positive, and in the process we made good products.

The reality was we sometimes built custom solutions for different customers which were valuable but not scalable. That’s not to say we shouldn’t have pursued it. Companies should branch out, test new ideas, and find creative solutions to new problems. Throughout this process, though, the outcome needs to serve the business’s North Star and value-creation objectives.

In our case, a pivot wasn’t completely revamping the product or service. Rather, we realized that the web search API product line was our foundation, and contributed to the majority of our revenues. However, it was not getting the majority of resources. We made the informed decision to reorient our company around this core product and went all-in.

This pivot essentially brought us home. When the company launched in 2021, we were the first to introduce LLMs in a search engine context. A year later, we were the first to connect an LLM to the web and started building the foundation for how AI uses the internet. From this, we developed easier ways for businesses to plug their AI into the web. Over time, we added different layers and custom-built different capabilities. But the pivot was to remove the layers and focus on the foundation.

If you’re facing a similar decision, go back to the people, product, and customer. If what’s working is 90% of the business, make it 100% of the business. If the energy comes from removal, and you give it an earnest shot to build the one thing you know you’re good at, watch what happens to the entire organization.

3. CREATE A SOFT LANDING FOR WHAT GETS LEFT

It’s important not to trivialize the 10% that gets removed, because those customers bought from us for a specific reason. Telling those customers we were moving on was delicate. Those relationships took years to build and I wanted them to feel honored on the way out.

Just like you found creative solutions to their original problems, continue to do that now. In our case, we spun out a joint venture with a professional services partner who could continue supporting businesses with custom-built solutions. Those customers had somewhere to go and didn’t get abandoned. And we could focus all the company resources around a core product.

Ninety days of listening and designing bought the next 90 days of full-speed work. People become aligned because you listened. The product is there because it’s what you are excited about and what you do best. The customer trusts you because you showed them focus, authenticity, and caring.

Alex Triplett is COO of You.com.
