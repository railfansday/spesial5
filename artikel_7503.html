Supreme Court defers decision on whether to dismiss major gun case

With just over a week remaining before the justices will hear oral argument in a pair of challenges to the constitutionality of bans on AR-15-style semiautomatic rifles, the Supreme Court on Tuesday afternoon put off a decision on a request by Cook County, Illinois, to dismiss the dispute over its ban as moot – that is, no longer a live controversy. The court’s brief, unsigned order, which came one day after the justices granted a request by the lead plaintiff to remove him from the case, indicated that the justices would instead “defer[]” that ruling “pending oral argument,” which is scheduled for Wednesday, December 2.

The skirmish over whether the Illinois case should still go forward began on October 29, when Cutberto Viramontes, the named plaintiff in the case, filed a motion seeking to be dismissed from the case. Viramontes, who has been part of the challenge since it was filed in 2021, did not provide any explanation for his request.

Lawyers for Cook County subsequently asked the Supreme Court to dismiss the case as moot, arguing that it is “doubtful” that any of the other challengers would have a legal right to sue. They emphasized that Viramontes was a Cook County resident who said that he wanted to own an AR-15-style semiautomatic rifle. By contrast, they wrote, the other individual challenger in the case, Christopher Khaya, has said that he wants to own an Industrial Military Industries Galil semiautomatic rifle, which, according to Cook County, is not the kind of AR-15-style rifle covered by the question before the court.

The challengers countered that the question presented is not limited to AR-15 rifles but also extends to “similar” semiautomatic rifles, which would include AK-47-style rifles like the IMI Galil. But in any event, they added, Khaya “also desires to possess an AR-15 platform rifle and would acquire one if it were lawful for him to do so.”

In its order deferring a decision on whether the Cook County case is moot because of the dismissal of Viramontes from the case, the Supreme Court cited its 2023 decision in Acheson Hotels, LLC v. Laufer, in which it unanimously agreed after oral argument that the case was moot because the plaintiff had voluntarily dismissed her lawsuit in the lower court. But the chances that the court will ultimately reach the merits of the ban are likely higher than in Acheson, in which there was only one plaintiff, because the dispute over semiautomatic rifles encompasses not only multiple plaintiffs but also another case – a challenge to Connecticut’s ban on AR-15-style rifles.

Amy Howe is the co-founder of SCOTUSblog and its primary reporter.
