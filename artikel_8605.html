Mortgage REITs Pay Double-Digit Dividends on Borrowed Money – Keep Your Stake Small

Mortgage real estate investment trusts (REITs) often yield 10% or more by borrowing several dollars for every dollar of their own to invest in mortgages. So a jump in interest rates can cut both the payout and the share price. If you buy one, keep it a small slice of your portfolio and hold it in an IRA.

Recent yields run higher still. An index of mortgage REITs from Nareit, the REIT industry group, yielded 16.59% at the end of October 2026, against 1.04% for the S&P 500.

Unlike REITs that own buildings, a mortgage REIT owns mortgages or bonds made from them and earns the difference between the interest they pay and its own borrowing costs. Agency mortgage REITs buy bonds whose payments Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, or Ginnie Mae guarantee, then borrow heavily against them. Annaly Capital Management, which holds mostly agency bonds, ended September 2026 with borrowing and commitments to buy bonds equal to 5.9 times its net worth, its third-quarter report shows. Commercial mortgage REITs make property loans that carry no such guarantee.

When rates jump, mortgage bonds lose value and the borrowing costs more, and shareholders take the loss on everything bought with borrowed money. In the third quarter of 2026, Annaly’s book value per share, what it owns minus what it owes for each share, fell $2.29 to $17.86, a loss three times its $0.75 quarterly dividend. Ignoring taxes and dividend reinvestment, someone who bought Annaly at the end of September 2016 for an 11% yield has seen its dividend cut in 2019, 2020, and 2023 and its share price fall 60% by Nov. 18, 2026. Counting every dividend, the investment gained 26%, about 2% a year.

Keep a mortgage REIT to a slice of your income portfolio small enough that losing half of it wouldn’t change your plans, and hold it in an IRA. Most REIT dividends don’t get the lower tax rate on qualified dividends, so outside an IRA they’re taxed at your regular rate, after a 20% deduction. In a traditional IRA they aren’t taxed until you withdraw, and a Roth IRA’s qualified withdrawals are tax-free.

Before you buy one, add its last four quarterly dividends to the year’s change in book value per share, both listed in its earnings releases. A negative total means the dividends didn’t cover the drop in book value.
