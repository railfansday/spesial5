The happiness curve has changed, and young adults are struggling most

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

A global study of nearly 54,000 adults reveals a troubling shift in happiness, with young people reporting the lowest levels of well-being.

For decades, research suggested that happiness reaches its lowest point in middle age, with people feeling better in their younger and later years.

Researchers surveying adults around the world have now found little sign of that midlife dip.

Young adults reported the lowest well-being of any age group, and scores rose fairly steadily from there with age. The researchers call the pattern alarming, and wealthy countries were not spared.

Kai Ruggeri, a professor of health policy and management at the Columbia University Mailman School of Public Health, led the work.

His team of 261 researchers, all of whom took part as volunteers, surveyed nearly 54,000 adults in 92 countries.

Most people answered online and chose to take part, so the results describe the people who responded rather than whole nations. The authors make that point again and again.

“These findings are more than just a sign to policymakers that solutions are necessary to improve mental health among young people or those struggling to make ends meet,” Ruggeri said.

“In fact, they are a flashing red light that entire populations are already struggling and feel their resilience is diminished here and now, not only a concern for an uncertain future.”

Each participant rated ten statements about their own life. The scale ran from 1, meaning absolutely disagree, to 7, meaning absolutely agree.

The statements included “I feel full of energy,” “I feel calm and peaceful,” and “I recover quickly from things that go wrong in my life.”

Together, the statements measure different aspects of well-being, including optimism, resilience, and a sense of purpose.

The ten-part framework came from the well-being researcher Felicia Huppert and a colleague. Huppert died shortly before the work began, and the team dedicated the study to her.

She was told the day before her death that it would be carried out in her name.

Popular rankings such as the World Happiness Report rely on a few broad questions.

The authors wrote that two countries with the same life satisfaction score, say 7 out of 10, offer no clue about how either one could improve.

Samples from Denmark and Norway made the point. Both had an average life satisfaction of 6.2 out of 10, yet across the ten statements Denmark’s scores were spread more widely than Norway’s.

Participants also answered four standard screening questions about depression and anxiety over the previous two weeks.

People took the survey between June and August 2025, in 50 languages, and a typical participant finished in about six minutes.

People rated their relationships and their sense of meaning highest. On the 7-point scale, having support from people they’re close to averaged 5.05, and a sense of meaning averaged 4.93. Feeling full of energy came last, at 4.20.

Calm and the ability to recover from setbacks scored near the bottom too. The authors suggest those low scores fit with high rates of stress and burnout.

The difference was also clear among participants who gave the highest ratings of 6 or 7.

Just over 38% gave that answer about having support when they need it, but only 19.3% said the same about feeling full of energy.

The authors offer one possible reading of that gap. People may judge their own lives fairly kindly while feeling harmed by things they can’t control, such as unstable politics and economies.

Polls have described something similar, an optimism gap in which people feel better about their own lives than about society.

Only 6.5% of participants met a rough bar for flourishing. That meant scoring at least 60 out of a possible 70 on the ten statements while showing few signs of depression or anxiety.

The 38,133 participants who completed the screening questions showed the same age pattern.

Among people aged 18 to 25, only 14.7% showed no signs of depression or anxiety, compared with 23.6% of all participants.

At the other end, 28.6% of the young adults scored in the moderate to severe range, compared with 22.6% overall. Older groups did better, almost without exception.

Young adults in some wealthy countries fared worse still. In the UK sample, for example, only 6.1% showed no signs of depression or anxiety, while 42.8% scored moderate to severe.

The researchers’ first instinct was that their sample had produced the result. But a young and highly educated sample would typically skew results the other way, they wrote.

Other recent research has also found that younger adults now report the most distress.

Being free of depression and anxiety wasn’t the same as doing well, since many participants with no signs of either still scored low on the ten statements.

Well-being scores were higher at nearly every step up in household income, and the team found no clear point where the gains stopped.

That cuts against the common claim that money stops adding happiness past a certain income.

People who grew up in wealthier families scored higher as well.

Education followed a similar pattern. Each additional level of schooling was associated with higher average well-being.

The authors wrote that the education pattern contradicts claims that a university degree has lost its value.

People without paid work, whatever the reason, scored lower than workers, students, and retirees.

“Leaders can no longer use vague platitudes that hand-wave toward investing in non-specific mental health initiatives,” Ruggeri said.

Instead, he said, leaders need to make specific commitments.

Those include protecting livelihoods against job losses driven by AI, tackling burnout in young and working-age people, and improving access to care for mental illness.

People who lived with more family members or housemates generally reported higher levels of well-being than those living alone. Living alone was particularly common among older adults.

Among participants aged 75 and older, 44.9% lived by themselves, compared with 27.1% of all participants.

Among workers, people who split their time about evenly between home and the workplace scored highest, at 48.2 on a scale running from 10 to 70.

Those who worked entirely from home or entirely on site scored lowest, at 46.3 and 46.6.

The gaps between work arrangements were small. The researchers also couldn’t tell whether hybrid work helps or whether jobs that offer it are simply better jobs to begin with.

People in military service, a small part of the sample, scored highest of any work group.

The authors wrote that this likely reflects the relationships, energy, and sense of purpose common in a military unit.

Men scored slightly higher than women, though the difference was extremely small.

Everyone answered once, so the survey shows links rather than causes. The samples were uneven too, and in Chad almost everyone who took part was an academic.

Ruggeri’s team warned against ranking countries, because the statements may not mean exactly the same thing in every language.

A low average for Sweden, they wrote, doesn’t mean an employed, well-educated Swede is worse off than an unemployed person with little schooling in Uzbekistan.

Young people with unaddressed worries may have been eager to take part, and the oldest participants may have been unusually connected and energetic.

The authors wrote that those effects alone can’t explain a pattern this large.

Why young adults scored so low remains open. The authors raised two possibilities they didn’t test, starting with a steady stream of messages about stress, conflict, and lack of opportunity.

The other is loneliness, since young adults meet people in person less often than in the past, according to an OECD report.

Ruggeri’s team plans deeper analyses of wealth and well-being and calls for local studies within countries. In the authors’ view, those studies can best show governments which changes would help.

The full study was published in the journal Social Indicators Research.
