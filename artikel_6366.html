A rare giant moa egg is returning to New Zealand after spending sixty years in an American collection

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Colossal Biosciences announced plans to return a rare giant moa egg, discovered in New Zealand in 1966.

A giant moa egg found in New Zealand on January 14, 1966, spent about six decades in a private American collection. On June 12, 2026, it resurfaced at a public auction run by Artemis Gallery in Louisville, Colorado.

Colossal Biosciences, a Dallas-based biotechnology company whose de-extinction projects include the moa, bought the egg. On October 20, 2026, it announced plans to return it to Aotearoa, the Māori name for New Zealand.

Fewer than 40 sufficiently complete moa eggs are known in public collections worldwide. Only eight keep at least 90 percent of their shell.

The egg measures about 8.7 by 6.7 inches (22 by 17 centimeters). That is roughly 1.5 times the length of an ostrich egg and nearly double its volume.

It has been reconstructed from many fragments, and more pieces still need to be reattached.

The egg may have come from a human burial site from the Moa Hunter period, around AD 1300. Further research is needed to confirm that origin.

Colossal made the announcement at the opening of the 11th International Meeting of the Society of Avian Palaeontology and Evolution. The meeting ran from October 19 to 23 at the Christchurch Town Hall and opened with a pōwhiri, a Māori welcome.

Sir Peter Jackson, the filmmaker and a partner in Colossal’s moa program, contacted the company on learning the egg was being sold abroad. Colossal offered to rescue it at its own expense and fly it home privately.

“Every New Zealander grows up knowing the moa and feeling its loss,” Jackson said in a statement.

Once restored, the egg “will very likely become only the ninth known nearly intact moa egg,” Jackson noted.

Colossal plans a repatriation ceremony in December. The egg will be handed to Professor Te Maire Tau, a Māori historian and tribal leader in the Canterbury region.

“When we launched the moa program we made a clear commitment that indigenous leadership would guide the science and every decision surrounding the project,” explained Ben Lamm, the CEO and co-founder of Colossal.

“We are honored to help return it home,” Lamm stated.

The remaining fragments will be reattached under the guidance of Ngāi Tahu researchers.

Non-destructive imaging, or sampling for DNA and isotopes, could confirm the egg’s species and origin. Ngāi Tahu will decide whether and when to do this, following tikanga, or Māori customary practice.

Kyle Davis is a Ngāi Tahu archaeologist and an expert in cultural heritage.

“To see an egg of this rarity return to Te Waipounamu is extraordinary. It carries information about the moa species that birthed it, the landscape it came from and potentially the people connected to it,” Davis said.

Te Waipounamu is the Māori name for New Zealand’s South Island, where the egg is headed.

The moa de-extinction program behind the purchase began in 2025 as a joint initiative of Colossal, the Ngāi Tahu Research Centre, Jackson and Canterbury Museum.

Fossil eggshell preserves ancient DNA from extinct moa, elephant birds and emus, a study of fossil eggshell by Murdoch University researchers showed in 2010. Moa shell carried roughly 125 times less bacterial DNA than moa bone.

A team led by Leon Huynen recovered DNA from the inner and outer surfaces of moa eggs. Sequences from 69 eggshells matched seven moa species.

Several thin-shelled eggs belonged to the heaviest moa, in the genera Dinornis, Euryapteryx and Emeus. They were the most fragile bird eggs measured to date, the team reported in a 2010 study of moa eggs.

Sex-specific DNA on the outer surfaces of Dinornis and Euryapteryx eggs suggests that the lighter males incubated them. Even so, breakage would have been common if they sat on the eggs in the usual way.

The same DNA revealed two distinct lineages within Euryapteryx, whose eggs differed in shell thickness.

Each moa egg “holds information we can get nowhere else,” pointed out Beth Shapiro, Ph.D., Colossal’s chief science officer.

The Canterbury Museum lists nine described moa species, in six genera and three families. In the coastal moa, females could reach 4.9 feet (1.5 meters) tall, while males were about the size of a turkey.

People reached New Zealand more than 600 years ago, and moa disappeared within 100 to 300 years of their arrival.

Frequent hunting of the birds and their eggs drove those losses, a 2025 modeling study by Curtin University and University of Adelaide researchers suggests.

Moa could only have survived alongside people if no-take harvest zones had covered more than half of New Zealand’s land.

Dr. Paul Scofield, senior curator of natural history at Canterbury Museum, is a collaborating scientist on Colossal’s moa work.

“Moa are central to the natural and cultural history of Aotearoa, and this egg has far greater scientific and cultural value here than it could ever have in a private collection overseas,” Scofield remarked.

The information was obtained from Colossal Biosciences.
