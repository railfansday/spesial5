Future heatwaves could make today’s record heat look mild

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

Future heatwaves could last longer, spread farther, and exceed recent records, even with sharp emissions cuts, models suggest.

When people plan for extreme heat, they usually plan around the worst summers they’ve already lived through.

New research suggests those summers won’t stay the worst, even if the world sharply cuts its emissions.

In climate model projections for nine regions around the world, many of this century’s heatwaves lasted longer, affected wider areas, and got hotter than the recent record-breakers in those places.

Philipp Aglas-Leitner, a researcher in the Department of Physics at Freie Universität Berlin, led the work.

Sarah Perkins-Kirkpatrick of the Australian National University and scientists in Germany, Switzerland, and New Zealand were co-authors of the study.

Because the results come from computer models, they show what could happen rather than predicting any particular summer. They also focus on the heat itself, not on what it would cost in lives or money.

“Recent observed events are outmatched by a substantial margin, particularly toward the end of the twenty-first century,” Aglas-Leitner said.

The researchers began with 22 heatwaves from 2010 to 2023 that broke records or caused serious harm.

From those, they picked one per region, including Europe’s summer of 2018 and the 2021 heatwave in the Pacific Northwest of the US and Canada.

For each region and each day of the year, the team found the temperature that only the hottest 10% of days had topped between 1961 and 1990.

A heatwave was any stretch of at least three days in a row when the region’s average daily high stayed above that mark.

For every heatwave, they tracked how long it lasted, how much of the region it covered, and how far temperatures climbed above normal. They also added up the extra heat above the mark.

The team used two temperature datasets to check the past heatwaves.

One is built from weather station readings by Berkeley Earth. The other, called ERA5, blends observations with a weather model.

For the future, the team used simulations from two generations of climate models, run for futures ranging from steep emissions cuts to very high emissions.

They checked how well each model matched each region’s hottest days and dropped the weakest quarter.

Europe’s 2018 heatwave lasted 34 days, from July 10 to August 12. If emissions stay high, the models suggest close to one in five future European heatwaves will last even longer, and India and Pakistan show the same pattern.

The team only looked at each region’s six warmest months, so no heatwave in the study could last longer than six months.

Even with steep emissions cuts, some projected heatwaves reached or nearly reached that limit in five of the nine regions.

Those five regions were Europe, northeast Siberia, North Africa and the Middle East, the Pacific Northwest, and South America.

Under high emissions, some projected heatwaves in those regions lasted the entire six-month study window.

A heatwave that runs a little hot for weeks can add up to more extra heat than one that’s very hot for a few days.

The researchers captured this with a number called cumulative heat, which adds up how many degrees each day’s high went above the heatwave mark.

Seven of the nine recent heatwaves had built up an unusually large amount of this heat.

Even with steep emissions cuts, the models gave most of those regions roughly a one-in-10 to one-in-five chance that a future heatwave would build up more than twice as much. Australia and China were the exceptions.

With high emissions, most of those regions faced similar odds of a heatwave with more than five times as much extra heat.

The team also sorted the results by how much the planet has warmed since the late 1800s. That makes it easier to see what different climate targets would mean.

Take the most intense day of Europe’s record summer.

In a world without global warming, the models gave a future heatwave less than a 0.3% chance of beating it. At 3.6°F (2°C) of warming, the upper limit in the Paris Agreement, nearly one in five projected European heatwaves did.

Countries’ current climate pledges would lead to about 4.7°F to 5°F (2.6°C to 2.8°C) of warming, according to the United Nations’ 2024 Emissions Gap Report.

At warming close to that level, 27% to 37% of projected European heatwaves beat the 2018 peak.

Even if countries keep their promises, then, a sizable share of Europe’s future heatwaves could top that record summer’s peak.

With more warming, the share grows. At 6.3°F to 8.1°F (3.5°C to 4.5°C), 36% to 64% of projected European heatwaves beat the 2018 event on every measure the team tracked, all at once.

In North Africa and the Middle East, the figure was 75% to 90%.

“Even ambitious emission reduction goals offer only limited protection,” Aglas-Leitner said.

“Without substantial emission reductions and consistent adaptation measures, however, a significant part of the twenty-first century will be defined by heat that goes far beyond our recent experiences.”

Another way to see the change is to ask how often a heatwave as extreme as each recent record would come along.

In model runs of the past, from 1951 to 2005, such heat showed up at most about once in 55 years, which was the length of that period.

Many models never produced a comparable event during that period, suggesting longer intervals between such extremes.

That gap shrinks fast. For 2015 to 2054, the wait in most regions drops to just a few years, whatever path emissions take.

By 2060 to 2099, most model groups project heatwaves matching or exceeding most recent benchmarks about once a year or more.

That pattern suggests heat like the recent record-breakers will become far more common than it was in past decades.

Australia’s heatwave of December 2019 is the exception. Its peak was so extreme that even late in the century, under low emissions, the models projected something similar only about once every 20 to 40 years.

A future heatwave that beats these records won’t necessarily cause more damage, the authors wrote. That depends partly on how well and how quickly people adapt, and partly on when in the summer the heat arrives.

People and ecosystems may adapt to some of these changes, but the human body has physiological limits on how much heat it can handle.

Adaptation efforts may also fall behind if heatwaves change as fast as the models project, the team wrote.

The study has limits of its own. Each region was drawn around the whole area a heatwave covered, so very local extremes don’t show up in the results.

The two temperature records also disagreed noticeably for some events, though the authors found this didn’t change their overall conclusions.

And the most extreme projections came more often from models that warm faster, though not only from them.

Understanding how these extremes would affect health, ecosystems, and electricity supplies will require studies at smaller, local scales.

The full study was published in the journal Nature Communications.
