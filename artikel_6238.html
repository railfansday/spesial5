15 Best Grand Canyon Hotels, Rentals, and Glampsites for Sleeping in Style Under the Stars

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

A mile deep and 277 miles long, the Grand Canyon is a jaw-droppingly majestic chasm in Arizona’s rust-red desert that’s speckled with pinyon pines. Standing on its edge, one can’t help but feel small, watching the mighty Colorado River churn vigorously along the ravine’s bottom. Whether you’re hoping for a challenging all-day hike to the canyon’s floor, a soul-stirring overlook near the visitor center, or a multi-day rafting expedition, this national park has all that (and more) in spades. It’s hardly a surprise that it drew more than 4.4 million visitors in 2025 alone, so you know you'll have some competition when it comes to finding the best place to stay nearby.

Personally, I like to stay as close as possible to the park, to make the most of dreamy desert sunrises and maximize my time on the trails. Grand Canyon is famous for its historic lodges and quirky Route 66 nostalgia, which can make picking the perfect spot for your crew a little overwhelming. Cozy cabin or fancy, family-sized rental? Canvas glamping tent under the stars or a fun getaway that’s in the heart of the action? I’ve sorted through the hundreds of options so you don’t have to. Here are my favorite Grand Canyon hotels, glamping sites, vacation rentals, and cabins for a memorable stay in and around this utterly striking national park.

When is the best time to visit Grand Canyon National Park?

With an elevation spanning 2,000 feet to 8,000 feet, Grand Canyon experiences a wide range of temperatures on any given day. Thus, the best season to visit greatly depends on what your adventure priorities are.

If you’re planning to raft “the big ditch” with a guide, go between April and October for warm, sunny days that perfectly contrast the 50-degree Colorado River. Hoping to hike to the bottom and camp at Bright Angel? Don your favorite traction devices atop the canyon to crunch through seasonal ice and book a backcountry permit for late fall, winter, or early spring. Auto tourists who want to explore the canyon’s south rim by car can travel in virtually any season—just be prepared for crowds in summer months and occasional snow closures at the height of winter.

Lastly, a good rule of thumb for intrepid travelers hiking down into the canyon is that you’ll gain roughly 5 degrees for every 1,000 feet you descend. Plan on it being 20 degrees warmer at the bottom of the canyon than it is at the rim. The National Park Service (NPS) does not recommend hiking to the bottom of the canyon during summer months, as temperatures at Phantom Ranch often surpass 100 degrees.

How do you get to Grand Canyon National Park?

If you’re planning to fly in, the closest major airports are Sky Harbor International in Phoenix and Harry Reid International in Las Vegas, but both are at least a three-hour drive away, so you’ll want to rent a car and crank up the tunes to brave the journey. Regional airports are scattered around the area, most notably in small-but-commercial Flagstaff, Sedona, and Tusayan. There’s also a vintage train service that runs daily from Williams, Arizona.

Notably, Grand Canyon National Park is a stone’s throw away from one of the most iconic road trips the US has to offer–historic Route 66. Running from Chicago all the way to the Pacific Coast in Santa Monica, California, this legendary trip ventures across northern Arizona on Interstate 40.

What are the best things to do in Grand Canyon National Park?

Spanning 23 breathtaking miles between Grand Canyon Village and the Desert View area, Desert View Drive is the only scenic byway in the park that’s open year-round to private vehicles. Enjoy Mary Colter’s iconic Desert View Watchtower, which was inspired by ancestral Puebloan architecture and features murals by Hopi artist Fred Kabotie, at the park’s far eastern edge.

Another popular drive along the South Rim is the winding Hermit Road, which ends at the historic stone structure of Hermit’s Rest that date back to 1914. The drive is only open to private vehicles in December, January, and February; in all other months, travelers must use the park’s free shuttle system, hopping on and off at popular sites.

Though the hiking distance between the canyon’s north and south rims is a scant 21 miles, the drive to get between the two is a circuitous 220 miles. As a result, only 10% of the national park’s visitors set foot on the just-as-striking North Rim. This high-elevation outpost (over 8,000 feet) and log cabin-style lodge have a shorter visitation season than the South Rim (you’ll have to travel there between May 15 and October 15 most years), but adventurous drivers desperate to escape the crowds should make a beeline for the pinyon and ponderosa pine–studded views of crumbling crimson sandstone along Cape Royal Road. Plus, you’ll be within easy driving distance of Zion, if you’d like to extend your journey.

If you’re into hiking past dramatic vermillion outcroppings, secret waterfalls, and hanging gardens, you’re in the right place. The Grand Canyon also boasts some of the most stunning wheelchair-accessible paths in the country (check out the 13-mile, mostly-paved Rim Trail), as well as hamstring-busting overnight treks into the belly of the ravine.

For an epic out-and-back excursion with much of the expansive red rock scenery you’d find on a longer jaunt, head to South Kaibab Trailhead and venture just under a mile downhill to the aptly named Ooh Aah Point. Want a little more bang for your buck? Continue down to Skeleton Point for a sweeping panorama of the Colorado River (six miles round trip). Bright Angel Trailhead is another iconic starting point for a day hike or overnight trek into the canyon. Lace up your boots for a three-mile round-trip stroll to the shade structure at Mile-and-a-Half Resthouse or, if you’re an experienced hiker looking to sweat, start early for an all-day 12-mile journey to jaw-dropping views of Plateau Point.

Trekking to the bottom of the Grand Canyon and back is the stuff hiking bucket lists are made of. But due to midday heat and the extremely strenuous nature of these hikes, the National Park Service does not recommend aiming to complete such a journey in a single day. It’s highly recommended to nab a backcountry permit and camp overnight at one of the many below-the-rim sites (like Bright Angel Campground or Havasupai Gardens). Pro tip: Use the free park shuttle and create a loop starting at South Kaibab and ending at the Bright Angel Trailhead. Heading for the quieter North Rim? Tramp down the North Kaibab Trail for two miles (one-way) to Supai Tunnel.

It’s rare that a national park offers a designated bike path along its most iconic sights, but at the Grand Canyon, visitors can spin for roughly 13 miles along the park’s dreamy South Rim vistas. BYO-bike or rent one at Canyon Adventures in the village, then steer onto the paved Greenway Trail to South Kaibab. Or, utilize the free shuttle system (yes, they’ve got bike racks) and ride the bus to Monument Creek Vista, then cruise along the South Rim to Hermits Rest.

North Rim visitors hoping to escape that South Rim fervor can enjoy loads of rugged mountain biking. A 12-mile swath of the enormous, 800-mile Arizona Trail runs right through the park.

Are there guided tours at Grand Canyon National Park?

There are dozens of phenomenal guided tours available for guests who aren’t comfortable backpacking alone or want a unique type of experience. Mule rides are a storied part of the canyon’s past and how many early tourists ventured into the canyon (sometimes in full Victorian regalia). Though it might seem like a rough place for a working animal, mules are much better equipped to handle heat and steep, rocky terrain than their horse counterparts. Plus, these rides offer below-the-rim access for visitors with mobility concerns. Today, both short day trips and extended overnight ventures to Phantom Ranch on muleback are available for those looking to pay homage to a bygone era.

For a brag-worthy addition to your hiking checklist, seek out Wildland Trekking’s guided rim-to-rim backpacking journeys (or, in winter, go big and test your skills on a rim-to-rim-to-rim hike). Craving something off the beaten path? The remote, five-day Hermit Loop offers miles of fire-red sandstone plateaus.

I’d be remiss if I didn’t mention the second-to-none river rafting to be had at Grand Canyon National Park. Grand Canyon Expeditions and Western River Expeditions have been guiding for decades and both offer first-class service for intrepid travelers looking to go the distance on the mighty Colorado.
