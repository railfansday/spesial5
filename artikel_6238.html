The Manufacturing Decision Boeing Was Forced To Make When It Developed The 737 MAX

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

In August 2011, Boeing officially launched its 737 MAX program after American Airlines made the historic decision to split its once all-Boeing fleet and buy 50% of its next-generation narrowbodies from Airbus. AA was facing rising fuel expenses and operating an aging fleet of roughly 300 McDonnell Douglas MD-80 jets. Thus, in July of that same year, AA revealed that of the 460 jets it planned to procure to modernize its fleet, 260 would be from the A320 family.

By mid-2011, Airbus had accumulated more than 1,000 orders for its re-engined A320neo. The airline chose to award more than half of its future fleet slots to Airbus because it already had a production-ready jet. In the aftermath, Boeing leadership abandoned plans for an all-new composite narrowbody within hours. Developing a new mainline jet would have required an estimated $10 billion to $12 billion and up to a decade of research. Instead, it committed to developing a re-engined 737 to retain its portion of the order.

Modifying the legacy 737 airframe was estimated to cost a third as much, with initial deliveries expected in 2017. Boeing also pursued certification as a derivative of the original 1967 type to entice operators with a cheap transition, even earning a $1 million per-aircraft rebate from launch customer Southwest Airlines. However, preserving grandfathered handling characteristics while integrating larger, higher-bypass turbofans onto the low-slung 1960s airframe imposed engineering constraints that forced Boeing to make a fateful compromise: the Maneuvering Characteristics Augmentation System.

At first, Boeing executives James Albaugh and Jim McNerney placed a wager on the Yellowstone Project, believing that airlines would wait for a 2020 clean-sheet composite plane instead of using the Airbus A320neo. Instead, to immediately save 15% on fuel consumption, operators quickly acquired more than 1,000 European narrowbodies. McNerney was then forced to abandon the Yellowstone study over the course of one weekend after American Airlines CEO Gerard Arpey threatened to terminate the airline's exclusive purchase agreement unless Boeing promptly supplied a re-engined 737.

Following the 737 MAX's formal debut on August 30, 2011, commercial contracts obliged Boeing to follow an accelerated derivative timetable. Southwest Airlines, the first client, ordered 150 aircraft in December 2011 under an arrangement that called for a $1 million rebate per plane if regulators imposed pilot simulator training. With Southwest eventually buying over 240 airframes, avoiding hundreds of millions of dollars in penalties necessitated certifying the 737 MAX using its original 1967 type certificate on a tight timetable.

Aerodynamic concessions were necessary to meet that deadline while mounting larger CFM International LEAP-1B engines onto the low-slung 1960s airframe. At high angles of attack, an uncontrollable nose-up pitching tendency was produced by cantilevering the nacelles forward and upward. Engineers used the Maneuvering Characteristics Augmentation System software to digitally mask the pitch anomaly, accelerating delivery while introducing a single-sensor flight control vulnerability, instead of implementing structural wing modifications that would cause certification delays and simulator training.

Boeing engineers had to extend the nose landing gear on the 737 MAX by 8 inches (20 cm) more than the Next Generation series, as well as cantilever the nacelles forward and upward using structural titanium pylons in order to fit 69-inch (175 cm) CFM International LEAP-1B engines onto the low-slung 1967 737 airframe. These larger engine cowls, which were positioned above the wing chord, produced excess aerodynamic lift ahead of the center of gravity at high angles of attack, creating an uncontrollable nose-up pitching tendency that went against federal regulations for linear control column force progression toward an aerodynamic stall.

Rather than executing costly structural wing redesigns or installing mechanical counterweights, Boeing relied on the Maneuvering Characteristics Augmentation System software to automatically command nose-down horizontal stabilizer trim without pilot input. To preserve grandfathered type-certificate commonality and avoid simulator training penalties, program officials positioned the software as a minor modification to the existing speed trim system and obtained Federal Aviation Administration approval to strip all references to the system from flight crew manuals, leaving operators and pilots unaware of its existence.

During certification flight testing, Boeing engineers increased the software's ability at lower airspeeds to push the horizontal stabilizer by 2.5 degrees based on input from a single angle-of-attack sensor, without reassessing single-point failure dangers. Boeing withheld internal simulator data from the Federal Aviation Administration, demonstrating that a company test pilot took more than 10 seconds to diagnose and counter an uncommanded activation, a delay classified as catastrophic internally when compared to the four-second federal response standard.

Boeing's Yellowstone Project originally divided commercial fleet renewal into three clean-sheet phases: Y1 for narrowbodies, Y2 for mid-sized jets, and Y3 for widebodies. While Y2 materialized as the composite 787 Dreamliner, severe delays and cost overruns on that program exhausted Boeing's engineering capacity and capital for Y1 and Y3. Simultaneously, Y1 studies stalled when engine manufacturers elected to frame their next-generation turbofans around the A320neo and air carriers ran out of patience, turning to the European planemaker.

Abandoning Y1 for a modified 737 forced engineers to integrate modern high-bypass engines on a fuselage created for 1960s crashworthiness and operating regulations. Grandfathering the type certificate allowed the 737 MAX to use older mechanical flight controls rather than adopt modern fly-by-wire. Thus, engineers could only use software-driven flight controls to a limited extent in compensating for the aircraft's size and weight. Instead, they modified the existing mechanical Speed Trim System with MCAS, using its jackscrew to move the horizontal stabilizer.

This reliance on grandfathered systems created direct performance gaps that a clean-sheet Y1 design would have eliminated through new systems and certification criteria. Unlike the 787, which features a digital alert system, the 737 MAX retained legacy warning lights and its AOA disagree alert. Disastrously, Boeing engineers discovered in August 2017 that the AOA alert was inoperable on more than 80% of delivered aircraft. Management deferred an MCAS software patch until 2020 and withheld the defect from operators and regulators until after the fatal crash in October 2018 of Lion Air Flight 610.

Shareholders have some serious questions for the manufacturer.

To reclaim narrowbody market share lost to the Airbus A320neo, Boeing structured the 737 MAX around minimizing airline transition costs and preserving existing fleet infrastructure. The high-bypass turbofans of the A320neo achieved 15% better fuel efficiency than the 737 NG thanks to large volumes of air around the engine core. Boeing required a very wide front fan diameter on its 737 MAX to match that, a design criterion that clashed with the 737's low ground clearance.

Boeing commercial leadership recognized that any derivative aircraft requiring full-flight simulator sessions would forfeit sales to legacy 737 operators. These commercial imperatives were codified directly into customer purchase agreements, most notably SWA's 240-strong order that included a $1 million rebate per delivered aircraft. Consequently, Boeing set minimal difference training as a core program objective. That meant pilot transition instruction from the 737 NG would be just a computer-based course, avoiding the expense of building and certifying new full-flight simulators before service entry.

Thus, to preserve the 1967 type certificate and avoid costly landing gear and wing-box redesigns, CFM International constrained the LEAP-1B to a 69-inch fan (175 cm) and a 9:1 bypass ratio, using 18 carbon-fiber composite blades to reduce rotational mass. Even with a flattened nacelle bottom, fitting the 6,129-pound (2,780 kg) engines required lengthening the nose gear by 8 inches (20 cm) and cantilevering the powerplants forward and upward on titanium pylons.

Positioned ahead of and above the wing chord, these enlarged cowls generated excess aerodynamic lift at high angles of attack, inducing an uncommanded nose-up pitching tendency that violated federal stall-recovery handling standards. Because extensive wing modifications would have invalidated the standing type certificate and triggered mandatory simulator retraining, Boeing engineers compensated digitally through MCAS to command 2.5 degrees of nose-down trim using data from a single external angle-of-attack vane.

The software enabled the airframe to meet certification handling rules on paper, but leadership went on to lobby the FAA to omit the software from flight crew manuals and differences tables, so Boeing could secure Level B training approval to win back airline orders. A conventional runaway stabilizer shows trim wheel rotation that pilots can recognize and disable. Meanwhile, MCAS was in discrete increments, and omitting the system deprived flight crews of the diagnostic knowledge to distinguish it as the source of a malfunction.

That software reliance was predetermined by 737 sales contracts negotiated years before the prototype flew. Southwest Airlines, the first customer, ordered 150 737 MAX aircraft in December 2011, with the agreement that Boeing would pay a $1 million rebate per jet if the Federal Aviation Administration permitted simple, simulator-based training for pilots migrating from the 737 NG to the MAX. With Southwest eventually buying over 240 airframes, avoiding hundreds of millions of dollars in penalties forced leadership to maintain common type ratings and remove system references from manuals.

During the initial 737 MAX 8 flight test campaign starting in January 2016, Boeing originally designed the Maneuvering Characteristics Augmentation System to operate exclusively during high-speed, flaps-up wind-up turns. Lift generated by the forward-mounted CFM International LEAP-1B28 nacelles lightened control column forces as angle of attack increased, violating FAA stall characteristic parameters. Subsequently, software authority was extended to lower airspeeds by developers, allowing 2.5 degrees of nose-down horizontal stabilizer trim based on single-sensor AOA input.

In 2017, 737 Chief Technical Pilot Mark Forkner emailed the FAA Seattle Aircraft Evaluation Group requesting permission to remove references to the MCAS from the Flight Crew Operations Manual, unaware of the extended speed range of MCAS authority. In November 2016, Forkner discovered the system activating aggressively at lower airspeeds in simulator testing, claiming he had unknowingly misled regulators. Yet, Boeing officials never briefed the Seattle Aircraft Evaluation Group on the expanded low-speed authority or corrected their earlier operational representations.
