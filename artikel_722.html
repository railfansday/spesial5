My Assistant Scammed Me. Then Did Far Worse to Demi Moore.

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Years after stealing from me, an identity thief went after the star. Now out of prison, she says she’s a brand new person.

In March of 2018, someone walked into a FedEx office in Beverly Hills to pick up a package addressed to Demi Moore.

This person wasn’t Moore’s assistant, though the badge said otherwise. He had never met the actress and had no legitimate reason to be collecting her mail. But he knew the tracking number, the original shipping address and exactly what was inside the package: Moore’s American Express Black Card.

Over the next three weeks, he’d charge nearly $170,000 to the card while Moore was in Idaho, unaware of what was happening back in Los Angeles. And the person who walked out of that FedEx office with her card was someone I knew.

David Matthew Read wasn’t what you would picture when you hear “mastermind criminal.” Blond-haired and blue-eyed with a dimple poking out of one cheek, he was disarmingly forthcoming, sweet and funny. We first met in 2011, when he was in his late 20s and I was in my early 40s. I needed help hanging curtains, and a friend introduced me to him as a friend from church. Soon, David was somewhere between my part-time assistant and bestie, helping me with everything from errands to booking travel to prepping for parties.

It was only once I’d given him access to everything — including my P.O. Box, where he picked up my mail for me — that the friend who’d introduced us told me that David had actually been in prison a few years earlier for committing bank fraud.

But, the friend assured me, David was so trustworthy now that the church let him use their credit card to buy décor for their office. And when I asked David about the fraud and prison sentence, he was so forthcoming, so lacking in excuses and so willing to admit he’d done something wrong that I immediately pushed his crime out of my head.

Still, a few months into our arrangement, I realized that as an underemployed freelance writer, I didn’t actually need an assistant. Also, something about David had begun to seem off; he’d claim to be able to do things he couldn’t, and I’d only realize this when he’d present the “finished” result — a website, an Ikea bookshelf, research — and it was so unfinished that I wouldn’t know what to even say.

When I let him know that I no longer had work for him, he was sweet and understanding; he drove off in his orange convertible Mitsubishi Eclipse, and I figured that was the end of that.

And it was — until a few months later, when I got a call from Citibank that I owed nearly $7,000 on a credit card bill. Positive that it was a mistake, I had the representative read off the card number.

Oh, but it was, she explained. It was an additional card on my account, issued in the name of David Read.

As she read off the list of charges, I fell into a shame spiral. How could I have been stupid enough to give a convicted criminal so much access to my life?

I don‘t remember exactly what David said when I first confronted him, just that he initially denied everything and then rather quickly confessed. He told me he’d only done it because the church needed him to buy certain things and, as a convicted felon, he couldn’t get a credit card, and so he’d intercepted my credit card bill one day when picking up my mail and ordered a card in his name under my account.

When I read out charges we both knew couldn’t have been made for even the loosest of churches — to, among other places, Good Microbrew, The Abbey, Mickey’s, Hooters, Malibu Family Wines, DMH Aesthetics and a dating website called Successful Match — he admitted that his motivation had nothing to do with the church. He’d just wanted a credit card and didn’t have another way to get one.

David didn’t cry or plead for forgiveness. He calmly explained that he’d never wanted me to be financially responsible, which is why he’d been paying the charges for months; he just needed some time to get some money together. In the end, the friend who’d introduced us paid off the credit card and told David to pay him back by making regular donations to their church. (David ended up leaving the church shortly after.)

And so David became a part of my past, the I-got-scammed-by-an-assistant story I eventually forgot about.

Until July of 2018, when the friend who’d introduced us sent me a link to an OK! magazine story that David had done something similar to Demi Moore.

A few months ago, mainly out of curiosity, I decided to see if I could find David. It took roughly four minutes: I logged onto a public records website, plopped in his name and emailed the four potential email addresses it provided: Are you the David Read that once worked for me?

A few minutes later, “Hi Anna please give me a call,” with a phone number appeared in my inbox.

A few minutes after that, my cell buzzed with a text: “Is this Anna you just emailed me.”

I was surprised by how easy it had been to track down a guy who’d spent 55 months in prison. But I was even more surprised by the name and photo that popped up on my iPhone alongside the text.

David Read was now Taylor Read Madison. The person I’d known as a man was now living as a woman.

Three days later, I sat across from Taylor at Liberation Café in Hollywood, splitting a breakfast burrito, Alanis Morissette playing in the background. The blond hair, blue eyes and dimple remained, but the hair was now long and tucked into a ponytail, the nails were painted, the dimple was decorated with a dash of blush and she was wearing a smattering of jewelry — including a diamond wedding ring.

Taylor felt both familiar and unfamiliar. As I watched her whip a hook out of her white purse and latch it onto the table before daintily placing the purse on it to keep it from sitting on the ground, I couldn’t help but notice that she seemed nervous.

And, of course, she had every reason to be. She also had every reason to not want to make her story public. So why was she here?

To tell this story, it helps to go back to the beginning.

Pennington Gap, Virginia, is the kind of place where you might expect a woman to receive a hefty settlement when her husband died of black lung.

And that was indeed what happened to Caroline Rhea, Taylor’s maternal grandmother. Because of the settlement and the fact that she was “good with credit cards” (a fact Taylor tells me without a hint of irony), Caroline could shower the grandchild she raised with clothing that she might not have otherwise been able to afford.

“My grandmother was a little bit over-caring,” Taylor says now. “I think she felt guilty that my mother wasn’t able to raise me, and so she overcompensated.” (Taylor Read Madison has given me permission to use her former name and pronouns when describing events that occurred before her transition.)

From an early age, David learned that “stuff” could make up for things like having a mother who couldn’t take care of you and a father who wasn’t around. Even though David and his grandmother lived in a single-wide trailer, “people thought I lived a completely different life,” Taylor explains. He helped perpetuate the delusion by getting off the bus early so the other kids didn’t see where he lived.

The fraud started innocently enough. When David was 19 and attending Mountain Empire Community College in Big Stone Gap, Virginia, he began dating a 50-something attorney named Sam (not his real name) and quickly moved into Sam’s three-story, six-bedroom house. After about a year, their relationship had cooled, and Sam actually moved a new boyfriend in. But the house was so chaotic and unstructured that instead of moving out, David just relocated to a different bedroom.

The chaos extended to Sam’s financial life. “Sam was terrible with money,” Taylor says. “He was paying off his credit cards with credit cards, just living well beyond his means. And he had me on all his cards.”

One day, it occurred to David that Sam probably wouldn’t even notice if David bought himself a car. And that’s how he ended up driving a 1995 Oldsmobile Aurora out to L.A., his pockets filled with roughly $6,000 that he’d accumulated slowly by making periodic ATM withdrawals from Sam’s account.

David came to L.A. ostensibly to meet a guy he’d been talking to online, but when he arrived and learned that the guy still lived with his ex-boyfriend, he didn’t care. He’d made it to L.A., the shiny place he’d always wanted to come to — a place where you could entirely reinvent yourself so long as you dressed the part.

“Why do you think God invented credit? For fabulous people like me.” Demi Moore (Jules) in St. Elmo’s Fire

Scamming Demi Moore was never part of the plan. It probably wouldn’t have ever happened if David hadn’t been doing a lot of meth and hanging out with a druggy crowd.

It all started when David met a guy named Ray in December 2017, and Ray told him that he had access to an account someone who’d worked at Federal Express had sold him. Ray explained the scheme: American Express sends all its cards via FedEx, and with access to the FedEx back end, you could log in and route the card to any location you wanted.

David had inadvertently stumbled upon the ideal project for a meth-using, tech-savvy guy with a history of credit card fraud: He got access to the account from Ray, downloaded what he found in the FedEx system and started cross-referencing names and addresses of cardholders using Google’s My Maps feature. Eventually, he was able to connect tracking numbers to people’s names, phone numbers, business names and addresses. (FedEx did not respond to repeated requests for comment.)

At first, he didn’t know why some names were marked “titanium.” Then he realized it was code for the coveted Black Card — also known as the Centurion card — an invitation-only program that allegedly requires a minimum annual spend of $500,000 and is said to include everything from private jet arrangements and exclusive restaurant reservations to VIP treatment at luxury hotels. “I freaked out a little,” Taylor recalls. “I remember thinking, ‘What can I get with this? Everything.’ ”

When David saw Demi Moore’s name on the spreadsheet with the word titanium next to it, he didn’t care about who it was. He just had an idea of how he could get his hands on her card. “Demi Moore wasn’t a target,” Taylor says. “She was a convenience.”

In the back end of the FedEx database, David redirected her card from her business manager at Nigro Karlin Segal Feldstein & Bolno in Century City to a FedEx store in Beverly Hills.

“At the time, all you needed to pick up a card was the tracking number, the name it was going to and the original shipping address,” Taylor says. For added insurance, David made himself a badge that said he was an assistant at Moore’s holding company, Pajama Party Productions. Once he knew the card had been sent, he parked his (fraudulently purchased) Mercedes E-Class in front of the FedEx store, walked in with his badge and announced he was there to pick up a package for Moore. The clerk handed it over.

Then David drove to a Century City parking lot and called a friend who had access to a black-market website. Within a few minutes (after sending the friend $8 through his Cash App), David had Moore’s Social Security number. He then called AmEx, claiming to be Moore, and provided the representative with the Social Security number to activate it.

Moore wasn‘t David‘s only victim. Taylor estimates that she stole between 25 and 30 other cards through the scheme and used them over a period of roughly 18 months to do everything from renting a Porsche 911 in Florida to eating caviar for the first time at Barton G. in West Hollywood to buying a Rolex.

(Taylor gave me a download of the names she found in the FedEx back end that were being sent Black Cards at that time, and while some of the names are recognizable — for example, the late billionaire owner of the Indianapolis Colts, Jim Irsay, and literary agent Esther Newberg — most are not.)

Out of all of David’s shopping sprees, the one he did on Moore’s card was definitely the most extensive. From March 8 through March 27 of 2018, he bought so many things that he can’t begin to remember them all, though he does recall that most of his shopping was done at Nordstrom, Saks Off 5th and Bloomingdale’s and that he bought Gucci shoes and Roberto Cavalli clothing. He also recalls making more quotidian purchases with Moore’s card at Rite Aid and Bed Bath & Beyond.

This wasn’t, Taylor says, a joyride meant to give a taste of the high life. Or at least it wasn’t only that. After acquiring a Birkin bag — which the Off 5th on La Cienega used to sell — “I’d carry it around for like three days just to feel bougie because I was like, ‘Oh my God, this is the life I want,’ ” she says. “Then I’d list it on Fashionphile because I needed to sell this stuff to have a place to stay and food and the basic necessities you need to survive.” She says she could expect to make $10,000 to $15,000 in cash for such an item.

David knew the scheme would eventually unravel. On April 5, 2018, he was stopping by the Extra Space Storage facility on Vine near the 101 — where he kept everything he bought before selling it — when two Secret Service agents followed him out and arrested him.

They’d gotten wind of his scheme because David had used his own Chase debit card to cover a balance on a Nordstrom purchase he’d made with Moore’s card. “I think I was trying to get caught,” Taylor says now. It was the Chase debit card, combined with the number of times David had called AmEx when the card would get turned down, that made AmEx catch on to what he’d been doing. (Moore, through her representative, declined to comment for this story.)

Within a few minutes of being arrested, David had told the agents everything. “I figured if I was just straight up about things, it would be a little less painful,” she says, adding, “One thing I can say about me is that if I’ve done something, I will cop to it.”

David had only been in L.A. County jail for a few days when he met someone who said he had a contact at OK! magazine. David called the contact — not, he says, because he wanted his name in the press but because “I was really over it all and felt bad. I also just wanted the money and wasn’t really thinking clearly.” (Taylor says the magazine paid her $500 for an interview.)

He spent nearly a year in pretrial detention — with a break where he was released and then arrested again — and ended up serving his sentence in FCI Phoenix from April 2019 through December 2022. He appealed for an early release — claiming that because he was HIV-positive, COVID exposure was dangerous — but was denied. David also claimed in his appeal that prison life was dangerous for someone who was nonbinary.

Gender identity wasn’t a new struggle; David had grown his hair long, done laser hair removal and started wearing women’s clothes in early adulthood but stopped after moving to L.A. In mid-2017, David began taking testosterone blockers and estradiol after learning they could be simply procured from a doctor. Even while occasionally wearing women’s clothes, David says he still “passed” as a man. “The hormones hadn’t really kicked in,” Taylor says now.

Despite what Taylor claimed in her appeal, no one in jail gave her a hard time for her gender identity. She says that the hormones had started working and her features had softened, but “I think mostly I was confusing people. They were just like, ‘I think they dropped you off at the wrong location.’ ” Does that mean, I asked, that her appeal wasn’t honest?

“There were real concerns [about COVID],” she says. “We were literally having outbreaks left and right, and they didn’t know what the fuck was going on.”

As for the abuse she supposedly feared she’d be subjected to, “my attorney wrote all that,” she says.

Once out and back in L.A., he got a list of resources from the Hollywood LGBT Center, which led her to an organization called the Trans Latina Coalition that was connected to a place called the Trans Wellness Center. It was after joining their reentry program that she began going by Taylor.

She also found a new career, working as a case manager at Trans Wellness for eight months before jumping to Access to Prevention, Advocacy, Intervention & Treatment [APAIT], another nonprofit that provides homeless services, where she works as an employment specialist.

But Taylor has bigger aspirations: She’s in the process of getting her bachelor’s degree in information technology and cybersecurity, with plans to pursue her master’s afterward. The reason for the degrees, she claims, is that American Express wants to hire her, but they told her they need to see that she’s “serious.” She says that AmEx would pay her “a substantial amount of money” to work in their fraud prevention department. American Express declined comment on this assertion.

Taylor also seems to be thriving in her personal life. In January 2024, she met an Ecuadorian man on Grindr, and they married in June of that year. Taylor, who quit meth in jail, quickly realized that her new husband was doing a lot of meth and told him she was serious about him but couldn’t be with him if he kept using after what she’d been through. According to Taylor, he quit cold turkey. (Taylor still drinks; during one of our Saturday afternoon Zoom interviews, she sipped whiskey with cherries.)

Still, to hear Taylor tell it, her life today is as innocent and pure as a brand-new Birkin. Whenever I question her about why I should believe anything she says when she has lied to me and so many other people so successfully so many times, she doesn’t do a hard sell.

“I don’t deserve it [for people to believe me],” she tells me at one point. “I mean, I can only show people, right?”

Perhaps the most surprising part of all is that after everything that happened between us, I would have thought I’d have a hard time sitting across from Taylor or having a series of Zooms with her. I’d imagined I’d be hiding my purse, blurring my background or worried for my safety. Instead, I felt the way I had the first time we met. I liked her.

When I tell her it doesn’t make sense to me that someone could spend more than 15 years committing fraud and then, without a spiritual epiphany, a 12-step program or some life-altering experience, become someone who works with the homeless and wants to end the kind of fraud she once perpetrated, she isn’t defensive. She just calmly attributes her change to therapy, transitioning and finally feeling comfortable with herself. “I think now I’m living authentically,” she says.

When I saw her photo in OK!, I thought I knew exactly where I fit in the story. I was one of the people Taylor had fooled before she got to Demi Moore. It hadn’t occurred to me that one day I’d want something from her again, or that what I’d want was her version of a story I’d spent years telling as mine.

I have no idea whether she’s reformed or not. Since watching You Can See Everything, I’ve been thinking a lot about how much likability plays into believing someone who has a history of dishonesty. The truth, so to speak, in this situation is that the same personality and candor that wooed me before seems to have worked all over again.
