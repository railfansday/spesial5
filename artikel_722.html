Trumper’s Remorse: Voters Look at the Last Two Years and Ask for a Redo

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

With a crushing Democratic rout in both the House and Senate, America seems to want to Replayola everything that's happened since 2024.

Even the most forgiving retailers tend to cap a return policy at 90 days. Show up after that, you’re out of luck. But voters across America on Tuesday took a look at what they purchased two Novembers ago and said: “How about 730 days, would you accept that? The merchandise is a little battered, but the tags are intact.”After months of political fighting both substantive and surreal, with debates over ideology and reflections on buttocks, American voters Tuesday delivered as strong a rebuff to a Republican president as has been delivered in four decades, turning the House of Representatives and Senate over to Democrats and essentially saying “you see if you can stop him.”Democrats won in swing states and they won in red states; they won in states where Trump had been ascendant and states where Republicans never gave up control. They won with moderates and they won with firebrands. They won, improbably, in rural counties all over the country.From Maine to Texas, Kansas to South Carolina — heck, as of this writing even Mississippi was in play — new Democratic Senators were springing up everywhere. They won so often cable news pundits ran out of water puns for their blue-wave metaphor. They won so much, they may have even gotten tired of winning.Democrats gained, as of this writing, a whopping 41 seats in the House, far more than the three needed to win control. But it was the Senate — where a flip was far from assured and in fact unlikely just weeks ago — where the most drama abided.The four-seat requirement was met. Then it became five. Then six. States where Democrats hadn’t won a Senate seat in 38 years (Texas) or in 94 years (Kansas). States where Trump two years ago won by double digits (Ohio) and states where Strom Thurmond presided for half a century (South Carolina).And even, it turned out, in a state where Democrats hadn’t beaten a Susan Collins in 30 years (Maine).As of this writing, Democrats had flipped seven Senate seats with an eye on an eighth in Alaska and a ninth in Mississippi, using every last vote of that 43-vote lead. They were also holding on to all their blue seats, including Michigan, called by CNN for Abdul El-Sayed at 2:30 am local time.There have been major Congressional shifts like his before, most notably in 1994 for Republicans and 2008 for Democrats. But both of those quakes occurred because of a galvanizing candidate in the winning party — Newt Gingrich in 1994 and Barack Obama in 2008. What was remarkable about this sweep is that it didn’t come from one central charismatic figure but a slew of grassroots personalities who understood their individual constituencies, state-by-state: James Talarico and Josh Turek and Annie Andrews and Adam Hamilton. (Also, speaking of Talarico and Hamilton, it’s nice Democrats finally figured out what the Republicans did decades ago: overtly Christian candidates play.)And, of course, the Trump remorse. Because there was a political figure who mobilized the electorate — just in the negative. How fittingly 2026 that the candidate who influenced voters most was the one people liked least, Donald Trump, with his policies on ICE and Iran, with his DOGE slashings and science evisceratings and health-care-benefit reducing and the fact that prices still seem to be going up while the only thing that seems to be coming down is the East Wing. “American people are hurting,” Van Jones said on CNN, boiling it to the quick, “and this dude’s out of touch.”Perhaps the best modern comp to all this is 1986. That Election Day, 40 years ago, Democrats scored big in the midterms — also in the second term of a fading Republican giant, netting eight seats during the presidency of Ronald Reagan.This election season has been one of the most entertaining, if also the most divisive, in recent memory as a showman president distrusted even by many of his own party paved the way to a series of colorful and ultimately losing showdowns.Books can be written about all the dynamics, but fights over Middle East policy of course roiled and at times threatened to break apart the Democratic party — right up until the final days with Haley Stevens’ shocking defection in Michigan. And yet somehow it all led to history — the first openly gay male senator in New Hampshire’s Chris Pappas, the first Black female governor in Georgia’s Keisha Lance Bottoms.Meanwhile, Republicans tried to look toward the future while grappling with the Donald Trump present, accepting his campaign appearances even as they kept him out of nearly all of their ads. (Democrats, on the other hand, were all too happy to tether their opponents to the president.)And through it all the president himself, getting distracted with boasts and random talk as he hit the hustings of a trail where he was only ambivalently wanted.The Democrats will face mountains ahead, not least thanks to the schisms in their own party; how will the Darializa Chevalier and Melat Kiros types in the House play with the mainstream types led by new speaker Hakeem Jeffries (the first Black Speaker in American history). And, in the Senate, how will Peggy Flanagan and Abdul El-Sayed meld with moderates like Turek and Hamilton, not to mention Majority Leader Chuck Schumer.The Democrats will also have to decide how much to lean into inquiries and investigations, as this is not what voters sent them to Washington for — it’s the economy, stupid, as James Carville’s whiteboard words continue to redound.But the bigger questions lie with Republicans, who now must decide in the minority how to oppose Democrats — how much to genuflect to a man they have spent much of the past decade paying fealty to, now newly irrelevant. Perhaps the person most confused today, strategically: JD Vance. What or whom exactly is he supposed to be defending, and how does he find a path to the 2028 primaries intact?

But all those questions loom in January. On this November day Democrats really only care about one: “Would you like that as cash or credit?”
