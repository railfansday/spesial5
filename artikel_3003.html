There's More Unapproved AI In Your Enterprise Than You're Counting

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

Nithya Nareshkumar is co-founder and President of DataBahn, a unified data and control plane for security, observability, and AI.

​There’s a question I’ve started asking every security leader I sit down with, and the answers have been humbling—for them, for me and maybe for you, too.

Which AI tools are your people using right now?

A few months ago, I put it to the head of security at a large enterprise. He counted on his fingers. Four. Two were approved, one was a pilot his team was running and the fourth, he said with a bit of a laugh, was the one he used himself. Then he looked at me and asked, “So what do you think is the real number?”

I didn’t have it. Nobody in that room did.

This scene is repeating itself more and more in different forms and with increasing regularity, which is the whole problem when you think about it. The leaders I meet aren’t careless people. They have asset inventories, procurement and policies stacked on policies. Every single one of those systems was built to see software that gets bought and installed. AI, by contrast, doesn’t get bought and installed. Somebody opens a browser tab, and off they go.

A January 2026 BlackFog survey of 2,000 workers at companies with more than 500 employees found that 49% of respondents said they’d adopted AI tools without their employer’s approval. Half. Additionally, 51% had connected one of those tools to a work system without IT knowing about it.

Put that next to IBM’s “2025 Cost of a Data Breach Report,” in which 63% of organizations said they have no AI governance policy. None.

Roughly half your workforce is using tools nobody approved, half of them have plugged those tools into your systems and most companies haven’t written down a single rule about any of it. That’s a hairball, and most of us are standing in the middle of it, unaware. Shadow AI is more dangerous than shadow IT ever was.

This thing starts at the top. Who feels the most pressure to move fast? Who’s got the least patience for a six-week procurement cycle and the most room to route around it without anybody asking? The C-suite.

I’m including myself in that. I went and looked at my own browser after a few meetings like this, and the list was longer than I would’ve guessed. There’s nothing scary on it, but it’s longer than I would’ve estimated.

In simple terms, everything you’d normally use to find software in an inventory is looking for the wrong thing. Asset management wants an install. Procurement wants a purchase order. License management is sitting there waiting for a contract to show up, and a browser session with a frontier model gives it nothing. No install, no PO and on the free tier, there’s not even a contract. The usage is completely real, the data walking out the door is completely real, and to every system you’ve ever built to track technology, it’s flat-out invisible.

This is why every governance conversation I’ve been part of stalls at the same spot. The team jumps straight to writing policy and then finds out you can’t enforce a software policy on AI because it’s a population it never imagined counting.

IBM puts a number on it. Breaches at organizations with high levels of shadow AI cost $670,000 more on average. Here’s the one I’d put in front of a board: 97% of the organizations that had an AI-related security incident said they were missing proper AI access controls. Almost every single time, these incidents happen where nobody’s watching.

I know it sounds backward to some people, but my advice is to not write the policy yet. Count first, then figure out what’s sensitive. Then write rules that fit what your people actually need to do their jobs.

The good news is you’re already sitting on most of the data. Your web proxy and DNS logs know which AI services your network is talking to and how often. Your identity provider logs every time somebody clicks “allow” to give a third-party app access to their work account, and those consent grants are about the most truthful record of shadow AI you’ll find anywhere. Your endpoint telemetry knows which browser extensions and desktop apps are installed. Your expense reports know who’s paying $20 a month out of pocket and filing it under “software.”

The good news is you don’t have to buy anything to do this. Somebody just has to decide it’s worth looking at.

Once you’ve counted, sort by what’s actually being sent. Somebody in marketing pasting a draft headline into a chatbot is one thing. A finance analyst uploading a spreadsheet is a very different thing. (That same BlackFog study found that 23% of workers had put company financial information into an AI tool.) Then, and only then, write the rules.

In my experience, rules written after you’ve counted come out shorter, and people actually follow them because they describe what’s already happening instead of what somebody imagined might happen.

I think about security leaders in these meetings a lot these days. They’re giving me honest answers out of an inventory that wasn’t built to contemplate the question I’m asking. You can fix that without a budget and without a policy.

Hit pause on the policies, and start with a count. I’d bet the number you find is bigger than four.​
