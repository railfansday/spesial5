Hybrids Are a Waypoint, Not the Destination

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

An earlier version of this article appeared in The Driven on 15 October 2026. This version has been revised and updated with the most recent registration data.

By Ray Wills, Raphael Wellmann, and Peter Newman

In January 2024, Toyota chairman Akio Toyoda said battery EVs would reach at most 30% market share, with the remainder going to hybrids, hydrogen, and combustion vehicles. Norway’s BEV share was already at about 89% that year. Norway alone cannot settle a prediction about the global market, but it shows that 30% is no inherent ceiling on BEV adoption.

PHEVs are conditional transition vehicles. They earn their environmental case through verified electric kilometres, not through the presence of a charging port.

Mature markets tell the more revealing story. Once battery-electric vehicles offer sufficient choice, charging confidence, and competitive economics, hybrids do not settle into a permanent share of the market. Hybrids may lead early, peak, and then retreat.

Norway. The year is 2015. Hybrid vehicles had gained material market share and plug-in hybrids were beginning to arrive in volume. In 2017, PHEVs accounted for about 18% of new registrations. BEV growth temporarily paused, while hybrids benefited from familiar factories, familiar supply chains, and familiar consumer expectation.

After another PHEV surge in 2020 and 2021, the direction changed. By October 2026, battery-electric vehicles accounted for 98.8% of Norway’s new passenger-car registrations. Petrol plug-in hybrids represented just 0.2%, and petrol full hybrids 0.3%. Across the first ten months of the year, BEVs held 98% of the market. Hybrids had not become a permanent destination: they had become marginal in new-car sales.

It would be easy to dismiss Norway as a special case: a small, affluent, and subsidy-supported market.

Singapore has a distinctive vehicle-ownership system, built around Certificates of Entitlement, high ownership costs, and tightly managed taxation. Yet its market also shows hybrids losing ground as BEVs expand. Conventional hybrids accounted for 28.3% of new registrations in October 2026, while BEVs held 59.6% and PHEVs 6.2%. The monthly figures fluctuate: hybrid share was higher than in August. The longer-term question is whether hybrids retain their earlier prominence as BEVs become mainstream—not whether their share falls every month.

Denmark provides another example. In October 2026, battery-electric vehicles accounted for 88.2% of all new passenger-car registrations and 97.4% of households’ new cars. The distinction matters: household purchases and the overall market do not electrify at precisely the same pace. But both now show BEVs becoming the dominant choice.

These three markets are not identical, and their policies are not interchangeable. But they show a repeating shape. In leading electrification markets, hybrid share has not settled into a stable plateau. It has risen, peaked and then declined.

For years, Norway was treated as the exception: small, rich, subsidy-heavy, and unrepresentative. But its basic market pattern is now appearing wherever electrification has advanced far enough.

Once the pattern is visible in several unrelated countries, the question becomes whether it is appearing more widely, including in markets still early in the transition.

Trailing-twelve-month registration data across the selected markets show not a world divided between “BEV countries” and “hybrid countries,” but markets at different stages of electrification and following different routes.

Some move relatively directly from combustion vehicles to BEVs. Others pass through a stronger hybrid phase, then a PHEV phase, before BEVs become dominant. In China, PHEVs and range-extended EVs have gained strength alongside BEVs, supported by rapid product cycles, intense domestic competition, expanding charging networks, regulatory settings, and strong domestic manufacturers.

This is not a claim that hybrids have already peaked everywhere. Across the European Union, BEVs accounted for 23.4% of new-car registrations in the first ten months of 2026, while hybrids remained a major part of the market. In Australia, the hybrid share is still close to its high, but in October 2026, BEVs overtook hybrids on a trailing-twelve-month basis for the first time (18.5% vs. 17.6%). The transition remains uneven across countries, buyer types, and vehicle segments.

The route differs. The starting point differs. The pace differs. The picture that comes out isn’t “some countries like BEVs, others like hybrids.” As BEVs meet more buyers’ needs at competitive cost, hybrids face increasing pressure on their share of new-car sales. Whether their absolute sales also fall depends on the size and growth of the market.

There is a practical implication for buyers. A hybrid purchased today in a market that has not yet reached its tipping point may face greater residual-value risk when it arrives on the used market. If new-car demand shifts rapidly towards BEVs while used supply reflects today’s hybrid-heavy sales, resale values may come under pressure.

Fuel prices, tax settings, used-BEV supply, charging access, and export markets will impact, but it is a risk buyers, fleets, and financiers should acknowledge.

If the pattern is visible in the data, why does public discussion so often present hybrids as a permanent pillar of the future vehicle market?

One reason is that the transition is uneven—the future is here, it’s just not well distributed yet. In many countries, people have only seen the rising half of the hybrid curve. The peak and decline have not yet arrived. It is easy to mistake “still growing” for “here to stay.”

Another is that manufacturers with decades of investment in combustion and hybrid platforms have commercial reasons to extend the life of those systems. That behaviour seems rational. Hybrid platforms preserve familiar engines, suppliers, dealer servicing, and factory investments.

But that commercial logic is not evidence that hybrid demand will remain durable once BEVs can do the same job more effectively. As with everything, the biggest cost may come not in going too fast, but in going too slow.

It is also important not to overstate the case against PHEVs. A PHEV that is routinely plugged in can cover much of its owner’s daily driving on electricity. The problem is that many of those PHEVs are not used that way.

European Commission data from 320,000 PHEVs first registered in 2021 found real-world CO₂ emissions averaging 3.5 times their official type-approval values. The Commission concluded that these vehicles were not being charged and driven electrically as frequently as assumed in laboratory testing.

Company vehicles are a particular problem. Petrol may be employer-paid, immediate, and simple to use, while home charging is paid by the employee, workplace charging is unavailable, and reimbursement is cumbersome. Under those conditions, the petrol engine becomes the default.

A PHEV is a bridge—but only if people use it to cross from the petrol side to the electricity side.

The registration data do not say that every hybrid buyer has made the wrong decision. Some households and fleets still need a transitional option. Some regional, towing, remote-area, and high-kilometre uses remain difficult for today’s BEV models and charging networks.

But those are specific use cases that are not representative of the mass market and thus not a reason to preserve combustion systems for everyone.

Conventional hybrids reduce petrol consumption, particularly in stop-start urban driving, but every kilometre still depends on fuel. PHEVs can replace a substantial share of petrol with electricity, but only if they are regularly charged, have enough real-world electric range, and do not unnecessarily run their engine.

BEVs eliminate routine petrol use and are the most energy-efficient option for the expanding share of households and fleets whose driving and charging conditions suit them.

The role of policy is not to pretend that all drivers are identical. It is to make electric driving easier: reliable charging at homes, apartments, workplaces, and regional routes; electricity reimbursement as straightforward as fuel reimbursement; and real-world reporting of petrol use and electric kilometres in PHEV fleets.

The market is not likely to move from combustion to BEV in exactly the same way everywhere. But as battery-electric vehicles become cheaper, more capable, and easier to charge, the addressable market for hybrids contracts.

Hybrids can help drivers transition from the combustion era into the battery electric era.

But that is all they are, a waypoint towards an electric future.

Ray Willis is a Perth-based energy futurist and director of Future Smart Strategies, and Adjunct Professor at The University of Western Australia, who writes on renewables, electrification, and the energy transition.
