Unlock Your Full Potential by Fixing These Common Triathlon Body Imbalances

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

Triathletes can face muscle imbalances. These off-season exercises help build proper strength to prepare for next season. (Photo: Brad Kaminski/Triathlete)

Triathlon training is all about repetition, with hours upon hours spent catching and pulling in the pool, turning the pedals on the bike, and turning the legs over on the run. With that repetition comes natural imbalance, with some muscle groups getting overworked while others get neglected (or even weaken with age).

Over time, these imbalances can impact performance directly or indirectly by contributing to injury development. That’s why taking time to identify and address these imbalances now will help keep you healthy in the long run.

By pinpointing areas of weakness or asymmetry early on, athletes can target the stabilizing muscles, improve biomechanical efficiency across all three disciplines, and significantly reduce the risk of overuse injuries that often arise from repetitive strain. Here’s how.

While swimming is generally thought of as the “safest” discipline in triathlon, issues can and do occur, often manifesting as “swimmer’s shoulder.” Swimmer’s shoulder is a bit of a catch-all term for pain in the front of the shoulder that encompasses several pathologies, including rotator cuff issues.

Typically, underlying laxity and instability exist. One factor that can contribute to shoulder problems is a strength imbalance between the shoulder internal and external rotators, with the internal rotators becoming disproportionately strong in comparison to the external rotators. This makes sense, as the shoulder internal rotators are active throughout the catch and pull, working to rotate the arm in and push against water.

But the external rotators still play an important role, as they help to stabilize the shoulder joint, keeping the ball of the joint centered in the socket to help prevent impingement and pain with overhead use and shoulder abnormalities. To decrease injury risk, external rotators should have about two-thirds of the strength of the internal rotators.

The most reliable, valid ways to check this involve specialized dynamometry equipment that measures force. But if you’re just looking to get a general idea, check how many reps of each of the below motions you can do until fatigue.

In this case, the test (lying prone, rotating the back of the arm up) can also be used as an effective strengthening exercise for external rotation. Start with higher reps, lower weights to learn the motion (3 sets of 10-12), then work up to higher weights, lower reps (4 sets of 6-8) to build more strength.

If this position causes pain or is too difficult at first, a side-lying shoulder external rotation is also a good option.

While the incidence of overuse injuries attributed to cycling is generally lower than those attributed to running, they can and do occur, most commonly impacting the knee and lower back. Although singling out contributing factors is difficult, the prolonged posture and muscle activations associated with cycling predispose athletes to overdeveloped quadriceps and an undeveloped posterior chain (glutes, hamstrings, back extensors) and lateral hip muscles.

Cyclists need strong quads – these are the predominant muscle used to power the pedal stroke. But the glutes and hamstrings do assist, so neglecting those leaves some power on the table. Plus, that injury-prone lower back needs adequate strength and endurance to withstand hours spent in a forward-flexed position.

Also, because muscle weakness and tightness often go hand-in-hand, related limitations in mobility throughout the posterior chain limit a cyclist’s ability to comfortably hold more aggressive aero positions. Those quads of steel will only help so much if you’re sitting up to ease back and glute discomfort by the end of a long ride!

The strength and endurance of the posterior chain (and also the lateral hip, which can play a role when it comes to knee pain on the bike) can be checked using a single-leg bridge hold.

The single-leg RDL is a good choice for eliciting both high force and stretch in the hamstrings and strengthening the gluteal muscles, back extensors, and lateral hips. Isolating each leg can also help with side-to-side imbalances.

When it comes to muscle strength and running, the glutes have been frequently implicated as the cause of (and solution to) many issues. But, while the glutes do play an important stability-related role in the run gait and deficits have been linked with injury, the true workhorse of the run gait – the calves – often gets overlooked and under-strengthened.

The soleus (one of the calf muscles) experiences forces of up to eight times body weight and produces up to 50% of forward propulsive power while running. Because the calves insert onto the heel via the Achilles tendon (which acts as a spring during running), the two are linked, with decreased calf strength seen in athletes with Achilles issues compared to healthy controls.

Strong calves can also help to protect against tibial bone stress injuries. Masters athletes lose calf strength (and Achilles tendon stiffness) disproportionately to other muscle groups with age, leading to both a reduction in propulsion and step length and an increased risk of lower leg soft tissue injuries such as strains and tendinopathies. So, when it comes to both performance and helping to guard against many common lower leg injuries, first check the powerhouse muscles below the knee!

So, while the different disciplines and repeated motions of triathlon might lend athletes to certain imbalances, not all is lost! Triathletes can make themselves stronger, more resilient, and faster with some awareness and work – so don’t neglect those details.
