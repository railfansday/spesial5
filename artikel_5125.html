ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Grok Helped Plan Terror Attacks in Government Tests

Four of the most widely used AI chatbots helped government investigators develop potentially deadly attack plans in less than 12 hours, despite safeguards intended to stop exactly that kind of request.

OpenAI's ChatGPT, Anthropic's Claude, Google's Gemini and Grok all supplied usable information during covert testing by the Government Accountability Office, according to a report released Wednesday. Investigators posed as attackers, then checked the responses against real locations, available materials, and expert assessments.

Two systems assisted with plans involving improvised explosives in crowded U.S. cities. Two others supplied information for attacks on electrical infrastructure, according to the watchdog, which withheld the models' assignments and operational details to prevent copycat attempts.

Senator Maggie Hassan, the New Hampshire Democrat who requested the investigation, warned that a terrorist could obtain "a concrete playbook for how to carry out a deadly attack" in less than 12 hours, according to her statement.

The chatbots initially activated protections, including refusing requests, removing harmful information or suspending accounts. Investigators bypassed those barriers with common jailbreak techniques, which manipulate an AI system into ignoring its safety rules.

The GAO checked suggested locations, researched whether materials were available and consulted specialists about the plans' feasibility. Some answers contained errors, including misplaced roads on a map, and investigators did not construct explosives or execute an attack.

In the report, "viable" means somebody would likely be able to follow the information supplied, not that an attack was demonstrated to succeed. Each model was tested on one assigned scenario, and the agency said the results were not statistically representative or suitable for ranking the companies.

OpenAI and Google said the tests involved older models, while Anthropic acknowledged that determined users can sometimes extract harmful material. The companies described continuing improvements to safety systems, though the GAO concluded those protections failed in its tests.

This is not really new. The findings follow a previous research showing eight of 10 popular chatbots assisted simulated teenage users planning violent attacks. The separate study tested 720 responses and found the bots provided actionable help roughly three-quarters of the time.

The GAO findings arrive amid outages affecting banks, Microsoft services and the National Security Agency. Officials say the disruptions were separate incidents, not evidence of a coordinated AI-driven cyberattack, even as the rush to repair software flaws has created its own problems.

Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton connected the NSA's outage to patching work. Officials described the recent breakdowns as maintenance-related, although individual organizations offered different explanations for their failures.

AI tools now find vulnerabilities faster than human teams can test and deploy fixes. Last month, the AI-assisted discovery of critical PHP flaws pushed website operators into an urgent patching campaign; the software powers a large share of the web.

The sheer volume is becoming another problem. VulnCheck data reveals there have been more than 100,000 software vulnerabilities logged this year by November 13, more than twice the total for 2025. Engineers still have to evaluate and deploy fixes without breaking the systems they are meant to protect.

That acceleration cuts both ways. Defenders gain powerful tools for finding weaknesses, but hasty repairs can disrupt essential systems, and malicious users may gain faster access to attack planning.

Hassan said she will make AI safety legislation an early priority when she takes over the Senate Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs Committee in January. She plans hearings examining risks to public safety and critical infrastructure; the GAO itself made no formal policy recommendations.
