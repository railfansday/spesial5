The Sadness of Borrowed Dogs

Updated November 13, 2026 | Reviewed by Monica Vilhauer Ph.D.

In Ron Howard’s new documentary Avedon, the internationally renowned photographer Richard Avedon recalls “the sadness of borrowed dogs.” Throughout his childhood, his family borrowed dogs from neighbors to complete the happy family photos.

"We were not satisfied with the way we were,” Avedon says, while reflecting on his sister, Louise, who suffered from mental illness. She was eventually admitted to a psychiatric hospital after becoming increasingly withdrawn from reality due to schizophrenia.

The borrowed dog is a small detail that reveals a larger family dynamic. John Bradshaw called this staged domestic harmony a shame-based family system: a family organized around hiding its defects. In my view, these polished photographs of the happy Avedon family are what shame looks like when an entire family maintains the pretense. The photos show the family as it wants to be seen.

Photographs are one way to keep up appearances. Another way, which is common in families affected by addiction or abuse, is living by three unwritten rules: don’t talk, don’t trust, and don’t feel. Those rules keep the outside image intact. I hear versions of these rules all the time from clients who grew up being told “children should be seen and not heard,” “don’t make waves,” or “keep the peace.”

Children who are exposed to these messages learn to make themselves small to avoid being singled out, perhaps by an alcoholic parent who flies into rages. In that house, shrinking is a survival skill the child learns: their needs don’t matter, and safety depends on staying unseen.

They carry it with them, hiding their defects, and it becomes armor. The armor goes with them when they leave home. Some go on to build successful lives despite this armor. But they do so at a cost: They never let themselves live an authentic life, because if the armor slipped for even a second, someone might see them. And that feels dangerous.

Avedon’s son says his father adored Louise but never visited her in the institute where she languished. Perhaps Avedon feared finding her somewhere like the hospital he photographed in 1963. That February, he spent time at the East Louisiana State Mental Hospital and made his Mental Institution series. This unflinching set of pictures was meant to support that year's mental health reform movement. When they appeared in Nothing Personal, his 1964 book with James Baldwin, the critic Robert Brustein accused Avedon of offering “a titillating peek into the obscene and ugly faces of the mad.”

Unlike Brustein, who saw exploitation, I see the borrowed-dog snapshot turned inside out. The family album hid Louise’s illness, and the asylum photographs refused to look away from it. Avedon’s career is proof of that refusal.

As a child, Avedon posed with a borrowed dog. As an adult, he spent his career photographing everything his family had taught him to hide, except the one person he couldn’t bear to visit.
