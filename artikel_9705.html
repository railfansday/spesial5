Crypto ETFs: Bringing Centralization Back to Crypto?

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

The whole point of cryptocurrencies is to offer a decentralized alternative to traditional financial infrastructure with middlemen. In this way, costs and risks, such as blockages and censorship, are reduced. And yet, here we are again, with digital assets reentering the traditional financial world in the form of Exchange-Traded Funds (ETFs).

An ETF is an investment vehicle whose shares trade on a stock exchange. In other words, it’s an asset that represents something else without direct exposure —in this case, cryptocurrencies. Spot products hold the underlying crypto, while futures products use contracts linked to its price.

So far, coins like Bitcoin (BTC), Ether (ETH), and Ripple (XRP) have their own ETF products available for investors. There’s a lot of money in there, too. According to CoinStats, crypto ETFs have over $171 billion of Assets Under Management (AUM).

Such a fact begs the question: is this really beneficial for the want-to-be decentralized crypto world, or is it just a blatant invasion from the old financial system? Let’s explore this topic.

The first thing we must consider is that crypto-related ETFs are not cryptocurrency themselves, in any way, shape, or form. Instead, with a spot ETF, the investor owns shares in a financial product that holds the underlying asset.

That means there’s a company (or another middleman) behind this product, handling all the real coins on your behalf —but you’ll never see the real coins themselves. Future-based products don’t even have third-party coins, and only obtain exposure through financial contracts.

Investing in a crypto ETF isn’t the same as “owning” crypto. You’re basically buying a ticket that represents a certain amount of gold, but it’s not, and it’ll never be, the gold itself. The value of that ticket is completely out of your control, and the middleman who sells these tickets takes a nice cut for handling them. If we think about it in simple terms, that’s it, that’s a crypto ETF.

Well, put that way, it sounds almost like a scam—though, of course, it isn't. There are many people, and especially institutions, that cannot or don’t wish to manage their own "gold" (their own cryptocurrencies). This investment vehicle is a service that offers them greater security and convenience, allowing them to gain some exposure without the major risks. After all, self-custody has its perils, too.

Self-custody requires work and responsibility, while many investors are more familiar with the brokerage environment. They often rely on regulated middlemen to handle sensitive financial transactions, and they’re comfortable within this system. Big companies, especially, aren’t suddenly learning how to properly set up and store a cold wallet with millions of dollars inside. Instead, they’re hiring the services or buying the products of experts in the matter, if they want to add crypto to their portfolios.

Therefore, trust is something ETFs can bring to the crypto world. And regulated trust is what institutions are usually looking for. Institutional participation can bring more liquidity (always a good thing) and connect crypto markets with traditional finance. The scale of recent inflows shows that these products have become an important bridge between the two worlds. As described by CoinStats, there was an inflow of over $5.2 billion to crypto ETFs in August alone.

Regulated investment products like ETFs introduce disclosure, reporting, and oversight requirements that many crypto platforms have historically lacked, so that’s why institutions may prefer them over self-custody. Access can be easier too, since you can buy through a traditional brokerage account. Someone else is responsible for every difficult part, and you just handle your investment. It’s fast and not cheap, but convenient.

Convenience is not synonymous with security—not by a long shot. Exchanges can be hacked or go bankrupt. The same can happen to other custodians, such as the firms behind ETFs. But beyond that lies the risk of the crypto industry’s sudden centralization. As we’ve mentioned above, crypto ETFs have over $171 billion AUM. That’s barely 6.5% of the whole cryptocurrency market cap… for now. We need to check the potential rise of these figures, too.

Since their inception in 2024, that same percentage has been growing. As we can see from CMC data, the AUM as a percentage of market cap started at 2.6% in 2024. Then, it was 5.8% in 2025, and now here we are. If they keep up like this, their average growth rate would be around 1.9% per year.

Does this mean ETFs and the firms behind them will take over most of the crypto industry in some years? It’s highly unlikely. At this rate, they’d need a stagnant crypto market capitalization and at least 23 years to reach 51%. However, it’s still concerning that billions in crypto arepooling towards centralized institutions, which could give them substantial power over decentralized crypto networks.

Especially in Proof-of-Stake (PoS) networks like Ethereum or Cardano, “validators” stake (lock) a certain amount to have the right to approve/reject transactions, partake in governance voting, and receive rewards. The more they stake, the more power they have. In a grim scenario, if an ETF brand decides to stake its billions, it could potentially manipulate entire networks. Smaller networks, with fewer community members and nodes, are the most vulnerable.

Centralization around the rules of a single country may also be possible, given that ETFs are products regulated according to their region. Currently, according to ETFLlama, the vast majority (144) are domiciled in Switzerland, followed by the US (76) and the EU (59). However, measured by Assets Under Management (AUM), the leaders are the US ($114.7 billion), Switzerland ($5.53 billion), and the UK ($5.36 billion).

A current reality is that ETFs have to comply with their country's laws, and that could mean censorship for certain users of PoS networks. For instance, for those listed by OFAC in the US. The fact is that the presence of big, powerful players always entails risks, and cryptocurrencies were created precisely to avoid those risks.

Crypto ETFs show how far digital assets have moved into traditional finance. They make access easier and attract institutional money, but every layer of intermediation can also concentrate control. That matters because decentralization is more than a technical feature.

It’s about keeping control distributed, reducing dependence on institutions, and preserving the ability to transact without asking a financial intermediary for permission. Obyte, for instance, takes this idea further by using a DAG-based network without miners or “validators”. There are no block producers deciding which transactions enter the ledger, creating a different model for distributing control across the network.

Now, ETFs can coexist with decentralized cryptocurrencies, and they can bring useful access to people who prefer traditional financial products. Yet the more crypto moves through centralized gateways, the more important its underlying decentralized infrastructure becomes. For projects like Obyte, maintaining that decentralization is part of the point itself. It keeps the original idea of crypto alive: giving users more direct control over their digital assets and transactions.

So, the ideal approach is to strike a balance. We can perfectly well coexist with the traditional financial system and benefit mutually, while remembering why cryptocurrencies exist in the first place and choosing those that are best for the job. After all, self-custody and direct, disintermediated control are the only things that grant us freedom and ownership.
