The entrepreneurship scorecard is broken. We’re measuring the wrong things.

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Pratham Mittal is the founder of Tetr College of Business. The student-venture figures in this piece come from Tetr’s own cohorts.

Business schools have become remarkably good at telling stories about entrepreneurial success. We count companies launched, capital raised, revenue generated and founders who go on to build something meaningful. These numbers make for compelling headlines and impressive institutional scorecards. But they leave out a more consequential question: what happens when the business doesn’t work?

At Tetr College of Business, we have had a front-row seat to both sides of this experience. Our students have built businesses, taken products to market, acquired customers, managed costs and, in some cases, discovered that their original assumptions did not hold up. When we examined the outcomes across our cohorts, what stood out was not simply how much revenue students had generated, but the considerable variation in what they had built, what they had earned and what they had lost.

That distinction matters because entrepreneurship is rarely a linear progression from idea to success. It is an exercise in navigating uncertainty, making decisions with incomplete information and constantly reconciling ambition with the realities of the market. If we are serious about preparing people to build businesses, we must be equally interested in understanding the ventures that struggle as we are in celebrating those that succeed.

Across 59 student-built drop shipping businesses, our cohorts generated more than $172,000 in reported revenue, with an overall reported profit margin of 21.86%. At first glance, these are encouraging numbers. But the individual businesses reveal a more nuanced picture of what it takes to make an idea commercially viable.

The scale of these ventures varied considerably, as did the economics behind them. Bike Arena, a student-led venture dealing in new, demo and pre-owned performance bicycles, emerged as the largest by revenue, recording $102,361 and a profit margin of 21.98%. Tafflo, which focused on technology accessories, generated $18,008 in revenue and a 63.64% profit margin.

Smaller ventures also demonstrated different levels of commercial performance. Juvak, a gaming accessories brand, recorded $2,396 in revenue at a profit margin of 53.21%. Claycia, which offers a curated range of bags, from travel bags to everyday totes, generated $5,342 in revenue, with a 12.47% margin.

The contrast is instructive. A business generating six figures in revenue is not necessarily more economically sound than one generating a few thousand dollars. Revenue tells us whether customers are buying; profitability reveals how much the business retains after accounting for costs. Neither, however, tells us on its own whether the business can sustain itself over time.

Our direct-to-consumer ventures offered another illustration. In the Class of 2028, 21 student-founded brands generated $186,862 in reported revenue, with an average reported profit margin of 30.62%. Ikrara, a silverware and jewellery brand, contributed $120,210 in revenue, with a 16.99% margin. Knotcultured, a premium streetwear venture, generated $11,460 but reported a considerably higher margin of 61.78%.

One demonstrated the ability to generate demand at scale; the other retained a greater proportion of its sales as profit. Both are meaningful outcomes, but they point to different aspects of building a business. Understanding that distinction is as important as learning how to generate the first sale.

The picture becomes more revealing when we look beyond the stronger performers.

Elantherm, a venture developing wearable period-relief belts with heat and massage functions, generated $1,906 in revenue but recorded a loss of $900. Welz Company, which sold smart rings, generated $2,491 in revenue and recorded a loss of $369 in the team-level records. Several other ventures in a subsequent cohort reported no revenue in the available reporting period. One team pivoted at the last minute, leaving insufficient information to assess its eventual commercial outcome.

It would be easy to leave these businesses out of the story. But doing so would give us an incomplete understanding of what students actually experienced.

A product can be developed, a website launched and a marketing campaign executed, yet the economics may still not work. Sales do not automatically translate into profit. A product that appears promising to its creators may struggle to find customers willing to pay for it. And even when demand exists, the cost of acquiring and serving those customers can make the business difficult to sustain.

The more important question is what founders do with this information. Do they revisit their pricing? Rethink their distribution strategy? Reconsider the customer they are trying to reach? Or recognise that the original idea needs to change altogether?

We should not assume that every loss has the same explanation. But learning to interrogate the numbers rather than explain them away is an essential part of building a business. The ability to recognise when an assumption is wrong, and the judgement to change course, can be as consequential as the conviction to pursue an idea in the first place.

Our Kickstarter projects offered another perspective on this process. Seventeen student teams launched live campaigns, collectively raising $79,532 from more than 1,100 backers, against a combined funding target of $75,000.

Some campaigns significantly exceeded expectations. Taowist, a glow-in-the-dark playing-card product, raised $12,032 against its target, reaching 1,475% of its goal. SlidePass, a smart passport wallet, raised $7,849, reaching 430%. Others fell short. ClickBuddy reached 67% of its funding target, while Brett reached 80%.

The distinction matters because Kickstarter operates on an all-or-nothing funding model: campaigns that do not meet their goals do not receive the pledged funds. For these students, the exercise was not simply about presenting an idea convincingly. It was about discovering whether people who had no obligation to support them were willing to put their money behind it.

That is a very different experience from receiving feedback on a business plan in a classroom. The market does not respond to the amount of effort invested in an idea or the elegance of its presentation. It responds to whether a product solves a problem, whether the price makes sense and whether the value is compelling enough for someone to pay for it.

This is where entrepreneurial education needs to evolve. Students should not only learn how to identify opportunities; they should have the opportunity to test their assumptions against actual customer behaviour, understand the consequences and make decisions based on what the market tells them.

There is a larger implication here for business education. If we evaluate entrepreneurial programmes primarily through the number of ventures launched, revenue generated or funding raised, we risk presenting an incomplete picture of what it means to build a business. We may also create an incentive to celebrate outcomes without examining the decisions that produced them.

The business world rarely offers the certainty of a well-structured case study. Markets shift, customer preferences change, costs rise and strategies that look convincing on paper encounter realities their founders did not anticipate. The ability to navigate these moments, interpret what is happening and make decisions with incomplete information is what separates an interesting idea from an enduring business.

Business schools must reflect that reality in how they teach and measure entrepreneurship. Alongside revenue and funding, we should pay attention to profitability, customer acquisition economics, capital efficiency, product-market fit and the ability to recognise when a business model needs to change. Equally, we must be willing to examine the ventures that did not work, rather than allowing their stories to disappear from the institutional record.

And this is an evolving story. A new wave of student ventures is already taking shape at Tetr College, with teams building SaaS businesses in the US, launching Kickstarter campaigns in China and developing direct-to-consumer brands in India. These ventures will bring a different set of ideas, markets and challenges to the table. Their outcomes are yet to be seen, but that is precisely the point: entrepreneurship is an ongoing process of testing, learning and building, not a collection of success stories captured at a single moment.

I do not believe every student should graduate with a successful company. That would be an unrealistic expectation and, more importantly, the wrong measure of what an education should achieve. The objective is not to turn everyone into a founder, but to ensure that every student develops the ability to recognise opportunities, make decisions with incomplete information and adapt when circumstances change. The discipline to follow the numbers, the confidence to act on evidence and the willingness to question one’s own assumptions are skills that extend well beyond starting a business.

The next generation will enter a world where uncertainty is the norm. A founder whose venture does not work may find themselves building a career within a startup or a large technology company. The ability to learn, unlearn and relearn – to make sense of unfamiliar situations, solve problems and find a way forward – is what will allow them to navigate that transition. These capabilities matter just as much to someone entering the workforce as they do to someone building a company. They make people more adaptable, resourceful and self-reliant, less dependent on any one role, organisation or economic cycle to define their next step.

Ultimately, entrepreneurship cannot be taught by celebrating only the businesses that make it. We must also examine those that struggle, understand where the assumptions fell short and learn what could be done differently. The real measure of entrepreneurial education is not simply how many businesses students build, but whether they leave better equipped to build, adapt and find their footing in an uncertain world.
