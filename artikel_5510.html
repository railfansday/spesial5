Opinion: If the AI bubble bursts, it could create a serious problem for health care

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

Adler is chief scientific officer at the PATHORA Institute of Pathology & Tissue Medicine.

The Oct. 30 sell-off on Wall Street ignited doubts about whether the huge investment in artificial intelligence will earn the returns investors expect. Those concerns have persisted, with even Anthropic’s initial public offering on Nov. 19 failing to halt the broader tech sell-off.

If the AI bubble bursts (assuming it is a bubble) and takes some medical AI companies down with it, health care will face a serious problem. A health AI company’s market value and the medical value of its technology are two different things. If funding runs out because investors decide to walk away, hospitals could lose the chance to complete evaluations of potentially useful medical tools. Giving up on medical AI would be a financial decision — but it may look like a clinical one. The financial markets will decide which medical AI technologies survive before hospitals have had the opportunity to establish whether these technologies benefit patients.

If investment in medical AI technologies experiences a sharp fall, companies will lose funding, forcing them to reduce their activities or stop supporting their technologies completely. The impact of such an event could be felt across the entire health care system: Companies are developing AI tools ranging from interpreting scans to identifying patients at risk of developing medical complications due to treatments. Clearly, some of these tools will eventually prove useful in clinical settings, while others will not. The point is that neither an impressive AI tool nor a falling stock price tells us which AI tool deserves a place within the health care system.

To illustrate this point, let’s take cancer clinical trials as an example. In one study, researchers from the University of Pennsylvania and Emory University examined electronic medical records using AI systems to identify patients who may meet the eligibility criteria for inclusion in clinical trials. Determining eligibility involves a demanding manual screening process examining things like tumor characteristics, previous treatments, and lab results.

Interestingly, the findings revealed that using the AI system improved accuracy of assessing enrollment eligibility for the cancer clinical trials, but it didn’t reduce screening time significantly. It’s not clear whether such improvements would lead to an increase in patient enrollment. In another study, researchers in Australia from the Victorian Cancer Registry at Cancer Council Victoria and Monash Health utilized an AI system to analyze pathology reports with the goal of identifying patients eligible for enrollment in cancer clinical trials. The AI system was able to successfully identify more eligible patients when compared to standard screening procedures, but ultimately utilizing the AI system didn’t result in increased patient enrollment into the clinical trials. The study didn’t establish why, which brings to focus that screening improvements doesn’t necessarily lead to higher clinical trial participation.

Taken together, these two studies illustrate the potential utility of AI systems in health care. They also make clear that improvements are needed before such AI systems become an integral part of health care system.

Based on my experience in senior leadership in oncology drug development, I think that medical AI systems should be developed and evaluated based on the general framework that we currently apply in the research and development of new innovative cancer drugs. What I mean by that is when it comes to the AI systems promising early results are important, but it is critical not to celebrate a success too early. Ultimately the value of the AI system depends on whether it eventually benefits the patient. We need to determine whether using a particular medical AI leads to better clinical decisions, better patient identification, reduction of unnecessary work, or improved access to treatment.

If the AI bubble bursts, these success metrics could become more difficult to achieve. Companies developing medical AI systems may lose their funding, downsize, or even withdraw support for their products. Worst of all, they could close before hospitals have finished assessing their AI systems.

Clinicians could, of course, return to doing their work as they did before the introduction of the medical AI system. But that would mean the health care system loses the opportunity (and valuable data) to determine whether such medical AI tools could have offered meaningful improvements and patient impact.

The clear answer is not to try and save each struggling AI company, but to make sure that hospitals and health care system are able to complete their assessments of medical AI systems even if a given company reduces its services or even goes out of business.

Before adopting such medical AI systems, hospitals should establish clear success criteria and build systems in place to track, store, and ensure that the research data generated about the medical AI systems are available for independent evaluation even if the AI company supporting such a system no longer exists. Such plans would make sure that any financial difficulties that may affect a relevant AI company would not prevent the hospital or the health care system from determining whether a given medical AI system could benefit patients.

David Adler is chief scientific officer at the PATHORA Institute of Pathology & Tissue Medicine, with more than 15 years of experience in oncology research and drug development.
