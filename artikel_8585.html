Extreme Couponing Still Works – Count Your Hours Before You Stockpile

Extreme couponing can still shrink a grocery bill when you stack a sale, a manufacturer coupon, a store coupon, and a rebate on one item, within each store’s coupon policy. Before you build a stockpile, divide one week’s savings on things you’d buy anyway by the hours you spent, and keep only the steps worth your time.

Manufacturers handed out 34 billion coupons in 2025, down from 53.1 billion the year before, according to Inmar Intelligence’s 2026 Promotion Industry Analysis. Shoppers redeemed 0.81 billion that year, fewer than 1 for every 40 handed out.

The method shown on TLC’s Extreme Couponing still works on that smaller supply. It runs into store coupon limits, though, and it takes hours that the savings have to cover.

Store policies now cap how many copies of one coupon you can use, and how much one coupon can take off. Walmart once paid a coupon’s full value even when it was worth more than the item. In September 2023 it stopped paying that difference and capped each household at 4 identical coupons a day, Coupons in the News reported. Walmart’s coupon policy also says it won’t double or triple a coupon, a practice in which the store adds its own money on top of the coupon’s value.

Target’s coupon policy sets the same daily cap, 4 identical coupons per household, and pays nothing back when a coupon is worth more than the item. Kroger’s coupon policy allows 3 uses of the same coupon per household each day.

Fewer of the coupons shoppers use come on paper, too. In 2025, the coupon inserts in Sunday newspapers made up 2.3% of the coupons shoppers redeemed, and digital offers loaded to a store account made up 53.9%, according to Inmar’s comparison of paper and digital coupons. Digital offers come with limits of their own. Under Target’s terms for its digital coupons, a digital manufacturer coupon works once per account and won’t combine with a paper manufacturer coupon on the same item.

Stacking is where the savings come from. A store coupon comes from the store and a manufacturer coupon from the brand. Target lets you use one manufacturer coupon on an item along with two of its own offers, one for the item and one for its category.

For the rebate, sign up with a rebate app, which pays cash back on specific products after you buy them. With Ibotta, you add the offers in its app before you shop, then submit your receipt or use a store loyalty account you’ve linked to it at checkout. Read each offer’s terms before you count on it. A store’s own sale doesn’t count against an Ibotta offer, but at Walmart and Dollar General, Ibotta won’t pay cash back on an item that also took a manufacturer coupon, paper or digital.

Say an item is on sale at Target for $10. A $2 Target coupon and a $2 manufacturer coupon bring it to $6 at the register, and $1 back from a rebate app makes it $5.

The savings total on the receipt counts every discount, even on things you’d never have bought, and it leaves out what the haul costs you in other ways.

Time is the biggest of those costs, since matching coupons to the ads, sorting them, and shopping several stores repeat every week. A stockpile also takes space you could use for something else, and food that expires before you eat it saves nothing, so keep no more of anything than you’ll use before its date.

Manufacturer coupons also steer you toward the brands that issue them. Take a name-brand cereal at $4.29 and a same-size store brand at $2.49. A $1 coupon brings the name brand to $3.29, still 80 cents more than the store brand.

To see what your hours earn, count only the savings on things you’d have bought anyway, then divide by the time it took. Take a household that tries two approaches, a week each.

Couponing only for what’s on the list takes 1 hour a week, spent checking the weekly ad, loading the store app’s digital coupons, adding rebate offers for items on the list, and submitting the receipt. It saves $21, so it pays $21 an hour.

Extreme couponing takes 8 hours, the same hour plus 3 hours collecting and matching coupons, 1 hour sorting them, and 3 hours shopping three stores. The register shows $100 saved, but $30 of that came on things the household wouldn’t otherwise buy, so the real savings are $70, or $8.75 an hour.

The 7 extra hours added $49, or $7 an hour. Weigh that $7 against what an hour of your work takes home, since money you save isn’t taxed.

Time one week of your own before you build a stockpile, and drop any step that saves less per hour than your work takes home.

Once you’ve timed your week, compare the best apps to save on groceries for the store and rebate apps that cover your list.
