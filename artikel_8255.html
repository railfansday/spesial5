Class 4 Terrain, Cozy Huts, Cold Beer: The Northern Alps Are Japan’s Best-Kept Secret

Author and photographer Andy Cochrane spent months crafting this route from home in Bend, Oregon, consulting with multiple Japanese mountain guides, before flying to Tokyo to bring it to life. Along with four others, he ran this 100-mile technical traverse across the Northern Alps in a week, staying at mountain huts and soaking in Japanese hospitality along the way.

It’s not often that a local guide recommends helmets for a hut-to-hut trail running trip, but it’s always a sign of a good adventure if you ask me.

A couple of months before flying across the Pacific to Japan, I was on a mid-afternoon Zoom call—my third such conversation of the week—when my jaw hit the floor. I was meeting virtually with guides in the Northern Alps to learn more about the trails and huts of the range, hoping to suss out if a line I had plotted on a map was actually a feasible endeavor to run.

At the time, I wasn’t sure if anyone had run the route before us. There certainly weren’t any blogs, articles, or posts I could find about it. Hut owners on the trip later confirmed my suspicion that our 100-mile, seven-day itinerary was pretty far out of the box. Connecting the outdoor hub of Kamikochi at the southern end of the range to the Sea of Japan in the north along a single ridge line was an unheard of idea to everyone we spoke with. That made it a lot more exciting—and far from certain it would work out. If it did, however, we would get to experience all that the Northern Alps had to offer in a single push.

Because the route required a mix of trail running, boulder hopping, ladder climbing, and Class 4 scrambling, our local guide Tatsuro was adamant that we bring helmets, even if their use wouldn’t be required every day. TL;DR: he was right.

Before I get into specifics of the route and experience, let’s set the scene. The Northern Alps, or Hida Mountains, are Japan’s most primal terrain. Although lesser-known than the mountain ranges of Europe, South America, or mainland Asia, the Northern Alps are just as technical and challenging, if not more so. The range is a jagged, granite spine that defies the country’s reputation for manicured serenity. Trails in the Hida are riddled with boulders, ladders, roots, scrambling, and thick brush. Located just a few dozen miles off the Sea of Japan, the weather is incredibly fickle in the Hida, too.

Geographically, the Northern Alps lie just a few hours drive west of Tokyo on the main island and stretch across Nagano, Toyama, Gifu, and Niigata prefectures. Most of the range is inside Chubu-Sangaku National Park, which protects around 670 square miles from being absorbed by the urban centers that surround it. The Hida are widely regarded as Japan’s premier alpine terrain and feature the country’s most developed hut system, Yamagoya. These staffed huts offer hot meals, sleeping mats, water, snacks, and beer, allowing for multi-day runs and traverses with lightweight packs sans camping gear—perfect for a trip like ours.

Three years ago I ran across the Southern Alps on an ancient postal route called Nakasendo. While the Southern and Northern Alps offer remarkably different terrain and technicality, my first trip taught me much about Japanese culture and logistics that was helpful while preparing for my return.

Perhaps my biggest takeaway from that initial experience was that the language barrier is a major challenge—especially in rural places, where many people don’t speak a lick of English. While apps like Google Translate can help to order a quick meal or get assistance with navigation in a pinch, planning a trip and booking mountain huts requires someone not only fluent in Japanese, but who also understands the mountain culture. For us, that liaison was Kota.

I was connected with Kota at Japan Adventures Co. through a friend, and I honestly can’t say enough good things about him. He is an incredibly organized planner, a great communicator, and an avid hiker and trail runner. He even helped negotiate our stay with a few huts that didn’t believe our route was possible, ensuring the entire trip ran smoothly. Kota and his team lead a variety of hiking, food, and cultural trips around Japan, and are currently rolling out new trail running itineraries in the Northern Alps and elsewhere.

With help from Kota and advice from guides like Tatsuro and Paul from Nagano Adventures, I was able to refine our route to stay at unique huts, run the best trails, and find a balance between challenging and doable for each day. It’s important to note that mountain huts in Japan are warm and welcoming, but they run on rigid schedules. Breakfast is typically at 5 a.m. and dinner is around 5 p.m. If you don’t check in by 4 p.m., they will notify search and rescue, so it’s important to start early and keep an eye on your pace throughout the day.

My goal was to start in Kamikochi and end at the Seas of Japan, creating a single linkup by connecting a number of popular two- and three-day hiking routes—the Panorama Ginza Route, Mount Yari, Ura Ginza Panorama Route, the ridgeline from Mount Eboshi and Funakubo-dake, the Hachimine Kiretto, the Kaerazu no Ken (ridge of no return), and Tsugami Shindo. It’s a mouthful to say, but when drawn on a map, the routes piece together nicely, forming a (mostly) straight line running from north to south.

Getting to and from the Northern Alps isn’t terribly difficult. After landing in Tokyo (either HND or NRT), a bullet train, or Shinkansen, will take you to Matsumoto in just a few hours. From there you can take a smaller train, bus, or taxi into the mountains to begin your adventure. Returning to Tokyo is just as easy by hopping on a bullet train in a coastal city like Toyama. There are plenty of stations to choose from and you usually can find seats just a day or two in advance.

There are a few ways to shuttle your luggage from the start to the finish, but I recommend Yamato Transport. Other options include dropping bags at the post office, the front desk if you’re staying in a hotel, or even convenience stores like Lawson’s or 7-Eleven. Though this might seem risky at face value, it worked flawlessly for us. We walked into a convenience store around 10 p.m., spent a meager ten minutes filling out the paperwork, and walked out. Our bags arrived at our final hotel just after we arrived seven days later.

Despite plenty of challenges, the trip ran as smoothly as I could have imagined. We dealt with heavy rain, 50 mph winds, and thick mist for our first few days, which forced us to slow down on the scrambling sections, layer up to stay warm, and take plenty of breaks in huts along the way. Some days had tons of vert (often more than 1,000 feet per mile) and rocky trails that made running difficult, so we embraced the shuffle. On day four, when covering our most remote section, we didn’t see a single other person. Fortunately the trails are well marked, even where they are less frequented and overgrown.

The crux of the route, the Kaerazu no Ken, is a long and serpentine downclimb on Class 4 granite slabs, offering a chain for protection in some areas while others did not. This was one of the dozen or so sections that having a helmet was a welcomed addition to our kits. For those comfortable with alpine climbing, this will feel like a fun change of pace. For those afraid of exposure, it’ll be challenging. The huts themselves vary considerably from small ridgetop outposts like Funakubo to much larger and busier outfits like Hakuba Sanso. Regardless of size, every hut owner we met was incredibly kind. Japanese hospitality is something they take very seriously.

Overall, our experience in the Hida was more than we could have asked for. Steep and technical trails, unique and cozy huts, fickle yet fruitful weather, and no other Americans or Europeans along the way. It’s an undiscovered gem for anyone outside Japan. With the help of Kota, Tatsuro, and others, I’m already planning a return trip next year.

One of the lightest climbing helmets on the market, the Vapor was perfect for our trip. We frequently had sections of running between scrambles and wouldn’t leave it on because it breathes so well. The Vapor fits over a hat and under a rain jacket hood, making it great for wet days, too.

The latest from Garmin is designed for routes like ours with a solar-powered display that ran the full week on a single charge. The maps were critical on wet days when I didn’t want to use my phone, and the flashlight was perfect for early mornings trying to not wake up other guests.

The perfect size pack for a trip like ours, the Adventure was able to carry everything we needed—spare clothes, first aid, snacks, camera, and ditty—with ease, plus poles, flasks, and a phone in the external pockets. It can be adjusted on the fly to reduce bounce and chafing.

On a week-long trip you end up eating a ton of snacks—gummies, chips, gels, and whatever you can buy at the huts—but nothing hit home like GU stroops. Whether it was with morning tea, on the top of a big climb, or preventing a bonk, stroops played a big role in our success.

With major improvements from the previous version, the Speedgoat 7 was the perfect shoe for this trip, offering plenty of grip on technical scrambles and ladders, a comfortable and cushy ride for the long days, and a lay-flat tongue that kept most rocks and dirt out.

The perfect mid-layer for cool mornings or windy days, the Dawner breathes well and is quite comfortable next to skin. The exterior is stretchy and durable, which is great for running through brush and rubbing against rocks— an inevitability in terrain like the Northern Alps.
