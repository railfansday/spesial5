Should the Oscars Keep Nominating Diane Warren?

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

As a third year in a row winning at the Hollywood Music in Media Awards primes songwriter Diane Warren for her 18th Best Original Song Oscar nomination, IndieWire Editor at Large Anne Thompson and Awards Editor Marcus Jones debate whether or not that recognition is still warranted.

Anne Thompson: For Hollywood outsiders, there’s an unsolved mystery every year at the Oscars. Why does songwriter Diane Warren keep getting nominated for movies nobody has ever heard of? Most Best Original Song nominees come from popular movies that many Music Branch voters have seen. But every year Diane Warren gets in there. She’s been nominated 17 times and never won. And even though the Academy presented her with an honorary Oscar in 2022, the Music Branch keeps nominating her anyway, thus taking her slot away from some other deserving songwriter. Why? Marcus Jones: Well, I would hardly say every Best Original Song nominee comes from a popular film. Did you see “Viva Verdi!” last year? And who is this other deserving songwriter you speak of? Let’s get into the crux of your arguments.

Anne Thompson: It’s politics, stupid. “She’s ruthless,” one Music Branch member told me. In other words, she campaigns hard for her slot every year. At the Middleburg Film Festival this fall, she flagged down people she knew in the hallways to show them her latest song on her mobile phone, a soaring duet between Jennifer Hudson and tenor Andrea Bocelli for the upcoming Al Pacino movie “Maserati: The Brothers,” which no one has seen. She even had a documentary made about her, “Relentless,” referring not only to how people in Hollywood describe her, but also to her bracelet inscribed “relentless as fuck.” The well-reviewed movie sells her brand perfectly: Its tagline is “Hitmaker. Legend. Pain in the ass.” You have to hand it to her: She’s a self-promotional marketing whiz. And by the way, she’s fun to hang out with, no question.

Marcus Jones: While that behavior at Middleburg that you described was quite forward, there is a positive spin on her relentless campaigning. At a time when the number of films nominated each year seems to be shrinking, and awards campaigns are getting more expensive, Warren’s one-woman awards run has turned truly independent films into Oscar nominees. Regardless of how you feel about the film or the song, it is truly inspiring to see films, often with no major studio backing, find a way into the conversation. Campaigning works!

Anne Thompson: Her fellow Music Branch members, especially in L.A., know her well. She’s been in the business for over four decades. She’s friends with most of them and invites them to her home every winter for a lavish holiday party.

Marcus Jones: Correct, she has had a head start with all these people, but this kind of indirect campaigning is not uncommon. For example, the president of the Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences was a guest at the biggest celebrity wedding of the year, the bride being another current Best Original Song contender.

Anne Thompson: I would call it direct campaigning. The Music Branch voters identify with Warren. Many of the members are on the senior side of their careers. They got into the Academy for music they wrote decades ago, like Warren. She writes melodic tunes that they like.

Marcus Jones: That is something that I feel is lost in the conversation around her nominations. Take away all 17 songs she’s been nominated for, and she is still one of the most accomplished songwriters of all time. This is a woman who wrote both Toni Braxton’s “Un-Break My Heart” and Cher’s “If I Could Turn Back Time” — definitive songs for those artists. As a songwriter, even if you reach the upper echelon of having a chart-topping run, that often lasts a decade at best. Warren has found a lane that has garnered her recognition from her community year after year, over four decades into her career.

Anne Thompson: But songs she wrote four decades ago should have nothing to do with how well her most recent song serves her current movie. She manages to get a few songs into movies every year and submits them. Winning a competitive Oscar is her goal in life! Her fellow members admire her pluck, tenacity, and drive. And there’s a question of whether the Music Branch watches the movies at all — most look and listen to video clips. Let’s be honest. The Music Branch is often swayed by concerns other than what is the best song in a movie, like, who is a major pop star they’d like to see perform on the Oscar telecast.

Marcus Jones: I personally find that to be one of the biggest misconceptions about the Music Branch. If they were actually swayed by who they think the most people would want to see on the telecast, then stadium acts like Taylor Swift, Ed Sheeran, and Miley Cyrus — who have all made several attempts at a Best Original Song nomination — would have gotten one by now. Bias is absolutely a factor in who gets nominated, but the members are also very discerning about how the song is used in the movie. The Best Original Song category was meant to honor music that holds significance to the film. Warren tends to be more mindful than many other musicians on that front. Even when producing music videos for her contenders, it’s never just about whether the song is good, but how much it is in conversation with the film it was written for. Her songs never feel tacked onto a project.

Anne Thompson: Another reason the Music Branch votes for her: Many of them owe her. “She got me into the Academy,” one voter said. According to one music publicist, she sponsors two Academy applicants a year. Those favors add up.

Marcus Jones: Hard to argue with that. Notable, too, that she keeps all her own publishing rights, which any songwriter will tell you is an incredible (and incredibly lucrative) feat. Though that likely creates a challenge for her when it comes to working with bigger studios. Many of those have record labels that are sister companies. We’re at a time where songwriters are often asked to give artists a cut of the publishing, to project the idea that they write all their own songs.

Anne Thompson: What bothers me most is the many slots that could have gone to someone else. Warren is successful and respected by her peers. She is being selfish and greedy. She doesn’t need a second Oscar. Let another worthy songwriter have a shot.

Marcus Jones: If you were successful enough in your discipline to keep getting nominated, why would you ever stop trying to win? No one accused composer Randy Newman or cinematographer Roger Deakins of taking the nomination slot of this phantom contender who is supposedly more deserving, and both were nominated over a dozen times before they won their first Oscar. The Best Sound category alone has a history of contenders breaking the record for most nominations with no wins. There wasn’t press telling re-recording mixer Kevin O’Connell to give up and get out of the way after 20 tries. One can dislike her music and her methods, but writing original songs for films is a task Warren takes very seriously. At this point, she sets the bar that songwriters must clear to have a chance at winning, and few do.
