The 2 ETFs I'd Buy Today: 1 for Growth, 1 for Dividends

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

One of these funds is down around 18% from its record while the other has risen. Why own both?

By Daniel Sparks – Nov 22, 2026 at 12:37AM EST

10 stocks we like better than Vanguard Morningstar Growth ETF ›

In the last seven weeks, two low-cost stock funds have moved in opposite directions.

The Vanguard Morningstar Growth ETF (VUG -5.32%) closed Oct. 6 at a record $92.64. As of this writing, it trades near $76, about 18% under that high. The Schwab U.S. Dividend Equity ETF (SCHD -0.76%), meanwhile, is up about 3% since Oct. 6 and is within about 6% of its own record.

That split is a big reason I like owning these two funds together. The growth fund holds many of the fastest-growing large businesses in the U.S., while the dividend fund pays a yield over eight times the growth fund's. And when one has a rough patch, the other has tended to hold up better.

The growth fund tracks the Morningstar US Large Cap Growth Index, and it owned 135 stocks at the end of September. At the time, Nvidia, Apple, Microsoft, and Alphabet accounted for about 45% of the fund's assets.

Technology stocks make up about 69% of the fund by Vanguard's classification. But it's not just a tech fund. Its 10 biggest holdings also include Amazon and Tesla, and consumer discretionary stocks are around 13% of the portfolio.

What investors get for that concentration is growth. By Vanguard's measure, the fund's current stocks grew their earnings around 33% a year on average in the last five years, and the fund was valued at about 29 times earnings at the end of October. Its share price has dropped about 11% since then, so that valuation arguably looks more reasonable now. In the 10 years through Sept. 30, the fund returned around 18% a year. What's more, its 0.03% expense ratio means just $3 in yearly costs per $10,000 invested.

Sure, that sort of growth can come with bigger drops. The fund lost around 33% in 2022, counting dividends.

Its 30-day SEC yield (a standardized gauge of the income a fund earns) is also just 0.37%. Put another way, dividends don't do much to cushion a fall like the current one.

I wouldn't bet on a quick bounce. But I think 10 years is long enough for companies that have grown this fast to grow into their prices.

Schwab's fund tracks the Dow Jones U.S. Dividend 100 Index, which chooses dividend payers using financial ratios meant to point to the quality and sustainability of their payouts. It owned 102 stocks as of Nov. 19.

Its biggest holdings were Procter & Gamble, Texas Instruments, Coca-Cola, and Home Depot, all between around 4% and 5% of the fund. The mix looks almost nothing like the growth fund's. Consumer staples, healthcare, and energy stocks made up around 55% of the portfolio in Schwab's newest breakdown, but information technology was about 12%.

Income is the top reason to own it. Its 30-day SEC yield was 3.18% as of Nov. 18, and it costs 0.06% a year, or $6 per $10,000.

Schwab, meanwhile, put the fund's holdings at around 18.5 times earnings at the end of October, well under the growth fund's 29 times earnings (but the two companies might calculate the number a bit differently).

This fund won't keep up when growth stocks are running, though. It returned around 12% a year in the decade ending Sept. 30, versus about 18% for the growth fund. Its dividend growth has also cooled. The last four quarterly distributions total around $1.05 a share, only about 2% more than the four before them.

The two funds' best and worst years haven't matched up. In 2022, the growth fund fell around 33% but the dividend fund lost only about 3%, including dividends. The next year flipped that, with the growth fund returning about 47% in 2023 and the dividend fund about 5%. And since Oct. 6 this year, the growth fund has slid around 18% but the dividend fund has risen about 3%.

Someone dividing $10,000 equally between them would pay about $4.50 a year in fund costs and earn a combined yield of around 1.8%.

Over the next decade, I'd expect the growth fund to do most of the heavy lifting on returns, but likely not at its 18% yearly rate of the last decade. The dividend fund's job is to keep cash coming and hold up when growth stocks sell off, the way they have since October.

I'd consider buying both funds today. And with the growth fund around 18% off its record, it's the one I'd add to first.
