How IPv4 Became An Asset Class Most Technology Leaders Still Misread

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

Vincentas Grinius is a tech entrepreneur and cofounder of IPXO, with a focus on internet infrastructure and sustainable digital innovation.

The internet ran out of new IPv4 addresses years ago. The regional registries that allocate them exhausted the pool between 2011 and 2020, depending on the region. Yet the world kept connecting billions of new devices, and it still runs, overwhelmingly, on the same 4 billion IPv4 addresses it has always had.

That gap between fixed supply and rising demand did something quiet yet consequential. It turned a block of numbers into a traded asset with a real, moving price. Address space is now bought, sold and leased on a secondary market most people outside the field have never heard of. Yet most technology leaders still treat it the way they always did, as free plumbing somewhere in the network stack.

That blind spot is getting expensive. If your business runs on distributed infrastructure, address space is a line item that behaves less like a utility and more like a scarce input you have to manage. It is not infinite, and it is not a simple commodity either. The pool cannot grow; IPv6 has been arriving for two decades without displacing IPv4 for most traffic, and the value of what already exists is real. In my years working in this market, I have watched capable teams misjudge it in the same few ways.

The most common mistake is timing. Acquiring address space and putting it to use are two distinct problems on two different clocks, and leaders routinely conflate them.

Acquisition is a capital problem. With a budget and a willing seller, a sizable block can change hands in a few weeks. Deployment is an operating problem. A block earns nothing while it is owned. It earns only when it is routing traffic for a real workload, and getting there requires provisioning, building routing reputation and clearing the hurdles that come with reputation-sensitive resources. That does not speed up just because you spend more. A team that budgets for the fast clock and plans around the slow one ends up with idle inventory and a timeline that slips by quarters.

The second mistake is treating price as a one-way bet. For most of this market’s history, prices rose, and much of the early narrative assumed they always would. They did not. Prices fell over the past couple of years as the largest buyers pulled back, then steadied. Anyone who underwrote address space as a guaranteed appreciation learned that the income it can generate, not the number on the screen, is what carries the case.

This is closer to real estate than to a commodity. Value comes from use over time and a gradual lease-up as the space is deployed, not from holding a static thing and waiting for it to be worth more. Block size matters, too. Large and small blocks sit in different price ranges and have moved differently. The asset is not a uniform thing.

There is one more factor that never appears in a price quote but quietly determines whether a purchase was smart. Address space is administered by five regional registries, and in some regions, a national layer sits on top. The rules vary widely. Some regions allow addresses to move freely. Others impose multiyear holding periods, restrict transfers or leave leasing in a legal gray zone. Two blocks can carry the same price yet be very different assets once you try to use or move them. A cheap block from a restrictive regime can be the more expensive choice.

None of this makes IPv4 inherently good or bad to hold. It makes it a real asset with real characteristics, and the error is treating it as anything less. In practice, that means a few shifts. Put address space on the managed-asset list, not the plumbing list. When you acquire, diligence the regime under which a block sits as carefully as the price. Plan capital and timelines against the slow deployment clock, not the fast acquisition clock. And weigh leasing against owning honestly because for many operators, using address space without carrying it on the balance sheet is now a rational choice rather than an exotic one.

While the industry spent a decade fixated on the application layer, the address layer beneath it became a scarce, priced, actively traded resource. Organizations that recognize this and treat their addresses accordingly will be better positioned to make sharper infrastructure and capital decisions than those still counting on plumbing to be free.
