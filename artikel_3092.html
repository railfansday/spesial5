Why Individual AI Gains Don’t Add Up To Company Value

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Dr. Son Nguyen is Orient Software’s CTO & co-founded Neurond AI, companies specializing in software development, AI, & data science services

​A few months ago, I asked our DevOps and finance teams the same question: What does it cost us each month to run the cloud infrastructure for one of our larger projects? Using different AI tools and data, they returned with different answers.

I couldn’t tell which answer to trust, where the information came from, whether it was current and whether correcting it would help others.

Now, imagine this across thousands of employees and AI tools.

Individual productivity is clearly improving with AI. A Gallup survey from May 2026 found that 65% of employees at organizations that have implemented AI reported a positive effect on their productivity and efficiency.​

But do thousands of assistants, each holding its own fragment of context, add up to a smarter company? I don’t think it necessarily does. While individual AI use can make each employee faster, only a company brain makes the company smarter.

McKinsey’s August 2026 report on the state of AI found that 89% of organizations are now using AI, and 80% of respondents say AI has improved their individual productivity. However, only 37% attribute at least some earnings before interest and taxes impact to AI, which was unchanged over the previous year.

We have become quite good at giving employees access to AI, but what happens to everything they learn from using it?

Each AI chat is its own island. Employees ask questions, refine answers and share business context. Yet that learning often stays within one interaction or workflow, and the next employee starts from scratch.

MIT’s NANDA initiative calls this the “learning gap.” Its preliminary study of 52 organizations found that most enterprise AI systems struggle to retain feedback, adapt or improve over time. The result? Thousands of employees become more productive without the organization becoming proportionally smarter.

Based on my experience, four issues explain why personal gains rarely become organizational gains:

1. Knowledge is scattered and often leaves with people. An employee develops a useful prompt, workflow or understanding of a customer issue, but that knowledge rarely becomes part of the company’s shared memory.

2. Companies lack a shared definition of what their information means. Without it, AI spreads existing inconsistencies faster.

3. Answers lack evidence. If AI doesn’t show the source of the information, it can produce an answer that sounds right without giving the organization a reliable basis for acting on it.

4. There is no shared learning loop. An employee discovers that an AI answer is wrong and corrects it, but that correction stays in their conversation. The next person can encounter the same error.

This is why I believe the quality of the underlying system matters at least as much as the model’s intelligence.

The BEAVER benchmark found that state-of-the-art AI agent frameworks using GPT-5.2 scored only 10.8% on questions drawn from real private data warehouses. As I discussed in my earlier article on AI system design, the model is only one layer. The surrounding architecture determines how information is retrieved, interpreted, governed and used.

A smarter model does not automatically create a smarter company.

Companies need a system that can turn individual interactions into shared organizational intelligence. I call this a company brain. The goal is to make organizational knowledge usable in decision-making. ​

A company brain is neither a chatbot on top of a data lake nor traditional knowledge management. It is not an off-the-shelf product or a platform project.

Instead, it is an operating model. Vendors can provide technology, but cannot decide what information is authoritative, who can access it or who is accountable when AI gets something wrong. When one person learns something valuable, that knowledge should become available to the right people and AI systems, with appropriate governance and evidence.

Before calling an AI system a company brain, I would test it against five requirements.

• Evidence: Every answer should show its source, owner and last update. I’ve explained before why every AI agent decision requires strong evidence. Without evidence, an AI answer may be useful, but not accountable. This matters most in regulated work, where every figure must be traceable. For high-risk uses such as hiring, credit and education, the EU AI Act requires high-quality data and traceability logs from December 2, 2027, for any company offering such systems in the EU. For those uses, the evidence standard is about to become a compliance standard.

• Meaning: Departments must share consistent definitions. If sales and finance mean different things by “revenue,” connecting their data won’t resolve the inconsistency.

• Permissions: People and AI agents should access only what they’re authorized to see and do, especially as AI moves from answering questions to taking actions.

• Learning: When an error is corrected once, the correction should improve the system for everyone. Outdated knowledge should also have a way to expire rather than remain available forever.

• Ownership: Someone at the executive level must own the outcome, with a dedicated budget and clear accountability. Otherwise, a company brain risks becoming everyone’s priority but nobody’s responsibility.

You don’t need a platform decision to begin building the brain. Start with questions your sales, finance, operations, customer success or engineering teams need to answer every week.

Measure how long it takes to get a trustworthy answer with a verifiable source, owner and date. Call this your time to trusted answers.

Then ask: Who corrects errors? Does that learning reach others? Can AI agents act safely within their permissions? These questions reveal more about AI maturity than license counts or daily usage.

To achieve success with enterprise AI, turning what one person learns into organizational intelligence is more important than making individuals faster. Your job isn’t to ask which model to buy, but what your AI knows about your company, and whether it can prove it.
