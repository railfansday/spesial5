Hong Kong Reaffirms Year-End Crypto Licensing Bill as Tokenized Funds Expand

Hong Kong’s Financial Secretary Paul Chan has reaffirmed plans to introduce a bill before the end of 2026 expanding the city’s digital-asset licensing framework. Speaking on November 16, he linked that regulatory work with a market moving toward regular use of tokenized investment products. The message was that more activity on blockchains still needs identifiable institutions responsible for the services around it.

Chan’s Bloomberg Global Regulatory Forum speech names dealing, advice, management and custody as activities to be brought within the perimeter. He also highlighted the two licensed stablecoin issuers and the emergence of around-the-clock trading in tokenized money-market funds.

The timetable is a reiteration, not a newly enacted rule. In a November 4 Legislative Council reply, the government said it planned to amend the Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorist Financing Ordinance and extend the existing trading-platform regime. That earlier explanation helps locate the proposal: the focus is the businesses handling digital assets and the functions they perform.

For a customer, the same investment can involve several such functions. One company might recommend it, another execute the purchase and another safeguard the resulting holding. A useful regulatory structure has to make responsibility visible at each handoff.

The November 4 government reply reported 17 authorised retail investment products with tokenized classes at the end of September, representing about HK$10.3 billion in those classes. These included money-market funds and gold-related products. The figure concerns a defined set of investment products; it should not be presented as the size of Hong Kong’s entire digital-asset market.

The Securities and Futures Commission’s October 26 approval allowed secondary trading of ChinaAMC’s Hong Kong-dollar and US-dollar digital money-market funds on a licensed virtual-asset platform. Secondary trading lets investors exchange existing interests rather than relying solely on subscriptions and redemptions with a fund.

That creates a different set of questions from simply recording ownership on a blockchain. Investors need meaningful prices, sufficient counterparties and a clear understanding of how traded units relate to their rights in the underlying product.

If a platform offers trading through a weekend, its customer support, security and controls have to account for that window. Yet the assets held by a fund may trade on a different schedule. A round-the-clock token does not ensure that its underlying market has become equally liquid at every hour.

The cash side is another dependency. Hong Kong’s EnsembleTX settlement work addresses how tokenized money and investment transactions can fit together. The trading venue, fund, custodian and payment service each need a consistent view of what has completed and what remains outstanding.

Stablecoin distribution raises similar questions about access and redemption. Anchorpoint’s planned native HKDAP expansion shows why a network integration and the conditions under which customers can use a product are separate milestones. A new route for transferring an asset does not by itself explain every holder’s rights.

Chan’s speech does not itself set final licence conditions or establish a commencement date for the expanded regime. Those details matter to firms deciding whether to add a service, change a custody arrangement or work through another authorised provider.

The most useful next evidence will be the legislative text and its treatment of different business models. Clear definitions can help firms determine where their obligations begin, while practical transition arrangements affect how existing customers are served.

Hong Kong’s challenge is now partly one of coordination: allowing more products and trading hours while keeping responsibility legible. A tokenized market will be easier to evaluate when investors can identify who owes them what, who controls a transaction and which regulator oversees each service.
