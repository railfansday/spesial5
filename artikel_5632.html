Why Do Empty-Nest Boomers Own So Many Homes in Top School Districts?

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

The best-performing school districts often bring stiff competition from families looking to break in, while older neighbors stay put. But helping free up housing in these districts isn't as simple as getting older people to move, a new study argues.

The paper from the Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland studied the household characteristics of the best-performing school districts in the country, which command higher real estate prices but also a higher barrier to entry.

The Cleveland Fed's findings show that seniors occupy a larger share of households in good school districts. But it pinned that on affordability challenges. Higher-income households of all types compete for housing in the best-performing school districts.

This is a conundrum for neighborhoods. Seniors are thus left in neighborhoods paying premiums for school districts they don't use. Meanwhile young families who'd like move into the district face limited options for homes there.

Of course, many seniors want to age in place in homes that they've lived in for decades. But others looking to downsize are "locked in" due to high mortgage rates. That can leave them in homes that they can't afford or, worse, in homes that pose safety risks for aging people.

"These findings suggest that policies aimed solely at encouraging older households to downsize are unlikely to substantially improve access to quality school districts," the paper says.

Low housing turnover in these districts drives up prices. The higher-rated public school districts are, on average, 49% more expensive, and homes there move faster, Realtor.com® data shows. But in some of the nation's top school districts—including in Dallas, Los Angeles, and San Fransisco—homes can sell for three times the price of surrounding metros.

Using data from the American Community Survey, the Cleveland Fed found that households over 65 occupy a disproportionate share of the housing stock in the best-performing school districts. While these account for 26.2% of all households nationally, they make up as much as 28.7% of households in the least affordable areas.

Meanwhile households with children, which make up about 31.2% of U.S. households, occupy 30.1% of housing in the best school districts and 40% of the housing stock in the lowest-quality districts. The same doesn't ring true for one-person households, which tend to be more evenly distributed.

Older homeowners move at about the same rate across all school districts—about 6.9% a year. About 81% of older adults in the best school districts own their homes. To the Cleveland Fed, that shows older households aren't limiting turnover of housing stock in high-quality and high-cost districts.

"While older households occupy a modestly larger share of housing in these expensive, high-quality areas, they do not appear to limit turnover," the paper states. "These patterns suggest that the challenge of accessing high-quality school districts is driven by a combination of income sorting and competition from multiple household types rather than solely by empty-nesters’ aging in place."

Realtor.com senior economist Jiayi Xu says a few issues are affecting seniors as they consider their futures. They include capital gains taxes they'd incur on homes that have appreciated in value. They also include the kinds of taxes they'd incur when they move to a new place. Then there are the social considerations.

"Right-sized, single-level, accessible homes near family and doctors are scarce," Xu says. "Moving also means leaving behind the neighbors and community that owners have built over decades. For many longtime owners, there are few good places to go."

Buyers can still find deals in some of these good markets, Realtor.com found, if they look carefully. But in some good districts, there simply aren't a lot of homes changing hands.

Xu adds that she's surprised that young professionals without children can also compete for housing in these expensive districts. Jobs, urban amenities, and investment opportunity for the real estate tend to make these areas attractive.

"Even if more seniors moved, the homes they left would likely go to higher-income buyers, because this is a sorting story. The districts wouldn’t become much more affordable," Xu says. "The more direct solution is building more homes in and near high-demand districts. Adding right-sized options could also give older owners who want to move somewhere to go, but supply is what would ease pressure on prices."

To Arick Morton, CEO of NIC MAP, the frozen market points to a need for more housing oriented toward seniors. Earlier this year, his group said the nation needs to add 1 million new senior housing units by 2040. That $1 trillion homebuilding frenzy would meet the moment 10 million baby boomers pass age 80.

"People stay at home much longer than they did previously," Morton said. "Of course folks stay at home as long as they can and then movie into senior housing when the need is there."

But when there isn't enough senior housing or they don't have good alternatives to downsize, those seniors end up displaced when age or an accident means they require more intensive care needs.

"The best policy options are those that help expedite the construction of senior housing," Morton said. "We don't have enough capacity to create a dent in that logjam."
