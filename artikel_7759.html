Retrospective Voting and the 2026 Election

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

A key "information shortcut" worked well in the 2026 election. But political ignorance remains a serious danger.

In my book Democracy and Political Ignorance  and various other writings going all the way back to my very first academic article (published in 1998), I have warned of the dangers of widespread voter ignorance. And I have argued that "information shortcuts" - small bits of knowledge that supposedly make up for ignorance about details - are generally ineffective or even counterproductive. Thus, I should acknowledge that one of those shortcuts - retrospective voting - worked fairly well in the recently concluded 2026 election. At the same time, the problem of political ignorance remains a grave menace.

Retrospective voting is probably the most often-used information shortcut, and the one most cited by scholars who argue that voter ignorance isn't much of a problem. The basic idea is simple: to be a good voter you don't need to know much about government and public policy. You just need to know whether things are going well or badly under the rule of the incumbents.

If things are going well, you vote to reelect them to continue their good work. If badly, you vote to "throw the bastards out." Then you elect a new set of bastards in their place; but the new bastards will hopefully know they need to do a good job, else they will get thrown out in their turn, at the next election. As Ronald Reagan famously put it during the 1980 election, "Next Tuesday all of you will go to the polls… I think when you make that decision it might be well if you would ask yourself: Are you better off than you were four years ago?"

I and other critics have long pointed out that, when ignorant voters use the retrospective voting shortcut, they often end up rewarding and punishing incumbents for things they did not cause (usually short-term economic trends), while ignoring more subtle long-term issues. In addition, they often don't know which officials are responsible for which issues, and tend to overlook the possibility that the opposition party's policies will be as bad or worse.

Nonetheless, retrospective voting actually worked pretty well in the 2026 election. As I noted in a previous post, polls show that many voters punished the Republicans for high prices caused by Trump's tariffs,  the failed Iran War, and the administration's cruel campaign of mass deportations (which particularly angered Hispanic voters in key states and congressional districts). One can also add the issue of corruption, which polls  indicate also had a big impact.

The Republicans really were responsible for all three of these policies. They weren't caused by exogenous factors outside of Trump's control. And the policies really did have massive negative effects in raising prices, an unsuccessful war, and massive cruelty and violations of civil liberties caused by the deportation campaign. The scope and effects of corruption are harder to measure. But this administration may be the most corrupt in American history. And, on all four issues, the Democrats are likely to be at least somewhat better.

But while retrospective voting worked reasonably well in the 2026 midterm election, we cannot conclude that the problem of political ignorance doesn't matter, or even that it's overblown. Rather, the shortcut was effective primarily because of the large and obvious nature of Trump's failures.

Along with other scholars, I have long recognized that retrospective voting can be effective if incumbents screw in ways that have large and obvious bad effects - ones that even very ignorant voters can easily see and understand. For example, I often cite economist Amartya Sen's famous finding that mass famines never or almost never occur under democracies (even as they happen much more often under dictatorships). Even ignorant voters will notice a famine is going on, blame incumbents for it, and vote accordingly.

Trump's failures over the last two years weren't quite as bad as a mass famine. But they were nonetheless large (including a major war and  the highest tariff schedule since the Great Depression), highly visible, and many of their effects were easy to understand. It isn't hard to notice that massive tariff increases cause prices to rise. The war and its effects on prices were also highly visible. Images of brutal deportations and resulting civil liberties violations were all over the news. Most voters probably didn't know about the details of the administration's corruption. But there was so much of it, that most probably heard at least something about it.

Many policy failures are far less readily apparent than that. And ignorant voters are therefore likely to overlook or misunderstand them. It's not hard to find examples even in the 2026 election itself. To take a particularly important case, the US faces a massive looming fiscal crisis, caused in large part by the combination of an aging population and out-of-control entitlement spending.  Yet most candidates of both parties largely ignored this problem, and promise continued massive spending, and avoiding tax increases for almost everyone, except perhaps a few billionaires and other very wealthy people. They could do that because surveys show most voters are ignorant about our fiscal situation and especially don't realize the key role of entitlement spending.

Voters' justified negative reaction in 2026 to tariffs, mass deportations, corruption, and the Iran War also highlight the failure of information shortcuts in the 2024 election. At that time, it was evident to informed observers that Trump would seek to institute massive tariffs and cruel deportation policies. Indeed, I myself was among those who cited them as among the principal reasons to oppose Trump in that election. Large-scale corruption was also predictable, given Trump's long history of skullduggery. Yet swing voters got these issues wrong - despite the fact that high prices were a major issue in 2024, and tariffs and mass deportations would predictably raise them (as I also pointed out at the time).

The Iran War was admittedly less predictable. Trump even promised to avoid new wars. But his well-documented combination of ignorance and impulsiveness meant that returning him to office created a significant risk of a foreign policy crisis spiraling out of control.

And on all these issues, it was predictable (and predicted by many, including me) that Trump 2.0 would surround himself with cronies and loyalists likely to indulge his worst instincts. No longer would he be constrained by "adults in the room," as occasionally happened during his first term.

Thus, while an information shortcut worked well in 2026, it's important to recognize that was only necessary because shortcuts failed miserably in 2024, which was an only modestly more difficult situation. In that earlier election, we did not yet see the effects of Trump's awful second-term policies. But most of those policies and effects were readily predictable by any moderately well-informed observer. Yet most swing voters were much more ignorant than that, and thus instead voted to punish Democrats for high prices, ignoring the high likelihood that electing Trump would make that problem worse, not better.

In addition, as I emphasized in my November 2024 post on voter ignorance in that year's election, a combination of ignorance and bias led many voters to underestimate the significance of Trump's assault on basic norms and principles of liberal democracy.

It is also important to remember that the problem of voter ignorance is not just that it leads voters to make "wrong" choices between the options put before them. It also makes those options worse in the first place. As I have long argued in my book and elsewhere, political ignorance also makes those options worse in the first place. Politicians and campaign strategists know they face a largely ignorant electorate, and act accordingly. Sweeping the fiscal crisis under the rug is just one of many examples of that tendency. Even if information shortcuts consistently helped voters choose the lesser of the evils before them (which, sadly, they don't!), ignorance would still make those options much more evil than they would be otherwise.

Trump is a particularly egregious long-time exploiter of political ignorance, which played key roles in his rise and return to power. But more conventional politicians also routinely exploit it, as Barack Obama did, for example.  For those keeping score, I have been arguing that political ignorance is a serious danger for over 25 years, under both Republican and Democratic administrations, and through many changes in the political winds.

The  2024 election led me to conclude that voter ignorance was an even more serious problem than I previously thought. This year's election is, perhaps, a data point for the other side.  But whether or not the problem is worse than I thought before 2024, it continues to be a serious danger.

There is no easy fix for widespread voter ignorance. I assessed  various possible options in my 2023 book chapter on "Top-Down and Bottom-Up Solutions to the Problem of Political Ignorance, and in Chapters 5-7 of Democracy and Political Ignorance. I think the most promising approach make fewer decisions at the ballot box and more by "voting with your feet," where incentives to seek out information and use it wisely are better. I also argue in both works that the idea of simply paying voters to increase their knowledge deserves more attention. But any remedy will take time, and we may need a combination of several strategies, not just one.

In sum, the 2026 election is an example of retrospective voting working relatively well. But we should not conclude this shortcut - or any other - is a good general solution to the problem of widespread political ignorance.
