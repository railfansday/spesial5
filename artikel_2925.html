Fed Chair Kevin Warsh Vowed to Hit the Fed's 2% Inflation Target After October's Rate Hold, Even as JPMorgan Warns Democratic Control of the Senate Could Make Him a One-Term Chair. Here's Why That Political Risk Matters for Markets.

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Federal Reserve Chairman Kevin Warsh is focusing on process, but that may not matter if he ignores this part of his job.

By Reuben Gregg Brewer – Nov 18, 2026 at 10:15PM EST

JPMorgan Chase (JPM -1.77%) recently published a report that went well beyond Wall Street analysis. In it, chief U.S. economist Michael Feroli warned that current Federal Reserve Chairman Kevin Warsh needs to broaden his focus, or all the changes he is making could prove for naught. It isn't an idle warning, and investors need to understand why politics matters for a position intended to be apolitical. The key to the story is that Kevin Warsh risks serving a one-term tenure as chair if he doesn't engage more with Congress. Here's why it matters.

From a big-picture perspective, the Fed's job is to ensure stable prices and full employment. That dual mandate can be difficult to manage because elevated inflation often accompanies a strong economy. It is a delicate balancing act meant to occur outside the political process. This is why the Fed is self-funded, board members serve long terms, and they can be removed only for cause. This setup is meant to curb the short-term thinking that often accompanies the political process, in which elections are held every two years.

Image source: Official Federal Reserve Photo.

That's not to suggest that the Fed can completely ignore politicians. The president appoints key members of the Fed to their positions, with the Senate's approval. The Fed also has to make regular updates to Congress. So while the job of Federal Reserve Chair is meant to be outside of the political process, it is still entwined with politics. That's the core of the warning that JPMorgan Chase's Michael Feroli is making.

According to Feroli, current Fed Chair Kevin Warsh is making a mistake by not engaging more with Congress. And given the shifting political winds in Washington, he could be replaced when his term ends in 2030 if he doesn't change what he is doing in this area. This warning is likely tied to the fact that Warsh is making major changes in other areas of his job.

Even as the Fed has made strong statements about returning inflation to its 2% target, Kevin Warsh has been vocal about reducing the amount of guidance it provides to the market. Wall Street got used to receiving material guidance after the dot-com crash and the Great Recession. Effectively, the Fed was looking to ease investor concerns during deep bear markets and economic downturns. However, the guidance didn't end after those difficult periods.

Warsh believes this has left the market reliant on the Fed rather than operating as it should, setting prices in real time based on economic conditions. As such, he's started to limit the amount of information the Fed shares. Wall Street isn't pleased, particularly with inflation elevated and rates on the rise. The bond market has been particularly volatile during this transition. For those who have been around long enough, however, this is just a return to more normal times, even though it is not an easy transition.

That is where Warsh's interactions with Congress come in. If he is only a one-term Fed chair, the changes could quickly be rolled back by the next chairman. If he takes the time to better explain what he is doing and why, he would increase the odds of being appointed to a second term. That would help the changes he has made stick, which, in turn, could give the Fed more leeway to address its dual mandate over the long term.

Warsh is focused on fixing "the process," which is laudable. There is a moral hazard in Wall Street expecting the Fed to provide excessive information and, in some quarters, to serve as a backstop for the market. But fixing the process isn't happening in a vacuum, and Warsh would increase the odds of success if he took the time to more fully, and perhaps personally, explain to Congress what he is doing and why.

While that sounds political, and it is to some extent, it may be more of a social issue. If Warsh doesn't build trust and respect, he risks reducing the support he'll receive when his term ends. The risk for investors is that the Fed's approach could swing between extremes, which isn't likely to be good for anybody.
