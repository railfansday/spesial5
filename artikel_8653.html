The S&P 500 is an index that tracks the 500 largest publicly traded companies in the United States, weighted by market capitalization. It includes Apple, Microsoft, Amazon, Nvidia, Alphabet (Google), Meta, Berkshire Hathaway, and approximately 493 other companies that collectively represent about 80% of the total U.S. stock market value.

The index is maintained by S&P Dow Jones Indices, which periodically adds and removes companies based on criteria including market cap (must exceed ~$18 billion), profitability, liquidity, and domicile. When a company is added to the S&P 500, every index fund tracking it automatically buys shares — and vice versa when companies are removed.

An S&P 500 index fund is a mutual fund or exchange-traded fund (ETF) that holds all 500 stocks in the same proportions as the index. Instead of picking individual stocks, you buy one fund and instantly own a piece of every major U.S. company. The fund mechanically replicates the index — no stock picking, no market timing, no overpaid fund managers.

The data is overwhelming. Over any 15-year period, approximately 92% of actively managed large-cap funds underperform the S&P 500 after fees, according to the SPIVA scorecard. Over 20 years, that number exceeds 95%. The reasons are simple: active fund managers charge 0.50% to 1.50% in annual fees, they generate taxable trading activity, and they cannot consistently pick winners.

Warren Buffett bet $1 million that an S&P 500 index fund would beat a selection of hedge funds over 10 years. He won decisively — the index fund returned 125.8% vs 36.0% for the hedge fund basket. As Buffett has said: "By periodically investing in an index fund, the know-nothing investor can actually outperform most investment professionals."

The top S&P 500 index funds deliver nearly identical performance because they all track the same index. The differences come down to expense ratio, minimum investment, fund structure (ETF vs mutual fund), and tax efficiency. Here is a detailed comparison of the five most popular options.

The total stock market index (tracked by VTI/VTSAX/FSKAX) holds approximately 3,600 stocks compared to the S&P 500's 500. The additional 3,100 stocks are mid-cap and small-cap companies. Here is how they compare:

Historically, the performance difference between the two is minimal — typically within 0.1-0.3% per year in either direction. The S&P 500 has outperformed in periods when large-cap growth stocks dominate. The total market has outperformed when small and mid-cap stocks lead. Over 20+ year periods, the returns converge closely.

The FI community's honest answer: it does not matter much which you choose. Investing $24,500 per year in VOO or VTI will produce nearly identical outcomes over 20 years. Consistency and contribution amount matter far more than the choice between S&P 500 and total market.

S&P 500 index funds typically yield 1.2% to 1.6% in annual dividends. In a tax-advantaged account (401k, IRA, Roth), dividends compound tax-free. In a taxable account, qualified dividends from S&P 500 funds are taxed at the favorable long-term capital gains rate (0%, 15%, or 20% depending on income).

ETFs (VOO, IVV) are generally more tax-efficient than mutual funds (FXAIX, SWPPX) in taxable accounts because ETFs rarely distribute capital gains. Vanguard's mutual funds are an exception — their patented ETF share class structure gives Vanguard mutual funds ETF-like tax efficiency. For taxable brokerage accounts, VOO or VFIAX (Vanguard's mutual fund version) are the most tax-efficient choices.

The most common mistake with S&P 500 index funds is waiting for the "right time" to invest. Research from Vanguard shows that investing a lump sum immediately outperforms dollar-cost averaging about two-thirds of the time, because markets go up more often than they go down. Missing just the 10 best trading days over a 20-year period can cut your returns nearly in half. If you have money to invest, the data overwhelmingly says to invest it now — not next week, not after the next pullback, not when it "feels right."

Open an account at Vanguard (for VOO/VFIAX), Fidelity (for FXAIX), or Schwab (for SWPPX). All three offer commission-free trading on their own index funds, excellent customer service, and robust retirement account options. You can also buy any of these funds at most brokerages.

ETFs (VOO, IVV) trade like stocks at real-time prices and are more tax-efficient in taxable accounts. Mutual funds (FXAIX, SWPPX) allow automatic dollar-amount investing and are better for set-it-and-forget-it contribution schedules. For retirement accounts, either works equally well.

Transfer cash to your brokerage account and buy your chosen fund. Start with whatever you have — there is no minimum too small. FXAIX and SWPPX have $0 minimums, and VOO can be purchased in fractional shares at most brokerages for as little as $1.

Automate monthly or per-paycheck contributions. This removes emotion from the process and ensures consistent dollar-cost averaging. Most brokerages let you schedule recurring purchases of mutual funds. For ETFs, some brokerages offer automatic ETF purchases as well.

If you hold other asset classes (bonds, international), rebalance once per year to maintain your target allocation. Otherwise, do nothing — do not check daily prices, do not sell during downturns, and do not chase performance. The S&P 500 has recovered from every crash in history. Time in the market beats timing the market.

Fidelity 500 Index Fund (FXAIX) has the lowest expense ratio at 0.015%. Schwab S&P 500 Index Fund (SWPPX) is next at 0.02%. VOO and IVV tie at 0.03%. All four are excellent — the fee difference between FXAIX (0.015%) and VOO (0.03%) on a $500,000 portfolio is just $75 per year. Choose the fund offered by your preferred brokerage.

VOO is better for long-term investors. It charges 0.03% vs SPY's 0.0945%, saving you about $325 per year on a $500,000 portfolio. VOO is also more tax-efficient due to its ETF share class structure. SPY's advantage is liquidity for active trading, which long-term index investors do not need. For buy-and-hold FI investing, VOO is the clear winner.

Either is an excellent choice. The S&P 500 covers approximately 80% of the U.S. stock market by value. A total market fund adds mid-cap and small-cap stocks for fuller diversification. Over long periods, performance is nearly identical — typically within 0.1-0.3% per year. The FI community consensus is that the best fund is the one you will consistently invest in without overthinking the decision.

As little as $1. FXAIX and SWPPX have zero minimum investments. VOO and IVV can be purchased as fractional shares at most brokerages. There is no minimum too small — starting with $50 or $100 per month and increasing contributions as your income grows is exactly how most people on the FI path begin.

The S&P 500 has returned approximately 10.3% per year on average since 1957 (including dividends). Adjusted for inflation, the real return is approximately 7.0% per year. These are long-term averages — individual years range from -38% (2008) to +38% (1995). The key is staying invested through the down years to capture the recovery gains that follow.

S&P 500 index funds carry market risk — they can lose significant value in the short term (the index dropped 34% in early 2020 and 38% in 2008). However, the S&P 500 has recovered from every decline in its history and has never lost money over any 20-year period. For investors with a 10+ year time horizon, the risk of not investing (missing out on 10% average annual returns) is far greater than the risk of temporary market declines.

S&P 500 index funds are the single most important building block for long-term wealth. With expense ratios as low as 0.015%, you keep virtually all of your returns while owning a piece of the 500 largest U.S. companies. VOO, FXAIX, IVV, and SWPPX all deliver near-identical performance — choose the fund offered by your preferred brokerage and automate your contributions. The FI community has proven through thousands of real-world examples that consistent investing in a low-cost S&P 500 index fund, held for decades without panic selling, is the most reliable path to building wealth and reaching financial independence.
