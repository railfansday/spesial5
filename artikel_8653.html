Safe Deposit Boxes Are for Papers You Rarely Need – Not Your Will

Put original papers that are hard to replace but rarely needed, like deeds and car titles, in a safe deposit box. Keep anything someone may need fast or after you die, including your will and powers of attorney, at home in a fireproof safe or with your lawyer.

The box also protects less than its name suggests, since the FDIC, the federal agency that insures bank deposits, lists “safe deposit boxes or their contents” among what its insurance doesn’t cover. To be paid for a theft or a fire at the bank, you need insurance of your own.

Getting into the box is the bigger problem. You can get to it only during branch hours, so a passport you need on a Sunday stays locked up until Monday.

A death or a sudden illness makes that worse. State law decides who may open a box after its renter dies, and opening a box rented in one person’s name can require a court order or the bank’s own paperwork. Picture your original will and powers of attorney, the papers that let someone act for you, in a box only you can open. Your family then waits on the bank, or a court, at the moment they need those papers.

Store the papers in a fireproof safe at home, or leave the legal ones with the lawyer who drew them up, and keep copies in the box. Move the cash to a high-yield savings account, where deposit insurance covers it and it earns interest. And ask your insurance agent to cover the heirlooms under your homeowners or renters policy.

Last, pick someone you trust to open the box when you can’t. Add them to the rental agreement as a co-renter, and ask the bank what your state requires before anyone else can open the box after you die.
