The Mets’ ridiculous Tarik Skubal contract is the last vestige of MLB’s old world

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

In the liminal space of the immediately pre-lockout MLB offseason, Tarik Skubal has conducted a contradictory orchestra. In signing a nine-year, $450 million contract with the New York Mets, he has managed to get both far more than he deserves and not as much as he should have. Let me explain.

It's a pretty terrible contract for the Mets, but we have no clue how terrible since we have no clue if there will be a salary cap, and even if there is, we have no clue how super contracts like Skubal's will count against it. However, if this was just a normal year with no apocalyptic labor showdown coming over the horizon like a 200-foot tsunami ready to wipe multiple cities off the map, Skubal probably would have signed for far more. The Los Angeles Dodgers, apparently, did not even make him an offer — with the lockout coming on December 1, this may have been Skubal's first, last and only offer.

If the Dodgers were out, then the Mets were bidding against … nobody. There are lots of fascinating reasons for why that is fascinating, so let's get into all of them and decide whether Skubal's deal is the prophet of doom or if we're all going to be fine.

The MLB world stands on the precipice of an earth-shattering labor war that could cancel an entire season. From where I currently stand (and I change where I stand quite often to get better views), it looks like the result of all this will be the introduction of a salary cap, probably the most disruptive change to MLB's competitive ecosystem in its history. To me, the owners have too much leverage to ultimately lose, and the MLB Players Association has too small a platform beyond essentially saying, "why are you guys being like this?"

Unless the owners somehow buckle, which I don't believe they will, Tarik Skubal was the last major signing of the free market era and got the largest pitcher contract ever, both by total and annual value, blowing Yoshinobu Yamamoto's deal completely out of the water and giving Steven Cohen one last moment to say he didn't care about how much his team cost.

That's a lie, of course, as Cohen is very much in league with the other owners to impose a cap on his players in order to skyrocket his franchise value and to share less money with those players. It's the ultimate under-the-table conspiracy, in which MLB's owners outwardly boast about trying to increase the quality of the sport while by far the greatest increase will come to the weight of their pockets. And they will cancel a season to do it, because the amount of money we're talking about here is nuts.

The fact that Cohen just signed Skubal and is still pursuing a salary cap to make sure he can never do such a thing in the future speaks to the titanic strength of the owners' position and why I see it as so likely that we will see a salary cap at the end of all this. Most people simply don't understand that big market owners also want a cap because their earning potential spikes just the same, and that owners don't think like fans — Cohen is happy to pay Skubal now to win goodwill from his fans for the next 10 years, while also stabbing his players in the back to secure yet more billions of dollars for himself and his family for the next quadrillion years.

The fact that the Mets and Dodgers always sign these big free agents continues to underscore how a salary cap would marginally improve baseball for many fans, especially those of small markets, but the cost of an entire season does not feel remotely worth those small improvements, especially since the primary beneficiaries are … uh, billionaires. I'm a bit of a broken record on that, but it's the only rational position I have found in the cesspool of public broadsides and polemics.

Okay, what about the baseball side of things? Because Skubal's $450 million and the many other super contracts of the free market era were signed before a cap was introduced, they will almost certainly not be counted simply against it. If a salary cap does emerge from this battle, contracts like Skubal's and Juan Soto's $765 million will probably have to be prorated somehow across whatever a maximum contract turns out to be in the new system. Otherwise, deferred money like with Shohei Ohtani's contract would become rather silly, with the ghost of Ohtani counting $68 million against the Dodgers salary cap in 2043. Skubal will obviously receive the full value of that contract somehow, but nobody on earth knows how an eventual cap will distribute the payroll of the Mets and Dodgers, who have so much money committed for so long it might take a Nobel Prize in Economics to figure it out. So we shall just put that aside.

In a vacuum, then, it's an awful contract. Unless your argument is that "there's no cap, so it doesn't matter, Steve Cohen has 25 gazillion dollars," which I guess can just invalidate anything anyone could ever say … then it's pretty inarguably a crazy contract for a 30-year-old pitcher who's had three left elbow surgeries. Skubal is a remarkable player, probably the best pitcher in the world if everything is equal, but a hard $50 million per year for a pitcher until they're 39 is one of those contracts you just know will be on a "worst contracts in MLB" list I'll make in 2033.

In not-a-vacuum, the contract is a real risk. The fact that the Dodgers were unwilling to even engage with Skubal shows two things: first, how likely it actually is that we get a salary cap since the Dodgers were concerned about overextending their finances and second, that whatever that cap looks like could be rather punishing to the Mets and Dodgers who will be loaded with max contracts right off the rip. The Mets just signed an incredible player, yes, but we're right on the edge of a Brave New World — there's no way this contract ages well.

After signing Skubal, the Mets rose to fifth in the 2027 World Series odds. Will there even be a 2027 World Series? Your guess is as good as mine.

None of that matters if the Mets can just win the World Series one of these years, but they're starting to feel a bit gimmicky with this "buy a World Series" thing. Their roster is shockingly terrible for how expensive it is, and the gap between Soto and the rest of their lineup was staggering for much of last season. Skubal can pitch every five days, great. They will have a lot more to fix than that.
