Ingrid Bergman and Roberto Rossellini: The Affair That Outraged America

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

The whole situation had become such a mess that their marriage had to take place by proxy, through lawyers thousands of miles away. The romance between actor Ingrid Bergman and director Roberto Rossellini was one of the great scandals of the 20th century. They were two married people who had fallen in love and embarked on an adulterous relationship—nothing that hadn’t been seen before, especially in the film industry—but something about their particular situation sparked a wave of international outrage with an intensity that’s rarely seen. There were condemnations from the Vatican, proclamations from the US Senate, insults of every kind, and an avid and destructive interest from the press and the public. There were also some glorious moments, erupting volcanoes, and even plates of spaghetti used as weapons.

I saw your films Open City and Paisan, and enjoyed them very much. If you need a Swedish actress who speaks English very well, who has not forgotten her German, who is not very understandable in French, and who in Italian knows only “ti amo,” I am ready to come and make a film with you.

This now legendary letter above has become part of film history. It’s no surprise that two artistic sensibilities of the caliber of Bergman and Rossellini would connect through the latter’s films—masterpieces of Italian neorealism that Bergman connected with emotionally and intellectually. “The realism and simplicity of Open City were heart-shocking,” she recalls in Ingrid Bergman: My Story. “No one looked like an actor, and no one talked like an actor. There was darkness and shadows, and sometimes you couldn’t even see it. But that’s the way it is in life…. You can’t always see and hear, but you know that something almost beyond understanding is going on.” Bergman left the movie theater where she had just seen the film on a spring night in 1948 deeply moved. She didn’t know it, but her life had just changed forever.

The world 80 years ago was very different from today, of course, and the actor had a hard time tracking down the director. When she finally did locate him, they began an exchange of letters filled with deep admiration, during which they agreed to collaborate on a screenplay that Rossellini was working on which would eventually become the film Stromboli. The involvement of someone as famous and talented as Bergman should have, in theory, made it easier for Rossellini to raise funds for another film. She was Sweden’s most successful export since Greta Garbo, the Ilsa of Casablanca, and an Oscar winner for her performance in Gaslight. She was admired worldwide for her naturalness and beauty. She was so popular that there was even a reported surge in women choosing to become nuns after the release of another film she starred in, The Bells of St. Mary’s, much to the chagrin of many parents. For his part, Rossellini might not have been well-known in the US, but his revolutionary ability to capture the realities of life on film had earned him a Palme d’Or at Cannes and the admiration of cineasts. He had also starred in various encounters with Italy’s fledgling paparazzi. Married for over a decade, he maintained a relationship that was an open secret, with his lover and muse, the spectacular actor Anna Magnani. Together they were a tumultuous and popular couple—that is, until Bergman appeared on the scene via her letter.

Their relationship was long limited to correspondence, but there was clearly some passion in those letters because, upon arriving at the Hotel Luna Convento in Amalfi with Magnani, Rossellini requested that any telegram or letter from England be delivered to him with the utmost discretion. (Bergman was, at the time, shooting Under Capricorn at the Elstree Studios outside London.) The staff assumed this didn’t apply to his well-known partner, and when they were in the hotel dining room, a waiter approached him and said, in full view of Magnani, “I was instructed that if a telegram arrived for you from England, I should give it to you in private. Here you go.” Magnani had already begun to hear rumors about the relationship between Rossellini and the famous Swedish actor, whom he was said to be planning to offer a role that had been written specifically for her. “Now, is this all right—eh, Roberto?” she asked kindly as she served herself more spaghetti with tomato sauce. “Ah, yes, yes, grazie,” he replied distractedly.

“Good. Here, you can have it,” and she tossed the heap of spaghetti straight into Rossellini’s face.

Magnani’s intuitions were right. When Bergman first arrived in Rome to then travel through the south of the country on her way to Stromboli, the island in the Aeolian archipelago where the film was to be shot, she and Rossellini became lovers: “I fell in love with him because he was so unique. I’d never met anyone like him, so free. He brought new dimensions to life, new boundaries, new emotions, and new horizons. And he gave me a courage I’d never known before.” She had been married for over 10 years to Petter Lindström, a doctor who was her first love, but she hadn’t been happy for a long time—neither in her relationship nor in Hollywood. In fact, she’d earlier asked him for a divorce; he refused to grant it, and since they had a young daughter, Pia, and their cohabitation was proceeding smoothly enough, they continued to live together under the same roof. In the eyes of the world, they were still husband and wife. But shortly before meeting Rossellini, Bergman had had a romance with another tormented artistic figure, the photographer Robert Capa. Since that affair had no major consequences, Lindström turned a blind eye. The story with Rossellini would be completely different. He represented a way out of a marriage and an industry in which she felt trapped. “I think I was just waiting for someone to come along and help me out of that marriage because I didn’t have the strength to go. That was over three years before I met Roberto Rossellini.” What she didn’t realize was how much her decision would turn her world upside down.

Italy would be a revelation for Bergman. Rossellini introduced her to his circle of friends, including figures such as Federico Fellini, and served as the best possible guide on her journey to Naples, Amalfi, Sorrento, and Messina. In Amalfi, the actress decided that this was more than just a passing fling; it was a love intense enough for her to end her marriage and leave the United States. She wrote to Lindström from the very same Hotel Luna Convento where Magnani and Rossellini had their confrontation, telling him that she had fallen in love and was staying in Italy. He refused to accept the news, and his denial would give rise to much of the drama that was about to unfold.

Meanwhile, the new happy couple drove along the country’s roads, which still bore the visible scars of World War II, through one of the most spectacular landscapes on earth. Everywhere the film crew went, crowds cheered them, delighted that the world’s most famous actor had decided to make a movie in their country. At one of the simple hotels where they stayed, Bergman had to leave her room to use a bathroom on a hallway. Fans cheered her on both on her way there and back.

It soon became clear that their partnership was more than just a creative one. Life magazine photographed them holding hands, and the images went around the world. By the time they arrived on the small island of Stromboli, the shoot, with its many ordinary people recruited to perform in the film, was swamped by press from all over the world eager to capture images of the lovers at the foot of the island’s volcano. The role of a refugee who agrees to a loveless marriage in order to be released from an internment camp and then ends up trapped in an unfamiliar world that is as oppressive as the one she escaped came naturally to Bergman. It reflected her emotional state: She was caught between the joy of being in love, the guilt of leaving her husband, and the doubts and pain regarding her 10-year-old daughter, Pia, who remained in the United States. In Ingrid Bergman: My Story, Bergman recounts, “It was absolute hell. I cried so much that I thought there couldn’t be any tears left. I felt the newspapers were right. I’d abandoned my husband and child. I was an awful woman.”

There are images from the shoot in which Bergman is seen in close-up, and behind her are several women in mourning, an oppressive and threatening presence. They evoke exactly what happened to her. The world exploded in anger against its once favorite star. She was portrayed as a lost woman, if not a whore, and an insult to every sense of propriety. Much of the intensity of the reaction was related to Bergman’s previous popularity and her nearly flawless, pristine image. She was seen as an embodiment of a strict Lutheran morality, a sincere and honest woman. (Lindström would later say she was, in fact, a heavy drinker and smoker, promiscuous, and cared only about her career, not her family.) That this almost angelic figure would give up everything to run off with an unattractive, tempestuous Italian director seemed like an unfathomable mistake. Added to this was the intrigue that she was now his new muse, following the landmark films shot with Magnani. And there was also, of course, Pia’s complicated situation. Rossellini prevented Bergman from returning to the United States, fearful that if she went, she wouldn’t come back to Italy, while Lindström refused to let their daughter travel to Italy. The divorce and custody battle became bitter, filled with mutual accusations and animosity. As a result, mother and daughter went two years without seeing each other. And then, after that, another six. This is how Pia, the main casualty of this love story, would recall those times: “I stayed with him [her father], and my mother left. She was offered a dramatic life, full of excitement, with romantic, wonderful love affairs. It was magnificent for her. But not what came of it. I didn’t experience the splendid side of the situation. In other words, I was left behind. That’s why I see things differently. I was part of what was left behind.”

As if there weren’t enough elements to this scandal already, it soon emerged that Bergman was pregnant. Senator Edwin C. Johnson referred to her in the Senate as “a powerful influence for evil.” Some of her friends, however, came to her defense. In 1961, she told Lillian Ross of The New Yorker: “When I gave birth to little Roberto, the whole world started to throw stones at me. But I knew I had friends when I received a letter from Ernest Hemingway saying he wanted to be the baby’s godfather. And when I came to New York to accept the New York Film Critics award for Anastasia, Hemingway said, ‘I’ll come with you and punch the first one in the nose who says anything bad to you.’”

The night Ingrid went into labor coincided with the Italian premiere of Volcano, a film with a plot similar to Stromboli—created by Anna Magnani to compete with her ex’s film and directed by a German émigré to the United States, William Dieterle. Midway through the screening, the press began to leave the theater upon learning that Bergman had gone into labor and been admitted to a clinic. Magnani resigned herself to the situation, knowing that her film could not compete with Bergman’s baby in terms of media interest. Volcano was a critical and commercial failure. And so, in 1950, the boy, whom they named Robertino, was born. The press hounded the hospital relentlessly: One photographer passed off his pregnant wife as a woman in labor by adding padding to her belly; another crawled through a drainpipe to reach the actor’s balcony; others tried to bribe nuns at the hospital with a million lire; and still others, resigned to not being able to get a photo, created montages with Bergman’s and Rossellini’s faces and published them.

The baby’s birth presented a tricky situation. Legally, Lindström could have claimed custody because his divorce from Bergman hadn’t been finalized, which infuriated Rossellini, so they registered the child under Rossellini’s name and that of a “mother whose identity would be revealed later.” Eventually Lindström’s and Bergman’s divorce would be finalized in a Mexican court, where Bergman also decided to get married to Rossellini by proxy. A producer and a lawyer, friends of the couple, stood in for each of them while, on the outskirts of Rome, the real couple stopped into a small church near the Via Appia. “I knelt down and Roberto held my hand and then we went home and told all our friends that now we were married and drank Champagne.”

The couple settled in an apartment on Viale Bruno Buozzi in Rome and spent their summers in Santa Marinella, a town on the coast about an hour by train from the capital. After years speaking a mix of broken English and French, Bergman managed to learn Italian. The twins, Isotta Ingrid and Isabella, were born in June 1952, and the young family enjoyed a period of intense happiness. But their challenges were not over yet, of course. Bergman’s eldest daughter was becoming a teenager and her relationship with her mother was conducted solely through letters. Living with Rossellini wasn’t easy either. At the height of the tension with Lindström, the director had threatened to kill himself by either shooting himself or crashing one of his sports cars into a tree. Moreover, he was jealous, domineering (he let Bergman work only with him), and at times violent. This was all behavior that Bergman appeared to excuse as simply part of his Italian temper. This is how she describes one episode: “Things were flying through the air, I rush up and I took him in my arms and I started to say…and bang, he threw me against the wall so hard I almost broke in pieces. Well, I mean, I couldn’t do anything. Even to get near him was to risk your life.”

And then there was the matter of money. Rossellini, who had grown up in affluence, was used to living the high life, but every single movie he made with Bergman was a commercial failure. In 1956, director Jean Renoir approached Bergman to offer her a role in Elena and Her Men. Rossellini admired Renoir so much that he allowed his wife to work on the film, which finally put an end to the frustrating professional slump she had been stuck in for years. After that, she was offered the lead role in a French adaptation of Robert Anderson’s Tea and Sympathy. This is how Bergman recounts what happened: “Tea and Sympathy is the story of a young boarding-school boy who is afraid he is homosexual. Roberto was always disturbed by any sort of homosexuality. When I wanted to send Robertino to a Swiss or English boarding school, he blew up. ‘What!’ he screamed, ‘That’s where it all starts, in those boarding schools.’…The whole theme was too distasteful to him. It wasn’t the writing or the writer; he just hated the general theme. He also hated the fact that in the play I was going to prove to this boy that he wasn’t a homosexual, lead him into sex. The whole part disturbed Roberto immensely.”

Rossellini tried to forbid his wife from performing in the play, but she ignored him, arguing that she had signed a contract and that she loved the script. He fought back by emphasizing that people would laugh at her and that it would be a flop. On opening night, he snapped at her, “Be prepared when the first intermission comes for half of them to walk out.”

That’s not what happened. She was greeted with a thunderous ovation. “The house went wild. You couldn’t stop them. They were standing up and screaming, standing up and applauding, and the ‘bravos’ never stopped. Then I took my solo bow in the center of the stage, and as I bent over, I turned my head and looked at Roberto. Our eyes met. We looked straight at each other. I knew then my marriage was over even though we might stay together.”

He, for his part, stated: “I separated from Ingrid Bergman because it was impossible for me to continue. We loved each other very much; we had three children…she could return to the industry, but I didn’t feel comfortable in the role of the man whose wife brings home the money. We decided to separate, and that’s why I went to India, as far away from her as possible.” In India, Rossellini began a romance with the wife of his film’s producer, Sonali Dasgupta. Bergman wasn’t outraged when she found out; instead, she calmly proposed a divorce. He insisted on two conditions: that the children never travel to the United States and that Bergman never remarry. She laughed at the absurdity of the requests and refused, saying, “That was just Roberto.” (Shortly thereafter, she began a relationship with the Swedish theater producer Lars Schmidt, whom she married in 1958.)

The custody battle over the Rossellini children was also contentious, almost as much as the one involving Pia. There were mutual accusations of kidnapping and bad faith, and in the end, Bergman ceded custody to Rossellini. The children split their time between Rome and Paris, and Bergman frequently traveled to both cities to be with her children. The couple never completely drifted apart; they remained connected by Isabella’s health issues, including a back operation that nearly left her paraplegic, and the emotional bond that tied them together.

The world changed very quickly, and the former persona non grata won her second Oscar in 1957 for her role in Anastasia, which was accepted on her behalf by her friend Cary Grant. Bergman heard the news on the radio while taking a bath at her hotel in Paris, where she was staying while performing in Tea and Sympathy. She cried tears of joy in front of her son, Robertino, and soon returned to the United States to resume filming. Her rehabilitation within the industry was complete. She became one of the most respected names in cinema across various countries and languages—a living legend. In 1972, an apology was read into the Congressional Record for the attack against Bergman that had taken place in 1950. At the time she stated, “When I left for Italy, there was a senator in Washington who made a speech against me, and he ended by saying that out of Hollywood’s ashes will grow a better Hollywood.” Bergman didn’t realize her mistake, saying “Hollywood’s ashes” instead of “the ashes of Ingrid Bergman” until she later heard a recording of her comments. She laughed and laughed. “A line I had been waiting 22 years to deliver, and I mess it up!”

Vengeance was never a part of Anna Magnani’s plans after her separation from Rossellini, beyond that infamous plate of pasta. She continued her career working with Italy’s top directors and even won an Oscar a year before Bergman won her second statuette. When she was diagnosed with cancer, Rossellini sent her flowers despite the years that had passed. She wrote to him asking him to come see her, and from then on they saw each other regularly. Bergman recalls, “When I found out, I called her to tell her I was happy about what she was doing. The circle had closed: She had the man she had loved above all others close to her again.” When Magnani died in 1973, Rossellini agreed to have her buried in his family crypt, and she still rests there alongside him. (He died in 1977.)

Petter Lindström remarried in 1956 to Dr. Agnes Ronavec, with whom he had four more children. He continued his career as a renowned brain surgeon and died in 2000 at the age of 93.

Over time, Bergman would also be reunited with Pia after many years apart. At age 20, following a failed marriage, the young woman—feeling lost and unsure of what to do with her life—moved to Paris to live with her mother and Lars Schmidt. “I came to see Mom in a new light and fell in love with her. Few young women have such an extraordinary, cheerful, fun-loving mother—always ready to go out and do things, see this or that, go to the movies and the theater, dine out, run around, get up early, and go shopping. She had boundless energy. It was amazing.” Shortly after that, the grandmother of her half-siblings—Isotta Ingrid, Isabella (who would go on to become an acclaimed actor), and Robertino—passed away. Pia decided to go to Rome without speaking a word of Italian and take charge of the household, with a cook and a governess, to get to know them as well as find herself. “That way I’d be doing something and mattering to someone. I spent three years with them. Mom sent me money; I paid the salaries and took my siblings to the dentist, horseback riding, and to their lessons. It was a wonderful experience that, in a way, I needed. I didn’t have a specific place in the world; I couldn’t imagine a profession I felt called to; I felt lost. I needed to put down roots, find a place to live, convince myself that I was useful, help someone, and do something that wasn’t just for the sake of doing it.” Rossellini would visit them at lunchtime, talk on the phone about business, movies, and money, and leave after giving everyone a kiss. Pia continued: “I liked Italy. I was happy to be there. I was glad to have met them and to get to know Roberto firsthand, without relying on other people’s opinions or ideas about what he was like; I’m glad to have met Sonali and everyone else, because it helped me observe and understand everything that was happening, to get a sense of how things had come to be.”

Bergman concludes in her biography: “Someone wrote somewhere about my life with Roberto that I had found a world that for me was probably better than most of us ever attain. And that is true. I did have marvelous happiness with Roberto as well as deep troubles. But trouble is part of one’s life. If you have never had any trouble, if you have never cried, if you have never been really miserable and thought that you could not go on, what kind of understanding would you have for other people who are in trouble?…I think a person who is always happy would be a big bore. And Roberto was never a bore.”
