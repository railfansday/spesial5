## Flying Business Class Across Two Continents for the Price of One Imagine settling into a fully flat bed on a Boeing 787-9 somewhere over the Pacific, a warm towel in your hand, Japan behind you and Singapore ahead. You've already spent three days eating ramen in Tokyo — a stopover you added to your award ticket for a mere 5,000 extra points. This isn't a once-in-a-lifetime splurge. This is what the ChooseFI community calls a **nearly free** trip, engineered through one of the most underappreciated sweet spots in the entire travel rewards ecosystem. The Air Canada Aeroplan stopover rule is genuinely one of the last great loopholes in loyalty travel. Here's the math that makes your jaw drop: a business class flight from Toronto to Singapore — routed through Tokyo with a multi-day stopover — retails for **$8,000 to $10,000 in cash**. You can book that same itinerary for **70,000 Aeroplan points** (plus 5,000 for the stopover, so 75,000 total). At a conservative cash value of $8,000, you're extracting **10.7 cents per point** — more than double what most programs offer, and squarely within Aeroplan's documented range of **5.7 to 14.3 cents per point**. To put that in FI terms: if you value your points at even the low end of that range — 5.7 cents per point — 75,000 Aeroplan points are worth **$4,275 in travel**. At the high end, you're looking at **$10,725**. That's a portfolio-level return on the credit card spending and transfer partners you've already been accumulating. What makes this feel different from other premium redemptions is the *shape* of the trip itself. You're not just flying business class — you're building a mini round-the-world itinerary. Tokyo and Singapore in the same award ticket. Two of the world's great cities. Two business class flights. One redemption. The inefficiency here isn't accidental — it's baked into how Aeroplan prices stopovers at a flat 5,000 miles, regardless of where you stop. The program is essentially inviting you to stack experiences on a single award, and the ChooseFI community is the group paying close enough attention to accept that invitation.

The core insight is this: Air Canada Aeroplan uses a fixed partner award chart for Star Alliance flights, and it prices stopovers at a flat 5,000-mile premium regardless of routing complexity. That single policy decision transforms a standard business class award into a multi-destination adventure at a fraction of the combined retail cost.

Here's the mechanism. When Aeroplan relaunched in 2020, it did something most programs had abandoned years earlier: it kept a fixed-rate award chart for partner airlines. While programs like United MileagePlus moved to fully dynamic pricing — where award costs fluctuate with demand — Aeroplan locked in predictable rates for Star Alliance partners. The YYZ to SIN corridor in business class sits at a rate that reflects the geography, not the current demand spike or revenue management algorithm. That's the pricing inefficiency the ChooseFI community can systematically exploit.

Layer on top of that the stopover rule: Aeroplan allows one free stopover on one-way partner awards for just 5,000 additional miles. A stopover is defined as a connection of more than 24 hours. So your Toronto → Tokyo → Singapore routing becomes a Toronto → Tokyo (stopover 3 days) → Singapore itinerary, all on one award ticket, for 75,000 points total. You've essentially purchased two separate international business class flights for the price of one, plus a nominal surcharge.

The other critical piece: Aeroplan charges zero fuel surcharges on partner awards. This was a deliberate policy decision in the 2020 relaunch and it's a massive advantage. Programs like British Airways Avios and Singapore KrisFlyer can add $500–$1,500 in surcharges to long-haul business class awards. With Aeroplan, you pay government taxes only — typically $50–$150 on most itineraries.

A stopover is a deliberate break in your journey at a connecting city, where you stay for more than 24 hours before continuing to your final destination. Most loyalty programs treat stopovers as separate tickets, requiring you to pay miles for each segment independently. Aeroplan breaks this convention by allowing one free stopover on one-way awards, meaning your connecting city becomes a bonus destination at zero additional mileage cost. For example, a flight from New York to Tokyo with a 5-day stopover in Istanbul costs the same 75,000 Aeroplan points as flying directly — but you get to explore Istanbul for nearly a week as part of the trip. The stopover must be at a connection point that makes geographic sense for your routing, but with 26+ Star Alliance airlines and hundreds of hub cities, the options are vast.

The Itinerary: You depart Toronto Pearson (YYZ) on an ANA Boeing 787-9 bound for Tokyo Narita (NRT). Then you board a Singapore Airlines flight from NRT to Singapore Changi (SIN) in Business Class.

The Cash Price: A business class itinerary covering this exact routing — YYZ to NRT to SIN — retails in the range of $8,000 to $10,000, depending on season and carrier. Booking two separate one-way tickets (YYZ-NRT and NRT-SIN) in business class would easily exceed that, often landing closer to $10,000–$12,000 combined.

The Points Cost: 70,000 Aeroplan points for the base award + 5,000 for the stopover = 75,000 points total, plus approximately $120–$180 in government taxes. No fuel surcharges.

The CPP Calculation: At a midpoint cash value of $9,000, your 75,000 points are generating 12 cents per point. Even at the conservative $8,000 end, you're at 10.7 cents per point — well within the documented 5.7–14.3 cent range and significantly above the ChooseFI rule of thumb of 2 cents per point for most redemptions.

On the ANA 787-9 YYZ–NRT segment, you're in ANA's Business Staggered seat — a fully flat bed with direct aisle access, Japanese hospitality, and one of the best business class dining experiences in the sky. The Tokyo stopover is yours to explore however you like. Then Singapore Airlines' Business Class on the NRT–SIN leg offers its own benchmark product — a refined seat, excellent service, and arrival into Changi, consistently ranked the world's best airport.

You've visited two of Asia's most celebrated cities, flown on two world-class airlines, slept flat across the Pacific, and your out-of-pocket cash cost was under $200. That is the ChooseFI travel rewards mindset in its purest form.

## Why This Is Rated Moderate — And How to Handle It This sweet spot earns a **moderate difficulty** rating — not because the math is complicated, but because the booking process has more moving parts than a simple domestic award. **What makes it moderate:** 1. **Complex routing with a stopover** — Aeroplan's online tool doesn't always handle multi-city stopover bookings cleanly. You may need to call Aeroplan to finalize the itinerary, which adds a step and requires you to have your routing clearly planned before you dial. 2. **Partner award availability** — You're booking seats on ANA, Singapore Airlines, or other Star Alliance carriers, not Air Canada. Partner space is real and accessible (this scores a 4/5 for availability), but it requires searching specifically for it on aeroplan.com and sometimes triangulating with partner airline websites. 3. **Points transfer timing** — You need points in your Aeroplan account before completing the booking, which means coordinating a transfer after confirming space exists. Chase's instant transfer largely solves this problem. **Three tips to make it manageable:** - **Use aeroplan.com's multi-city search first** to understand what routing options exist before calling. Come to any phone call with a primary and backup itinerary already researched. - **Book 6–12 months out** using the 355-day window. More availability, more flexibility, less stress. - **Do one transfer before you need to** — move a small amount of points to Aeroplan just to practice the process and verify your accounts are linked. If you've successfully booked a domestic business class award before, this is a natural next step. The complexity is manageable, and the reward — business class across two ocean crossings with a Tokyo stopover — is absolutely worth the extra 45 minutes of work.
