Neanderthal heel bones reveal feet built for a demanding life

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

Neanderthals may have had flatter feet that helped them handle heavy loads and stay steady on rough ground.

A flatter foot may have helped Neanderthals support their heavy bodies and stay steady on rough ground.

A new study of fossil heel bones suggests that Neanderthal feet usually rolled inward and had a lower arch along the outside edge.

Researchers think this build may have suited the demands of their daily lives.

The study also found differences within our own species, Homo sapiens.

People who lived in Bologna, Italy, in the 19th and 20th centuries had slimmer heel bones than prehistoric hunter-gatherers, and their footwear and less active lives may help explain why.

Rita Sorrentino, now a professor at the University of Palermo, led the study, which University of Bologna professor Maria Giovanna Belcastro coordinated.

Belcastro said the analysis allowed the team “to highlight both an extraordinary biomechanical adaptation to withstand heavy loads and rough terrains, and the impact of modern life and footwear on our feet.”

No one can watch a Neanderthal walk, so the researchers looked for clues in the bones left behind. They used laser scans and CT scans to create 3D models of 71 adult heel bones.

Seven belonged to Neanderthals from sites in France, Spain, and Belgium. The youngest came from Spy Cave in Belgium and belonged to one of the last known Neanderthals in Europe.

The other 64 belonged to Homo sapiens. The oldest came from hunter-gatherers at Qafzeh and Skhul caves in Israel and dated to between 120,000 and 90,000 years ago.

The sample also included later Ice Age hunter-gatherers, Iron Age farmers from Sicily, and 46 people from a Bologna cemetery collection.

The researchers considered the Bologna group the least physically active population in the study.

The researchers marked 276 points along the edges and curved surfaces of each digital bone.

These points let them compare the heels in detail, while software estimated missing points on broken fossils from the parts that remained.

Neanderthal heels stood out in every comparison with Homo sapiens.

They were shorter from front to back, wider from side to side, and taller, although their overall size fell within the range seen in our species.

The biggest difference appeared at the back of the heel, where the Achilles tendon attaches. From behind, this area looked oval in Neanderthals and more triangular in Homo sapiens.

Neanderthals also had a much smaller bump underneath the heel, where a ligament that supports the arch attaches.

The researchers suggest this ligament may have provided less support, allowing the foot to bend more easily.

Belcastro linked the sturdy heels to the rest of the Neanderthal body.

“The robustness of the heel reflects the overall body structure of Neanderthals, which is characterized by a remarkable muscular and bone mass and an extremely intense level of physical activity,” Belcastro said.

“These physical characteristics indicate an adaptation aimed at promoting a greater load-bearing capacity on the back of the foot and a high mobility on uneven ground.”

The joints offered another clue. Where the heel meets the ankle bone, the joint surfaces were flatter and wider toward the outside of the foot.

That shape suggests Neanderthal feet normally rolled inward, a movement specialists call pronation.

Earlier research by Sorrentino’s group found signs of the same posture in Neanderthal ankle bones, on the other side of this joint.

In living people, rolling the foot inward brings more of those joint surfaces into contact and helps spread the load.

Neanderthals are thought to have carried more body mass than Homo sapiens, including more muscle, so this posture may have helped support their weight.

The researchers also propose that Neanderthals had a lower outer arch and a more flexible middle section of the foot.

Together, those features may have helped them stay steady as they crossed uneven ground.

An earlier study by the team found that a Neanderthal midfoot bone shared some features with those of adults diagnosed with flexible flatfoot. Most of those adults were overweight or obese.

Extra body weight may make feet roll inward and flatten their arches in people today. Still, similarities in bone shape do not prove that Neanderthal feet worked in exactly the same way.

Some differences may reflect ancestry rather than daily habits.

A small tunnel between the heel and ankle bones was shortest in Neanderthals, and its short length in other ancient human fossils suggests an inherited trait.

Within Homo sapiens, however, heel shape tended to vary with lifestyle.

In some ways, hunter-gatherers and even Iron Age farmers who also foraged had heels more like those of Neanderthals than those of the Bologna group.

Fossil footprints suggest that Neanderthals in Europe and later Ice Age Homo sapiens walked barefoot.

The Bologna group habitually wore heavy shoes and boots at work, and their heels differed from those of every earlier Homo sapiens group in the study.

Rigid footwear limits how far a foot can tilt from side to side.

The researchers suggest this restriction helped shape the joint at the front of the Bologna heels, which was taller and more curved.

The team also linked the Bologna group’s slimmer heels to footwear and a less active city life. But shoes alone cannot explain every difference.

“The morphology of our skeleton is not only the product of a strict genetic program, but also the consequence of physical stimuli and our daily life habits,” Belcastro said.

Ancestry, nutrition, and environment can also affect heel shape, the researchers wrote. The comparisons suggest that daily life matters, but they do not show how much each influence contributed.

The Neanderthal findings rest on just seven heels, several of which were damaged at the back and needed partial reconstruction.

The researchers also grouped men and women together because the samples did not allow them to test whether sex affected the results.

All the bones came from adults, leaving another question unanswered: how did Neanderthal heels develop that shape?

Their feet may have grown differently, responded to the strain of walking during childhood, or both.

A lower Neanderthal arch remains a hypothesis because bones alone cannot show exactly how a foot moved.

The researchers want future work to combine these shape comparisons with 3D models of foot motion to test the idea.

The study is published in the Journal of Human Evolution.
