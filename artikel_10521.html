Measles is back, and it’s a dire warning against shrinking the U.S. government

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Sometimes, you really don’t know what you’ve got ’til it’s gone. Take, for instance, the United States’ measles elimination status. Formally rescinded this week, after a 26-year run, the Pan American Health Organization’s new determination is about to make a lot of people realize what a quiet miracle it was to never have to think about measles.

Unfortunately, this genre of realization stands to become a lot more common. The official return of measles is just the starkest domestic example yet of the consequences that come with being led by an administration pot-committed to shrinking the federal government.

The U.S. spent the last century building institutions that provided federal education programs, environmental regulations, consumer protections, and other services benefiting the public. Although these institutions didn’t always work perfectly, they generally either improved U.S. citizens’ lives or shielded them from harm that local governments weren’t equipped to manage on their own. And for at least the 45 years since Ronald Reagan famously declared government was not the solution to America’s problem, but rather the problem itself, tearing down these institutions has been a holy-grail goal for some conservatives.

The most ardent proponents of that goal saw in Trump 2 a glimmering opportunity to dismantle the administrative state, as outlined extensively in the Project 2025 agenda. With the architect of Project 2025, Russell Vought, installed as White House budget director, the last two years have seen the steady evaporation or reorientation of institutions like the Department of Education, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB), and of course, the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS), whose current incarnation helped usher in the measles resurgence.

That result may be a preview of what happens when we lose mechanisms that solve certain problems so thoroughly that people seem to forget they were ever problems in the first place. It’s proof that weakening public protections doesn’t remove the need for those protections; it just makes us much less ready to combat everything they were protecting us from.

How we got measles again

Hours before news broke about America’s measles elimination status, current head of HHS Robert Kennedy Jr. used social media to respond to a different breaking news story. He posted on X an AI-generated image of himself mimicking the photo Amazon MGM used to promote Robert Pattinson as the new James Bond. In the image, a tuxedo-clad Kennedy is holding an enormous carrot instead of 007’s gun, and the caption reads: “Food. Real food.”

It would be hard to find a more apt representation of Kennedy’s tenure as the agency’s leader—a ridiculous, self-aggrandizing folly that depicts Kennedy as laser-focused on improving the quality of Americans’ food while actively ignoring the measles catastrophe he helped create.

Vaccination rates had already been declining since the pandemic, when hesitancy and outright opposition spread virally through so-called wellness influencers and other bad actors. Instead of addressing this trend as the dire threat it was, Trump put in charge of HHS someone who has promoted anti-vaccine positions for decades. Within months, Kennedy was undermining the conventional wisdom around measles vaccinations in settings as public as the Dr. Phil TV show.

Even more damaging: Kennedy kicked off his time at HHS by cutting $11 billion in state and local public-health grants. Those cuts forced health departments across the country to reduce staff, scale back critical services and, yes, either trim down or close various vaccination programs. Suddenly, the wastewater inspections, outbreak response teams, and buses traveling to areas riven by vaccine hesitancy were cut off at the knees.

Amid Kennedy’s efforts to change the childhood vaccination schedule and dispute the number of measles cases and deaths, the U.S. has reported 5,412 confirmed cases through November 13—more than double the 2,289 cases recorded in all of 2025. State health departments have also reported 10 measles-related deaths so far this year, up from three last year. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, cases have now surfaced in 46 states as well as Washington, D.C.

This is exactly the sort of result HHS’s supposedly bloated budget existed to prevent.

Shrinkage

The same governing philosophy that helped set U.S. measles prevention back to the 1950s has also led to downsizing in several other institutions over the past two years—with even more devastating consequences in some cases.

Shortly after Trump’s second inauguration last year, his White House functionally destroyed the United States Agency for International Development (USAID), the primary department coordinating foreign development and humanitarian assistance around the globe. Before January 2025, USAID had approximately 13,600 employees; by April, only 241 remained. Annual U.S. foreign aid disbursements fell by $26 billion throughout the year, and the White House canceled roughly $80.5 billion in USAID and State Department foreign aid awards.

Although Secretary of State Marco Rubio issued a waiver for lifesaving humanitarian assistance programs, he added a laundry list of politically charged exemptions. These included programs involving abortions, family planning, “gender or DEI ideology programs,” and transgender surgeries. Because HIV/AIDS prevention didn’t fall under that waiver, and thanks to severe cuts to the George W. Bush-created President’s Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief (PEPFAR), 1,700 HIV treatment sites have since closed. One-year estimates of the global death toll from the loss of USAID stand at around 781,000, and long-term projections are in the range of 14 million. 

Beyond the staggering human toll, there’s bound to be a less obvious domestic cost from the justified erosion of goodwill and diplomacy wrought by this administration’s callous actions.

Stateside, the Trump White House has also taken an axe to the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA), arguing that disaster relief should be shifted to individual states. Never mind the obvious risk of exhausting state budgets, creating uneven recovery patterns, and losing large-scale coordination for major crises—the prospect of weaning disaster-stricken Americans off the government teat was simply too attractive!

Through staffing reductions that reduced FEMA’s workforce by 17% last year, terminated grants that left billions in funding up in the air, and the addition of administrative hurdles, FEMA is now much weaker than it was before inauguration. That weakness has thankfully only reared its head in relatively mild ways thus far. No domestic disaster in Trump 2 has yet been substantially exacerbated by FEMA Lite. A lot of disaster fund requests in that time have been delayed or denied, however, and a disproportionate amount of them have been in Democrat-led states.

Meanwhile, slashing roughly 28% of the Treasury Department’s workforce has degraded taxpayer services; hollowing out the CFPB has cost Americans $15 billion in overdraft and credit card late fees alone; and gutting the Office for Civil Rights within the Department of Education has slowed or recklessly dismissed thousands of discrimination cases. 

Little by little, the value of having high-functioning federal infrastructure is starting to become harder for anyone not in deep denial to dismiss or ignore.

The toothpaste is out of the tube

Perhaps the worst part about Trump and his enablers diving whole-hog into dismantling the administrative state is that it won’t be rebuilt any time soon, if ever. It’s a whole lot easier to swing a wrecking ball, after all, than it is to mount an ambitious construction project (unless it’s a ballroom-size monument to the president’s ego).

When experienced staff leave specialized jobs, they often leave a crucial knowledge gap in their wake. When working relationships with outside agencies and other countries are severed, it takes a lot of time and effort to forge new ones—especially if those previous partners now consider your team unreliable. And when research and data collection projects come to a stop because of lost grant money, they seldom start back up again.

Many of the problems Americans now face because of our weakened institutions, and the ones we may face in the near future, cannot be resolved with the stroke of a pen from an incoming majority-Democrat government. For instance, Kennedy could be impeached on January 4 and billions in public-health grants restored the day after, and the U.S. would still be far away from where we stood in January 2025. An epidemiology professor at Johns Hopkins University recently estimated it will take at least five years to reclaim measles elimination status in the U.S.

Getting rid of measles required decades of unflashy work involving vaccination campaigns and other public-health initiatives. Getting rid of our elimination status took less than two years.

If we ever want to get it back, not only will we have to play the Uno Reverse Card on much of what’s happened during Trump 2, but we’ll also have to collectively let go of the myth that strong federal institutions are somehow the biggest problem in America, rather than a source of solutions.
