After 114 Years, the Beverly Hills Hotel Gets a (Night)life

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

The landmark’s biggest expansion yet adds a supper club, a cocktail bar and, at long last… a screening room.

Wearing a hard hat and a tailored suit, Beverly Hills Hotel general manager John Scanlon surveys the most significant expansion in the iconic Pink Palace’s 114-year history. Amid the BRRRAAAP! of drills and SKREEEE! of scraping metal, he outlines a slate of new amenities, a decade in the making, including a supper club, a screening room and a cocktail bar.

“I’m going to bed at night dreaming about this and waking up thinking about it,” says Scanlon, a Dorchester Collection veteran who previously ran London’s 45 Park Lane. “It’s the first thought that comes into my head in the morning: ‘Where are we at? Right now, in the lead-up to their simultaneous debut in the coming weeks, the answer is: a lot. Finalize the menus. Hire more staff. Approve the live acts. Close out permits. Eyeball every aesthetic detail. On a terrace, among the property’s paddle-shaped banana leaf plants, Scanlon says in his Irish brogue, with traces of exhilaration and exhaustion, “I’m obsessed.”

Later, over a lunch of McCarthy salads at the Polo Lounge, alongside the hotel’s new food-and-beverage head Megan Doherty, Scanlon explains that this is all an attempt to better serve guests, especially locals. A consultant — in his accounting, a “social anthropologist” — asked regulars what they wanted as the hotel considered renovations to a sizable swath of neglected, interconnected square footage, on the lobby level to the left of the red carpeted entrance, ranging from an unpopular bedroom suite to a ’90s-era business center that “nobody was using anymore.”

Foremost, it turned out, was a desire for a real nightlife option for party-hardly Beverly Hills. The solution is The Supper Club: a 94-seat, high-glam ovular room with skyline views of Century City that aims for neo-retro, akin to Delilah in West Hollywood. Open until 1 a.m., it will feature a no-photos policy, throwback palm tree motifs, coastal Italian dishes from chef Mattia Agazzi (previously of Gucci Osteria) and a sequence of live evening performances, including five-piece bands, which are curated by music director Alex Le Roux, an in-demand industry booker for events like the wedding of Brooklyn Beckham and Nicola Peltz. “This concept perfectly fits into the heritage of this hotel, but allows us to do something fresh,” says Doherty, who previously ran operations for star L.A. chef Evan Funke.

The research also produced a 14-seat screening room, which is tricked out for sound to handle record-release listening events. Its design by Ken Fulk (clients include Pharrell Williams and Gigi Hadid) is inspired by the Holmby Hills screening room of , Louis B. Mayer’s daughter and Hollywood’s preeminent hostess, where she showed films to friends before release.

For his part, Scanlon seems perhaps most enthused about a new, yet-unnamed cocktail bar — “very intimate, very elegant” — which he promises will rival the Dorchester’s offerings, as well as a lobby-oriented lounge, boasting a wraparound veranda, which will present afternoon tea service: “We’ll have bespoke teas and hand-painted china from Royal Crown Derby and custom sterling silver tea stands that have been made by Arthur Price. It’s a Claridge’s-level experience.”

A key challenge for the new spaces is competing with the landmark existing ones, chiefly the Polo Lounge, the Fountain Coffee Room and the Cabana Café. “Our guests know and love and really connect with them, and there’s this nostalgia there,” Scanlon explains. “They are tractor beams. So, with what we’re doing now, it’s been about, ‘What can we do that’s distinctive?’ They need to shine on their own.” He adds, looking up from his McCarthy salad, “a lot of places take the most popular dish off the menu. That’s crazy to me. Make another popular dish!”
