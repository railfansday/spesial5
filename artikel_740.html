Pioneer or coach-eater? The complex world of Pozzo's Watford

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

Gino Pozzo (left) has presided over 25 permanent Watford bosses in 14 years, with Alessio Dionisi the latest to take charge of the Hornets

A week is said to be a long time in football, and few clubs offer a more compelling test of that theory than Watford.

It has been 14 years since the Pozzo family completed their takeover of the Hornets and Alessio Dionisi's dismissal leaves them searching for the 25th permanent boss of their tenure.

The Italian's dismissal was announced on Sunday lunchtime with his 18th and final game in charge proving to be the 4-0 defeat by Sheffield United less than 24 hours earlier.

An average incumbency of about 210 days per manager has generated all too familiar headlines of the Hornets seeking a new face in the dugout, but is there method in the madness?

The Hornets were taken over by the Pozzos on 29 June 2012, with the family having also owned Serie A side Udinese since 1986.

Gino Pozzo has been in charge ever since, overseeing an ownership model rooted in a vast global scouting network famed for buying low and selling high, with Brazil internationals Richarlison and Joao Pedro among those flipped for big fees.

In the 61-year-old's first seven years, the club shifted from existential crisis to FA Cup finalists.

Under Javi Gracia, they came close to qualifying for Europe through the Premier League in 2019 before arriving at a Wembley showpiece where they came up second best to Manchester City.

But it proved to be a peak from which they have steadily spiralled.

Relegation from the Premier League a year later was followed by an immediate return - but a further relegation from which they have not recovered.

Last season's 16th-place finish was their lowest in the second tier under Pozzo - with a frequent turnover of manager seemingly utilised as a failed antidote to their stagnation.

That sense of inertia - and a poor start to 2026-27 which has left them 21st in the Championship, has brightened the spotlight on the owner and his methods.

With more than a third of the league season complete, Watford are one place outside of the Championship relegation zone

After back-to-back hammerings by Burnley and Cardiff City in October, former Hornets midfielder Tom Ince said he feared for the club he left at the end of last season.

"There are a lot of different beliefs on how the football club should move forward." Ince told the 72+ podcast.

He pointed to Gracia ending his second spell in charge last season after only three months - and with the club four points off the Championship play-offs - as a "big, big problem" that summed up their instability.

"There is so much control away from the manager at that club that it will always be an issue," said Ince. "The players don't see the manager as the focal point. He [Pozzo] is the one in control.

"That is always going to be a problem because you can't look at a manager and not have any fear [in them].

"I look at Watford, the players in the dressing room. You have a lot of young players, who have not played in England before. When this hiring and firing of managers is going on, they're just like 'OK, onto the next one [manager]'."

While Ince, now at League Two side Tranmere Rovers, added his respect for positives to Pozzo's practise, he is not alone in speaking out about the board's influence.

Speaking on No Tippy Tappy Football,, external former England boss Sam Allardyce said he had turned down the chance to manage the club prior to the appointment of Nigel Pearson in 2019.

"I got interviewed at Watford before Nigel [Pearson]," Allardyce revealed.

"[Sir] Elton [John] rang me up and said, 'Come on, Sam, take the job'.

"It was very nice having a phone call from Elton John, I must admit. But I met with the board, sat in the boardroom, and went, 'I can't take this job'.

"They were telling me what to do, saying, 'Play this way, play that player, this manager's not been playing that player' and so on."

Gino Pozzo has been Watford's owner since 2012

Ince and Allardyce's remarks came after former Watford owner John said he was "upset and disappointed" by the club's form after their 4-0 defeat by Burnley.

"I really feel for the supporters who have to watch this," he wrote in response to a club post on Instagram.

When supporter group The 1881 Movement published an open letter declaring a "complete loss of confidence" in Pozzo, the Italian, who has rarely spoken publically had no choice but to.

His meeting with prominent supporter Roy Moore was recorded and released as an extraordinary 114-minute podcast episode by the club's official channels.

"I don't have a social presence, I don't like to speak publicly, I don't enjoy that, I don't think it's productive for the work we do," Pozzo explained.

"I don't like to answer to speculation, because a lot of the people that are out there want a bit of visibility for themselves. Unfortunately social media creates this sort of character.

"I'm not expecting everybody to understand our model. In order to compete with the other clubs, we need to be something different. Otherwise we would not even be in the Championship right now."

Though Udinese is formally owned by his father, Giampaolo, Pozzo is still heavily involved in the management of the Italian club.

It is for this reason that he dismisses a reputation for devouring head coaches - Udinese boss Kosta Runjaic was, until his sacking earlier this month,, external Serie A's longest-serving manager having been appointed before the 2024-25 season.

"I tend to have a very good relationship with the coach, because the coach is a fundamental part," said Pozzo. "Whenever we sign a coach there is normally strong bond and good idea and we share that.

"It [sacking managers] has cost me a lot, I know I'm not looking good. Every time I change a coach, it's not a good image. But I cannot stand the fact that my image is worth more than the club's results. I put the club in front."

Watford have finished outside the top 10 in each of their past four Championship seasons

Before the win over Charlton Athletic on 17 October, some supporters tried to orchestrate a protest against Pozzo's ownership.

It is a far cry from the high point of his reign, when a tifo bearing his name was unfurled at Vicarage Road.

Tom Bodell, a Hornets supporter who contributes to the Watford Buzz podcast, said outside views of the club being a "basket case" who change manager two or three times a season was "the tip of the iceberg".

"We've seen over certainly three years, possibly five years now, a real reduction in overheads and outlay on the football side," he said.

"This summer we saw quite a bit of talent leave for reasonable money and next to nothing spent. We paid a fee for the goalkeeper Federico Ravaglia, but I believe that was it.

"Everybody else was either a loan, a free transfer or came from Udinese. So, you know, passing money from one pocket to the other on the same pair of jeans."

Bodell said chief executive and chairman Scott Duxbury had failed to deliver on promises of signing players with proven EFL experience, with concerns also mounting over the downgrading of the club's academy.

"There have been enough instances now of supporters feeling that they've been at best let down, at worst kind of misled or lied to that it is very hard to rebuild that trust," said Bodell.

"They [the owners] are at very real risk of leaving us in a worse position than when they arrived and I think that is probably the thing that has tipped people into action."

Alessio Dionisi lost more than half of his games in charge of Watford

But while the optics of Pozzo's model and methods have hit an all-time low for English observers, his image in his homeland is assured, according to Vincenzo Credendino, a journalist with Sport Italia.

"The [Pozzo] business model is a winning model because he is not an American billionaire or an Arab Sultan, he has to apply the model he worked on to buy young players and sell them," said Credendino.

"This is a model that allows Gino Pozzo with Udinese to compete in the Champions League, and reaching high levels at least in Italian football.

"If you look at many teams in Serie A, you can find many players who came out from Udinese. Piotr Zielinski, at Inter Milan is just one example."

For the frustration of Hornets' fans, it is a point that is difficult to deny.

Rodrigo de Paul, Alexis Sanchez, Alex Meret and Juan Cuadrado are all examples of players bought low and sold high at Udinese.

While at Watford, Abdoulaye Doucoure and Odion Ighalo are further examples of players to bring money into the Hertfordshire club.

"When you speak about Gino Pozzo, you are speaking about a piece of history." added Credendino.

"He's really pioneering. He has visions of the future. He has agreed a new perspective about many things, and many clubs look at his model and decided to make something similar."

So, is there method in the madness? Undoubtedly. But for those associated with Watford, the madness alone might prove to be Pozzo's lasting legacy.
