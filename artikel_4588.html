How to Lie with Statistics

Any psychology curriculum includes considerable training in statistics. I first taught statistics in 1996, and I have enjoyed teaching it ever since. Students are often surprised at how versatile and applicable this content is. Understanding statistics can help us better understand practical issues such as housing market trends, weather conditions, bird behavior at the feeder, the timing of blossoms in one's garden, people's behavior, our own behavior and internal workings, and more. Ultimately, statistics is a tool to understand the world around us. When students ask, "Why do I have to take statistics?" I am prepared with answers.

People often hear about "lying with statistics." While this topic is an issue, statistical inferences should be taken seriously. When examining information based on statistical processes, we need to ask certain questions to help us make sure the implications of the findings are warranted. Understanding statistics at a basic level helps ensure we take in information based on statistical findings with our eyes wide open.

To help people keep an eye out for situations in which someone might be "lying with statistics," here are five specific issues to watch out for when presented with statistical findings of any kind:

Most statistical processes yield both "effect sizes" and information on "statistical significance." In short, an effect size indicates how large the observed impact is; statistical significance speaks to whether it is likely that the effects obtained (however large they are) can be generalized to the broader population. As such, it is highly possible that a finding may be "statistically significant" even if it is a small effect size. For instance, a researcher might find that a drug designed to help people lose weight results in a statistically significant effect, with people taking the drug losing more weight than people taking a placebo. Yet the average difference in weight loss between the two groups might be only half a pound. Playing up the statistical significance, in this case, while neglecting to comment on the effect size, would be a bit dishonest.

Insinuating Causation with Correlational Data

Statistics, in and of itself, does not allow you to determine if one variable causes changes in another variable. The ability to infer causality is based on research design (such as the use of experimental methods) and not on statistical processes per se. That said, it is often the case that researchers who find statistical relationships among variables without using experimental methods may overstep in their verbiage. For instance, if a researcher found that household income is predictive of relatively high levels of exercise, it would be questionable if that researcher insinuated a causal relationship by saying something like "so having money seems to make people exercise more." As all psychology students (rightfully) hear from their professors, "correlation does not necessarily imply causation."

In many statistical processes, the researcher determines an "alpha level," which refers to the odds that an inference from a sample to a broader population might not be warranted. Researchers need to define that alpha level ahead of time and stick to that determination. If you obtain a finding that could be unreliable 3 percent of the time, and your alpha level was chosen as 1 percent from the outset, it would be problematic if you change your alpha level to 5 percent just to be able to say that your finding is "statistically reliable" and that the pattern that you found in a sample likely generalizes to the broader population.

In statistics, we often choose to use what is called a "one-tailed test" versus a "two-tailed test." In short, a one-tailed test should be used when you predict in a specific direction (for example, imagine a study that predicted drinking coffee before an exam corresponded to relatively high exam scores). A two-tailed test should be used when you are predicting a difference, but you do not specify the direction of that difference (for example, imagine designing the coffee-exam study to test whether coffee drinkers scored significantly higher or lower than everyone else). Given how our statistical processes operate, it is easier to obtain a statistically significant finding with a one-tailed test than with a two-tailed test. If you start with a two-tailed test, but obtain a result that is only significant if you use a one-tailed test, you are being dishonest with the data.

When we refer to a statistical fishing expedition, this is a study that includes a high number of statistical tests. By chance alone, some percentage of statistical tests will naturally show as statistically significant (implying that the findings in the sample represent how things are at the population level) even if the inference about the population is incorrect. If you conduct 100 statistical tests, by chance alone, some of the results will erroneously emerge as "statistically significant." These effects are considered spurious. If you conduct a high number of statistical tests, you, the researcher, need to take steps to reduce the possibility of presenting such spurious findings as meaningful.

The statistics field provides extraordinary tools to help us understand the world around us. Including the psychological states and behaviors of ourselves and others. Can researchers lie with statistics? Unfortunately, yes! Getting educated in the basics of statistics can help us process statistical-based findings in a way that we can help us catch such "lies." Want to understand the world around us better? Take a course in statistics.
