How Professionals May Overlook Intrafamilial Child Torture

Coauthored with Pamela J. Miller, JD., MSW

Intrafamilial child torture (“ICT”) usually involves a combination of physical, sexual, and psychological abuse, and intentional neglect, of a child, that is 1) severe and egregious, 2) prolonged without intervention, and 3) deliberate on the part of the offending parent (Miller, et al, 2021). By definition, ICT occurs within the family context and is perpetrated by someone who is considered a child's family member or caretaker.

ICT is different from most physical abuse, which is episodic and often impulsive. Most physical abuse results from parents losing control and/or accidentally hurting a child more than intended. Part of the harm of ICT is its insidious and all-encompassing nature; the child has few avenues for healthy development.

While ICT is thought to be relatively rare, it's still common enough that anyone who works with children or families over a decade is apt to encounter a child who has been or is being tortured. Often, the professional will mistake the ICT for simple abuse or neglect, and will fail to offer the child meaningful assistance.

We have each seen more than a few cases of ICT over the course of our professional lives (Miller about 50, Fontes about 15). Many professionals have never heard of ICT. But even when they have, they might mistakenly discard the ICT framework if the case before them doesn’t seem to fit their idea of what ICT looks like. This can have fatal consequences.

Our goal here is to expand professionals’ internal schema of how ICT appears by discussing diverse presentations that we’ve encountered. This is a stand-in until research devises undoubtedly different, more robust, evidence-based typologies. We link descriptions to real cases in the popular media, so readers can learn more.

Parents who torture their children don’t necessarily lead marginal lives. They may be wealthy professionals and appear to be caring and competent in their work lives (Miller et al., 2021). Ruby Franke, a parenting influencer, appears to have tortured her children and eventually pleaded guilty to aggravated child abuse. Larry and Carri Williams were popular at their church before their convictions involving assault on one child and the death by torture of another.

While most family violence is committed by men, some ICT involves a female caregiver as the primary perpetrator. Adoptive mother Maribel Loera recently pleaded guilty to second-degree murder, concealment of a body, and multiple counts of severe child abuse, thus avoiding the death penalty. According to media reports, two of her surviving children testified to years of severe violence, including burns, beatings, and a knife attack that left permanent scars. Professionals should be aware that ICT can be driven by female perpetrators.

Some child torturers’ lives follow a pattern of abuse and withdrawal from drugs; they could be especially dangerous at any part of this cycle. They may act in extremely harmful ways when “out of their wits” from alcohol, methamphetamines, fentanyl, or other substances. However, while the substances may make episodic violence more intense or more frequent, the substances are not the cause. By definition, ICT is purposeful and deliberate.

While there is plentiful research on the higher prevalence of child abuse and neglect among caregivers who abuse substances, we were unable to find research on child torture specifically. But we’ve both seen drug-addled caretakers who seem to have lost any sense of care or morality and torture a child or children over time, sometimes fatally.

The first study to analyze a large body of ICT cases statistically (Rycus et al. 2026) found that non-biological parents are more likely to be perpetrators of ICT than biological parents. This includes stepparents, biological parents’ romantic partners, adoptive parents, foster parents, and relatives caring for children. The case of Larry and Carri Williams (mentioned above) stands out here, where parents of seven biological children adopted two children from Ethiopia, and the mother tortured the older child, Hana, until she died from malnutrition and hypothermia. While the parents used abusive punishments with their biological children, they apparently tortured only their adopted children.

Sometimes one partner (usually but not always the man in heterosexual couples) coercively controls and tortures not just one or more children, but also his adult partner. In fact, the abuser’s torture of the children may be aimed at dominating his adult partner.

The abuser’s coercive control over his partner incapacitates her ability to protect the children effectively. She may try to deflect the abuser’s rage towards herself. She may sneak the children food when they are locked in the closet. Adult victims may fail to contact authorities due to a combination of fear that their children will be removed and fear that the abuser will kill them and the children before police arrive.

Vanity Fair reported that when the police came to arrest attorney Joel Steinberg and his girlfriend, Editor Hedda Nussbaum, for the death of their adoptive child, Lisa Steinberg, Nussbaum looked like a boxer who had sustained beatings. Nussbaum had a fractured nose and jaw, nine broken ribs, and gangrenous legs.

In some ICT cases, the parent(s) abuse all the children (e.g., the Turpins, Nixmary Brown, the family of Elisa Izquierdo). In others, the parents seem to differentiate among the children, targeting those children who are not biologically related (as in Hana Williams), who are the oldest, or who are disabled.

Many caretakers hide their tortured children from public view by homeschooling or “no- schooling” them. The under-regulation of homeschooling allows caretakers to torture children with no oversight from teachers or school officials who might otherwise make child abuse reports (Bartholet, 2020). In a typical case, the Hart parents removed their six adopted children from public school after reports of suspected child abuse. In several of the cases mentioned here, the caregivers withdrew the children from school, including the Turpin, Franke, and Williams cases.

At first glance, it might seem that anyone who tortures a child is “crazy.” However, research is lacking on the possible diagnoses of caretakers who torture their children. In our experience, ICT is often perpetrated by people who show no outward signs of mental illness. People in an acute psychotic episode might kill their children, perhaps thinking that this will “save” them or because they are suffering from persecutory delusions or postpartum psychosis. However, this filicide would fail to meet the criteria of ICT as a deliberate and prolonged series of acts.

Sometimes organized groups participate in children’s torture. These can include cults, along with sex and labor trafficking networks. These networks often include adult family members who inflict or permit the abuse, thus qualifying as ICT. One example is Children of God (now renamed Family International), where children were routinely beaten, whipped, sexually abused, and denied education. Sex trafficking of minors involves physical and psychological torture as well as neglect, thus qualifying as ICT when family members are involved.

Caregivers who torture their children can be of high or low social status; related by blood or not; substance abusing or sober; of any gender; and loving or cruel to other children in the home. They are apt to keep the tortured child hidden from public view, through home or no-schooling, or networks organized around cults or crime. Learn about ICT so you can tune in to signs that children you suspect are abused may be suffering far worse: torture.
