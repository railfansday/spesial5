The Hidden Costs Of DIY AI: Why The Real Expense Begins After The Prototype

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Sundar Balasubramanian is Senior Vice President of Product Management at Icertis.

​Artificial intelligence (AI) has created the false impression that enterprise software has become remarkably inexpensive to build. In many ways, that perception is justified. Business teams can now create applications, agents, workflows and copilots in a matter of days or weeks instead of months. Capabilities that once required large development teams and technical expertise are now accessible through natural language prompts and off-the-shelf AI services.

What many organizations often underestimate is that the prototype is the easiest part of the journey. The real challenge begins when AI applications become business-critical and require ongoing governance, support, security, maintenance, model management and operational accountability over time.

As companies rush to build AI-powered solutions, they are discovering that while the economics of creating software have changed dramatically, the economics of operating enterprise software have not. Reliability, accountability, governance, support and continuous improvement remain essential requirements. The difference is that organizations often encounter these realities later in the process, after the excitement of rapid deployment has taken hold.

One of the most exciting aspects of AI is the speed at which teams can transform an idea into a working application. This capability has unlocked a new wave of experimentation across the enterprise. Departments that previously relied on centralized development resources can now create solutions tailored to their own needs, accelerating innovation and expanding access to technology.

But leaders should be careful not to confuse a successful pilot with a scalable enterprise solution. An application serving a small group of users within a single department operates in a relatively controlled environment. The requirements change significantly when that same solution is expected to support thousands of employees across multiple business units, regions and regulatory environments. At that point, reliability becomes a critical business requirement.

This is where many organizations encounter an unexpected reality: Scaling an AI solution introduces more complexity than building it in the first place. What begins as a low-cost experiment can become a long-term operational commitment, requiring sustained investment in maintenance, support and continuous improvement as the business evolves.​

Organizations not only have to maintain the technology itself, but also ensure the expertise required to keep it effective. AI models, technologies and business requirements continue to evolve. Therefore, keeping pace requires ongoing investment in specialized skills, research and development, and product improvement long after the initial AI tool is deployed. This is one reason many organizations rely on specialized software providers rather than building everything themselves. They gain access to teams whose primary responsibility is staying ahead of technological change, benefitting from expertise and best practices developed across a broader customer ecosystem. Perhaps most importantly, they establish long-term accountability for support, product evolution and continuity.

Contract lifecycle management is a timely example of where the economics of building and operating often diverge. AI can accelerate the creation of contract review tools, clause extraction workflows or conversational interfaces. What is less visible is the ongoing investment required to support a system that manages sensitive commercial data, integrates with enterprise systems and provides the business context needed for AI to deliver reliable, actionable outcomes. These costs extend far beyond maintenance alone and appear in places organizations least expect.

AI has introduced a fundamentally different cost structure than traditional enterprise software. Consumption, utilization and optimization matter now more than ever before as the economics of how AI is deployed become just as important as what it can do. Specialized software is engineered to address business requirements at enterprise scale, while minimizing token spend.

Generalized AI applications, in comparison, can turn what appears to be a modest operating cost into a substantial recurring expense as usage grows because they are not purpose-built for the complex, high-volume processes of an enterprise.

AI consumption has already become a boardroom discussion, shaping budgeting, governance and long-term planning in much the same way that cloud computing did. Many organizations initially focused on the flexibility and speed cloud platforms provided, only to discover later that ongoing consumption required deliberate oversight to keep costs aligned with business outcomes. As adoption scales, organizations need to consider not just where AI is being used, but how to apply it most efficiently for the needs of their business.

Many organizations approach governance as something that must be addressed before deployment. Policies are established. Reviews are conducted. Controls are defined. Once those steps are complete, there can be a tendency to assume the governance challenge has been solved.

In reality, governance often becomes more important after deployment than before it. Once employees begin relying on AI to support decisions and workflows, new questions inevitably arise. Who owns the application? Who approves modifications to prompts or underlying models? How should outputs be monitored and evaluated? What metrics should be used to measure performance and business value?

These are not questions that can be answered once and forgotten. They require continuous attention. Governance evolves from a compliance-oriented exercise into an operational discipline focused on ensuring that controls remain effective as technology, regulations and business requirements continue to change.

Governance also carries direct cost implications. Without clear ownership and oversight, organizations can encounter duplicated solutions, inconsistent outputs, excessive model usage and unexpected expenses associated with resolving issues related to accuracy, security or compliance. What initially appears to be a faster path to innovation can ultimately create inefficiencies that erode the value the initiative was intended to deliver.

Organizations that underestimate this responsibility may find themselves devoting far more time and resources to managing governance than they anticipated when building the original solution.

The broader lesson, however, is that AI has changed the cost of building software, but it has not eliminated the responsibilities that come with operating enterprise technology. The most successful organizations look beyond the speed of development and evaluate AI through a total cost of ownership lens. The prototype may be inexpensive. The real investment begins when the solution becomes part of how the business runs.
