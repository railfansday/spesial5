The Secret to Saving Shellfish From Acidifying Oceans Could Be Hiding in Crushed Shells

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

Crushed shells helped young clams grow larger shells in laboratory tests, showing how a long-standing Indigenous coastal practice can offer local protection against ocean acidification.

For a young clam, the surrounding water can threaten the shell it is trying to grow. When seawater absorbs excess carbon dioxide from the atmosphere, it becomes more acidic, making shell growth more difficult and energetically costly for animals such as clams, oysters, and mussels. Acidic seawater can even dissolve shells. Larvae and juveniles are thought to be especially vulnerable to this process, known as ocean acidification.

Indigenous communities have long added crushed shells to coastal sands to help keep these ecosystems healthy. Research from the University of California, Davis now helps explain how this material, called shell hash, can protect young clams from some of acidification’s effects. Published in Biogeosciences, the study connects the chemistry of water between sand grains with changes in clam growth and gene activity.

At the UC Davis Bodega Marine Laboratory, researchers raised baby Pacific littleneck clams in four conditions. Two groups burrowed in sediment beneath either non-acidified or acidified seawater. The other two experienced the same seawater conditions, but their sediment contained shell hash. During the 90-day experiment, the team tracked shell growth and sampled the seawater three times a week, monitoring acidity, salinity, temperature, and dissolved oxygen.

“We found that, on average, clams grew larger shells when shell hash was present in their treatment,” said study first author Hannah Kempf, an environmental scientist at the California State Water Board who graduated from UC Davis with a Ph.D. in earth and planetary sciences in 2024. “Interestingly, we also found that, on a genetic level, the ‘acidification’ signal was dramatically minimized when shell hash was present.”

The crushed shells altered the chemistry of the water filling the tiny spaces between sand particles, reducing its overall acidity and creating better conditions for shell growth. That finding places the protection at a very local scale, in the water surrounding the burrowing clams.

“Adding crushed shells removed the negative genetic changes that occur when young clams are raised in acidified water,” said study corresponding author David Gold, an associate professor of earth and planetary sciences at UC Davis.

To examine gene activity, the researchers collected samples of the clams’ mantle tissue, which produces their shells, after 90 days and used them for follow-up gene sequencing. The analysis revealed gene expression profiles, or patterns of activity across genes. In clams raised in acidified seawater with shell hash, those profiles were largely indistinguishable from those of clams raised in non-acidified water.

“This was the first time the question was studied from a genetic perspective,” Gold said.

Traditional ecological knowledge provides significant evidence that shell hash benefits shell-making animals, said study co-author Tsim Schneider, an associate professor of anthropology at UC Santa Cruz. In the Pacific Northwest, some Indigenous communities build large-scale structures that expand intertidal spaces, the areas of shore between high and low tide. These structures promote habitat for invertebrates and other marine organisms.

“For me, one of the interesting aspects of this research is the potential to engage more fully with tribes on designing projects that promote their long-standing expertise and Indigenous science,” said Schneider, who is also affiliated with the National Science Foundation’s Center for Braiding Indigenous Knowledges and Science. “If there’s a history of adding shell hash to clam beds in California, for example, western science might be able to better support communities interested in reawakening dormant cultural knowledge and coastal stewardship practices.”

Reference: “Shell hash promotes growth in Pacific littleneck clams (Leukoma staminea) by altering pore water chemistry” by Hannah L. Kempf, Alyssa J. Griffin, Gabriel Ng, Tessa M. Hill, Tsim D. Schneider, Sandra J. Carlson and David A. Gold, 29 October 2026, Biogeosciences. DOI: 10.5194/bg-23-7627-2026

The research was supported by a California Sea Grant and the Russell J. and Dorothy S. Bilinski Fellowship.

“When seawater absorbs EXCESS carbon dioxide from the atmosphere, it becomes more acidic, making shell growth more difficult and energetically costly for animals such as clams, oysters, and mussels. Acidic seawater can even dissolve shells.”

What is “excess” CO2? The oceans are alkaline (basic), defined as having a pH more than 7. While there are diurnal and seasonal changes in the pH, the average pH is commonly claimed to be about 8.1 today, supposedly having declined from about 8.2 over the last 100-years. However, Konrad Krauskopf, formerly of Stanford University and arguably the father of modern geochemistry, stated in his 1967 textbook, Introduction to Geochemistry, “The pH of ocean water sampled near the surface is almost always between the narrow limits 8.1 and 8.3. Locally and temporarily [e.g. up-welling of abyssal water] it may deviate from this range, …” In other words, the pH claimed for today (without an attendant uncertainty) is not outside the bounds observed about 60 years ago. Make of it what you will, but a claim for a mean value (8.1), without at least a 1-sigma uncertainty, and no mention if the range is skewed, is not what I would call high quality research. And indeed, the claim for a decline is based on a model, not empirical measurements.

The quoted claim from this article is also grammatically incorrect. One can have a dilute solution become concentrated, or a concentrated solution become more concentrated, but it cannot become ‘more’ concentrated if it starts out dilute. This kind of careless, lack of attention to detail is seen all too often in published work on climatology.

Lastly, because marine organisms have evolved adaptations to changing pH and temperatures, which influence solubility, they have the ability to control pH, with the expenditure of energy, at the leading edge of a growing shell. Living organisms also grow chitin, which is less soluble than calcite/aragonite, and also exude mucous that isolates the shell from seawater. Thus, it is the shells of dead organisms, and the larvae and juveniles of calcifiers that have not yet had time to develop their defense mechanisms that are most at risk of damage by lowered-pH — but still basic — seawater.

“Ocean Acidification” is a misnomer that has its origins in politics rather than chemistry. Krauskopf has also stated that the oceans have probably always been basic and probably always will be because of the (bi)carbonate and borate buffering systems that stabilize pH. You can’t have “acidification” taking place if there are natural barriers to seawater ever becoming acidic. At best, it may approach a neutral (H+/OH- = 1) condition. Thus, a better description is “neutralization.” However, that isn’t as scary as a fictional ‘acidification.’

“Traditional ecological knowledge provides significant evidence that shell hash benefits shell-making animals, …”

If the benefits of ‘shell hash’ were so obvious even to those unfamiliar with the Scientific Method and no access to modern chemistry labs, why did so many coastal people develop huge piles of discarded shells — called “middens?”

“…, Pacific Northwest commercial oyster hatcheries experienced widespread collapse associated with coastal acidification.”

That is a misstatement. As commonly used, “Ocean Acidification” refers to a change in pH resulting from solution of CO2 derived from the atmosphere. What actually happened was a transposition of abyssal water for water blown away from the coast by winds, leading to approximately neutral water (~pH 7) up-welling and replacing estuarine brackish water. The process was NOT “Ocean Acidification,” even as commonly misused today.

Note that the experiment was performed with natural seawater with a pH of approximately 7.8, considerably lower than than the claimed reduction in the open ocean over the last century, and “experimentally acidified” seawater without specifying what acid was used to lower the pH to 7.3. This is a serious oversight to not specify what acid was used. Anything other than carbonic acid raises questions about the behavior of the proxy acid because it may not participate in the (bi)carbonate buffering. If something like hydrochloric acid was used there could be contaminants present in commercial acid that is toxic to the organisms, and its solubility is much higher than carbonic acid. That is, carbonic acid alone may not allow the pH to get to 7.3. This is precisely why laboratory experiments often fail to confirm empirical observations. One changes one variable at a time to determine what variable does what. This is poor experiment design and provides them with little opportunity to respond to criticism!
