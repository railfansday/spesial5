Arizona Trail: Part 4

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

The transition from a double zero back to the trail was especially tough this time around because I was sick. Seppo and Emma had both come down with colds, and after sleeping next to them in a garage at a trail angel’s house, it seemed the sickness had made its way to me too. It was funny because up until that point, I had been finding the trail relatively easy. The terrain was mostly cruisy, and we weren’t hiking huge miles. I tried to convince myself that getting a cold was just a fun little extra challenge to add to the trail.

I stuffed my red bandana, now officially my snot rag, into the water bottle holder on the side of my backpack and set off. Everyone had already left camp, but I was moving a little slower given the circumstances. I quickly caught up to Alex on an uphill, who had somehow remained unaware that we were gaining 5,000 feet that morning. After breaking the news to him, I continued on.

With my trusty snot rag in hand, I slowly climbed the mountain, with stunning views gradually revealing themselves along the way. As I crested the ridgeline, I noticed some concerning dark clouds hovering nearby. Before long, I found myself caught in a mixture of hail and rain. I spotted Seppo and Emma up ahead, who had found a lovely log to sit on. By the time I joined them and started eating lunch, the rain had stopped, giving me a chance to pull out my tent and let it dry.

Alex showed up a little while later, and we were both feeling pretty tired and less than thrilled about the morning’s events. After a long lunch break, we continued on, heading back down all the elevation we had just worked so hard to gain. Some people might refer to this as “pointless ups and downs,” or PUDs for short, but I like to think of it as the trail making us work for Mexico.

We moseyed along all afternoon, moving at barely 2 mph despite the fact that it was all downhill. Alex and I chatted about an assortment of topics, ranging from our dream of opening a guiding business to his attempts to teach me about the stock market. As the sun began to set, we decided to aim for the next good campsite. The only problem was that the next good campsite never seemed to come. The terrain was incredibly slanted, and after passing a spot that was barely half the size of our tent and deciding we didn’t want to attempt squeezing into it, we pulled out our headlamps and kept going.

We eventually made it to 21.4 miles before calling it a day at one of the most crowded campsites we’d seen on trail so far. Clearly, we weren’t the only ones struggling to find a decent place to sleep. We squeezed our tent in among eight others, trying to be as quiet as possible since it was already 7 p.m., dark, and everyone else seemed to be fast asleep.

When I woke up the next morning, I was unsurprisingly still tired and sick. But the sun was shining, and we had 12 downhill miles to cover before lunch. I plugged in my earbuds, put on some Noah Kahan, and started hiking. The halfway mark was calling.

Up ahead, I spotted Kayla, Seppo, and Emma already working on building the 400-mile marker out of rocks. After taking some pictures and celebrating the halfway point, we continued on, the sun warming us with every step.

The day before, Alex had told me that this was the hardest part of the hike for him. We had just as much trail ahead of us as we had already completed, and that felt daunting. He felt like he was ONLY halfway. I, on the other hand, was feeling sad because we WERE halfway. Having already completed two trails of over 2,000 miles each, this trail was feeling a little too short for my liking. I never wanted it to end.

Later that afternoon, we passed a group of cows that mooed loudly at us before we began another climb. As I gained elevation, the sun started to set, and Emma and I took turns setting the pace up the switchbacks. Seppo and Kayla had booked it up the climb, eager to reach camp before sunset, while we could see Alex far below, just beginning the switchbacks as we approached the ridgeline.

The sunset was gorgeous. With the days getting shorter, we now had the privilege of watching both the sunrise and sunset every single day. This one was especially spectacular. Emma and I kept stopping to take pictures as the sky shifted from orange to pink to purple. With half a mile left to camp, Emma went ahead while I waited for Alex. We finally arrived just as the sun dipped below the horizon.

Our campsite was perched on the side of a hill, and the colorful sky soon gave way to a blanket of sparkling stars. We sat in a circle on the ground, eating dinner and enjoying the evening. It was my favorite campsite of the trail so far.

The next morning, we watched the sunrise over Lake Roosevelt as we made our way down a dirt road. The first 11 miles of the day were spent walking along dirt roads, which are actually some of my favorite miles on trail. I find dirt roads so fun and exciting, which is quite a hot take among thru-hikers. There’s just something about the monotony of a dirt road, kicking up dust with every step and occasionally having an ATV drive by, that makes me feel like a cowboy. Dirt road miles also tend to fly by much quicker than trail miles, and the morning seemed to disappear in no time.

We stopped for a quick lunch at a creek before continuing on. Unfortunately, the afternoon was quite the opposite of the morning and brought some of my least favorite terrain on the trail. It was beautiful, but we spent much of the afternoon walking along washed-out cliffs, with the trail precariously carved into the mountainside. Progress was slow, but I put on some podcasts and tried to zone out rather than overthink just how slowly I was moving.

Eventually, I made it to camp and set up our tent on a lovely patch of sandy ground. Once the sun went down, the temperature dropped quickly, and I was more than grateful to crawl into my sleeping bag for the night.

In the morning, we crossed into the Four Peaks Wilderness and began descending into the low desert. The terrain slowly shifted from high desert shrubs to towering saguaro cacti stretching as far as I could see. Our first saguaros of the trail! This was exactly what I had imagined the entire Arizona Trail would look like. Of course, within the first two days of hiking, I had already realized that wasn’t quite the case. Maybe I should do a little more research before setting off on these trails. Still, I was so stoked to finally be surrounded by some of the oldest cacti in the world.

As we neared the road crossing and our next resupply point, I spotted a black truck with a white camper parked nearby. It looked strangely familiar, and when I reached the road, I realized it was Kayla’s husband doing trail magic! He whipped up smoothies for us, and we happily feasted on fruit and soda. I can confidently say that smoothie was some of the best trail magic I’ve ever had.

After saying our thanks, we headed over to the Lake Roosevelt Marina for a rather unconventional resupply. The marina was stocked with food geared more toward boaters than thru-hikers, but I managed to make it work. We grabbed a quick burger at the restaurant before heading back out onto the trail. Three miles later, we found a spot to call home for the night.

Immediately after leaving camp that morning, we entered a wash and followed it for the first couple miles of the day. The trail through this section was heavily overgrown with spiky bushes, so we decided to follow the more open wash instead. It turned out to be quite fun, weaving our way through the canyon with cacti growing all around us. It felt like a little adventure of its own. The path had been carved out by flowing water over countless years, and we got to walk along the dry remains of that process, making our way over sand and gravel through the canyon.

Eventually, the wash narrowed, and we hopped onto a dirt road that led us back to the trail. The road was steep, and we trudged uphill under the exposed sun. After what felt like ages, I finally crested the ridgeline and found myself walking along relatively flat ground, with views stretching out in every direction. It was stunning, but I was having a hard time appreciating the beauty. I was tired and just wasn’t feeling it. I stopped to dump the rocks out of my shoes, and Alex caught up with me. We decided to take a lunch break in the shade. After shoving a tortilla stuffed with pepperoni, cheese, and crushed sour cream and onion chips into my dry mouth, we continued on.

The afternoon was just as hard as the morning. We continued climbing deeper into the Superstition Mountains, at one point gaining 1,000 feet in just over a mile. It was absolutely brutal, and both Alex and I ran out of water. It was the only time that happened during the entire trail. Eventually, we reached some cruisy downhill, but by then, it was too late. The sun was setting, we’d managed just fifteen miles in nine hours, and I was completely exhausted. We decided to call it an early day and scrolled through the FarOut comments, looking for a flat spot to camp a little farther ahead.

By the time Alex and I arrived at the campsite in the dark, all of our friends and fellow thru-hikers were already set up. It turned out we weren’t the only ones who had struggled that day. Everyone agreed that the 17 miles that day had been the hardest on trail so far.

I woke up grumpy, with the difficulties of the previous day still weighing on my mood. Luckily, the miles came much easier that morning, and I quickly walked myself out of my funk. There was another storm on the way, the weekly plague of Super El Niño looming over us. Before dropping down into the low desert, I checked the weather report using the lingering one bar of cell service I had. Rain, rain, and more rain. There were over four inches of rain predicted over the following three days. Not exactly ideal hiking weather.

My brain immediately went into problem-solving mode. What dirt roads could we bail out on? Could we flip south, where the weather might be less severe? Could we road walk along the highway? I didn’t want to take a triple zero, but I also didn’t want to put myself in a dangerous situation by attempting to hike through flash flooding and thunderstorms.

I came up with a plan and proposed it to Alex, Seppo, and Emma. What if we hiked into town that day, did a super quick resupply and laundry, then hiked out early the following morning? We could crush some miles over the next two days and hopefully reach the next town before the worst of the storm hit. To my surprise, everyone agreed to my slightly crazy plan. I think we were all getting pretty tired of taking zero days just to wait out the storms.

We reached a dirt road crossing, where we met Ace and his dog Charlie. Ace made us pizza and gave us beer before we headed off to walk the last couple of miles into Superior. Little did I know that Ace would become such an important part of my Arizona Trail experience, but that’s a story for a later blog…

We had the most efficient town stop of my thru-hiking career. It certainly helped that Superior is a tiny town, with pretty much everything a hiker could need within a quarter-mile walk of the motel. We arrived at the motel around 7 p.m. and immediately got to work. We threw our laundry in the washing machine, walked to a Mexican restaurant for dinner, then headed back to the motel to switch everything into the dryer. Next, we walked to Family Dollar to resupply for the next two days. Back at the motel, we collected our clean laundry, showered, and were fast asleep by 10 p.m. Pretty efficient, right?

I fell asleep scrolling through weather reports and studying topo maps of the next section, trying to find potential campsites that weren’t too high and exposed in case of thunderstorms, but also weren’t too low in case of flash flooding. I was nervous, but also excited. Another adventure was just around the corner.
