Virginia Feito on the Film Adaptation of Her Novel ‘Victorian Psycho’

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

The writer is the lowest form of life on a movie.- Martin McDonagh

When I finished editing my second novel, Victorian Psycho, I could not imagine we would be shooting the film adaptation the following year, mere months after the book’s publication. Generally, making a movie is one big miracle produced by an amalgamation of thousands of other tiny miracles. But when I met Zach Wigon, I understood that this was a joyfully tenacious person who was going to make this movie if it cost him his life.

I decided then to undertake this journey that would take me metaphorically to Hollywood but geographically to a county just outside of Dublin, where we would recreate the Ensor House of my novel. Among film stars, an Irish crew, and (rumored) ticks, the adaptation of Victorian Psycho—based on my screenplay, which is based on my novel, which is based on my ego, I guess—was born. Throughout the shoot I kept a sort of pseudo diary in order to remember every surreal detail from which I still haven’t recovered. I’ve chosen to reproduce some of it here.

Director Zach Wigon and producer Lisa Kelly call me from the set on the first day of shooting (Zach and I have agreed I won’t visit the set until after the first week so the cast and crew can acclimate without me hovering over them, because I am an intense fame whore, and also, I suppose, to signal I’m not important enough to pull a P.L. Travers and attempt to boycott the movie).

Over the phone, the producer says she can’t wait to meet in person. I tell her I’m underwhelming.

They promise to send me the dailies. I spend all day waiting for the dailies. The dailies never come.

At around 9 p.m., I spiral because I don’t know whether to celebrate with Champagne or KFC. I decide to have both.

I finally get the dailies. I fall in love with Maika Monroe’s character so hard it’s like I have an obsessive crush circa 2006 whose Facebook photos I’m compulsively looking at. But in this case it’s the same scene throughout five almost identical takes of Maika calling a 10-year-old boy a little idiot.

Over the phone, Zach mentions concerns about a specific scene because we don’t have the budget for that many extras. I get carried away and suggest shipping over a whole battalion of free extras from Spain because, in my head, it’s going to be me and my friends, drunk and decked out in period clothing like we’re at a cheesy bachelorette party. One friend starts making childcare arrangements and another cancels her Holter monitor appointment so she can come to the shoot. What could go wrong?

I am informed we are probably no longer going to need the extras, and I feel a huge wave of relief as I uninvite my excited friends. I pack a suitcase for Ireland. It weighs 57 pounds.

I have a steak on the plane to Dublin at, like, 3 p.m., because when you’re flying business and somebody offers you steak, you take it.

The hotel room is wonderful, but I jokingly tell Zach over the phone that it’s on a first floor, so someone might crawl up and break in through the window and rape me, and he laughs at first but then is concerned and tells me to request a room on a higher floor, and I tell him it was a joke, but it wasn’t really.

I fill up the hotel room bathtub and call reception for a glass of red wine because I am a queen and I am queening. The glass doesn’t come, so in my anxious impatience I attempt to open a bottle of red wine sitting above the minibar. The cork crumbles because I’ve never understood how to use a corkscrew—like the compass in math class, I just faked my way through it my entire life and hoped for the best. So I call reception for another glass of red wine. They bring me all the glasses of wine and also properly uncork the bottle, and I watch Seinfeld in the bathtub while I drink red wine. I get drunk and paint my nails.

I arrive on set slightly Bromazepamed. It is extremely surreal to exist in a set labeled “Mr. Pounds’s Bedroom.” I am used to living in the fictional world inside my head but not used to it becoming more real than real life itself.

Maika hugs me when we meet, and I swallow a sob and refrain from smelling her hair (partly because it is, in fact, a wig). Everyone on set appears so happy to meet me, I suspect a Good Bye, Lenin! situation in which I have been diagnosed with cancer and for some reason the cast and crew all know but I don’t know. This isn’t the plot of Good Bye, Lenin!, but you’ll know what I mean if you’ve seen it.

Zach asks me to read the VOs in every scene, which means I’m in the scene with Ruth Wilson, Jason Isaacs, and Maika, and I’m about to have a stroke.

Jason eats for real in every single take and has already gulped down seven sausage rolls, so if I fuck up my VO, I will kill myself.

Suddenly experiencing fiction collectively feels very superior to experiencing it privately. This does not bode well for the rest of my career as a novelist.

There is a scene in a cramped drawing room in which I must read the VO, but I’m supposed to hide from the camera, so I crawl on all fours behind an antique couch and grovel my way around the room while the boom op steps on my hands. I secretly think the boom op hates me because I’m always in his way and have an irritating habit of apologizing when he slams into me.

I start developing Irish inflections, so I say it, “I start developing Irish inflections,” but the Irish seem happy about it, so I pursue it.

Jason Isaacs starts doing card tricks between takes because Jason Isaacs is actually magic and also indefatigable. Only one thing could ever defeat Jason Isaacs, and it’s the ghost allegedly living on the third floor.

Even though Zach asked me to oversee the second unit, I don’t think anyone believes I’m actually going to do this—and between you and me, I don’t really even know what a second unit is. It doesn’t help I can’t concentrate on what we’re shooting because we’re in the middle of the woods and someone said there were ticks, so I’m wrapping Sellotape around my ankles, hoping this way they won’t be able to crawl up my legs.

Like your worst trauma, I insert myself into the movie forever via a small cameo. I literally just have to stand in a wig while I smile and nod, but I do it in such a way that I ruin every single take. “Just smile…like a normal person,” the second-unit director says. I reason Zach can just cut me out of the movie, and I will only have wasted everybody’s time and money. There are worse things, like smiling normally but having no cameo. I cope by chewing on another Bromazepam and another chocolate bar. (Every morning I take the chocolate bar from the hotel minibar. It will invariably become an asset in the long afternoons on set, kind of like in prison or high school.)

I change hotel rooms because I have developed an intense FOMO with which I’m always fearing there is a better hotel room for the same price somewhere in the vicinity. This FOMO is also rampant on set between the different units—in the first unit, you have the cinematographer, Nico Aguilar, laughing in the sunlight, talking about color palettes, while in the second unit, we’re shooting clocks and screaming at each other because we still have 17 establishing shots to go.

Anyway, I change hotel rooms, and the new room is so superior to my previous room it’s like an entirely new hotel. I consider changing hotel rooms further. Perhaps I can somehow upgrade my way into a presidential suite.

My parents visit the set. My dad peers over his glasses at the fake lobster cake, and when I tell him the toilets are in trailers, he sighs gravely.

Jason Isaacs convinces my dad to take up tennis again even though my dad’s not supposed to play tennis after his seven hernias. But my dad is so inspired by this actor, who is not a doctor but whose performance in The Patriot my dad can’t help but praise at least once a month, that he will immediately start playing tennis again upon his return to Spain, and whatever medical issues arise henceforth shall all be Jason Isaacs’s fault.

At the Shelbourne restaurant that night, my mom tells the sommelier how she can’t drink red wine anymore because it makes her anxious, and he kneels on the floor beside her and places a hand on his heart as if she were confiding something intimate to him like her fear of death.

Suddenly, real life feels less real than fictional life.

Obama is visiting Dublin and he’s booked the entire floor, so he essentially kicks me out of the Shelbourne (probably to stay in the JFK Suite next door to my room, which is disappointingly predictable), so I go to The Hoxton, because what is life if you’re not constantly changing hotel rooms? The Hoxton is really lovely, but I get OCD about the light bulbs in the room because they appear mismatched, so I ask housekeeping to change them, but they claim not to have any extra light bulbs, which sounds suspect—like, what do they do if a light bulb suddenly goes out? Do they just never replace it?

We end the shoot at 5 a.m. I am wearing a thick puffer coat and a thermal T-shirt and a furry hat, plus fur-lined boots over my Sellotaped ankles, although I wonder, If ticks can survive this temperature, they kind of deserve to live off my blood. Nobody else seems as concerned about the cold as me, but then again, most of them are Irish.

Sadly, when we wrap, we are all so exhausted everyone races to clean up and leave, and it is sort of anticlimactic. I don’t know what I was expecting, but balloons falling from the skies did flash through my mind.

In the car on the way to our respective quarters, Zach and I look out the window, wide-eyed, like kidnapped children. Zach starts discussing a new project for us. I wonder if he is a sociopath.

I type this over a year later, sitting at my desk in Madrid, striving to work on my next novel. I feel empty inside knowing there is no car to pick me up and take me to set and thereby escape reality for extended periods of time, until set becomes reality and reality becomes a limbo in which you wait for said reality.

I am working on a novel, but also on four new scripts. I hope to return to reality someday.
