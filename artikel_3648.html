Parties That Changed the World: Barbie’s Premiere and Gone With the Wind’s Debut

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

The world’s most famous doll was inspired by a spunky, high-end call girl named Lilli. Described as a “gold digger, exhibitionist, and floozy,” the character of Bild Lilli was created in 1952 by German cartoonist Reinhard Beuthien after he was asked to draw a filler cartoon for the inaugural issue of the tabloid Bild-Zeitung. Lilli, a buxom blonde with a perky ponytail and sky-high stilettos, sat beside a fortune teller. “Can’t you give me the name and address of this tall, handsome, rich man?” she cooed. The cartoon was an instant hit, and in 1955, Lilli was immortalized in plastic.

A year later, Ruth Handler discovered the Lilli doll while on a vacation in Europe. She bought several. One was for her teenage daughter, Barbara, to keep; the rest were brought back to Mattel, the toy company she had cofounded with her husband, Elliot, and businessman Harold Matson. (Mattel is a portmanteau of both men’s names.) Lilli—with comically large breasts, chic outfits, and heavy makeup—was precisely what Handler had been looking for.

Robin Gerber, author of Barbie and Ruth, wrote: “The doll looked too much like a prostitute for the company’s taste,” so Mattel’s design team (slightly) softened Lilli’s exaggerated proportions, renamed her Barbie, after Barbara, and before long, she would become the physical ideal for generations of young girls and women everywhere.

Sixty-four years after Barbie hit shelves, she finally got her close-up in Barbie, a feminist manifesto in a pair of pink stilettos. The film’s premiere and after-party at the Shrine Auditorium marked Warner Bros.’ largest premiere to date—and the cherry on top of a bubble-gum-flavored, years-long marketing blitz. According to Event Marketer, “the affair was like Barbie herself: high-energy, glamorous and very, very, colorful.”

It was a beautiful day in Hollywood. Even by Los Angeles standards, the weather on Sunday, July 9, 2023, was almost impossibly perfect—sunshine, low 70s, and a cool breeze—as if Hollywood itself has been dropped into the fantasy world of Barbie Land.

The event kicked off with a splash. The pink carpet was tricked out with replicas of pieces from the film’s beach scenes: a lifeguard station, a towering lighthouse, faux palm trees, and a 3D ocean wave, with carpeting that shifted colors to mimic sand. A wide range of influencers were invited to get first dibs on photo ops before the stars arrived. But the real showstopper was the two-story replica of Barbie’s Dreamhouse, complete with a 2D faux pool, a DJ spinning tracks, and the trademark spiral slide. “We had to have that slide,” Wendy Robino, SVP of Warner Bros. marketing events, said at the time.

On Sunday, they wore pink: Writer and director Greta Gerwig, along with cast members Issa Rae, America Ferrera, Kate McKinnon, Ryan Gosling, and Michael Cera, turned out for the premiere in every shade of Barbie’s signature color, turning something long dismissed as girly into a celebration of individuality, joy, and girl power.

But the belle of the ball wasn’t one to blend in. After weeks of thinking pink, Margot Robbie—the film’s star and producer—arrived in black. Her custom Schiaparelli look re-created 1960s’ Solo in the Spotlight Barbie: a black sequined dress with a single red rose stitched onto her tulle skirt, matching opera gloves, Manolo Blahnik mules, and a diamond necklace. Movie Star Barbie fully committed to the bit, even clutching a pink handkerchief as she posed in front of a pink Corvette. Robbie’s blond hair was swept into a high ponytail—reminiscent of the one first worn by Lilli.

Following the official screening—marking the first time Barbie was revealed to the public—it was time to party.

The theme was “Barbie’s Blowout Disco Party.” Fittingly, one attraction invited guests inside a glittering, 12-foot pink disco ball. Justin Lefkovitch, whose company Mirrored Media, partnered with Mattel to create it, tells VF the entrance created “a shroud of mystery” for partygoers. Inside, a six-foot 1:1 Dreamhouse showcasing the new Barbie the Movie doll collection was surrounded by gold and silver streamers and over a dozen custom pink disco balls. The goal, Lefkovitch recalls, was to “transport guests into the world of Barbie.”

Nicki Minaj was the first guest to arrive at the party, event producer Austin Johnston tells VF—“five-foot-long hair all the way down to the floor.” A theme park designer by trade, Johnston and his company, AKJohnston, transformed the after-party into what he calls a “pop-up museum.” Barbie Land’s pastel-colored cul-de-sac was re-created as “a 65-foot-tall, 70-foot-wide” inflatable, its landmarks crammed together like a kitschy postcard. At the end of Barbie’s driveway, Mark Ronson presided over a hot pink DJ booth, playing hits from the soundtrack by Ice Spice, Dua Lipa (a.k.a. Mermaid Barbie), and Nicki Minaj—whom Johnston had to “very kindly” ask to get off Barbie’s Corvette.

Tucked beneath the second-floor mezzanine, a series of alcoves became larger-than-life photo sets, with photographers on hand to capture guests in action. Partygoers could retrace Barbie’s journey to the Real World—by bike, boat, and rocket ship—or step inside the Kendom man cave, Weird Barbie’s house, and life-size Barbie and Ken toy boxes. Not everyone hired to re-create Barbie Land had actually seen the movie by then; only a handful were invited to be part of that “really restricted small group,” Johnston says.

At the center of the room, guests sipped pink cocktails under a giant disco ball while an 11-foot, glitter-coated pink B twirled above. Another bar doubled as a fashion show, with four life-size mannequins dolled up for disco in original costumes from the film’s dance number, including Barbie’s gold sequined jumpsuit and the Kens’ matching white-and-gold tracksuits.

Obviously, the food was on theme too. Wolfgang Puck catered the evening’s Barbie-Q, serving grilled salmon, summer melon salad, roasted Pink Fir apple potatoes, char siu glazed grilled chicken, and charred tricolor cauliflower.

Even the night’s corporate sponsors knew better than to steal Barbie’s spotlight. OPI set up a nail salon, and Xbox provided a gaming station where players had their pick of two General Motors rides: Barbie’s Corvette or Ken’s Hummer. A highlight for Johnston was spotting Robbie racing a virtual Hummer while her Australian parents cheered her on. “No one paid her to do that,” he says. “She’s just like, I want to have a drink and play a video game at my premiere.”

For one night in Hollywood, women could be anything. And 84 years earlier, another city had hosted another rager in honor of another larger-than-life heroine. While Los Angeles turned pink for Barbie, Atlanta resurrected the Old South for Scarlett O’Hara.

All hell broke loose when British actor Vivien Leigh was cast as beloved heroine Scarlett O’Hara in the big-screen adaptation of Margaret Mitchell’s epic, Gone With the Wind. Fans and journalists were outraged that such an iconic Southern Belle would be played by a foreigner. At the time, Leigh was yet to star in a Hollywood film and was best known stateside for her affair with Laurence Olivier. Gossip columnist Hedda Hopper sniped: “Out of millions of American women, [producer David O. Selznick] couldn’t find one to suit him.”

Hopper was exaggerating, but not by a lot: During a grueling two-year nationwide search for his leading lady, Selznick reportedly met with over 1,400 actresses. After angry fan letters poured in, Selznick’s publicity machine kicked into overdrive—and eventually, the public came around to the idea that a refined Brit was somehow easier to swallow than a Yankee.

But perhaps the most convincing endorsement came from Leigh’s own leading man. Even Clark Gable, who initially considered Leigh “too demure” for the Southern firecracker, was won over by her dedication and declared she “was Scarlett every minute.” His own casting, meanwhile, had been a dream come true from the thousands who flooded MGM and Selznick’s offices with letters demanding he play Rhett Butler

The shoot was famously plagued by drama, and by the time filming wrapped, the cast and crew had earned themselves a party.

But Selznick had bigger fish to fry—namely, finishing the movie in time for its premiere. He left the party planning to local organizers and his trusted story editor Kay Brown, who had convinced her boss to buy the film rights in the first place. But since Atlanta was still tightly in the grip of Jim Crow, not everyone could join in. Leigh’s costars Hattie McDaniel and Butterfly McQueen were excluded from the festivities.

Selznick argued, unsuccessfully, with local organizers to get McDaniel on the guest list. Gable threatened to not attend the premiere in solidarity with his friend, The Hollywood Reporter stated, but McDaniel ultimately talked him out of it. “While it was unfortunate to exclude Mammy, it was the wisest policy,” Brown wrote, per Matthew Bernstein, former professor of film and media studies at Emory University.

Meanwhile, white Atlanta was gearing up for the party of the century. Former president Jimmy Carter later called it the South’s “biggest event” in his lifetime. According to Time, then governor Eurith D. Rivers was prepared to call in the National Guard, while Mayor Hartsfield urged every woman of Atlanta to dust off her hoop skirts and each man to “sprout a goatee, sideburns, and Kentucky colonel whiskers.”

Hollywood came in hot. Leigh and Gable made their grand entrance by limousine, waving to the 300,000 spectators who lined seven miles of streets in downtown Atlanta—including a number of nonagenarian Confederate veterans who were invited as special guests. Leigh arrived with Olivier while Gable brought his new wife, Carole Lombard. Fun fact: Gable reportedly hadn’t even wanted to play Rhett Butler until Louis B. Mayer offered to help bankroll his divorce so he could marry Lombard.

Of the motorcade, Time wrote: “Crowds larger than the combined armies that fought at Atlanta in July 1864 waved Confederate flags, tossed confetti till it seemed to be snowing, gave three different versions of the Rebel yell, whistled, cheered, goggled.” Leigh wept as the crowd welcomed the British actress “back home,” as if she’d just returned from battle.

The multiday event included a costume ball thrown by the Junior League that would have Abraham Lincoln rolling over in his grave. Decked out in hoop skirts, bustles, and Confederate-era uniforms, 6,000 guests descended on the Municipal Auditorium, which had been transformed into a recreation of the film’s Monster Bazaar. Black participants were relegated to serving or entertaining. Among them was the Ebenezer Baptist Church choir, whose singers reportedly performed dressed as enslaved people in front of a façade of Tara, a fictional plantation from the film. One of the singers was 10-year-old future civil rights hero Martin Luther King Jr.

Vivien Leigh arrived in a velvet gown designed by Walter Plunkett. Though Olivier escorted her costar Olivia de Havilland instead—to keep up appearances—Leigh was the belle of the ball, especially in hometown hero Margaret Mitchell’s absence. Eighteen years earlier, the young novelist had performed a risqué “apache dance” at a charity gala, which cost her her Junior League membership. Now, with Hollywood celebrating her masterpiece, some suspected Mitchell was getting the last laugh by snubbing the biggest party in town.

Young women fangirled all over Gable. According to Time, one woman fainted at the sight of him. Another was given a choice: a Christmas gift or a chance to meet the heartthrob. She chose Gable. After he kissed the 11-year-old, she gasped, “Now am I a woman?”

Governor Rivers declared December 15, 1939, a statewide holiday in honor of Gone With the Wind’s world premiere, the grand finale of the three-day extravaganza. Searchlights swept the Southern sky, and flashbulbs popped as 18,000 spectators crowded Peachtree Street. Even Loew’s Grand Theater got a glow-up. Its Romanesque façade was disguised as a grand Southern plantation—four towering white columns were erected to support a temporary façade with floral garlands, potted trees, and an enormous portrait of Leigh and Gable hung above the entrance.

Inside, all 2,000-plus seats sold out at $10 a pop—the equivalent of about $240 today. That’s not exactly chump change, considering Atlanta was still licking its wounds after the Great Depression. Young women in antebellum costumes greeted guests before Mayor Hartsfield took the stage to kick things off. Selznick and the film’s stars received standing ovations that lasted a full minute. But the loudest cheers were for Mitchell, now in attendance, who joined Leigh and Gable onstage.

The lights dimmed for nearly four hours of melodrama. When Rhett Butler delivered his immortal parting shot—“Frankly, my dear, I don’t give a damn”—the audience reportedly gasped, then erupted in applause as he left Scarlett in his dust.

Before Gone With the Wind—still the highest-grossing film of all time when adjusted for inflation—premieres were usually exclusive screenings in Hollywood or New York. 1939’s antebellum extravaganza turned an entire city into a movie set, setting the stage for modern spectacles like Barbie.

Both parties also offer a revealing snapshot of the societies that threw them. For Gone With the Wind, the industry glorified an imagined past; for Barbie, Hollywood toasted an imagined future. The Barbie premiere wasn’t just a love letter to a feminist film: It was also a giant corporate juggernaut designed to sell a product whose history was tangled up with the very stereotypes the film was questioning. It paid off: Following the film’s theatrical release, Mattel reported a third-quarter increase in worldwide gross billings of 27%, and Barbie toy sales in the United States increased 25% for the July-August combined months compared to the same two-month period a year prior. The immersive premiere, brand collaborations, worldwide activations, and fan participation blurred the line between marketing a movie and creating a cultural event.

The film also became Warner Bros.’ most successful release of all time, grossing $1.4 billion worldwide and making Greta Gerwig the first solo female director admitted into the hallowed halls of the billion-dollar boys club. The premiere celebrated a movie that changed the cultural conversation while handily outgrossing the testosterone-fueled Oppenheimer—a spectacular rebuke to the assumption that chick flicks have a glass ceiling.

Its timing made the celebration particularly potent. In that moment, Hollywood was still finding its footing after the pandemic; the premiere offered something audiences were missing. “It eventized going back to the movies. It eventized the premiere,” says Lefkovitch.

For one night, partygoers got to revel in a fairy tale where women ruled the world. But outside Barbie Land, the irony had all the subtlety of Ken’s Mojo Dojo Casa House. Hollywood was throwing an enormous party celebrating a fantasy of female empowerment while women were still fighting for equality in the real world. There is “definitely some hypocrisy,” agrees Lefkovitch, who made a point of thanking his all-female team.

Decades earlier, Atlanta had thrown its own enormous party, celebrating a fantasy of Southern womanhood while reinforcing the racial and social hierarchies of the real South—and conveniently leaving the horrors of slavery out of the picture. (The film itself did just that, presenting a fictional plantation where the most loyal individuals enjoyed being enslaved.) Black performers were welcome to entertain white audiences, but not to celebrate alongside them; not a single seat inside Loew’s Grand Theater was available to Black moviegoers.

Together, these indignities galvanized the local Black press. In a column for Atlanta Daily World, Reverend John Clarence Wright wrote, “As Atlanta goes mad with the Wind one hundred thousand of her citizens should become more thoughtful than ever of the course that must be pursued to gain their rights as free men and citizens in a land where the spirit of secession and slavery still lives.” With Black moviegoers barred from Southern theaters, the Pittsburgh Courier dryly observed, “Negro reaction… will have to wait until the film comes North.”

The premiere and the film’s success helped propel the NAACP to fight not just individual films, but toward a broader campaign to change Hollywood itself. In 1942, NAACP leader Walter White spoke directly to studio heads in an attempt to persuade them to steer away from demeaning Black stereotypes—like the ones onscreen in Gone With the Wind—and to increase the roles available to Black actors in Hollywood.

White’s approach infuriated the very woman whose absence from the Atlanta premiere had helped expose Hollywood’s hypocrisy: Hattie McDaniel. As a Black woman, McDaniel’s choices were limited—“I’d rather play a maid than be one,” she once said. Writing for Vanity Fair, Hadley Hall Meares noted that McDaniel was “incensed” that White had negotiated with the studios on her behalf, and believed she and her fellow Black actors deserved their own seat at the negotiating table.

McDaniel did get a seat at the 1940 Oscars—but it was at a small table at the edge of the room, separated from her Gone With the Wind costars. The ceremony, after all, was held at the Cocoanut Grove nightclub, which had a strict color line. When her name was called, McDaniel gushed: “I sincerely hope I shall always be a credit to my race and to the motion picture industry.”

Meares described McDaniel’s life as “a tightrope walk.” She was expected to satisfy the white executives who controlled her career, the Black audiences who wanted better representation, and her own ambitions as an actress.

Vivien Leigh also made history that night, becoming the first Brit to win the Oscar for best actress. She dedicated her award to Selznick for his “energy, courage, and very great kindness.” After all, he had been partly responsible for convincing an initially outraged public that Leigh was worthy to play their beloved Scarlett O’Hara. Years later, the Academy lavished Barbie with eight nominations, though it snubbed Gerwig for best director and Robbie for best actress. (Frankly, my dear, they didn’t give a damn about the two women who created Barbie Land.) If Hollywood could build an entire universe in which women ruled the world, why was it still so difficult to imagine one outside the movie theater?

Billie Eilish and Finneas O’Connell took home Barbie’s only Oscar, for best original song. Their winning composition poses Barbie’s existential question while echoing the expectations heaped upon Scarlett, Leigh, McDaniel, Robbie, and Gerwig: “What Was I Made For?”

It turns out the inspiration for Barbie—the quintessential American toy—wasn’t even made for little girls. Lilli dolls were sold to grown men in European tobacco shops and adult toy stores, becoming popular gag gifts. “Men… put them on their car dashboard, dangled them from the rearview mirror, or gave them to girlfriends as a suggestive keepsake,” Gerber wrote. Long before Barbie could be anything, she was something for men.

Eighty-four years apart, Hollywood threw two spectacular parties for women who refused to play by the rules. But the rules were still very much in place when the lights came back up.
