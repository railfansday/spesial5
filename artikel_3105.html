AI Outlook For 2027: Top Four Trends To Watch

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Alessio Alionço is the founder and CEO of Pipefy, a global leader in AI-driven business process automation solutions.

​In 2026, we were captivated by what AI could do. Every new model promised to be smarter, to be faster and to fix the workflows that never quite worked.

In 2027, the honest answer will often be that it’s not the AI but, rather, the process. A more powerful model merged into a fractured process is just a faster way to compound a multisystem pile-up. The work ahead is less glamorous, but we’ll need to focus on structuring inputs, fixing processes, strengthening guardrails and orchestration.​

In the year ahead, instead of pinning hopes on the capabilities of new AI models, it’ll be time to focus on work that enables you to scale your results or else risk getting stuck in a loop of pilot projects that never pay off.​

I’ll be watching four AI trends closely. Leaders who treat these trends as mere technology topics will miss the business consequences. Here’s what they mean for the business:​​

Governance decides which processes AI’s allowed to touch. An April 2026 Deloitte survey of IT and business leaders found that only 21% of respondents reported having a mature governance model for agentic AI.

For everyone else, the ceiling isn’t the technology. Companies without execution-level controls can run pilots, but they can’t put AI into regulated, high-value processes, which is where the real returns are. Controls aren’t the brake on scale. They’re the license for it.

Having AI agents that aren’t connected is as helpful as having four wheels but no car. Organizations will need to shift from a more-is-better mindset to connecting agents to how work actually flows.

An April 2026 Harvard Business Review Analytic Services report found that among surveyed organizations using or exploring AI, 39% said most AI-enabled workflows still relied on stand-alone tools, while only 12% said AI was embedded directly in the flow of work. Stand-alone tools create activity, while orchestrated processes create throughput.

Business leaders need to identify where AI can change the economics of the business. Financial evidence, not the impressiveness of the demo, will fund projects.

An October 2025 Forrester report found that fewer than one-third of decision makers could tie the value of AI to their organization’s financial growth. That leaves most AI portfolios exposed when budgets are reviewed. Internal teams and vendors alike will be measured on outcomes they can attribute to a process.​ The demo gets the meeting, but the P&L gets the budget.

As AI usage scales, cost scales with it. In McKinsey’s 2026 State of AI survey, one in five responding organizations said AI operating costs, including tokens, already constrained their use. Without unit economics, AI spending grows faster than the value it creates, and nobody notices until the invoice arrives.​

Finance is already stepping in. A September 2026 Deloitte report found that 54% of surveyed finance leaders were leading enterprise AI and technology capital allocation. The companies that scale will manage AI the way they manage any other operating input: by the unit.​

To find the clearest sign that AI has entered a new phase, look at where AI now sits in the budget. For the last few years, AI was funded as innovation: pilots, labs and experiments with loose expectations.

Today, it’s increasingly funded as operations, approved by finance and measured against the same standards as any other investment. AI has graduated from capability to accountability. The first phase proved what AI can do. The next one will be judged on the impact it has inside the business: in which process, under which rules, at what cost, and with what result. That shifts the center of gravity of the AI program and the entire organization.

The real advantage goes to the companies that can make AI dependable, measurable, governed and orchestrated. The first phase was about intelligence. This next one is about execution.​
