The mortgage industry was built to help Americans buy homes. An aging America needs more from it.

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Survey of 2,000 homeowners 55 plus shows 88% prioritize staying home and 90% prioritize independence

For decades, the mortgage industry has helped Americans achieve one of their biggest financial goals: buying a home and building equity over time.

It’s a model we know well. Homeowners purchase properties, make mortgage payments, refinance when it makes financial sense and gradually accumulate wealth. Much of the mortgage industry is structured around supporting that journey.

But what happens after decades of building that wealth?

As homeowners approach retirement, the financial equation shifts. Instead of focusing primarily on acquiring a home and paying down debt, the question becomes how to use the wealth they’ve accumulated to support the years ahead. Housing presents a unique opportunity but also a challenge because, for many Americans, their largest financial asset is also the place they call home.

You can sell a stock without moving. Selling a home is another matter. Trading down and moving to a less expensive home to tap home equity is both expensive and difficult. This might be why our recent research shows that the desire to remain at home continues to grow stronger with age.

In a recent Longbridge Financial, LLC (NMLS #957935) study of more than 2,000 U.S. homeowners ages 55 and older, 90% said maintaining their independence was extremely or very important to their ideal retirement. Nearly as many (88%) said the same about remaining in their homes. Both priorities ranked above paying for health care, paying off debt, long-term care and providing financial support to family members.

The desire to age in place isn’t surprising. What’s particularly revealing is how strongly that sentiment differs across age groups.

Among homeowners ages 55 to 64, 85% rated remaining in their homes as extremely or very important. That figure rose to 88% among those ages 65 to 74 and 93% among homeowners 75 and older. The importance of maintaining independence followed a similar pattern, climbing from 87% in the youngest group to 96% in the oldest.

These are differences between age groups at one point in time, rather than a measure of how individual preferences change over time. Still, the pattern points to an important finding in understanding how homeowners think about retirement.

Over decades, homeowners accumulate substantial equity through mortgage payments and increases in property values. Yet the very home that holds the wealth is also where many want to spend their later years. Its financial value tells only part of the story. Giving a homeowner a choice to move to a less expensive, and thus less attractive, home to use their home equity may not be much of a choice at all.

Consider two retired households with the same net worth. One holds most of its wealth in savings and investments. The other holds much of it in home equity.

Their balance sheets might look similar, but their financial flexibility is different. The first household may be able to draw from liquid accounts to cover an unexpected expense. The second has wealth, too, but accessing it may involve borrowing against the home or selling it altogether. Both options come with trade-offs, particularly when remaining in the home is a priority.

For an industry accustomed to measuring property values, loan balances and equity, this is an important distinction. These figures describe a homeowner’s financial position, but not necessarily how easily they can access that wealth — or what they might have to give up in the process.

The same survey reinforces why this distinction matters. Among homeowners ages 55 to 64, 73% rated paying for health care as extremely or very important to their ideal retirement. That figure climbed to 85% among those 75 and older. While these findings don’t tell us how much homeowners will spend or whether they’ll tap into their home equity, they do highlight how financial priorities can shift across different stages of life.

Mortgage finance has traditionally centered on transactions: purchasing, refinancing, servicing and eventually paying off a loan. Those functions remain essential. But paying off a mortgage doesn’t end a homeowner’s financial relationship with their home.

For some older homeowners, selling or downsizing may be the right choice. Others may have sufficient income and savings to remain in place. Still others may consider home equity loans, lines of credit or reverse mortgages, depending on their financial circumstances, eligibility and the associated costs and risks.

The opportunity for our industry extends beyond any single product. It’s about understanding the role home equity can play in supporting homeowners through retirement, while recognizing the personal value they place on remaining in their homes.

It also requires recognizing that homeowners’ priorities differ. In our study, 93% of women rated independence as extremely or very important, compared with 88% of men. Similar five-point gaps appeared for remaining at home and paying for health care. While the research doesn’t explain why these differences exist, it underscores the importance of understanding homeowners as individuals rather than treating homeowners 55 and over as a single market with identical needs and priorities.

Housing has always been both an investment and a place to live. As homeowners age, the balance between those roles deserves greater attention from the industry that helped them achieve homeownership in the first place.

We’ve spent decades helping Americans build wealth through homeownership. The next opportunity is helping them understand how that wealth can support the years ahead, without losing sight of what they value most: independence, financial flexibility and, for many, the ability to remain at home.
