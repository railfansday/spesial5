Two screenings of Pakistani TV show cancelled in India after backlash

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Pakistan's Dar-e-Nijaat TV show has become a sensation in India

Until Wednesday, the weekend plans of some television enthusiasts in the Indian city of Bengaluru involved dressing up, having an elaborate dinner and finding out what happens next in the lives of a wildly popular fictional Pakistani couple.

Two private screenings of Dar-e-Nijaat (Door to Salvation), a Pakistani television drama that has become something of a sensation on Indian social media, were called off after an online backlash and police scrutiny.

The serial, which airs on Pakistan's ARY Digital, is available to Indian viewers through some YouTube channels, though the broadcaster's official channel is blocked in India.

The controversy comes amid a renewed appetite for Pakistani entertainment in India, even though cultural engagement remains a sensitive issue following a brief but intense military confrontation between the two nuclear-armed neighbours in May last year.

A similar gathering in Delhi, featuring quizzes and even a virtual interaction with the show's director, had already divided opinion online last week.

In Bengaluru, one screening, scheduled for Saturday at a restaurant, offered a Persian and Arabic dinner alongside two episodes of the serial for a small ticket charge. The other - scheduled at a private theatre and meant only for family members - asked guests to dress in their "best Pakistani ensemble".

As promotional posters circulated online, several social media users questioned the appropriateness of such gatherings and called for police intervention. Both events have since been called off.

The backlash has coincided with calls to block the release of another Pakistani drama in India.

The All Indian Cine Workers Association (AICWA) has written to Prime Minister Narendra Modi demanding that Netflix's upcoming Pakistani series, Jo Bachay Hain Sang Samait Lo (Gather whatever is left), be barred from release in India.

Featuring Fawad Khan, Mahira Khan, Sanam Saeed and other stars with many Indian fans, the series is Pakistan's first Netflix original. Its global premiere is scheduled for 4 December.

In its letter to Modi on Tuesday, the AICWA argued that releasing the series in India would disregard the sentiments of families affected by terrorism. India has long accused Pakistan of supporting cross-border terrorism, an allegation Islamabad denies.

One of the event invitations asked guests to dress in Pakistani attire

The developments have revived an old debate in India over whether cultural ties with Pakistan should be kept separate from political differences.

India and Pakistan share a rich cultural heritage, but relations between them have often been fraught since they were carved out of British India as independent nations in 1947.

And cultural exchanges have often been among the first casualties of political tensions. Pakistani ghazal singers with devoted audiences in India have periodically found themselves unable to perform there. Indian films, immensely popular in Pakistan, have disappeared from its cinemas. Artistic collaborations have flourished during periods of relative calm, only to unravel when tensions flare again.

The early 2010s were a particularly fertile period. Pakistani singers Atif Aslam and Rahat Fateh Ali Khan were among the most recognisable voices in Hindi cinema. The launch of the television channel Zindagi in 2014 brought Pakistani serials into Indian living rooms, introducing viewers to stories whose language and emotional worlds were familiar, but whose treatment of everyday life often felt refreshingly different.

By 2016, Fawad Khan was appearing in some of Bollywood's most prominent productions. But attacks on Indian security forces in Uri that year and in Pulwama in 2019, both of which India blamed on Pakistan-based militant groups, narrowed the space for cultural collaboration. Pakistan - which has constantly denied any involvement - also imposed restrictions on Indian entertainment.

Even as formal exchanges dwindled, Pakistani dramas found new audiences in India on YouTube, while music continued to travel across the border through streaming platforms.

But the events of last year disrupted even this relatively informal relationship.

Pakistani actor Fawad Khan has starred in a few Bollywood films and has many fans in India

On 22 April 2025, militants killed 26 people, most of them tourists, in Pahalgam in Indian-administered Kashmir. India blamed Pakistan-linked militants; Islamabad denied involvement. In May, India launched military strikes on what it described as terrorist infrastructure in Pakistan and Pakistan-administered Kashmir. Four days of fighting followed before a ceasefire was announced.

Amid widespread anger in India, the government asked streaming platforms to discontinue Pakistani-origin content, while social media accounts of several Pakistani celebrities were blocked.

Fawad Khan's much-anticipated return to Hindi cinema with Abir Gulaal was thwarted when the film was denied permission for release in India. Criticism of India's military action by Pakistani artistes fuelled further anger.

For much of the past year, Indian audiences have encountered Pakistan on screen largely through the prism of national security. In December, millions flocked to watch Dhurandhar, Aditya Dhar's espionage thriller, whose success inspired a fascination with the gangsters, spies and political intrigues of Karachi.

But in recent months, a more familiar kind of fascination with Pakistan has begun to resurface.

Pakistani songs like Alfaaz have become hugely popular on Indian social media, while Dar-e-Nijaat, a story about marriage, heartbreak and domestic life, has found an enthusiastic audience.

Written by the celebrated Urdu novelist Umera Ahmed, the serial revolves around the marriage of Zaryab (Durefishan Saleem), a young woman recovering from a romantic betrayal, and Abu Bakar (Sheheryar Munawar), a deeply religious man.

Though the drama explores questions of faith and redemption, it is Abu Bakar's tenderness towards his wife that has earned him perhaps the highest accolade in the contemporary vocabulary of courtship: a "green flag".

It was this enthusiasm for the drama that prompted the organiser of one of the screenings in Bengaluru to plan an event at a private theatre in her apartment complex.

What she had envisaged as a small family gathering, she said, had been interpreted quite differently once the invitation found its way onto X.

"My family was saying why should we cancel?" she said. But ultimately, she decided to call it off.
