Mountain Lions, Wildfires, & Volcanoes: Washington Finally

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

After our longest day on the PCT (46 miles!) we took a well-deserved rest at a spa hotel with Nina and Rebecca. We sleep in, soak in the hot tub until we’re pruney, and eat as much as we can. There’s no laundry onsite so we wash our clothes in the bathtub and hang them to dry outside. After our attempt at running I have shin splints. My back chafed under the unfamiliar day pack and my skin is tender. Other than those issues and the expected fatigue, we’re mostly unscathed from our monumentous effort.

We spend time in the hotel prepping for our last few weeks on trail. We mail home items we’d shipped up to Portland from further down the trail, and I switch my film camera out for my mirrorless one. Finally, we’re ready to get to it.

We head to Cascade Locks and see some hiker pals out in town. They’re planning to stick around for PCT Trail Days. We had debated staying, but it would be another several days before the event starts and we don’t want to stick around that long.

We bid farewell to Nina and Rebecca and walk across the Bridge of the Gods, officially entering our last state of the PCT. The gorge is beautiful and windy and crossing the Columbia River via foot is a new experience.

We quickly enter the woods on the Washington side and begin to climb. The path is lined with blackberries and poison oak. One is good for snacking as we walk, the other we avoid. We’ve started to see many Southbound hikers at this point making their way into Cascade Locks for Trail Days. The trail quickly becomes hot and humid and we’re sweaty as we make our way uphill, climbing out of the gorge.

Now that we’re finally in Washington, it feels like the soap bubble has popped. The fires we’ve been hearing about are no longer far away. We have about 500 miles left of the PCT, something we’d accomplish in a few short weeks at our current pace if we could. But, we have to face the music – we can’t. The trail to the North of Snoqualmie Pass is closed up to Stevens Pass, and then the trail North of Rainy Pass to the Northern Terminus is closed as well. That’s about 100 miles of the remaining trail we cannot do. There are alternates, which we discuss ad nauseam. Should we go around the Snoqualmie closure and keep a continuous footpath? Should we end the hike at Rainy, and go to the border later? Should we break our footpath and just hike the available PCT miles? Or should we attempt an alternate? The SOBOs (southbound hikers) are bringing us news of poor air quality around Stevens Pass and Snoqualmie Pass and our lungs took a beating already in Oregon. I don’t want to hike alternates through Washington in poor air quality just to reach a border – that feels like it would be a pointless exercise in suffering. And on top of it all, we live in Washington. We are getting closer to home every day, and some of the alternates bring me less than an hour away from my house by car. I can’t imagine road walking in the smoke and being so close to home. It would be unenjoyable and not at all how I’d want to end my hike. At this point we’ve been hiking for almost 130 days. We have run out of things to talk about, so discussing what to do and how we’d feel about each option occupies most of our days.

We push on through Southern Washington. The forested trails can be humid and have more climbing that we’d seen in Oregon. We’re both feeling the effects of our huge day well into the next week. We are exhausted and end each day with less miles than planned. The scenery is nice though, and we get glimpses of the volcanos. One day we take an alternate to see a waterfall, and camp next to a group of popular lakes. With our shorter miles we arrive at camp earlier. Its nice to enjoy eating in the daylight and hanging around at camp. We even have time to swim.

My coworker Thom offered to bring us a re-supply outside of Trout Lake. This will keep us from having to take a day off trail, which will help keep our momentum going. He has also offered to bring us “freshies” and we send him a list of our current cravings. We hike to meet him at a trailhead and find a feast set up for us. He makes us meatball subs and has fresh fruit for us to devour. We sit in comfortable chairs for hours. While we skipped the popular town of Trout Lake, we’re happy with the quick detour. We’ve seen the town before and if we’d gotten off trail we fear we might not have gotten back on. This was also part of our reasoning for skipping Trail Days. Many of our friends who went decided to end their hike at Cascade Locks. The fires were too much to navigate, and people want to enjoy Washington. I don’t blame them.

We depart our personal re-supply stop with an extra special late birthday gift – fully cooked, shelf stable bacon. We mix it in with our pasta and have some for breakfast the next day. It hits the spot.

Thom’s resupply helped us with getting enough food to make it to White Pass. We head North, excited to pass by Mount Adams and through the Goat Rocks Wilderness. As we hike on, the first of the hikers getting back on trail from Trail Days start to filter in and we get to camp with thru hikers again. We get a bit of cell service and I immediately check Watch Duty – one of my most used apps right now. I see that there’s another fire, this one on the PCT. The trail between Steven’s Pass and Steheiken is closed now. Our hearts sink and its a sad evening. We now have only two options – take alternate routes for the entire last 250 miles of trail, or get off the trail at Snoqualmie Pass.

We head into the Mount Adams Wilderness with heavy hearts. The burn scars feel a bit more tragic than normal. We’ve been hiking apart more and more lately, just walking in our thoughts. I’m trailing Sarah by a few minutes, listening to my audiobook when I hear her say “STOP.” I stop dead in my tracks and look around. She’s ahead at the top of a small hill. I join her and she says she just saw a large mountain lion heading into the copse of trees to the left of where the trail wraps around a bend. We walk together, making noise and scanning the surroundings. We pass the trees and see a small cat hopping around playfully in the underbrush. “I thought you saw a big cat….” I say. “I did.” she responds.

The implication that the mother to this young mountain lion is somewhere nearby sinks in. We stand back to back and make our way down the trail. We don’t see another cat, but we hike within sight of each other for the rest of the trail.

The Mount Adams wilderness is gorgeous. We cross glacial streams, take in the massive mountain views, and see the sunset on the glaciers. It passes by so quickly, and before 2 days are over we’ve put the mountain behind us.

We enter the Goat Rocks Wilderness, a highly anticipated section for us. We’ve made plans to come here both when we’ve lived in Oregon and Washington and they’ve never panned out. The smoke forecast looks good and we’re excited for epic views. We push through another grueling, humid climb, exiting the forest to an amazing meadow. The trail continues to climb up to Cispus Pass. We marvel at the scenery. It feels like we’re in a different world. After crossing the pass we descend into another meadow. There are endless wildflowers and an icy stream flowing through our path. We look high up onto the rocks and see goats!! It feels like a fairytale. We set an early alarm for the next morning and watch the sunset reflect off of Mount Adams.

The next day we start hiking early and are glad of it. There’s an incredible inversion and the smoke layer has settled enough to give us good views.

We climb up towards Old Snowy mountain, pausing every few steps to turn around and take in Mount Adams. We can see Mount Saint Helen’s as well, and tons of wildflowers. We reach continue to climb and reach a turnoff to Old Snowy. We aren’t going to summit the peak, but we are going to take the alternate that brings us up closer to the summit and skips a sketchy, icy snow traverse. There’s a small bit of snow to navigate on the alternate but it isn’t nearly as steep as the one we’re skipping. Then we’re on shale, getting epic views of Mount Rainier. Sadly, we can see the smoke in the surrounding peaks from here as well. I know if the air was clear, we’d be able to see much more.

We descend, and the loose gravelly stone is a challenge. The steep exposure doesn’t help our footing. We’re much slower than normal but the views are epic. We make our way back onto the main PCT and hit the Knife’s Edge, a famous section of trail. It’s incredible but the loose footing is still sketchy.

Finally we get back onto dirt and cross more picturesque meadows. We add this as another place to return to soon, this one actually reasonably close to home. As we descend we consult our maps on the remaining distance to the White Pass Cracker Barrel. According to FarOut, the gas station closes at 6 and showers and laundry close at 5. We’re going to have to hoof it if we want either of those things in addition to the package we’re getting there.

We both put in our audiobooks and fly. The scenery is still stunning, and we walk across an absolutely gorgeous section of trail. I realize that this is one of the last times I’ll be cruising along in an incredible place, trying to make it to a gas station in time to get a cold drink. This is likely going to be my last resupply on the entire trail. I take out my headphones (Dungeon Crawler Carl isn’t really appropriate for such a moment) and try to fully appreciate where I am and how far we’ve come.

We dip into the forest and are hiking along the White Pass ski area. We’re making good time, but we’re not fast enough and will likely miss out on showers and laundry. This is a bummer, since we haven’t done real laundry since my birthday and haven’t taken a shower in Washington yet. I dig through old FarOut comments and find an alternate people used to take down the road through the ski area. It’ll get us right to the Cracker Barrel, and maybe save us just enough time. We scurry along the shortcut and to the road. Pounding away on a gravel surface always hurts my feet but we’re both hopeful and push the pace. We make it to the gas station just in time; they tell us laundry is closing in 10 minutes. It’s quick work to throw in everything we have besides our rain jackets and sleeping pants. We shower, get our box, and choose a dinner from our re-supply. We have far too much food to justify buying anything.

The hiker area behind the gas station is busy and social. We charge our things and add more than a few items to the hiker box. Most people are taking their time heading north, hoping the fires will go out and the trail will re-open. We are not so optimistic and have family meeting us in Snoqualmie Pass in 4 days. Our plan is to hike there and in all likelihood, conclude our thru hike at that point. Its sad, and we’re more than a little downtrodden. Our bodies are tired though. Sarah has been experiencing an achilles issue that is getting worse and worse. She limps to our camp that night. I roll my ankle on the road and join her in limping. We talk about the possibility of continuing the trail from Snoqualmie, but at this point the alternates do not look good to us. We want to hike the PCT, and with this section in our backyard that opportunity will be available to us another year. We cozy up in the tent and vow to make the best of our final days on trail.
