A jellyfish rots in days, so why did the first soft-bodied animals leave so many fossils?

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

Collagen-eating microbes spread into oxygen-rich seas as animals diversified, and that shift may explain why Ediacaran fossils are so common.

A jellyfish that washes up on a beach is gone within days, and nothing of it ends up in a rock. Yet the oldest animals known, soft-bodied creatures with no shell, bone, or tooth, left fossils so often that whole seafloor communities survive in stone.

Half a billion years ago, the microbes that rot a carcass may not yet have been able to digest the protein holding it together. A new study traces when they acquired that ability.

The protein is collagen, which gives animal tissue its strength. Microbes break it down with enzymes called collagenases, and the new work follows the genes for those enzymes through 700 microbial genomes.

The genes are far older than animals. For most of Earth’s history, though, only microbes that can’t live around oxygen had them.

Philip Vixseboxse led the study as a Ph.D. student in the Department of Earth Sciences at the University of Cambridge, and at the University of Edinburgh.

In an interview with Earth.com, he described the result as a one-way change in how fossils form. “Once collagenases proliferated into microbes in those environments, there was no going back,” he said.

Most fossils are shells, bones, and teeth, because hard parts last long enough to turn to stone. Soft tissue has to be sealed off from decay quickly, and that rarely happens.

The exception is the late Ediacaran Period, from about 579 million to 539 million years ago, when soft-bodied animals were preserved in many kinds of seafloor sediment.

Ediacaran fossils are the oldest animal fossils known, and some sites preserve whole communities. Researchers have argued for decades about why.

The usual explanations point to unusual seawater chemistry, different kinds of tissue, or a mat of microbial slime across the seafloor. Each works at some sites but not at all of them.

In 1999, paleontologist Jerzy Dzik proposed a different cause. The microbes of the time hadn’t yet evolved collagenases, he argued, so a carcass kept its shape for long enough to fossilize.

There was no way to test it then, but genome sequencing has since made it testable, and the new study did just that.

Vixseboxse’s team searched the 700 genomes for two collagenase genes and found the main one in 242 species.

They then compared the enzymes’ family trees with a dated tree of microbial life. Where a gene turned up in a lineage that couldn’t have inherited it, another microbe had passed it sideways, and the dated tree says roughly when.

The main collagenase arose billions of years before animals, in microbes that live without oxygen, such as methane-producers and fermenters. So Dzik was wrong about one thing. Collagenases didn’t first appear when the Ediacaran fossils stopped. They had been around for ages, working only in the oxygen-free zones deeper in the seafloor mud.

In an oxygen-rich Ediacaran sea, a dead animal would have kept its collagen until the oxygen in the surrounding sediment ran out.

The authors put that delay at years, which is long enough for minerals to build up on the tissue and preserve its shape. The collagen scaffold of the body, flexible and slow to decay, would have acted as a kind of organic skeleton.

Starting in the Neoproterozoic, the era in which animals arose, the gene for the main collagenase began jumping between lineages far more often.

The team counted an average of 63 sideways transfers across its larger set of trees. Oxygen-tolerant and oxygen-using microbes picked it up, including the groups that dominate decay today.

Vixseboxse told Earth.com the pace of that change was the surprise. “I was shocked by just how stark the increase in diversity of collagen-degrading microbes was when animals first appeared on the scene!” he said.

The timing matters because the microbes that dominate the rotting of an animal carcass in modern oceans acquired collagenases in the latest Neoproterozoic or afterward. Before the Cambrian, the authors wrote, the main agents of modern decay couldn’t efficiently digest collagen at all.

That shift coincides with a sharp drop in soft-bodied fossils. “These results suggest there may be an overarching biological mechanism that has shaped the fossil record of soft tissues across Earth’s history,” said co-author Alex Liu, a professor in the Cambridge Department of Earth Sciences.

Collagen is far from the only material that lasts until something evolves to eat it. The authors wrote that decay-resistant polymers should pile up in sediments before the enzymes that digest them spread, and more sparingly afterward.

Asked by Earth.com about other findings, Vixseboxse pointed to plastics as a modern case, with a fossil record that may one day decline the same way.

Microbial enzymes that break down some plastics already exist, the authors wrote, but they’re not yet common across environments.

The team tracked genes, not decay itself. The gene trees can’t say how fast an Ediacaran carcass rotted, only that the microbes present lacked a tool that modern decomposers have.

Other changes around the same time, including animals that burrow, scavenge, and hunt, also made soft-tissue fossils rarer. The authors call the enzymes a major driver rather than the only one.

The dated tree also gives ranges, not dates. The moment any one group gained the enzyme can be a window of more than a hundred million years.

Vixseboxse said the next test would be a community of microbes that can’t digest collagen, and then a look at how an animal body decays among them.

“It would be really neat to produce a microbial community that was incapable of degrading collagen,” he said. His team came close by soaking a soft coral in a strong soap solution for three days, which stripped out the cells and left only the collagen framework behind.

Until a microbial version of that experiment runs, the case rests on gene trees, and on the fossils that stopped appearing when the genes spread.

The full study was published in the journal Current Biology.
