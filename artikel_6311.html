What is fueling the brightest gamma-ray star pair ever found?

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

A warm gas trail near LMC P3 offers new clues to the extraordinary gamma rays coming from this mysterious binary system.

One pair of stars in a small galaxy next to the Milky Way gives off more gamma rays than any other pair of its kind.

Gamma rays carry more energy than any other form of light, and astronomers have struggled to explain where this pair’s gamma rays come from.

In the cold gas near the stars, they have now found a long, straight streak of warmer gas that lines up with the pair.

The warm streak is strong evidence that the pair, called LMC P3, is shooting out a jet of fast-moving matter. A jet like that could account for the gamma rays.

The work was led by Yasuo Fukui, a professor emeritus at Nagoya University, with colleagues at Gifu University.

“We discovered a gigantic molecular jet,” said Fukui. The team didn’t see the jet itself, though. What they measured is gas they think the jet has heated.

LMC P3 lies in the Large Magellanic Cloud, a small galaxy that orbits our own. When astronomers look toward the middle of the Milky Way, dust blocks much of the view.

The Large Magellanic Cloud has far less of that problem.

One member of the pair is a big, very hot star. The other is a compact object, the crushed core left behind when a giant star exploded.

That core is either a black hole or a neutron star, and nobody has yet confirmed which.

The two circle each other about every 10 days. They orbit inside the debris from the explosion that made the compact object, a leftover cloud called DEM L241.

LMC P3 is the most luminous known gamma-ray binary and the first discovered outside the Milky Way. Astronomers first reported it in 2016.

Starting in 2022, the team pointed the ALMA observatory in Chile at the region.

The array of radio dishes picked up the faint signal of carbon monoxide in the cold gas near the stars.

That signal told the researchers how the gas was spread out, how it moved, and how warm it was.

One streak of gas stood out. It runs about 26 light-years long and three light-years wide, and it lines up with LMC P3.

That length is roughly six times the distance from the Sun to the nearest star.

The streak is still extremely cold: minus 400°F to minus 352°F (33 to 60 K). The gas around it measured about minus 433°F (15 K), which is normal for quiet gas clouds in that galaxy.

Measured from absolute zero, the streak is two to four times warmer than its surroundings.

The team found no nearby hot stars that could have warmed the gas by shining on it.

Fukui said the warm temperatures, spread across a large part of the gas cloud, make “a very strong case” that the gas is being struck by a high-energy jet from the compact object.

That same kind of jet can power gamma rays.

For years, there were two main explanations for LMC P3. In the first, the compact object is a fast-spinning neutron star that blows out a wind of charged particles.

The wind slams into the wind coming off the big star, and the collision speeds particles up enough to make gamma rays.

In the second, the compact object pulls gas off its partner. The gas swirls into a flat, spinning disk around it, which astronomers call an accretion disk.

A disk like that can launch narrow jets of matter moving close to the speed of light.

The second setup is a small version of a quasar.

Quasars, among the brightest objects in the universe, are powered by giant black holes feeding on gas at galactic centers. Some also launch powerful jets.

A star-sized version is called a microquasar. The new streak supports the microquasar idea.

Because black holes generally drive stronger jets than neutron stars do, Fukui sees LMC P3’s huge gamma-ray glow as a clue to what kind of object powers it.

The high gamma-ray output “provides a very strong support for the possibility that its central object is a black hole” rather than a neutron star, Fukui said.

In the paper, the team argues for a jet but doesn’t name the compact object.

Roughly 20 microquasars are known in the Milky Way. Most were found with optical telescopes, radio dishes, or X-ray instruments, aimed at parts of the sky that are mostly clear of dust.

Few microquasars have turned up in the crowded, dusty middle of our galaxy.

The researchers suggest that searching for straight streaks of warm carbon monoxide could turn up microquasars there.

LMC P3 is the second object where this kind of carbon monoxide streak has been found. The first was a gamma-ray source in the Milky Way called HESS J1023-575.

A jet can only build a streak where there’s enough gas in its path. LMC P3 is close to a gas-rich region where new stars are forming.

Around the well-known microquasar SS 433, the gas is thinner, which the team thinks is why only scattered clumps formed there instead of one long streak.

No telescope has detected the jet directly, in radio waves, X-rays, or gamma rays. The researchers wrote that this fits with how sensitive current instruments are.

The streak also shows up only on the north side of the pair, possibly because that’s where the gas cloud is.

The ALMA map didn’t cover the south side, so a matching streak there hasn’t been searched for at this level of detail.

A second, fainter streak runs at an angle of about 16 degrees to the first.

If the jet slowly changes direction over time, it could have made both, but that idea hasn’t been tested.

The team plans sharper ALMA observations that can pick out details smaller than about three light-years.

Those would give better temperature readings and could show whether the jet has been shifting direction.

The full study was published in the journal The Astrophysical Journal Letters.
