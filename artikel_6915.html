What’s a Vintage Car Rally Really Like? Inside the Louis Vuitton Dolomites Classic Run.

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

How I got mixed up in a race with a bunch of priceless historic automobiles and views of Italy’s spectacular Alpine scenery—and won.

In vintage rally driving, there are generally two types of competitor. Both share a passion for keeping priceless and nearly century-old vehicles in use on the open road rather than displaying them under glass. But some see the drive itself as challenge enough, while stalwarts adhere to what’s called the road book. This is a hundred or so pages of complex navigational directions that the copilot uses to navigate instead of a map, maintaining a constant flow of instructions to the driver. It is this book and how closely you follow it that decides the eventual winner.

Gianandrea Redaelli, a car nut whose family has long roots in the Italian printing business, is firmly in the “road book” camp. In early September, I climbed into the copilot seat of his 1939 Aston Martin C-Type Speed Model Roadster Bertelli—just one of two ever built, with an experimental aerodynamic body that earned it the nickname “the Flying Pea”—and stayed there as he drove six hundred kilometers of “left at the traffic lights, right at the cow” for the Louis Vuitton Dolomites Classic Run. The rally started in Venice and ended, after a scenic but challenging detour high into the Dolomite mountains, at Monza, the home of Italian Formula 1, just in time for practice day of the Grand Prix.

Venice might not seem the most logical starting line for a car rally given the proverbial wetness of the streets. But the city was chosen this year because it was also the end point of the last LV rally of its kind: the La Serenissima Run from Monte Carlo to Venice in 2012. Gianandrea won that one as copilot, with his father, Giuseppe, at the wheel. The pair also won the one before it—the Boheme across Hungary, the Czech Republic, and Austria—in 2006. This time, however, the pressure was on me too.

I have been a copilot before, and I can tell you it’s no picnic. Rally racing in vintage cars is less about speed and more about timing. It’s an exercise that requires pinpoint accuracy at the time-stamp points in the overall course and, most critically, in the regularities. These are fiendishly short time-over-distance sections in which crossing the line just a few hundredths of a second late—or early—earns strict penalties. Sensibly, the day before the race began, Gianandrea took me out in the Aston in the Veneto countryside to practice, especially those critical regularities.

I could have opted for an easy life. The rally allowed competing cars, dating from 1931 to 1969, to use the road book or follow the course using a map in a dedicated phone app. But where’s the fun in that?

“If you join a competition, you follow the regulations and strive to do your best,” Gianandrea says. “As an Italian, if you paint numbers on the door of my car, I will want to win.”

Every car in a rally like this comes with its own idiosyncrasies. On flat ground, someone driving a souped-up 2026 sports car would find these tests difficult to nail. In the reality of race conditions, the regularities often involve steep climbs or descents and unexpected corners, alling for an expert hand at the wheel—and foot on the gas pedal. The tendency of some car doors to swing open at hard bends (ours has the “suicide” doors with hinges on the back instead of the front, beloved by early carmakers) only adds to the excitement. The purpose of driver and copilot is to battle and overcome all the mechanical and computational challenges—and get our ride to the finish line bang on time.

Louis Vuitton has been involved in these glamorous and exacting vintage rallies since 1993. The Dolomites Run represents an enthusiastic return to the genre after a fourteen-year gap and, perhaps tellingly, the company’s global sponsorship (since 2025) of Formula 1. Unconfirmed rumors circulated in the field that the choice of ending the rally at a Grand Prix could well be repeated—and soon—in other countries in the future.

It’s easy, after all, to see why a fusion of style and cars with six- and seven-figure price tags might appeal to Louis Vuitton and competitors alike. Not surprisingly, at every coffee and lunch stop, the LV effect sprinkled the race with an added sense of luxury. In between, our snaking caravan carried the glamour of a century of motoring along with it. At every hairpin you got a glimpse of a gull-wing Mercedes, a 1931 Bugatti two-seater (my personal fave), and a shimmering behemoth of a Bentley in quick succession. These cars would turn heads anywhere. But in Italy, arguably the home of the sports car, they brought smiles, applause, and gasps in even the tiniest village that passed by in our slipstream. I swear I even saw a nun with a glint in her eye at one set of traffic lights.

Navigating, I was quickly learning, is not so much about providing a constant babble of information as it is knowing how to give clear and simple instructions—and only when strictly needed. As the finish neared in Monza at the end of two long days on the road, I was able to give Gianandrea the most helpful instruction of all: “Nothing for 15 kilometers.”

“That,” he very kindly replied, “is the mark of a pro.”

Even after the engines cooled, we were still clueless about how well we’d done overall. That changed at the final event, a dinner inside the Sforzesco Castle in Milan. Gianandrea stepped up, beaming, as winner of the race, and for the first time as driver. I stood somewhat sheepishly behind him as copilot as he received the trophy from Pietro Beccari, CEO of both Louis Vuitton and Louis Vuitton Fashion Group. It was an impressive swirl of Murano glass created by Venini, housed in its own equally impressive traveling case, made (naturellement) at Louis Vuitton’s atelier in Asnières.

Gianandrea has promised me visiting rights.
