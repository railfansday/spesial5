This Heritage Pattern Is Replacing Flat, Beige Walls—Here’s How Designers Are Using It in 2026

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

The centuries-old motif also makes lampshades, rugs, and seating feel bespoke.

In an age of encroaching AI, designers are turning to heritage looks to create bespoke interiors with personality. That may be one reason decorative marbling is having a moment.

"People are tired of flat, safe walls,” says designer Cara Woodhouse. “After years of greige, clients want surfaces with a point of view, and marbling has an artisanal history behind it. It's also flexible: it can read painterly, graphic, or soft depending on the colorway."

Its versatility is part of the appeal. “It doesn't necessarily have to be traditional or even executed on paper,” adds designer Sasha Bikoff. “It's really about the movement of color and the organic, unpredictable patterns that emerge, which can translate beautifully across wallpaper, furniture, textiles, and decorative objects.”

The paper marbling technique has a long history. In 10th-century Japan, artisans created “floating ink” designs by placing ink on water and manipulating it as it moved. By the 15th century, the practice was known in Turkey as “the art of clouds,” before making its way to Venetian and Florentine bookbinders.

The painterly look is appealing because its undulating lines feel expressive and vibrant without harsh edges, says Jo Plant, chief creative officer at Pooky, which is a brand well-known for its marbled lampshades.

Designers are bringing marbling into all kinds of interiors, from murals and upholstery to lampshades and even sink skirts. Analisse Taft-Gersten, cofounder of the 1818 Collective, used billowy marbled silk to elevate a powder room. And studios like Rule of Three are experimenting with the technique in floral fabrics that resemble vines.

Should you need further convincing, Woodhouse puts it best: "Marbling is pattern without repetition. No two passes are the same, so a wall reads more like art than wallpaper. It brings depth and a handmade quality to a room that might otherwise feel too finished."

Here are nine designer-approved ways to bring marbled motifs into your home.

Designer Michelle Fahmy of Haus of Meeshie likes things cheeky and playful. So it was a no-brainer to introduce groovy swirls into a Culver City pied-à-terre via a large-scale mural from Mitchell Black.

“I wanted something dramatic for the stairwell wall which bleeds into the living room and is the first thing you see when you walk in the door,” she says. “When I came across this wallpaper, it stopped me in my tracks! Usually, you see this paper marbling technique done on fabric or in smaller applications, like a lampshade, so this was unusual in scale and exactly the conversation starter I was looking for.”

For Bikoff, interior design should feel like a natural extension of a home’s architectural language. To bring her own sense of color, movement, and personality into a project within a Zaha Hadid building, she opted for Pierre Frey wallpaper with a liquid, dripping marble effect rather than a traditional, tightly swirled pattern.

“I love how the elongated, flowing lines create a sense of movement, almost as though the walls are melting or the colors are cascading down the surface,” Bikoff says. “Traditional marbling can feel very classical, whereas this interpretation feels more contemporary, psychedelic, and expressive.”

The dialogue between the wallpaper and Hadid's architecture is all about challenging conventional forms and embracing organic movement. “I wanted the room to feel immersive, almost like stepping inside a piece of abstract art,” she adds.

It was love at first sight. Marta St. Amour, interior designer and project manager at Braswell Design + Build was enchanted by the beautiful movement and layered tones of amber, soft blue, and sandy beige portrayed in the Lee Jofa Taplow wallpaper.

“The pattern became the inspiration for the bathroom's color palette, tying together the warm veining of Taj Mahal quartzite, rich caramel-colored shower tile, and soft blue tile behind the freestanding tub,” she says.

“We wanted the tub to feel like a statement piece, and the wallpaper's organic, flowing pattern complements its sculptural shape and warm metallic finish.”

To capture the spirit of the marbling technique but in a contemporary way, Bikoff opted for resin chairs with the telltale pattern in the above space.

“The way the colors blend and swirl through the resin gives them this incredible sense of depth and movement, almost like a liquid material that has been frozen in time,” she says.

Juxtaposing materials can challenge the perception of design. Here, the chairs feel like functional sculptures as they balance the visual richness of marble and the playful transparency of resin.

“They bring together color, texture, and fluidity while reinforcing the playful, futuristic spirit of the space."

If a full room of swirls is a bit much for you, settle on something smaller-scale, such as a lampshade. Pooky’s hand-marbled shades show off the dance of acrylic paint.

“Marbling is having a resurgence because we’re all craving craft, texture, and character in our homes,” says Plant. “People want pieces that feel human and handmade rather than mass-produced.” Adding a marbled shade is like decor candy that can add just the right amount of maximalism.

What makes the marbling technique unique is that the patterns are inherently organic, and so they lend themselves to pairing well with other organic shapes. Taft-Gersten anchored this powder room with a silk sink skirt featuring Rule of Three fabric that almost looks like it was kissed by bubbles.

“There’s a rhythm to it, but also an irregularity that keeps your eye moving; it never feels rigid,” Taft-Gersten says. The print also echoes the convex pebble mirrors above.

This girl’s bedroom may look dreamy, but that’s not what Cara Woodhouse was after when she landed on cotton candy-like Merenda Wallpaper by artist Michelle Weinberg.

“I wanted movement,” Woodhouse says. “A kid's room should feel alive, and marbled paper gives the walls energy without a single literal motif. I'd call it soft modern with an edge: rounded forms, blush tones, and a pattern that keeps it from going saccharine. The swirl does the work so the furniture can stay simple and sculptural."

The look of poured paint over a rug is one that will surely raise an eyebrow. Floor_Story designer Bekah Smith was experimenting with duct tape when she stumbled upon unexpected patterns that look like creases and shadows.

“There’s a similar sense of spontaneity to marbling, where patterns emerge through experimentation rather than being entirely predetermined,” Smith says.

For those looking for a ripple effect without the psychedelic swirls, consider ceramics as a way to dabble in the style without being so literal. Henry Holland Studio employs a traditional Japanese ceramic technique, nerikomi, to create irregular earthenware marbled motifs.

The pattern is created by stacking, folding, and rolling colored clay into slabs, then hand-molding them into functional shapes like lamp bases, bowls, and vases.
