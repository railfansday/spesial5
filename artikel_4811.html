The In Real Life Act: The Antidote to Loneliness

For the last three years, my husband has headed off to our local park most Sundays. Braving the sweltering Texas summers and occasional winter frost, he is joined by an impromptu group of fellow players from different countries, jobs, ages, and backgrounds. The seeming sacredness of this act may come from a shared love of soccer, or fútbol, but what I see as most magnificent is the emergent nature of the collective. There is no sign-up or group text; whoever arrives plays. The group trusts people will turn out, and whatever that looks like will work.

A few hundred feet away, my daughter does something similar on the playground. I imagined when she was a baby that we would meet up with other families there, and sometimes we do, but more often we trust in the shared experience of whatever the park provides. This serendipity depends on a space that holds it.

In May 2021, Julianne Holt-Lunstad and I wrote about the power of place and of designing places to be inclusive of all ages, to reconnect us with nature, and to provide a welcoming space and a reason to connect with others. These are foundational structures in combating loneliness and fostering social health—and what the U.S. Surgeon General’s Advisory on Social Connection calls a part of social infrastructure. These relatively modest investments can advance long-term human connection.

In the absence of these resources, people with the means are increasingly paying to fulfill their social needs. This trend is part of a long lineage of proposed solutions that treat loneliness as a personal problem. But addressing loneliness is inherently a collective challenge requiring collective action.

This fall, U.S. Senator Chris Murphy (D-Conn) proposed a way to fund this collective need: the In Real Life Act. The IRL Act would create a tax on social media platforms that make at least $500 million in ad revenue and use those dollars to create a Social Capital Fund. This fund would flow directly to cities, towns, and tribes to support places that foster social ties, such as parks, libraries, and community spaces, and the groups and programs that keep these places filled with life.

No algorithms would have matched those soccer players; it required a shared space, not a shared app. This makes those connections more valuable, not less; in a study of more than 70 million U.S. Facebook users, friendships across economic lines were among the strongest predictors of upward mobility. And Trust for Public Land found that nearly two-thirds of Americans had a positive conversation with a stranger in an outdoor public space over the past year. These shared spaces become exponentially stronger when they are combined with inclusive programming.

StationSoccer in Atlanta, Georgia, offers a glimpse at the benefits this type of support from the proposed Social Capital Fund might offer. Putting soccer fields at Atlanta-regional transit stations, StationSoccer is a program of the nonprofit Soccer in the Streets partnered with the City of Atlanta, their transit agency (MARTA), Atlanta United Community Fund, Georgia Power, and Children’s Healthcare of Atlanta.

What started as an idea to activate a single dead space quickly burgeoned into a vibrant citywide connective movement that garnered an honorable mention from the Fast Company Innovation by Design Awards.

Part of what makes this program so successful is that it meets the needs for supportive design: It is inherently accessible and inclusive because it sits where commuters and neighbors already travel, it gets kids and their families outdoors, and its programming keeps them coming back. Taking once underused land and transforming it into well-used social infrastructure has likely strengthened the social capital of thousands of Atlanta children and their families. Programs like these show what becomes possible when connection is funded collectively, rather than left to those who can afford to pay for it.

What we choose to invest in at every scale—as individuals, neighborhoods, and nations—creates the shape of the worlds we inhabit. May we choose to invest in real-life serendipity and the spaces that hold it.
