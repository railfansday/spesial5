After Launching McGee & Co., Shea McGee Shares 8 Lessons for Opening a Store

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

My husband Syd and I are both lovers of retail, and have always known one day we’d have a McGee & Co. shop of our own. Over the years, we’ve spent a lot of time jotting down ideas for our own store. Our new flagship is a repository for all that knowledge that we’ve been squirreling away—but it also taught us a few lessons of its own.

Speaking to you now, on the other side of its debut, there are some things we only learned through actually doing. From the importance of developing a training manual to the do-or-die value of a soft launch, here are eight hard-won learnings from our McGee & Co. store launch.

It was important to us that the shop feel like the homes we create for our clients—layered and collected, with a variety of patterns and objects for your eye to explore. Despite the fact that the dollars-per-square-foot model is the traditional method of conceiving an in-store assortment, it was more important for us to remain true to ourselves with this first location: we’re a designer-led brand, not a merchant-led one.

“I’m getting to see our products through new eyes—and I was the one that concepted them!” says Shea. “When you see them in a store setting, pieces can have a lot more detail than you can see online.”

We started by coming up with the sections we envisioned, like a kids’ area, a kitchen zone, and so on. Then we created planograms to see how much we could actually fit, and reconciled with our merchandising team to see what made business sense. Sometimes, that meant pivoting from the early vision. A lighting gallery we had planned, for instance, ended up shifting: We loved the idea, but when we looked at the percentage of lighting to the rest of our sales, we could see clearly that it was a smarter business decision to use this space to showcase more furniture. Instead of the sprawling lighting gallery we had imagined, we created a moody lounge area that can flex to showcase different pieces.

We don’t think small: Our aspirations are to grow nationwide. But however much we scale, it’s critical that we not lose the warm, personal feeling that comes from a standalone store. As McGee & Co. grows its brick-and-mortar operation, that’s one of my biggest goals. And there’s another layer here, too: Being a personality-led business, the team members in our store aren’t just representing our brand—they’re representing me, for better or worse. The design world can sometimes feel closed-off and unapproachable, and it’s always been the McGee mantra—in our content, in our personality on social media, in how we work with clients—to be warm and accessible. Now that attitude needs to trickle down to our store associates, too.

Before we opened, I spent months developing a comprehensive training guide for our associates. It’s essentially Design 101, McGee-style, covering everything from how many inches I place a rug away from a wall, to how to pair and arrange accent tables, to how to layer bedding. We cover every level of the decorating process. Thank goodness for AI—it’s been a lifesaver: I’ll narrate as we go on our walk-throughs, and use those notes as the basis for the training manual. And it’s not a one-and-done deal. We’ll continue to add to this document as we evolve and grow.

Having that Design 101 established is a first step, but how do you ensure that the team is really internalizing the lessons? For us, the answer was checkpoints. As our design associates go through training and familiarize themselves with our ways of doing things, we hold check-ins with them to make sure that they’re absorbing our ways of operating. It’s the exact same as we do for the junior interior designers at Studio McGee: The checkpoints allow us to build up mutual confidence, with the aim for them to eventually fly on their own.

Shea plans to have the studio’s senior-level designers hosting regular check-ins with the shop’s design associates.

Personally dropping in myself can serve as another kind of checkpoint, too. With the flagship local, I’m able to stop by regularly in these early days to make sure that our standards are upheld, that our visuals are tight, and the operation is humming. That won’t work for us in every location, but it’s certainly a perk of opening up in our own hometown.

As much as I love a good plan, there’s nothing like real-time feedback to show you what’s working and what’s not. Which is why we kicked off our flagship back in September with a whisper and not a bang. We did a very soft launch with no announcement, no social post. We just opened the doors. As folks wandered in, we learned, and we tweaked. And two weeks later, we opened officially. That soft-launch period was vital for us in getting the kinks out.

One benefit of having had an ecommerce business for so long is that we have rich data on exactly where our customers are. That means that from day one, we already know where we need to open next. While launching in Salt Lake City was a logical place for our flagship, we’re already thinking ahead to how we’ll expand to some of our other biggest markets, like Texas and California.

Located in Salt Lake City’s Trolley Square, the “shop,” as Shea calls it, is 7,500 square feet.

We want to capitalize on the fact that the retail-ecommerce loop is an advantageous cycle: Where you have a retail store, your ecommerce sales lift. People who are aware of your product can go see it in person, give it a sit test, think about it, and then finish the sale online. In store, we can educate them, give them styling suggestions, and show off the quality of the product in ways we can’t online. Even for us, we’re discovering new things about our assortment by merchandising it in the store—we’re seeing it with fresh eyes, and we know our customers will too.

Aside from the obvious metric of profitability, to me, success in these early days means nailing down our systems and processes. It’s not the most glamorous side of this business, but it’s a must. I want to be able to say that we know what it takes to run a store and to make the shopper experience in it as smooth as possible. For instance, I want customers to know how to get to our free design services, and to have a great experience using them. If we can do that, we know we’re on our way to some of our loftier goals, too.

I’m not under any illusions here: This first store is a baptism by fire. Not only are we opening a flagship and planting seeds for our future locations—we’re also doing it in Q4, right before the holidays. But my hope is that at the end of it, we can catch our breath and take a moment to reflect on what worked, what didn’t, and then get to work fixing it. In this business, honesty with yourself is the only way up.

“The kitchen is a working kitchen,” says Shea, who envisions cooking demos and community events enlivening the space.
