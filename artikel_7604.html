Lawyers connect with clients over addiction, mental health challenges

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

As a young lawyer, Evan Levow would use cocaine every now and then to give himself the boost he needed to work all day and through the night.

That was Levow’s norm until around 2013, when he started turning to cocaine, as well as marijuana and alcohol, even more often. Over the next several years, his drug abuse “just got out of hand,” he says.

“I was high pretty much constantly,” says Levow, managing partner of Levow DWI Law in Cherry Hill, New Jersey. “I would go to court, and nobody had a clue. Nobody knew that I had just done a bump.”

Levow, who says he has been sober for more than eight years, credits rehab, therapy and his state’s lawyer assistance program for helping him get his life back on track. He shares this not only with other lawyers but also with his clients in hopes they will ask for help if they need it.

As a DWI lawyer, Levow adds that being honest about his past helps him relate to his clients. He recently spoke about his struggles with two clients, who opened up about their own addictions and later made medical and therapy appointments.

“I never tell anybody to go to rehab if you have a drug problem,” Levow says. “That’s not my role. But if you tell me you’ve got a problem and you want help, I will, one hundred percent, lead you in the right direction.”

Like Levow, other lawyers see benefits to sharing their personal experiences with clients. If they have faced similar challenges—whether it be with addiction, mental or physical health or childhood trauma—they can empathize with clients and establish a closer connection, they say. They can also be more authentic, which goes a long way toward building trust and rapport with clients.

In his work as a Louisville, Kentucky-based employment law solo practitioner, Chris Stewart often represents people who have been terminated from their jobs because of a disability or requested accommodation.

Stewart, who is blind, brings to the job an inherent understanding of what it’s like to have a disability. He lost his eyesight just before starting kindergarten and has been affected by that experience both positively and negatively, he says.

Stewart knows how to talk with his clients about their disabilities, which is “a great way to help people feel more at home,” he says. Since he has learned to navigate a world that wasn’t built for him, he identifies with clients who are trying to do the same.

“When people don’t feel as though they have to explain themselves and explain everything about what just caused them to be traumatized, like if they’re terminated or something else because they’re disabled, I think that really helps to bolster the connection between lawyer and client,” Stewart says.

Throughout his life, Stewart has struggled with his disability. He contemplated suicide in high school, which led to a diagnosis of major depressive disorder. He began abusing alcohol and then cocaine, prescription pills and other drugs in college and after he dropped out.

Stewart, an accomplished violinist, knew something had to change and applied to and was accepted to the California Institute of the Arts in 2007, he says. With the help of his now-wife, he says he became sober, started regular therapy and decided to become a lawyer. He graduated from the University of Kentucky J. David Rosenberg College of Law in 2016.

His experiences with substance abuse and his mental health help him relate to any client who has dealt with emotional distress, Stewart says. Many of his clients who have lost their jobs also go to therapy, and they’re able to talk about it with him once they realize he is in the same position.

“Not only does that help us become closer, not only does it help me empathize with them, but it helps me turn that around and convey that emotional distress to opposing counsel, or to the judge, or to the jury, or to whoever it needs to be communicated to,” Stewart says.

Erica Bloom knows lawyers who avoid talking about their personal lives, and she couldn’t disagree with that approach more.

“That’s how you’re a real person, and how clients get to know you,” says Bloom, a family law attorney who opened her own firm in Carlsbad, California in 2016.

As part of her practice, Bloom represents clients who have experienced domestic violence and are going through contentious child custody battles. She also serves as a court-appointed minor’s counsel, representing children so they have a voice in their families’ proceedings.

Bloom’s own childhood was chaotic, she says. Her dad has bipolar disorder, and her mom has dealt with alcoholism, and she and her younger brother often needed to escape the house during their arguments.

She talks about these experiences with her young clients, many of whom have encountered much more trauma in their lives, she says. She tells them she understands how difficult and isolating it can be to watch their parents struggle.

“I totally resonate with them,” Bloom says. “Every kid who has the same or similar issues with their parents, I’m like, ‘Look, I get it. This sucks. But let’s figure out how we can get through it.’”

This creates a bond between her and her clients and helps them see her as an adult they can trust, says Bloom, who is also president of the North County Bar Association. While she wants them to talk openly with her, she also wants them to feel safe when she encourages them to talk with a therapist.

“All of my kiddos are in therapy, and that’s something that I get them in right away,” Bloom says. “It’s so important to have that therapeutic support.”

Lawyers who are candid about their challenges also say it benefits them in their personal and professional lives.

Stewart, who found it difficult to deal with his depression during the COVID-19 pandemic, discovered the Kentucky Lawyer Assistance Program. He began attending Zoom meetings, where he both received help from other lawyers and supported them in their recoveries. Now he is an active volunteer.

Serving others through the organization or through his work as an attorney is integral to his own recovery, Stewart says.

“If you start feeling sorry for yourself, you are in danger of relapse,” says Stewart, who now lives in New York City and also serves as assistant general counsel at the U.S. AbilityOne Commission. “What you should do at that point is go out and try to help someone else.”

Levow agrees, saying that sharing his story with clients keeps him grounded. He also has spoken at several legal conferences, including in Chicago, San Diego and Columbus, Ohio.

“If you’re not being mindful or being aware, then you’re going to have a problem,” Levow says.
