PS5 Does Not Have a 'Monopoly' on Digital Games as £2 Billion Lawsuit Falls Through

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Do you remember a few years back when news broke of a potential class action lawsuit against Sony? The case was reported to be worth billions of pounds, which would be dished out as compensation to over 12 million PlayStation users.

Consumer rights campaigner Alex Neill argued that the PlayStation maker had — and continues to have — a monopoly on digital game (and associated DLC) purchases through the PS Store.

Essentially, Neill claimed that Sony was getting away with being able to charge whatever it wanted because of its position as market leader — and consumers were being squeezed dry as a result.

The case really gained traction in 2023 when it was given the greenlight by the UK's Competition Appeal Tribunal, allowing the whole thing to go to trial.

Well, here we are three years later, and Neill's case has ultimately been shot down.

On the 17th November 2026 (that's today, at the time of writing), the aforementioned Tribunal has ruled that the case doesn't prove that Sony has broken the country's competition law. Neill's case has been completely dismissed as a result, although this decision can still be appealed.

The Tribunal also didn't find Sony's pricing to be "excessive and unfair", as per Consumer Voice's report.

This entire case has obviously been given a fresh angle, what with Sony declaring its intentions to stop manufacturing physical game discs in 2028 — a move that will force users to spend money through its digital storefront.

As of right now, though, "the team bringing the claim is considering its options" in regards to appealing the decision.

I'd be surprised if anything more comes of this, however; my understanding is that a totally new case would have to be put forward in order to bring the physical media stuff into the equation.

The judgment is rather more nuanced than the headline suggests.

The Tribunal did not find that Sony lacks exclusive control over digital sales on PlayStation. It expressly accepted that digital PlayStation content can only be obtained through the PS Store and that Sony completely controls the digital retail function. If the market could be narrowly defined as "PlayStation digital games" then the Claimant would probably have won.

The case principally failed because the claimant did not establish that “digital PlayStation games” was the correct legal market. The Tribunal found that physical PlayStation games were likely substitutes and that multi-homing (having more than one platform at home), cross-commerce and competition between console systems also constrained Sony. It therefore considered a wider systems market more appropriate. Since the claimant had not advanced an abuse case on that wider market, the case necessarily failed.

Nor did the Tribunal simply find that PlayStation prices are fair, per se. It found that excessive and unfair pricing had not been proved. The claimant’s case depended on an unreliable digital-versus-physical price comparison, and the profitability analysis did not properly account for the way game revenues cross-subsidise console hardware or for relevant intangible assets (e.g. brand goodwill, PSN and PS Store infrastructure).

So the accurate summary is not “Sony does not have a monopoly because Xbox, Nintendo and Steam exist”. It is that the claimant failed to prove the narrower market, dominance and abuse required by competition law.
