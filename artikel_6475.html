Ultra-Thin "Rainbow" Sails Could Power Spacecraft and Shade the Earth

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

For years, whimsically minded space enthusiasts have been dreaming of sailing across the vastness of the void on beams of sunlight. Photons, the massless particles that make up those beams of sunlight, still carry momentum, and can therefore impart a gentle push when they bounce off a surface. This force is the concept behind âsolar sailingâ, which has been having a bit of a moment in the last decade as several proof-of-concept flights have shown it is indeed actually feasible. Now a new paper, available in pre-print on arXiv from researchers at the Rochester Institute of Technology (RIT), has unveiled a clever technique that will allow these giant sails to steer sideways using a technology more commonly associated with flat-screen displays.

That technology is liquid crystals, most commonly used in liquid crystal displays (LCDs). Instead of having the sail itself act like a giant mirror and requiring it to angle itself in order to turn relative to the Sun, the sail described by the RIT researchers bends sunlight at a steep angle via diffraction, allowing the spacecraft to steer sideways while still facing directly at the Sun.

This solution offers several advantages over traditional solar sails. The best analogy to describe how is to think of holding an umbrella against a stiff breeze. If you hold the umbrella directly facing the direction of the wind, it will push you straight back â simple enough. But, if you want to âturnâ compared to the direction of the wind, you have to angle the umbrella. In so doing, you not only lower the total amount of wind hitting the umbrella, but also make it much harder to control.

Traditional solar sails face the exact same problem, only in space instead of in an atmosphere and with sunlight instead of wind. Nearly a decade ago, Grover Swartzlander Jr, one of the paperâs authors and a professor at RIT, realized that microscopic optical patterns that reflected light to the side would replace mechanical tilting in solar sails. To prove his point, he received backing from NASAâs Innovative Advanced Concepts (NIAC) program, and laid out the groundwork for what became diffractive solar sails.

When light hits a diffraction grating, the technical term for the microscopic patterns embedded in the sail similar to what is etched onto a CD, it bounces off at steep, predictable angles even when the surface is facing the Sun head-on. Pushing the light to one side provides the sail with an equal and opposite kick in the other direction, steering the spacecraft using nothing more than a simple roll.

While much of the early work on diffractive sail prototypes focused on patterns etched into metal-coated films, they struggled with scaling and had a hard time rolling into a rocket fairing. To figure out how to solve this problem, the researchers turned to computer simulations, eventually using an optimization algorithm to design a three-layer sandwich of liquid crystals. The top layer catches blue and visible light, the middle layer grabs infrared, and the bottom layer interacts only with longer infrared rays. Even with those three separate layers, the combined film is only 5.5 micrometers thick, one-tenth the width of a human hair.

With traditional materials, such a shade would need to tilt in order to keep its position, consequently shrinking its shadow. But one made out of diffractive material could hold its position without compromising its size.
