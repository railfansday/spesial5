Helping children reach healthier weights may start with one change

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

An afterschool program focused on replacing sugary drinks with water helped shift children’s BMI in a healthier direction after one year.

Most programs that try to help children reach a healthier weight ask families to change a lot at once: more exercise, better meals, earlier bedtimes. Those plans are hard to keep going at home.

A team at Boston University School of Public Health tried asking for one change instead.

Children in an afterschool program were coached to drink fewer sugary drinks and more water.

After a year, their body mass index had moved in a healthier direction compared with children at clubs that ran their usual activities.

“One of the most striking findings from this study is that we were able to shift our study participants’ BMI in a healthier direction at a time when childhood obesity trends nationally were moving the opposite way,” said Monica Wang, the corresponding author and an associate professor of community health sciences.

“That tells us that well-designed, community-based behavioral interventions can make a meaningful difference, even against a difficult broader backdrop.”

In the United States, childhood obesity affects nearly 1 in 5 school-aged children. Sugary drinks include regular soda, fruit-flavored drinks, sports drinks, and energy drinks.

They are the largest source of added sugar in children’s diets, but drinking them may do little to satisfy hunger.

Wang said the single target came from listening to families before the study began.

“Our formative work with families and community partners taught us that asking families to change many dietary behaviors at once can quickly become overwhelming, particularly for families facing competing time and financial pressures,” she said.

The program, called H2GO!, was built with Boys & Girls Clubs of America, which runs afterschool and summer programs at about 5,500 sites. Trained club staff led 12 group sessions over about six weeks.

Kids did taste tests and watched demonstrations of how much sugar a drink holds. They made print, music, and video projects about why water mattered to them.

They took activities home to do with a parent, and the program ended with a community event where they showed their work.

The trial ran from 2020 to 2025 at 11 clubs across Massachusetts. Researchers randomly assigned clubs to offer H2GO! or continue their usual activities, such as lessons on online safety.

In all, 437 children aged 8 to 13 took part, each with a parent or caregiver. About two-thirds qualified for free or reduced-price school lunch.

At the start, about 17% had overweight and about 37% had obesity.

Staff measured each child’s height and weight. The team turned those into a BMI z-score, which shows how far a child’s BMI sits from the average for children of the same age and sex.

Researchers use it to track BMI in children who are still growing.

The average z-score fell by 0.11 among children in the program and rose by 0.08 at the other clubs. On its own, neither change was large enough to rule out chance.

The gap between the two groups was the result the trial was designed to test. It came to 0.19 after a year, a difference the authors reported as statistically significant.

They noted that it is similar to or slightly larger than the effects reported in other childhood obesity prevention trials.

The gap had not appeared at two months or at six. It held when the team left out children who enrolled in 2020, early in the pandemic, and when it accounted for siblings.

One alternative analysis pointed the same way but fell short of significance.

In a further analysis, which the authors label exploratory, the team looked at weight categories.

Among children who started with overweight, 44.4% of those in the program reached the healthy-weight range, compared to 31.6% at comparison clubs.

Fewer of them moved up into obesity, 5.6% compared to 21.1%. Children who began at a healthy weight mostly stayed there.

“Community organizations are critical partners in prevention because they are where children spend time, build relationships, and learn from one another,” Wang said.

Children in the program reported drinking about 3.7 fewer ounces (108 milliliters) of sugary drinks a day after a year. They also reported about 6.6 ounces (196 milliliters) more water.

At the start, the average child reported drinking about 12 ounces (355 milliliters) of sugary drinks a day.

But the difference in drinking between the two groups was not statistically significant. The authors wrote that this calls for caution about what drove the change in BMI.

Children recalled how much they drank, making those estimates less precise than the height and weight measurements.

The authors suggested that the focus on sugar may have shifted other habits too, such as eating sugary foods or getting enough sleep.

Attendance was uneven. Children came to about half of the sessions on average, often missing them for schedule conflicts or illness. About 69% of children were still in the study at 12 months.

Families knew which kind of club they were in. All the clubs were in one state.

One comparison club stopped enrolling partway through and was replaced, and the authors wrote that unmeasured differences between sites may have affected the results.

The team also expected the program to work by boosting children’s sense of empowerment, measured with a survey. It did not find that link.

Next, the researchers want to test the approach in other community settings and on other habits, such as eating ultra-processed foods.

“What I find most encouraging about H2GO! is that it shows we do not have to wait for large-scale policy or environmental change to make progress,” Wang said.

“Those changes are essential, but we can simultaneously give children and families tools to make healthier choices within the environments they live in now.”

The study was funded by the National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases.

The full study was published in the journal JAMA Network Open.
