Jensen Huang's failed bid and Rene Haas's IPO: 10 things to know about Arm

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

How Arm chip licensing works, explained in 10 parts covering architecture licenses, core designs, royalties, Jensen Huang's failed Nvidia bid and Rene Haas's leadership

Arm Holdings does not manufacture a single chip, yet its technology runs inside processors designed by Apple $AAPL, Qualcomm $QCOM, Nvidia $NVDA and Samsung. The company, based in Cambridge, U.K., sells something less tangible than silicon. It licenses the rules a processor follows and, in many cases, ready-made blueprints for the processor cores themselves. Partner companies turn those designs into finished chips. Contract manufacturers such as TSMC $TSM then fabricate them.

That division of labor lets a company with no factories of its own shape the design of chips that other companies build and sell. Arm leaves the expensive work of building and running fabrication plants to others.

Understanding how Arm chip licensing works starts with two kinds of payment. A company that wants to use Arm technology generally pays an upfront license fee. It then pays a royalty on each chip it ships. Arm earns money when a partner signs on and again when that partner's products reach customers.

The rights a company buys vary. Some companies license ready-made core designs and build their chips around them. Others, such as Apple, hold an architecture license that lets them design their own cores to Arm's instruction set. The choice affects how much control a company has over its chip's design. It also sat at the center of a legal dispute between Arm and Qualcomm.

The business has changed hands and leaders in recent years. In September 2020, Nvidia agreed to buy Arm from SoftBank for $40 billion. Jensen Huang, Nvidia's co-founder and CEO, led the company at the time. The U.S. Federal Trade Commission sued to block the purchase in December 2021. U.K. and EU regulators had also raised concerns. Nvidia abandoned the deal in February 2022. That same month, Rene Haas became Arm's CEO, succeeding Simon Segars. Under Haas, Arm listed on Nasdaq $NDAQ in September 2023, and SoftBank kept a majority stake.

The 10 slides that follow cover a selection of the pieces that make up the Arm licensing model. They explain what an instruction set architecture is, how the company began and which license types chipmakers choose. They also look at how RISC-V and x86 compete with Arm. Each slide covers one part of what Arm does and can be read on its own.

Arm does not manufacture semiconductors. The company, based in Cambridge, U.K., creates processor designs and sets the rules those processors follow. It then sells other companies the right to use that work.

Arm owns no chip factories. What it produces is intellectual property — detailed specifications and circuit designs that exist as digital files rather than as physical silicon. A customer receives those files, not a box of finished parts.

Other companies design the chips that carry Arm technology. Apple $AAPL, Qualcomm $QCOM, Nvidia $NVDA and Samsung are among the companies that build chips on Arm's foundation. Each one takes Arm's work as a starting point and turns it into a product of its own.

A finished processor for a phone or laptop usually contains far more than Arm's contribution. Chip designers combine Arm-based processor cores with their own graphics units, memory controllers and other circuits on a single piece of silicon. The industry calls that combined design a system-on-chip. The Arm portion handles general computing tasks. The rest of the chip reflects each company's own engineering.

Manufacturing is a separate step, handled by yet another company. When a chip designer finishes its layout, it sends the design to a foundry for fabrication. TSMC $TSM, short for Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Co., is a contract foundry that fabricates chips for other companies. Its customers include many of the same companies that license Arm technology.

Companies that design chips but outsource production are described as fabless. Qualcomm and Nvidia work this way. Arm sits one layer further back in the chain. It supplies the designs that those chip companies start from.

The three-way split lets each company specialize. Arm concentrates on processor design. Chip companies concentrate on complete products. Foundries concentrate on production technology. A single Arm design can appear in chips from many different companies at the same time.

Arm's revenue comes from that same chain. The company collects money from the businesses that use its technology, not from selling hardware. Its income therefore depends on what its partners design, build and ship.

Arm started in 1990 as Advanced RISC Machines Ltd, a joint venture formed by Acorn Computers, Apple $AAPL and VLSI Technology. The company grew out of processor work at Acorn, a British computer maker. Its name was later shortened to Arm.

Acorn was based in Cambridge, U.K. It was best known for the BBC Micro, a home computer used widely in British schools during the 1980s. For its next generation of machines, Acorn designed its own processor instead of buying one from another supplier. Sophie Wilson and Steve Furber led that design work. The first chip, the ARM1, ran its first code in 1985.

At that stage, the letters ARM stood for Acorn RISC Machine. Acorn used the chip family in its Archimedes computers, launched in 1987. Its simple design also meant the chip drew relatively little power. That trait later suited battery-powered devices.

The 1990 spinout changed who the processor was for. The design moved out of Acorn and into a separate company that could supply it to others. Each of the three founders brought something different. Acorn contributed the processor design and the engineers behind it. Apple was a prospective customer for the chips. VLSI Technology, a U.S. chip company, brought manufacturing capability.

Apple's interest centered on the Newton, its handheld personal digital assistant. The Newton MessagePad, released in 1993, ran on an ARM processor. The device never sold in large numbers. It still gave the young company an early commercial product in the market.

Robin Saxby became the company's first chief executive. Under his leadership, the company licensed its designs to other firms instead of building and selling chips itself. In 1998, it listed its shares in London and on Nasdaq $NDAQ.

That period as a listed company ended in 2016. SoftBank, the Japanese technology investor, acquired Arm that year for about £24 billion, or roughly $32 billion. The deal took Arm private.

The Arm instruction set architecture is the list of basic commands an Arm processor understands and carries out. It acts as the shared language between software and hardware. Any program meant to run on an Arm chip must first be translated into those commands.

An instruction set architecture is usually shortened to ISA. It defines operations such as adding two numbers, loading data from memory or jumping to another part of a program. It also sets out the registers a processor exposes and how memory is addressed.

The ISA is a specification, not a physical circuit. Two chips can look very different inside and still run the same software, as long as both follow the same ISA.

This separation explains why compilers matter. A compiler converts code written in a language such as C or Rust into machine instructions for one specific ISA. A program compiled for Arm will not run natively on an x86 processor from Intel $INTC or AMD $AMD. The reverse is also true. Developers either recompile their code for each architecture or rely on translation software.

The Arm architecture follows RISC, short for reduced instruction set computing. The RISC approach favors a small set of simple instructions that each do one job. Arm is also a load/store design. Arithmetic happens on values held in registers, and separate instructions move data to and from memory.

The x86 family follows the complex instruction set computing, or CISC, approach. Some of its instructions combine a memory access and a calculation in a single step. Its instructions also vary in length.

Arm's 64-bit era began with Armv8, which introduced the AArch64 execution state. AArch64 instructions are a fixed 32 bits long. A fixed size makes them simpler for a processor to decode. AArch64 also provides 31 general-purpose registers. Armv8 kept a separate 32-bit state, called AArch32, so older software could still run on many chips.

Armv9 was announced in 2021 as the next major version of the architecture. It includes Scalable Vector Extension 2, known as SVE2. This feature lets a single instruction work on many pieces of data at once. That style of processing is used in machine learning, image handling and signal processing.

Each new version builds on the previous one rather than replacing it. Software written for earlier 64-bit Arm chips generally continues to run on newer ones.

Arm earns money in two stages. A company pays an upfront license fee to gain access to Arm's designs, and then it pays a royalty on each chip it ships that uses that technology.

The license fee comes first. A chip company pays it when it signs an agreement, long before any product reaches a customer. The fee buys the right to use Arm's intellectual property. That property can be the instruction set itself or a finished processor core design. With it, the licensee can start building a chip around Arm technology.

The royalty comes later. It applies to each unit the licensee sells once its chip is in production. A processor in a smartphone, a microcontroller in a household appliance and a server chip in a data center can each generate a royalty for Arm.

That timing creates a gap between a deal and the money it produces. Designing a chip, verifying it and moving it into manufacturing at a foundry takes time. An agreement signed today may not produce royalty income until the resulting chip ships. That can be years later.

The gap runs the other way at the end of a product's life. A chip design can stay in production for years after launch. Every unit shipped during that time adds to the royalty stream. An older license can keep paying Arm long after the original fee was collected.

The two streams track different things. License fees reflect how many companies are starting new projects with Arm. Royalties reflect how many chips those companies actually sell. A licensee that ships chips in large volumes generates more royalty income than one that ships few.

This structure ties Arm's income to its partners' sales. When companies such as Apple $AAPL, Qualcomm $QCOM, Samsung and Nvidia $NVDA ship more Arm-based processors, Arm collects more in royalties. When demand for those products slows, royalty income slows too.

The specific fees and royalty terms are set in contracts negotiated between Arm and each licensee. The basic two-part model applies whether a company licenses a ready-made core or designs its own cores to Arm's instruction set.

An Arm architecture license gives a company the right to design its own processor cores from scratch, as long as those cores run Arm's instruction set. Apple $AAPL and Qualcomm $QCOM are two of the best-known companies that use this route to build custom CPU designs.

Fewer companies take this path than license Arm's ready-made cores. Many Arm customers do not design cores at all. They license finished core designs from Arm and place them inside their chips. An architecture licensee takes on that engineering work itself. It decides how the core is organized internally, how it processes instructions and how it balances speed against power use.

Compatibility is the part that does not change. A custom core built under this license must execute the same Arm instructions as any other Arm processor. Software compiled for Arm can therefore run on it. The licensee gains control over the hardware design without giving up access to the wider pool of Arm software.

Apple is one example. Its M1 chip, announced in November 2020, used Apple-designed cores built to Arm's instruction set. The M1 began Apple's shift away from Intel $INTC processors in its Mac computers. The custom approach lets Apple shape its chips around its own devices and operating systems.

Qualcomm is another. Its newer custom CPU designs trace back to Nuvia, a chip startup that Qualcomm acquired. Nuvia held its own architecture license with Arm before that deal.

Those designs became the subject of a court fight. Arm sued Qualcomm in 2022 in a dispute over which license terms applied to the Nuvia-based designs after the acquisition. A jury in Delaware returned a verdict in December 2024 that went mainly in Qualcomm's favor.

The case put the terms of an architecture license in public view. It showed that the specific wording of such a license, including which company holds it, can decide who may use a design after a change in ownership.

The custom route also carries practical trade-offs. Designing a competitive CPU core requires large engineering teams and years of development. The companies that take this path are generally those that want processors tuned closely to their own products rather than a design shared with competitors.

A core license gives a chip company a finished processor design from Arm that it can place directly into its own chip. Arm sells these ready-made cores in families aimed at different markets. They include Cortex-A for phones, Cortex-M for microcontrollers and Neoverse for servers and data centers.

The licensee does not have to design a processor core from scratch. It takes Arm's completed core and builds a larger chip around it. That chip can also include graphics processors, memory controllers, wireless modems and other circuits the company designs or sources elsewhere.

Cortex-A is the family built for application processors. These are the chips that run full operating systems, such as Android on a smartphone or tablet. Phone chips often combine several Cortex-A cores of different sizes on one piece of silicon. Larger cores take on demanding work. Smaller cores handle lighter tasks and use less power.

Cortex-M targets microcontrollers. These are small, low-power chips that control a single device or a narrow set of tasks. They show up in appliances, sensors, wearables and industrial equipment. They typically run simple firmware or a small real-time operating system. That is far lighter than the software found on a phone or laptop.

Arm also sells Cortex-R cores for real-time systems. These are used where a chip must respond within strict time limits, such as in automotive systems and storage controllers.

Neoverse is Arm's line for servers, cloud computing and networking infrastructure. It is meant for chips that sit in data centers rather than in consumer devices. Amazon $AMZN's Graviton processors, which power servers in Amazon Web Services, are one example. Amazon designs the Graviton chips itself but builds them around Neoverse designs.

A ready-made core removes a large engineering task from a licensee's project. Creating an original processor core requires a specialized engineering team and extensive testing. Licensing a finished core lets a company focus its effort on the surrounding circuits instead.

The trade-off is control over the core. A company using a standard Arm core gets the same core design that its competitors can license. It can still set its product apart through the other components on its chip, its software and its manufacturing choices. Companies that want to change the internal design of the core itself take a different route, through an architecture license.

Arm Flexible Access, launched in 2019, lets a company try out a wide range of Arm designs before signing a full license for any of them. Compute Subsystems take a different approach to a related problem: they bundle several Arm designs into one pre-integrated package that a chip team can build around.

Both programs aim to lower the cost and effort of building an Arm-based chip. Under the traditional model, a company negotiates a license for a specific design and pays for it before engineering work goes far. For a large chipmaker, that is routine. For a startup, a university lab or a small device maker, it can mean committing money before knowing which design fits.

Flexible Access changes the order of those steps. A participant gets access to a portfolio of Arm intellectual property, along with tools and design support. Engineers can evaluate cores, run simulations and build prototypes using several options side by side. The company pays a license fee only for the designs it decides to take into production. Royalties still apply once finished chips ship.

The program is built for companies that have not yet settled on a processor. A maker of connected sensors, for example, might compare several microcontroller-class cores before choosing one. Under a conventional arrangement, each of those comparisons could require its own license negotiation.

Compute Subsystems address a later stage of the design process. A modern processor is more than its CPU cores. The cores have to connect to each other, to memory and to other parts of the chip. Integrating and verifying those pieces takes significant engineering time.

A Compute Subsystem packages Arm cores with surrounding components that Arm has already integrated and tested together. A licensee starts from that working foundation instead of assembling the pieces itself. The aim is to cut the integration and verification work a chip team would otherwise do on its own.

The trade-off is control. A company that builds on a pre-integrated bundle accepts more of Arm's design choices. A company that assembles individual cores, or designs its own under an architecture license, keeps more room to differentiate its chip.

Nvidia $NVDA's $40 billion agreement to buy Arm from SoftBank, announced in September 2020, was abandoned in February 2022 after regulators in the U.S., U.K. and EU objected. Their central worry was that one chipmaker would own the designs its rivals license.

Jensen Huang co-founded Nvidia in 1993 and has led it as CEO since then. The company built its business on graphics processors. Those chips later became widely used hardware for training AI models. Buying Arm would have added a widely licensed CPU architecture to a company already known for GPUs.

SoftBank had owned Arm since 2016. SoftBank does not design or sell chips. Under the proposed deal, Arm would have moved to an owner that designs and sells chips in large volumes. That change sat at the center of the regulatory fight.

Arm's model depends on licensing the same technology to many companies, including firms that compete with one another. Several of those licensees also compete with Nvidia. The deal raised the question of whether Arm could stay neutral under an owner with its own chip products to sell.

In December 2021, the U.S. Federal Trade Commission sued to block the acquisition. The agency's complaint argued that the merger would give Nvidia control over computing technology and designs that its rivals rely on. The lawsuit meant the deal faced a court process in the U.S. on top of reviews in other jurisdictions.

Regulators in the U.K. and the EU had also raised concerns. Arm's headquarters in Cambridge, U.K., placed the deal under British review. With objections on several fronts, the companies walked away from the agreement. That came about 17 months after the deal was announced.

The collapse did not end Nvidia's use of Arm technology. Nvidia remains an Arm licensee. Its Grace CPU, designed for data centers, is built on Arm designs. Nvidia kept access to the architecture through the same licensing route that other chip designers use, rather than through ownership of Arm itself.

Rene Haas has run Arm as chief executive since February 2022, when he succeeded Simon Segars. Under Haas, Arm listed on Nasdaq $NDAQ on September 14, 2023, under the ticker ARM, with shares priced at $51.

Haas was promoted from inside the company. Before becoming CEO, he led Arm's IP products group. IP stands for intellectual property. At Arm, that term covers the processor designs and instruction set rights the company licenses to chipmakers. The group Haas ran was responsible for the products Arm sells to its customers.

Earlier in his career, Haas worked at Nvidia $NVDA. Jensen Huang co-founded that company and serves as its CEO. Nvidia is one of the chip designers that license Arm technology.

Haas took charge in the same month that Nvidia abandoned its agreement to buy Arm from SoftBank. With that sale off the table, SoftBank, which had acquired Arm in 2016, remained the owner. The Nasdaq listing followed in September 2023.

That listing was an initial public offering. In an IPO, a privately held company sells shares to outside investors. Those shares then trade on a stock exchange. The $51 figure was the offering price, set before trading began. Once trading opened, the share price was set by buyers and sellers on the market.

SoftBank kept a majority stake after the offering. Holding more than half the shares means SoftBank owns more of Arm than all other shareholders combined. Public investors gained a piece of the company, but control stayed with SoftBank.

The ticker ARM is the short code investors use to buy and sell the stock on Nasdaq. Nasdaq is a U.S. exchange based in New York. Arm's shares trade there even though the company's headquarters are in Cambridge, U.K.

The listing changed who owns Arm. It did not change the licensing business at the center of the company. A public listing does add a daily share price. That price gives investors a running measure of how they value the business Haas oversees.

Arm faces competition from two architectures that sit at opposite ends of the licensing spectrum. RISC-V is an open instruction set that companies can use without paying license fees. x86, at the other end, is a closed architecture made by Intel $INTC and AMD $AMD.

RISC-V began as a project at the University of California, Berkeley. Like Arm, it follows the reduced instruction set computing approach, which relies on a small set of simple instructions. The difference lies in ownership. The RISC-V specification is published openly. Any company can design a processor that implements it without signing a license with an architecture owner.

That openness offers a direct alternative to the Arm licensing model. Arm charges companies for the right to use its instruction set and designs. A chipmaker that builds on RISC-V does not pay an architecture owner for the instruction set itself.

Free access to the specification does not mean free chips. A company choosing RISC-V still has to design its own processor cores or obtain finished designs from another supplier. It also has to make sure compilers, operating systems and applications run properly on the result. That engineering work carries real cost. It is also part of what Arm sells when it offers ready-made designs.

x86 presents a different kind of competition. Intel created the architecture, and Intel and AMD are its principal makers. The two companies design and sell their own x86 processors. They do not license designs to a wide pool of customers the way Arm does. Decades of desktop, laptop and server software were written for x86. That installed base keeps the architecture entrenched in PCs and in many data centers.

Arm's position against both rivals rests less on any single chip than on its ecosystem. Operating systems, development tools and applications have been compiled for Arm over many years. Software is built for a specific instruction set. A large library of programs that already runs on Arm makes switching away more costly for device makers.

The same principle works against Arm where x86 holds the advantage. Moving PC or server software to Arm requires recompiling it or running it through translation. The contest among these three architectures turns largely on which software already runs where. It also turns on what it costs a chip designer to change.
