My Ski Helmet Looks Perfectly Fine. Here’s Why I’m Replacing It Anyway.

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

Last February, on the final day of SKI’s annual ski test, I caught an edge in variable snow conditions while testing carving skis. I went, as we say, over the handlebars, landing directly on top of my head and compressing my neck.

I knew I’d hit my head, but at the time, I didn’t think much of it. It wasn’t until the following day that I began to understand how hard I’d actually hit the snow.

A few days later, I was sitting in my doctor’s office being diagnosed with a concussion and a neck injury. For months afterward, I dealt with headaches, neck pain, and sensitivity to light and sound. Some nights I’d wake up at 3 or 4 a.m. with pain radiating from the base of my skull, unable to get comfortable enough to fall back asleep. The hardest part was how unpredictable it all felt. I’d think I was getting better, only to have the symptoms flare up again.

The good news? I was wearing a helmet that may have spared me a much more serious head injury. The bad news? Now I have to throw that helmet away.

If I hadn’t developed concussion symptoms, I probably wouldn’t have thought twice about wearing it again. My helmet was brand new, and from the outside, there’s no obvious damage. But apparently, that says little about what’s going on underneath.

Needing a little extra convincing to toss out what looks like a perfectly good, well-fitting helmet, I turned to Madelen Fahlstedt, Ph.D., a biomechanical specialist at MIPS, a helmet-safety technology company. Fahlstedt holds a doctorate in medical engineering from Sweden’s KTH Royal Institute of Technology, where she researched head-injury biomechanics and accident reconstruction.

She explains that most ski helmets contain a layer of expanded polystyrene (EPS) foam designed to absorb impact energy by compressing or deforming. Once it takes a serious hit, that deformation is permanent, meaning the affected foam can’t absorb the same energy again.

“The outer shell can hide these deformations in the EPS. Even from the inside of the helmet, the deformations can be hard to detect,” Fahlstedt says. “To that sense, a helmet is essentially a one-hit-wonder.”

Traditional ski helmets are designed primarily to absorb the energy of an impact, particularly the forces associated with linear motion, when your head strikes something and comes to an abrupt stop. These forces can cause serious injuries, including skull fractures.

But real-world ski crashes aren’t always so straightforward.

When your head hits the snow at an angle, it can also rotate suddenly. That rotational motion can cause different parts of the brain to move relative to one another, stretching delicate tissues.

“The brain is typically more sensitive to rotational motion than linear motion because it has shear properties similar to water or gel,” Fahlstedt explains.

Both linear and rotational forces can contribute to brain injuries, which is why some helmet manufacturers have introduced technologies designed to address rotational motion.

MIPS, for example, doesn’t manufacture helmets. Rather, it develops safety technology that manufacturers can incorporate into their designs. So you might find a MIPS system inside a Smith helmet or a Giro helmet, even though the two models look, fit, and function differently.

Its best-known technology allows a layer inside the helmet to move about 10 to 15 millimeters during certain angled impacts. That small amount of movement is designed to reduce the rotational motion transferred to your head. Depending on the helmet, the layer might be visible or built into the padding.

MIPS is just one approach, however, and no technology can guarantee protection against concussion.

In fact, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention is clear that no helmet is concussion-proof. Still, helmets provide meaningful protection. According to a 2022 review by the American Academy of Pediatrics, skiers and snowboarders who wear helmets have roughly 34 percent lower odds of suffering a head injury than those who don’t.

According to Fahlstedt, a helmet should be replaced after a significant impact, regardless of whether there’s visible damage.

The challenge, of course, is figuring out what counts as significant.

A helmet that rolls off a tailgate or gets knocked against the chairlift safety bar isn’t necessarily in the same category as one that absorbs the force of your head hitting hardpack. But a crash doesn’t have to look or feel dramatic to involve substantial forces.

Mine certainly didn’t. Without the concussion diagnosis, I’m not sure I would have considered it a significant impact at all. But as the CDC notes, concussion symptoms can take hours or even days to appear, so how you feel immediately after a crash isn’t necessarily a reliable indicator of injury. If you suspect a concussion, stop skiing and seek medical evaluation.

It’s tempting to look at nearly new gear, especially something that wasn’t cheap, and convince yourself it’s probably fine. But a helmet isn’t like a scratched pair of goggles or a jacket with a torn sleeve that can make it through another season with a little duct tape.

Mine has already done its job once. I’m not asking it to do it again.
