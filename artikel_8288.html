Swallowed by the Camino Green Tunnel

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

The room we slept in was the top floor of a centuries-old, three-story building with a modern interior. Tuck-pointed right under the roof, we slept soundly through the night as periodic thunder and a heavy rain provided peaceful white noise overhead.

Stepping outside the next morning, sunrise illuminated a surreal sky, backlighting the massive monastery. We paused at a local cafe for a quick breakfast before leaving Samos behind.

Our hike started with a few miles of road walk before the Camino veered onto a forested, ancient track. Thick rock walls lined the path, guarded by groves of massive chestnut trees.

As the morning sun broke through, the air naturally turned humid. We began a steady climb along a narrow path where pilgrims over the years had woven stick crosses directly into the fencing alongside the trail.

Highlight of the Day: We ran into Anthony from Oxford–who was walking his fifth Camino. He explained that this sunken section of path is called a Holloway. Worn deep into the earth over centuries of foot traffic, it creates a stunning green tunnel with 12-foot mossy walls and a dense canopy of ancient trees overhead.

Our “tramily” friends, Jill and Robert, caught up with us midmorning, and the miles flew by as we chatted. We meandered from one quaint hamlet to the next along quite country roads. By 10:30 AM, we only had 2.5 miles left for the day.

We soon reached Foxos, a tiny village with cute medieval farm houses, old barns, and a historic church. From there, the path climbed steeply toward Aguiada, where our trail merged back on the main Camino Frances route. We were completely out of water, but this small village didn’t have any water sources available.

Reconnecting with the main route brought a dramatic shift. Because you only need to walk the final 100 km to earn a Compostela certificate, and since it was the start of the weekend, the trail was suddenly packed with weekend hikers and new pilgrims starting their journey from Sarria.

It instantly felt like a different Camino–less intimate, much more crowded, and distinctly touristy.

We rolled into Sarria at 1:00 PM. Built into a hillside, Sarria is a charming medieval city full of narrow, winding cobblestone streets.

Our accommodation was easily one of our favorites so far:

While Knockers immediately crashed for a two-hour nap, I got to work planning our journey past Santiago.

Next Steps: We officially decided that after reaching Santiago, we will hike out to Finisterre—traditionally known as “the end of the world” on the Atlantic coast. From there, we’ll return to Santiago and head south along the Portuguese Caminos. (There are a few ferry connections and complex logistics to map out for that stretch!)

Later that evening, we walked downhill to a local grocery store and definitely bought too much food. Being hungry and finding fresh, real ingredients for the first time in a long while made it impossible to resist.

Back at the apartment, we cooked up a huge meal and ate until we couldn’t take another bite. By the time “hiker midnight” rolled around, we crashed hard for another peaceful night’s rest.
