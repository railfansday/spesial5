Mapping the ADHD Brain: How Neurotransmitters and Medication Work

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

The unique structures, wiring, and functionality of neurodivergent brains, according to science.

Why do one person’s ADHD symptoms differ from another’s? The answer lurks inside the structural and chemical differences of the ADHD brain.

No single brain region is the source of ADHD. However, subtle variations in brain structure, function, connectivity, and neurochemistry shape the neural networks that govern attention, motivation, reward, emotion, and self-control.

The brain comprises billions of nerve cells called neurons. These neurons work in circuits, passing signals to each other that allow our bodies to think, move, remember, manage emotions, and control impulses.

Between neurons is a tiny gap called a synapse. To get messages across, neurons release chemical messengers called neurotransmitters into the gap. The neurotransmitters cross the gap and attach to receptors on the next neuron to help the message continue. Afterward, the brain clears neurotransmitters away in a process called reuptake.

The neurotransmitters dopamine (DE) and norepinephrine (NE) help the PFC hold goals and instructions in mind, filter distractions, and guide behavior. They also help reward circuits decide what feels interesting, urgent, or worth doing.

In ADHD, these signals don’t support those circuits and processes as strongly or consistently as they should. Task-relevant information may fade too quickly, while distractions, impulses, emotions or immediate rewards may pull more strongly. This is why ADHD is sometimes described as a “signal-to-noise” problem: the useful signal is there, but it may be drowned out by competing noise.

ADHD is linked to differences in how brain networks communicate and coordinate attention, motivation, emotion, and self-regulation.

ADHD medication can optimize brain signals by slowing the reuptake of DE or NE, increasing their release, or activating receptors that strengthen signaling. All of this helps guide attention, motivation, and self-control.

COMPARING THE IMPACTS OF ADHD MEDICATIONS

New insights: Functional connectivity data show that stimulants enhance performance by increasing vigilance and perceived task value.11 In other words, stimulants help the ADHD brain stay motivated and engaged with non-preferred tasks.

In addition to dopamine and norepinephrine, a third critical neurotransmitter is serotonin, which helps to regulate mood and emotion, both deeply relevant to the ADHD experience.

Emotional dysregulation is a core feature of ADHD, and many people with the condition also live with mood disorders. Almost half of adults with ADHD are also prescribed selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) – medications that increase serotonin levels to regulate mood, emotion, and sleep.

Viloxazine, a non-stimulant, became the first ADHD medication in the U.S. to also modulate serotonin. Beyond targeting ADHD symptoms, the medication was shown to improve co-occurring depression and anxiety in adults with all three conditions.12

Centanafadine (Simtriyo), a stimulant ADHD medication recently approved for children aged 6 years and older and adults, is a norepinephrine, dopamine, and serotonin reuptake inhibitor (NDSRI). Trials show that the medication improves symptoms in both groups and in adults with ADHD and co-occurring anxiety.13

Untreated ADHD impacts neural connectivity.14 Unstable brain connections increase risk for the development of anxiety, depression, insomnia, emotional frustration, and other challenges. ADHD medication enhances neural connectivity, but there are other ways to promote neural growth, protect the brain, and decrease risk for secondary issues.
