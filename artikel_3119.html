The Nasdaq Composite Just Shattered Its Single-Session Point-Loss Record: 3 Reasons These Elevator-Down Moves May Be Just Beginning

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

The artificial intelligence (AI)-driven Nasdaq Composite plunged 1,422 points on Nov. 5.

To say it was a rough day for Wall Street's major stock indexes would be an understatement. The timeless Dow Jones Industrial Average (^DJI -3.44%) shed 1,833 points, the benchmark S&P 500 (^GSPC -4.36%) lost 335 points, and the artificial intelligence (AI)-driven Nasdaq Composite (^IXIC -5.52%) plummeted 1,422 points on Nov. 5.

While the Nasdaq Composite's 5.52% decline didn't crack the 20 largest percentage drops on record, its 1,422-point single-session loss shattered its previous record tumble of 1,122 points, set on June 5, 2026.

The uncomfortable reality for investors is that there are three reasons these elevator-down moves may be just beginning.

Prediction markets widely forecast that Democrats would sweep Congress on Election Night, and that's exactly what happened. By early January, President Donald Trump will be working with a Democrat-led Congress.

Trump has repeatedly stated that he'll use his presidential veto power to effectively maintain the status quo. The Dow, S&P 500, and Nasdaq Composite initially rallied on Nov. 4 with the understanding that the president could veto stricter AI regulation bills sent to his desk from a Democrat-controlled Congress.

But what investors overlooked was a "blue wave" at the gubernatorial level. Democratic governors flipped five former Republican seats (two state elections have yet to be called by the Associated Press). Three of these flipped seats (Texas, Georgia, and Ohio) are focal points for data center planning and construction.

Democratic Governor-elect Gina Hinojosa of Texas has pledged to ban data center approvals until her state's legislature passes laws on electricity, water, air, and property rights. Most Democratic lawmakers favor limiting data center expansion and stricter testing/vetting on large language models.

In other words, stricter AI regulation at the state level can circumvent Trump's veto power.

This could be particularly damaging to AI hardware companies that have enjoyed never-before-seen pricing power. In addition to insatiable enterprise demand for AI infrastructure, persistent supply shortages have lifted the pricing power of AI hardware providers. If data center approval moratoriums result in a more balanced supply-vs-demand outlook for hardware, the pricing power and gross margin of leading AI stocks may crater.

Secondly, the bond market has, for months, been sending an unmistakable warning to equity markets that the AI infrastructure build-out was in trouble.

When public companies issue debt, it's typically priced at a par value of $1,000, which is quoted as $1 on financial websites. Given that bond prices and yields are inversely related, yields go up if a bond trades below its par value.

The reasons bond prices might decline include skepticism about the long-term solvency of the underlying company or bond traders' desire to receive a higher yield given the implied risk factors.

Credit markets are flashing WARNING SIGNS across the AI trade: Oracle, $ORCL, 5-year credit default swap (CDS) spread has surged to ~263 basis points, while SpaceX's has risen to ~203 basis points, both at record highs. At the same time, CoreWeave's $CRWV, 5-year CDS is up to a record 982 basis points. This comes as tech companies have issued nearly $500 billion in new debt this year to finance AI infrastructure, with 9 US investment-grade bond deals worth at least $25 billion each. The growing debt burden is colliding with uncertainty over whether AI revenues will justify the enormous spending. As a result, investors are increasingly demanding higher compensation to take on credit risk. Oracle's market-implied 5-year default risk has risen above 20%, compared with around 16% for SpaceX and more than 7% for Nvidia. The pressure could intensify further, with Goldman Sachs projecting hyperscaler debt issuance to jump from $250 billion in 2026 to a massive $420 billion in 2027, as 35% of next year's capital spending is expected to be debt-funded. The AI boom is now testing credit markets, as investors begin repricing the risks behind Big Tech's unprecedented borrowing spree.

Several foundational companies tied to the AI infrastructure build-out have seen their bond prices collapse. Space Exploration Technologies' (SPCX -9.05%) recently issued 2056 notes are trading at $0.83 on the dollar; Nvidia's (NVDA -9.26%) 2060 notes are clocking in at $0.63 on the dollar; and CoreWeave's (CRWV -9.83%) 2031 notes (issued this year) are trading at $0.78 on the dollar.

These discounts to par value denote mounting skepticism regarding the sustainability of the AI data center build-out.

The stock market also entered 2026 at its second-priciest valuation in history, according to the S&P 500's Shiller Price-to-Earnings (P/E) Ratio, also known as the Cyclically Adjusted P/E Ratio (CAPE Ratio).

Whereas the time-tested P/E ratio is arrived at using trailing 12-month earnings, the Shiller P/E Ratio is based on average inflation-adjusted earnings over the trailing decade. When recessions arise and corporate earnings turn negative, the Shiller P/E Ratio remains useful, which isn't always the case for the traditional P/E ratio.

When backtested to January 1871, the CAPE Ratio has surpassed 30 on six occasions, including the present. Following each of the previous five instances, the Dow Jones Industrial Average, S&P 500, and/or Nasdaq Composite eventually (keyword!) plunged between 20% and 89%.

Although the Shiller P/E Ratio offers no help in deciphering precisely when the stock market will reverse course or which catalyst will push it over the edge, 156 years of history conclusively show that premium valuations aren't sustainable over the long run.

While AI growth rates have been off the charts, every next-big-thing investment trend for more than 30 years has eventually endured a bubble-bursting event early in its expansion. Historical precedent suggests it's not a matter of if but of when the AI bubble bursts.
