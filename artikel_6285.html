Pregnancy has a hidden genetic tug-of-war, and a tiny fish offers new clues

Tanggal: 2026-11-15

A small fish offers new clues to a hidden genetic tug-of-war during pregnancy, when mothers and developing babies may have competing needs.

A pregnant mother and the baby growing inside her are usually assumed to want the same thing.

Evolutionary theory suggests their interests do not always align: a developing baby may benefit from receiving more nutrients than its mother can afford to provide.

Genetic signs of that struggle have now been found in a small fish called the blackstripe livebearer, which feeds its young through a placenta.

In its placenta, some genes are active mainly from the copy inherited from the father, while others are active mainly from the mother’s copy. This pattern is known as genomic imprinting.

Until now the pattern had been firmly shown only in mammals and flowering plants. To the knowledge of the research team, this fish is the first vertebrate outside the mammals in which it has been found.

The genetic work was led by Ying Zhang and Xu Wang at Auburn University College of Veterinary Medicine together with the evolutionary biologist David Reznick at the University of California, Riverside.

All of the work was carried out on a single species of fish bred in the laboratory.

The theory behind the study was not always accepted by Reznick himself. When it was first suggested to him years ago that mothers and unborn babies might have competing interests, he dismissed the idea.

“I thought it was the silliest thing I’d ever heard,” Reznick said. “I rejected the idea out of hand.”

The idea depends on the number of genes that are shared between relatives. A mother is equally related to all of her children.

From an evolutionary perspective, it benefits her to balance the needs of her current offspring with her own survival and future reproduction.

Only half of a baby’s genes are shared with a full brother or sister, and only a quarter are shared with a half sibling.

For this reason, genes that help one baby obtain extra food can spread through a population even when the cost is paid by the mother’s later children.

“What’s in the best interest of the baby to get from its mother is more than what’s in the mother’s best interest to give,” Reznick said.

The greatest benefit from that extra demand is gained by genes inherited from the father, because the mother’s next baby may have a different father.

In mammals this is thought to explain why one parent’s copy of certain growth genes is switched off while only the other parent’s copy is used.

In most fish an egg is supplied with all the food a baby will need before it is fertilized. The family that includes guppies and mollies is different.

Live young are produced by every species in this family. A placenta has also evolved within the family on at least nine separate occasions.

The blackstripe livebearer is one of those cases. A newborn is seven times heavier in dry weight than the egg from which it developed, and the difference is supplied by its mother during pregnancy.

A female usually carries three or four broods at once, each at a different developmental stage.

Theory predicts that genes inherited from the two parents may favor different levels of nutrient transfer from mother to offspring.

Earlier searches in other animals produced no such result. In egg-laying mammals, in birds and in a lizard with a placenta, both parents’ copies were used for genes that are imprinted in humans and mice.

The fish were originally collected from Rio El Palillo in Sinaloa, Mexico.

Two separate family lines were bred by Reznick’s team. Within each line, brothers were mated with sisters for nine to 12 generations until the fish within each line were nearly genetically identical.

The two lines were then crossed in both directions. In one cross a mother from the first line was paired with a father from the second line, and in the other cross the arrangement was reversed.

Small spelling differences between the DNA of the two lines were used to determine which parent each copy of a gene had come from.

The body of the baby, the baby’s side of the placenta and the mother’s surrounding tissue were separated from late-stage embryos.

The active genes in each tissue were then read, and 351,362 spelling differences were tracked across more than 24,000 genes.

A gene was counted as imprinted only when the same parent’s copy dominated in both directions of the cross. Under the strictest test, 84 protein-making genes met this standard.

Of those genes, 55 were used mainly from the mother’s copy and 29 mainly from the father’s copy.

Most of these genes were imprinted only in the placenta and not in the body of the baby. This pattern was expected by the researchers if imprinting exists to settle how much food passes from mother to baby.

More than 2,000 genes were found to be far more active in the placenta than in the body of the baby.

On statistical grounds, a gene called dpp4 stood out most clearly. In a second laboratory test, between 81% and 91% of its activity was found to come from the father’s copy.

Directly beside it lies a gene called gcga, which was used mainly from the mother’s copy. In mammals the equivalent genes are known to work against each other.

In mammals, the equivalent of gcga produces a hormone that limits nutrient transfer through the placenta. The protein produced by dpp4 inactivates that hormone.

If the same roles are played by the fish genes, a limit set by the mother’s gene would be switched off by the father’s gene.

These roles have not yet been tested in fish by the researchers. However, the arrangement matches a known pair of growth genes in mammal pregnancies.

A fish version of grb10, a gene that slows the growth of the placenta and the fetus in people and mice, was used mainly from the mother’s copy. The same is true in human pregnancy.

In people a gene that moves amino acids, the building blocks of protein, through the placenta is used from the father’s copy. In the fish the same was found for a close relative of that gene.

“We like to think there’s something special about mammals,” Reznick said. “But it turns out these aren’t such special features.”

Fish and mammals last shared an ancestor hundreds of millions of years ago, before either had developed a placenta. Their similar gene patterns therefore evolved separately.

One gene, dpp4, offers a clue. In humans and zebrafish, it is most active in the gut, which absorbs nutrients.

Researchers also studied genes linked to dpp4 across 71 mammal species. Nine of its 15 closest partners were at least twice as active in the fish placenta as in the baby’s body.

This suggests that fish and mammals independently used similar gut genes to develop their placentas.

All of these results come from a single species. Only three genes could be checked with a second laboratory test because very little material can be obtained from a fish placenta.

In two of those three genes, the parent bias was weaker or less consistent than in the first test.

Chemical tags were also found on the DNA beside dpp4 and beside the amino acid gene. In mammals, tags of this kind help to silence one copy.

In the fish, it could not yet be determined which parent each tagged copy had come from.

In people, errors in imprinted genes are linked to developmental disorders. How the tags are set and maintained in the fish placenta remains unknown.

A ready test is offered by the fish family itself. A placenta evolved within it on at least eight further occasions and often alongside close relatives that lack one.

Future research could examine whether imprinting appears every time a placenta does.

The full study was published in the journal Science Advances.
