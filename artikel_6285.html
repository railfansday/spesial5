The biggest concern about toddler screen time may be what children are missing

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Many toddlers use screens daily, potentially missing valuable opportunities to play, talk, and learn through real human interaction.

At 18 months, children are learning to name things, put words together and make sense of the world around them. Many are also becoming quite familiar with television and smartphones.

In Hungary, researchers found that 49% of children around this age used screens every day.

There has been plenty of debate over how much screen time is appropriate for young children. A new review, however, looks at a different side of the problem.

How much time is left for talking, playing and being with other people?

Krisztina Kopcsó, a researcher at the HCSO Institute for Quantitative Population and Economic Research in Budapest, led the review.

Kopcsó and her colleagues drew on data from a Hungarian national survey and research from around the world published since 2015.

Among the authors was Ákos Pogány from the Department of Ethology at Eötvös Loránd University (ELTE).

“Young children learn primarily through actively exploring the world and interacting with others, rather than through screens. One of the greatest risks of introducing digital devices too early is that their use takes time away from precisely these essential experiences,” Pogány said.

Screen exposure before age two is discouraged by the World Health Organization and the American Academy of Pediatrics. Video chatting with distant family members is treated as an exception.

A more restrictive position has been adopted in France. Complete screen abstinence until age three was recommended by an expert committee appointed by the French President.

A recent Hungarian expert guideline also recommends avoiding screens before age three.

Despite these recommendations, early screen exposure is widespread. A 2022 meta-analysis included 63 studies and more than 89,000 children.

Only 24.7% of children under two were found to meet the guideline of no screen use.

In the United States, children under two averaged 63 minutes of screen media per day in 2024. These data were reported in the Common Sense Media census. By age two, 40% of children owned their own tablet.

Objective measurements were used in the Australian EUiLO cohort, described by Brushe and colleagues in 2023. A child-worn device recorded electronic noise for 16 hours on a typical day.

Families with lower educational attainment were oversampled among the 207 children. At 24 months, no screen exposure was recorded for only 2.4% of them.

Mean daily exposure in that cohort was 76.1 minutes at 6 months and 148.3 minutes at 24 months.

The Hungarian data were obtained from the Cohort ’18 Growing Up in Hungary panel study. A representative sample was recruited during pregnancy.

Maternal reports for 2,569 children were collected in 2019 and 2020 and analyzed by Kopcsó and colleagues in 2024. The children were aged between 15 and 23 months.

The database was weighted for maternal age, education, parity and marital status.

Daily screen exposure was reported for 49% of the children. More than one hour of exposure on a typical day was recorded for 37.5%. No exposure was reported for 14%.

Among daily users, mean screen time on a typical day was 122.5 minutes.

Most children were cared for at home. Only 6.7% attended formal childcare at the time of the survey.

Television viewing was identified as the most frequent screen activity. It was watched every day by 43.6% of the children.

Videos on mobile devices were watched daily by 21.2% of children. Active touchscreen use on a daily basis was reported for 18.7%.

Higher screen time was observed in lower-income households and among children of less educated mothers. Both gradients were statistically significant in the 2024 analysis.

A similar gradient was reported in a 2024 German study of 3,035 children by Paulus and colleagues. Parents there described using screens when time was short.

The contexts of screen use were also recorded in the Cohort ’18 survey.

Screen use while having fun with the child was reported by 62.1% of mothers.

Screens were allowed during household chores by 53.5%. Use for perceived developmental purposes was reported by 52.8%.

Background television was reported as a regular practice in 31.4% of families. It was rare or absent in 39.1%. The mother’s own relaxation was cited as a reason by 13.9%.

In Hungary, 19.6% of toddlers watched screens during meals, and 12.4% used them while falling asleep.

Children who spent more time with screens were read to and played with slightly less often, even after accounting for family income and mothers’ education.

However, busy parents may have less time for these activities, regardless of screen use.

A 2017 study linked screen use during more daily routines to delays in social and emotional development.

Another study in 2018 found that children who spent less time with screens at age two had better self-control at age four.

Apps and streaming content for babies are often marketed as educational.

A 2021 meta-analysis published in Child Development examined how well young children learn from videos compared with real-life demonstrations. Children aged 0 to 6 were included.

Less learning was found from video than from equivalent live demonstrations, and the gap narrowed with age.

Word learning from video was supported by adult co-viewing in a 2018 study by Strouse and colleagues.

Video chat was examined by Myers and colleagues in 2017 and by McClure and colleagues in 2018. Reciprocal interaction and joint attention cues are preserved in that format.

Screens were used to soothe or distract the child by 43% of Hungarian mothers. Frequent reliance on this strategy may reduce chances to practice self-regulation, the authors wrote.

Reciprocal associations between device use for calming and emotional reactivity were reported in a 2023 JAMA Pediatrics study.

Children aged 3 to 5 were followed. Similar associations were reported by Konok and colleagues at ELTE in 2024.

Autoplay, infinite scrolling and algorithmic recommendations are listed in the review as persuasive design features. Rapid cuts and high visual salience may also place heavy demands on immature attention.

A parent’s phone can interrupt even the simplest moments with a child. In 2018, researchers McDaniel and Radesky used the term technoference to describe these interruptions.

Studies conducted in restaurants and public places in the United States and Israel found that parents paid less attention to their children while using phones.

Some even responded rather sharply when their children sought their attention.

A 2015 study found that just hearing a phone notification was enough to distract someone, even if they ignored it.

Babies seem to notice these interruptions, too. In a 2018 study, infants whose mothers frequently used phones had a harder time returning to a happy mood, even after their mothers put the devices away.

In the French ELFE cohort, mean daily screen time at age two was 49 minutes in 2013, among 12,099 children.

Television exposure during family meals at age two was associated with poorer expressive language.

Less advanced general cognitive development was found at age 3.5, Yang and colleagues reported in 2024. Overall screen time was not the factor identified.

Earth.com has previously covered how a parent’s own phone use relates to vocabulary growth. The role of eye contact in infant language learning has also been reported.

A harm-reduction approach to infant media use was proposed by Heller in Infant Behavior and Development in 2021.

Age-appropriate limits combined with harm-reduction strategies are recommended in the review.

Background and parallel media use during caregiving routines and play should be reduced. Screen exposure during meals and before bedtime should be avoided.

Caregiver co-use is prioritized when screens are introduced in the second or third year.

Information could be provided to expecting parents through obstetric, prenatal and primary care services. Complaints about the no-screens-before-two rule have also been reported by parents.

The European Union has rules to keep children safe online. Article 28 of its Digital Services Act focuses specifically on protecting young users.

On July 14, 2025, the European Commission also issued guidelines addressing features that could make it difficult for children to stop using digital platforms.

However, these measures do not specifically address the needs of babies and toddlers.

The authors argue that keeping children safe from the potential harms of digital media should not be the responsibility of parents alone.

Reducing screen time sounds like a sensible idea, but researchers still have much to learn about its benefits for very young children.

A 2025 review of experiments involving preschoolers found that programs teaching parents about screen habits helped reduce children’s screen use.

Few studies, however, have tested similar approaches in children under two. Even fewer have followed children long enough to see whether their language skills, sleep or ability to manage emotions improved.

Future studies could test whether replacing screen time with play and reading improves language development in children under two.

For young children, what they do after the screen goes off may be just as important as switching it off in the first place.

The review was published in the journal Biologia Futura.
