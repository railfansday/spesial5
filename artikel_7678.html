Environmentalists successfully block California’s first carbon capture project

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

The ruling is a win for local environmental justice and conservation groups, including the Sierra Club and the Center for Biological Diversity, who argued the county had left “multiple environmental, public health and safety issues” unaddressed when it approved the landmark project.

Located at the Elk Hills Oil Field in the San Joaquin Valley and built by the California Resources Corporation, the state’s largest oil and gas company, TerraVault began capturing carbon dioxide from oil wells and injecting it underground in May. Once completed, the two vaults, made from depleted oil reservoirs, are expected to capture more than 2 million tons of carbon dioxide every year âÂ the equivalent of what more than 400,000 cars would emit annually.

In an email, President and CEO of the California Resources Corporation Francisco Leon expressed disappointment in the ruling, writing: “This project is the result of years of work, including close consultation with surrounding communities where we received overwhelming support for our efforts. This order is a step in the wrong direction for addressing the impacts of climate change and reflects the weaponization of CEQA by a narrow group of special interests. Some environmental groups have said TerraVault I will, in effect, extend the life of the Elk Hills Oil Field, the seventh most productive in the U.S. The California Resources Corporation has said it could also store carbon from a range of not-yet-built factories in the region, which could produce, among other things, cement and steel. These new industries, opponents say, would further pollute a low-income area already struggling with bad air quality.

In their CEQA lawsuit, the plaintiffs argued the environmental impact report, prepared by the California Resources Corporation and approved by Kern County, didn’t properly account for the eight possible industries TerraVault I could capture carbon for, even though the new factories were a “reasonably foreseeable consequence” of the project. Superior Court Judge Marcos Camacho agreed.

“The record evidence shows that all CO2 capture equipment, transportation pipelines, compression facilities, injection and monitoring wells, and underground pore space exist solely to receive and store CO2,” Camacho wrote in the ruling. “Thus, the source facilities and industries unquestionably change the scope and nature of the project… The Court finds that such omission indicates that the whole of the project was not considered, in violation of CEQA.”

The judge found plenty of other shortcomings in the environmental report, referred to in the ruling as the REIR.Â “The issue of atmospheric leakage of CO2,” for example, was not “fully addressed.” He also found the report “fails to adequately mitigate the project’s greenhouse gas impacts.”

“This is a lack of analysis in the REIR, not a disagreement among experts,” Camacho wrote.

The ruling means TerraVault must temporarily shut down. The California Resources Corporation will have to prepare a new environmental impact report, a long and costly process, before it can restart operations.

The fight over TerraVault exposes a deep and long-standing divide within the environmental movement. Some groups believe climate change is the biggest threat to life on earth, and to avoid the most dire consequences, we must employ any and all tactics to remove carbon from the atmosphere. Other groups, including the plaintiffs suing to stop TerraVault, say the way to stop climate change is to cut emissions.
